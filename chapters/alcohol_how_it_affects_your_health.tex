\chapter{Alcohol: How It Affects Your Health}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Alcohol is a psychoactive substance that affects nearly every organ in the body. Its health effects range from the short-term problems of intoxication and hangover to the serious long-term diseases caused by many years of heavy drinking. The risk is dose-related: the more a person drinks, the greater the harm, and there is no level of drinking that is entirely without risk.

This chapter describes how alcohol affects the different parts of the body, how those effects are recognised and investigated, and what can be done to reduce the harm. Because alcohol-related disease often develops silently over many years, awareness of the early warning signs and regular health checks are important.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Alcohol is one of the leading preventable causes of illness, injury, and premature death worldwide. It contributes to a large share of road crashes, falls, violence, and alcohol poisoning, and it is linked with many chronic diseases, including liver disease, heart disease, and several cancers.

A substantial proportion of adults drink at levels that increase their long-term health risk, and alcohol-related harm is a major cause of hospital admission. Alcohol misuse is also closely associated with mental health problems, and heavy drinking at a young age predicts a higher lifetime risk of dependence and alcohol-related disease.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
When alcohol is drunk, it is absorbed into the bloodstream and carried to every organ. The liver breaks it down, but the process produces toxic by-products that can damage liver cells, and heavy use can overwhelm the liver's capacity. Alcohol crosses into the brain, where it slows nerve activity, and it interferes with the way cells throughout the body use nutrients and hormones.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Liver:} alcohol is processed by the liver, and long-term heavy use causes fatty liver, inflammation, scarring (cirrhosis), and liver cancer
    \item \textbf{Brain:} it suppresses brain activity, impairs memory, and with prolonged use can cause permanent damage
    \item \textbf{Heart and blood vessels:} it raises blood pressure and can weaken the heart muscle
    \item \textbf{Digestive system:} it irritates the stomach lining and can inflame the pancreas
    \item \textbf{Hormones and nutrition:} it interferes with absorption of vitamins, particularly thiamine (vitamin B1)
    \item \textbf{Immune system:} heavy drinking weakens immunity to infection
    \item \textbf{Cancer:} alcohol damages DNA and raises the risk of several cancers, including of the mouth, throat, breast, bowel, and liver
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features depend on whether the effects are short-term or result from years of drinking.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Short-term:} relaxation, impaired judgement, slowed reactions, unsteadiness, vomiting, hangover, and blackouts
    \item \textbf{Injury:} falls, burns, drowning, and road crashes while intoxicated
    \item \textbf{Liver:} fatigue, jaundice (yellowing of the skin and eyes), abdominal swelling, and easy bruising
    \item \textbf{Stomach and pancreas:} abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and bleeding
    \item \textbf{Heart:} high blood pressure, palpitations, and shortness of breath
    \item \textbf{Brain and nerves:} poor memory, numbness or tingling in the hands and feet, tremor, and mood changes
    \item \textbf{Cancer:} alcohol is linked with cancers of the mouth, throat, voice box, breast, bowel, and liver
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of alcohol-related illness can be confused with those of other conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Liver disease from other causes, such as fatty liver in obesity or viral hepatitis
    \item Depression and anxiety, which both cause and are worsened by heavy drinking
    \item Head injury, low blood sugar, or drug overdose in someone found confused
    \item Heart disease, pancreatitis, or diabetes presenting with similar symptoms
    \item Medication side effects that mimic the effects of alcohol
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical help if you are worried about how much you drink, or if you have symptoms that may relate to alcohol such as jaundice, severe abdominal pain, blackouts, or problems with memory. Do not stop drinking suddenly without medical advice if you are dependent, because withdrawal can be dangerous.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unconsciousness, vomiting while drowsy, or slow breathing after drinking
    \item A seizure, or severe confusion or hallucinations
    \item Severe abdominal pain or vomiting of blood
    \item Chest pain, or thoughts of suicide or self-harm
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} a detailed account of drinking patterns, symptoms, and risk factors
    \item \textbf{Screening questionnaires:} such as the AUDIT, to identify hazardous and harmful drinking
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} checking for signs of liver disease, high blood pressure, and nerve damage
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} liver function tests, full blood count, blood sugar, and markers of heavy drinking such as GGT
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} ultrasound of the liver or other scans if complications are suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reducing intake:} staying within low-risk drinking guidelines and planning alcohol-free days
    \item \textbf{Treating complications:} liver, heart, and other complications are managed in their own right once identified
    \item \textbf{Nutrition:} a balanced diet, with thiamine supplements for people at risk of deficiency
    \item \textbf{Stopping smoking:} smoking greatly multiplies the cancer risk of alcohol
    \item \textbf{Treating dependence:} counselling, support groups, and medication for people who cannot cut down on their own
    \item \textbf{Regular follow-up:} monitoring liver function, blood pressure, and other health measures
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Liver disease, from fatty liver through to cirrhosis and liver failure
    \item Cancers of the mouth, throat, voice box, breast, bowel, and liver
    \item High blood pressure, heart disease, and stroke
    \item Pancreatitis and damage to the stomach lining
    \item Brain and nerve damage, including memory loss and Wernicke--Korsakoff syndrome
    \item Dependence, withdrawal, and alcohol poisoning
    \item Harm to others through accidents, violence, and family stress
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The harm from alcohol is dose-related, and reducing intake lowers the risk of most alcohol-related diseases. Some damage, such as early fatty liver, can improve substantially if drinking stops or is reduced early enough. Once cirrhosis or alcohol-related cancer has developed, the damage may not be reversible, but stopping drinking still improves the outlook. People who recognise a problem early and make changes tend to do well.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low-risk limits:} no more than ten standard drinks a week and no more than four on any one day
    \item \textbf{Alcohol-free days:} have two or more alcohol-free days each week
    \item \textbf{Avoid binge drinking:} drinking four or more standard drinks on one occasion is particularly harmful
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} do not drink when pregnant, planning pregnancy, or breastfeeding
    \item \textbf{Safety:} do not drink and drive, and avoid drinking before swimming or other risky activities
    \item \textbf{Health conditions:} if you have high blood pressure, liver disease, or other conditions, discuss alcohol with your doctor
    \item \textbf{Health check:} see your GP for a check if you drink regularly and have not had one recently
\end{itemize}
