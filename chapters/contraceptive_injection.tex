\chapter{Contraceptive injection}
\index{Contraceptive injection}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The contraceptive injection is a highly effective, long-acting reversible contraceptive (LARC) method that delivers a synthetic progestogen to prevent pregnancy. The most widely used formulation globally and in many countries is depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA), administered either as a deep intramuscular injection (150\,mg/1\,mL, e.g., Depo-Provera) or a subcutaneous injection (104\,mg/0.65\,mL, e.g., Sayana Press) every 12 to 14 weeks. By providing a continuous release of progestogen, it eliminates the need for daily compliance or use at the time of intercourse, making it a reliable and convenient option. Its clinical significance extends to managing heavy menstrual bleeding and dysmenorrhoea, although its potential impact on bone mineral density and delayed return to fertility require careful patient selection and counselling.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Globally, millions of women utilise the contraceptive injection, with usage patterns varying significantly by region. In many countries, it is estimated that approximately 2\% to 3\% of reproductive-aged women use DMPA, though it remains a particularly popular choice among adolescents, women in rural or remote areas, and individuals for whom oral contraceptives or intra-uterine devices (IUDs) are contraindicated or impractical. Peak usage is generally seen in women aged 18 to 30. Unlike user-dependent methods, its typical-use failure rate is relatively low, making it an important tool in reducing rates of unplanned pregnancy.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The primary mechanism of the contraceptive injection is the systemic delivery of high doses of progestogen, which exerts multiple contraceptive effects.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inhibition of ovulation:} DMPA suppresses the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis, preventing the mid-cycle surge of luteinising hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), thereby preventing follicular maturation and ovulation.
    \item \textbf{Cervical mucus alteration:} Progestogen increases the viscosity and density of the cervical mucus, creating a barrier that is highly hostile to sperm penetration.
    \item \textbf{Endometrial thinning:} The constant progestogen exposure induces endometrial atrophy, making the uterine lining thin, inactive, and unreceptive to blastocyst implantation should fertilisation occur.
    \item \textbf{Tubal motility reduction:} It alters the ciliary action and peristalsis within the fallopian tubes, slowing the transport of ova and sperm.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of a patient using the contraceptive injection is defined primarily by its therapeutic effects and common side-effect profile.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Menstrual changes:} Irregular spotting or breakthrough bleeding is common in the first few months. With continued use, progressive endometrial atrophy leads to amenorrhoea in up to 50\% of women by 12 months.
    \item \textbf{Weight gain:} Users may experience modest weight gain, often associated with an increase in appetite, averaging 1.5\,kg to 3\,kg over the first two years of use.
    \item \textbf{Mood changes and headaches:} Some individuals report mood fluctuations, depressive symptoms, fatigue, or mild tension headaches.
    \item \textbf{Delayed return to fertility:} Unlike other LARC methods, there is a characteristic delay in the return of regular ovulation and fertility, averaging 8 to 10 months after the last injection, and occasionally up to 18 months.
    \item \textbf{Injection site reactions:} Localised pain, redness, swelling, or rare sterile abscesses and cutaneous atrophy at the injection site.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When evaluating patients on the contraceptive injection who present with unexpected symptoms, alternative medical conditions must be ruled out.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} Despite high efficacy, irregular bleeding or amenorrhoea may occasionally prompt investigations to rule out intrauterine or ectopic pregnancy.
    \item \textbf{Pathological amenorrhoea:} Secondary amenorrhoea due to polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), hyperprolactinaemia, thyroid dysfunction, or premature ovarian insufficiency must be considered if amenorrhoea persists after discontinuing DMPA.
    \item \textbf{Gynaecological pathology:} Severe or prolonged breakthrough bleeding should not be assumed to be solely due to DMPA; pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), endometrial polyps, cervical lesions, or sexually transmitted infections (STIs) must be excluded.
    \item \textbf{Primary depressive disorders:} New-onset or worsening mood disturbances should be differentiated from clinical depression unrelated to hormonal therapy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should consult a healthcare practitioner if they experience persistent or intolerable side effects, suspect they might be pregnant, or are approaching the two-year mark of continuous use to review bone health.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe chest pain, sudden shortness of breath, or coughing up blood (possible pulmonary embolism)
    \item Sudden weakness or numbness in the face, arm, or leg, difficulty speaking, or sudden visual disturbances (signs of stroke)
    \item Severe, persistent pain or swelling in one calf (suggestive of deep vein thrombosis)
    \item Severe, acute lower abdominal or pelvic pain (to rule out ectopic pregnancy or ovarian cyst accidents)
    \item Signs of a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) immediately following the injection, such as hives, swelling of the face, tongue, or throat, and difficulty breathing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The initiation and monitoring of the contraceptive injection rely primarily on clinical assessment, although selective investigations may be indicated.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical history and risk assessment:} Assessing for contraindications such as active breast cancer, severe cardiovascular disease, multiple cardiovascular risk factors, or unexplained vaginal bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test:} A urine or serum beta-hCG test must be performed if the injection is administered outside the standard five-day window of a normal menstrual cycle or if pregnancy is suspected.
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure and weight monitoring:} Regular assessment of blood pressure and body mass index (BMI) to monitor cardiovascular and metabolic risks.
    \item \textbf{Bone mineral density (BMD) scan:} Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) is not routinely recommended for all users but may be considered for women with existing risk factors for osteoporosis who wish to use the injection long-term.
    \item \textbf{STI screening:} Performed as part of routine sexual health check-ups, as DMPA does not protect against STIs.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of patients using the contraceptive injection involves proper administration, adherence to schedules, and lifestyle advice.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Administration technique:} Intramuscular DMPA must be injected deeply into the gluteal or deltoid muscle without massaging the site, which could accelerate hormone absorption. Subcutaneous DMPA is injected into the anterior thigh or abdomen.
    \item \textbf{Scheduling and timing:} Injections must be scheduled every 12 weeks (up to 14 weeks maximum). If the interval exceeds 14 weeks, pregnancy must be ruled out, and barrier contraception used for 7 days post-injection.
    \item \textbf{Managing bleeding disturbances:} Breakthrough bleeding can be managed short-term with a course of combined oral contraceptive pills (if not contraindicated) or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs).
    \item \textbf{Bone health optimisation:} Encouraging adequate dietary calcium intake, vitamin D supplementation, and weight-bearing exercise, particularly for those using the method for more than two years.
    \item \textbf{Transition planning:} Discussion of future reproductive plans, highlighting the potential delay in the return of fertility when planning to discontinue the injection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While generally safe, the contraceptive injection is associated with specific clinical complications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Loss of bone mineral density:} DMPA reduces endogenous oestrogen levels, leading to a small, dose-dependent decrease in BMD. While this loss is largely reversible upon discontinuation, long-term use (more than 2 years) requires a careful risk-benefit evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Prolonged return to fertility:} Delay in conception can cause distress if not anticipated, making it less suitable for women planning a pregnancy in the near future.
    \item \textbf{Persistent metabolic changes:} Some women experience glucose intolerance or adverse lipid profile changes, compounding cardiovascular risk in pre-disposed individuals.
    \item \textbf{Heavy or prolonged bleeding:} Although amenorrhoea is common, a minority of patients suffer from persistent, bothersome spotting or heavy bleeding.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for contraceptive efficacy is excellent, with a failure rate of less than 0.2\% with perfect use and approximately 6\% with typical use. Normal ovarian function and endometrial thickness recover after discontinuation, though the return of fertility is characteristically delayed. Studies show that bone mineral density recovers substantially in most women within 1 to 2 years after stopping DMPA, with no long-term increase in the risk of osteoporotic fractures demonstrated in the general population.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Patients can take proactive steps to manage their treatment and minimise side effects.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Maintain a strict calendar:} Keep track of injection due dates using digital reminders or clinic booking systems to avoid gaps in protection.
    \item \textbf{Optimise bone health:} Ensure a daily intake of 1,000\,mg of calcium and adequate vitamin D through diet, safe sun exposure, or supplements.
    \item \textbf{Engage in weight-bearing exercise:} Participate regularly in activities like walking, running, or strength training to support bone strength and manage weight.
    \item \textbf{Avoid smoking and excessive alcohol:} Both smoking and high alcohol consumption accelerate bone loss and increase cardiovascular risk.
    \item \textbf{Use dual protection:} Always use condoms alongside the injection if there is a risk of sexually transmitted infections.
\end{itemize}
