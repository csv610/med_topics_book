\chapter{GORD (Gastro-Oesophageal Reflux Disease)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) is a common chronic condition in which stomach acid and contents flow backwards into the oesophagus (the food pipe), causing troublesome symptoms and sometimes damage to the oesophageal lining. Occasional reflux, such as heartburn after a large meal, is normal, but GORD is defined by symptoms occurring twice a week or more, or by evidence of oesophagitis on endoscopy. The most typical symptoms are heartburn and regurgitation. GORD affects people of all ages and is managed with lifestyle measures, medicines that reduce acid production, and in selected cases surgery. Most people with GORD respond well to treatment and maintain a good quality of life.

\section*{Epidemiology}
GORD is one of the most common gastrointestinal conditions worldwide. In Australia, around 10--15\% of adults experience heartburn or reflux symptoms at least weekly, and up to 1 in 4 report symptoms monthly. Prevalence increases with age and is higher in people who are overweight, in smokers, and in those who drink alcohol. GORD is more common in Western countries, partly reflecting diet, obesity rates, and body fat distribution. The condition accounts for a large number of GP consultations and prescriptions for acid-suppressing medicines, which are among the most widely used medicines in the world.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
GORD results from failure of the normal barriers that keep stomach contents out of the oesophagus.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lower oesophageal sphincter dysfunction:} the ring of muscle at the junction of the oesophagus and stomach relaxes inappropriately or has low resting pressure, allowing reflux
    \item \textbf{Hiatus hernia:} part of the stomach slides up into the chest, weakening the barrier and promoting reflux
    \item \textbf{Obesity:} increased abdominal pressure pushes stomach contents upward
    \item \textbf{Delayed gastric emptying:} a full stomach is more likely to reflux
    \item \textbf{Dietary triggers:} fatty foods, chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, and spicy foods can relax the sphincter or increase acid
    \item \textbf{Smoking and certain medicines:} relax the sphincter or irritate the oesophagus
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} hormonal changes and abdominal pressure commonly cause reflux
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms of GORD are usually characteristic, but they vary between individuals in severity and frequency.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Heartburn: a burning sensation rising from the stomach into the chest or throat, often after meals or when lying down
    \item Regurgitation: sour or bitter-tasting fluid coming back into the mouth or throat
    \item Chest pain that can mimic heart pain
    \item Difficulty or pain on swallowing
    \item A sensation of a lump in the throat (globus sensation)
    \item Chronic cough, especially at night
    \item Hoarse voice, sore throat, and clearing the throat frequently
    \item Excess saliva or water brash
    \item Symptoms worse after large meals, at night, or when bending forward
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Chest and upper abdominal symptoms have many causes, and some are serious, so careful assessment matters.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cardiac disease:} angina and myocardial infarction can cause chest pain and must be excluded, especially in people with risk factors
    \item Peptic ulcer disease and gastritis
    \item Gallbladder disease, including gallstones and cholecystitis
    \item Oesophageal disorders: achalasia, oesophageal spasm, and eosinophilic oesophagitis
    \item Functional dyspepsia, where symptoms occur without visible inflammation
    \item Pancreatic disease
    \item Oesophageal cancer, which is rare but must be considered with persistent difficulty swallowing, weight loss, or bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See your GP if heartburn occurs two or more times a week, if symptoms persist despite lifestyle measures, or if they interfere with sleep or daily life. Seek prompt review for difficulty or pain on swallowing, unexplained weight loss, vomiting, or signs of bleeding, as these need investigation.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain with shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, or pain spreading to the arm, neck, or jaw, which may indicate a heart attack
    \item Vomiting blood or material that looks like coffee grounds
    \item Black, tarry, or bloody stools
    \item Sudden severe abdominal or chest pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Many people with typical symptoms and no warning features are treated without testing, but investigations are used when the diagnosis is uncertain, symptoms are severe, or warning features are present.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical history:} typical symptoms of heartburn and regurgitation are often sufficient for diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Trial of treatment:} a response to acid-suppressing medicines supports the diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Gastroscopy (endoscopy):} visual inspection of the oesophagus and stomach to detect oesophagitis, hiatus hernia, Barrett oesophagus, and other conditions
    \item \textbf{Ambulatory pH monitoring:} measures acid exposure in the oesophagus over 24 hours, useful when symptoms persist despite treatment or before surgery
    \item \textbf{Oesophageal manometry:} measures sphincter pressure and muscle function, mainly before antireflux surgery
    \item \textbf{Barium swallow:} X-ray study of the swallowing process, used less commonly now
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management aims to relieve symptoms, heal the oesophageal lining, and prevent complications. Treatment is stepped from lifestyle measures to medicines and, for selected patients, surgery.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} weight loss, smaller meals, avoiding food within three hours of bed, elevating the head of the bed, and avoiding trigger foods and drinks
    \item \textbf{Antacids and alginates:} provide rapid but short-lived relief for mild or occasional symptoms
    \item \textbf{H2-receptor antagonists:} such as ranitidine-type medicines reduce acid for a few hours and suit mild to moderate symptoms
    \item \textbf{Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs):} such as omeprazole and esomeprazole are the most effective treatment, healing oesophagitis and controlling symptoms in most people
    \item \textbf{Long-term management:} the lowest effective dose is used, and medicines are sometimes taken only when symptoms occur
    \item \textbf{Antireflux surgery (fundoplication):} an option for people with severe symptoms who do not respond to medicines or wish to avoid long-term medication
    \item \textbf{Endoscopic procedures:} newer techniques tighten the junction between stomach and oesophagus in selected patients
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Oesophagitis: inflammation, ulceration, and bleeding of the oesophageal lining
    \item Oesophageal stricture: scarring that narrows the oesophagus and causes difficulty swallowing
    \item Barrett oesophagus: replacement of the normal oesophageal lining with intestinal-type cells, which slightly increases the risk of oesophageal cancer
    \item Aspiration pneumonia from reflux of stomach contents into the lungs
    \item Chronic cough, hoarseness, and worsening of asthma from airway irritation
    \item Erosion of tooth enamel from acid reaching the mouth
    \item Effects on sleep and quality of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
GORD is a chronic condition, but the outlook is excellent with treatment. The vast majority of people gain good symptom control with proton pump inhibitors, and many can step down to lower doses or on-demand treatment over time. Lifestyle measures such as weight loss can reduce or eliminate the need for medicines. Regular surveillance is recommended for people with Barrett oesophagus. For those who need surgery, fundoplication is effective in the majority, though some people resume medication in later years. Serious complications are uncommon with modern management.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Achieve and maintain a healthy weight
    \item Eat smaller, more frequent meals and avoid overeating
    \item Identify and avoid personal trigger foods, including fatty, spicy, and acidic foods
    \item Avoid alcohol, caffeine, and carbonated drinks if they worsen symptoms
    \item Do not eat within two to three hours of going to bed
    \item Elevate the head of the bed by 10--15 cm for night-time symptoms
    \item Stop smoking
    \item Avoid tight clothing around the waist
    \item Take medicines as prescribed and review them regularly with your GP
    \item See a doctor promptly for difficulty swallowing, weight loss, or bleeding
\end{itemize}
