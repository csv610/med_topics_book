\chapter{Ebola}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Ebola virus disease is a severe and often fatal illness caused by infection with the Ebola virus, a member of the filovirus family. The virus is transmitted to people from wild animals and spreads through human-to-human contact with blood, body fluids, or contaminated surfaces. Ebola causes a sudden fever followed by vomiting, diarrhoea, rash, and, in severe cases, bleeding and organ failure. Outbreaks occur mainly in Central and West Africa, and while there is no cure, supportive care and treatments developed during recent outbreaks have greatly improved survival.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Ebola outbreaks occur sporadically in parts of Africa, with the largest outbreak in history occurring in West Africa between 2014 and 2016, affecting tens of thousands of people. The virus is thought to persist in animal reservoirs such as fruit bats, and outbreaks begin when the virus spills over into humans, then spread within communities and healthcare settings. In Australia and other developed countries, Ebola is rare and seen almost exclusively in travellers from affected regions, with rigorous infection control preventing local spread.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Ebola virus disease is caused by one of several species of Ebola virus, with Zaire ebolavirus being the most common and deadly.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} through direct contact with the blood or body fluids of an infected person or animal, contaminated objects, or, rarely, semen from recovered men
    \item \textbf{Not airborne:} the virus does not spread through the air, and casual contact without contact with body fluids does not transmit it
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} the virus infects immune cells and cells lining the blood vessels, triggering massive inflammation, damage to blood vessels, and impaired clotting
    \item \textbf{Incubation:} symptoms appear 2 to 21 days after exposure
    \item \textbf{Infectiousness:} people are infectious once symptoms begin, and those who have died remain highly infectious
    \item \textbf{Severity:} without treatment, the case fatality rate is high, though it varies by outbreak and species
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Ebola begins like many other infections, then progresses in severe cases.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden onset of fever, fatigue, headache, and muscle and joint pain
    \item Sore throat and weakness
    \item Vomiting, diarrhoea, and abdominal pain
    \item A rash in some people
    \item Bleeding, including bleeding from the gums, nose, or injection sites, and blood in vomit or stools, in severe cases
    \item Impaired kidney and liver function
    \item Confusion and shock in the final stages
    \item Recovery, when it occurs, is slow, with prolonged weakness and complications
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Early Ebola is indistinguishable from many common infections.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Malaria, which is common in the same regions
    \item Typhoid fever and other bacterial infections
    \item Viral haemorrhagic fevers, including Lassa fever and Marburg virus disease
    \item Dengue and other mosquito-borne viruses
    \item Gastroenteritis and other causes of fever with vomiting and diarrhoea
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with fever and other symptoms who has travelled to a region with Ebola transmission, or who has been in contact with a confirmed case, should seek medical care immediately and disclose their travel or contact history, so that appropriate testing and precautions can be arranged.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe bleeding, collapse, or signs of shock
    \item Difficulty breathing
    \item Confusion, seizures, or reduced consciousness
    \item Signs of severe dehydration
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis requires laboratory testing, performed under strict safety conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} PCR testing detects the virus's genetic material and is positive early in illness
    \item \textbf{Antigen and antibody tests:} used in diagnosis and surveillance
    \item \textbf{Full blood count and biochemistry:} to assess organ function and complications
    \item \textbf{Infection control:} people suspected of having Ebola are isolated while awaiting results
    \item \textbf{Public health:} cases are notified and contacts are traced
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management focuses on intensive supportive care and specific treatments.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fluid and electrolyte replacement:} the cornerstone of care, given intravenously
    \item \textbf{Treatment of symptoms:} control of vomiting, diarrhoea, pain, and fever
    \item \textbf{Specific antiviral treatments:} monoclonal antibody therapies developed after the West Africa outbreak substantially improve survival when given early
    \item \textbf{Treatment of complications:} management of bleeding, organ failure, and secondary infections
    \item \textbf{Infection control:} strict isolation, protective equipment, and safe handling of body fluids
    \item \textbf{Vaccination:} ring vaccination of contacts and healthcare workers in outbreaks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe bleeding and shock
    \item Multiple organ failure, including kidney and liver failure
    \item Dehydration and electrolyte disturbance
    \item Secondary infections
    \item Long-term effects in survivors, including joint pain, vision problems, and fatigue
    \item Persistence of the virus in some body fluids after recovery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Ebola is a serious illness, but outcomes have improved markedly with modern care. With intensive supportive care and monoclonal antibody treatment, survival rates in recent outbreaks have been much higher than in the past. Recovery is slow, and survivors may experience long-term health problems. Prevention through outbreak control, safe practices, and vaccination remains the most important strategy.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Avoid travel to areas with active Ebola transmission unless essential
    \item Avoid contact with the blood and body fluids of people with suspected Ebola
    \item Practise strict hand hygiene with soap and water or sanitiser
    \item Avoid handling bats, primates, and bushmeat in affected regions
    \item Follow public health advice during outbreaks, including vaccination where offered
    \item Seek immediate medical care and disclose travel history if symptoms develop after travel
\end{itemize}
