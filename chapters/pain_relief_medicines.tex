\chapter{Pain-relief medicines}
\index{Pain-relief medicines}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Pain-relief medicines, also known as analgesics, are pharmacological agents specifically designed to reduce or eliminate the sensation of pain. These agents act on various pathways within the peripheral and central nervous systems, either by inhibiting the synthesis of pain-mediating chemicals, blocking the transmission of nociceptive signals, or modulating the central perception of pain. The clinical application of pain-relief medicines is guided by the character of the pain (nociceptive, neuropathic, or nociplastic), its temporal pattern (acute versus chronic), and patient-specific factors such as age, organ function, and potential drug-drug interactions. Rational prescribing utilizes a systematic approach to balance analgesic efficacy with the risk of adverse drug reactions and long-term dependency.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Analgesic medications represent some of the most frequently prescribed and over-the-counter (OTC) purchased pharmaceutical products globally. In many countries, millions of prescriptions for analgesics are dispensed annually through public or private reimbursement schemes, with many more purchased OTC. Paracetamol (acetaminophen) and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen are the most widely consumed non-opioid analgesics. Opioid analgesics, while critical for managing severe acute pain and cancer-related pain, have seen significant regulatory changes in many countries in response to public health concerns regarding opioid-related morbidity and mortality. Additionally, prescriptions for neuropathic pain modulators, particularly gabapentinoids, have increased dramatically in recent years.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The therapeutic actions of pain-relief medicines are determined by their mechanisms of action at different points along the pain pathway.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Peripheral cyclooxygenase (COX) inhibition:} The primary mechanism of classic NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen, naproxen) and selective COX-2 inhibitors (e.g., celecoxib). By inhibiting COX enzymes, these drugs prevent the conversion of arachidonic acid to prostaglandins, which normally sensitise peripheral nociceptors and mediate inflammatory hyperalgesia.
    \item \textbf{Central prostaglandin synthesis modulation:} The mechanism of paracetamol, which is thought to inhibit prostaglandin synthesis primarily within the central nervous system (specifically targeting the peroxidase site of COX enzymes) and interact with descending serotonergic and endocannabinoid pathways.
    \item \textbf{Mu-opioid receptor agonism:} The mechanism of opioids (e.g., morphine, oxycodone, fentanyl, buprenorphine). Opioids bind to G-protein-coupled mu-opioid receptors on pre- and post-synaptic membranes in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord and the brainstem. This inhibits the release of excitatory neurotransmitters (such as glutamate and substance P) and hyperpolarises postsynaptic neurons.
    \item \textbf{Voltage-gated calcium channel modulation:} The mechanism of gabapentinoids (pregabalin and gabapentin). They bind to the $\alpha_2\delta$ subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels in the spinal cord, reducing calcium influx and the subsequent release of pronociceptive neurotransmitters.
    \item \textbf{Monoamine reuptake inhibition:} The mechanism of adjuvant antidepressants (e.g., tricyclic antidepressants like amitriptyline; serotonin-noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors like duloxetine). They increase concentrations of noradrenaline and serotonin in the synaptic cleft, enhancing the activity of the descending inhibitory pain pathways.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical utility of pain-relief medicines is reflected in the resolution of specific pain qualities and the presentation of drug-specific physiological effects.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Resolution of nociceptive pain:} Alleviation of somatic or visceral pain characterized as sharp, aching, throbbing, or cramping, which is typically highly responsive to paracetamol, NSAIDs, and opioids.
    \item \textbf{Control of neuropathic features:} Reduction in burning, shooting, electric shock-like sensations, paresthesia, and mechanical allodynia, which are classically responsive to gabapentinoids, tricyclic antidepressants, and SNRIs.
    \item \textbf{Reduction in inflammatory signs:} Improvement in pain accompanied by decreased localized swelling, erythema, joint stiffness, and warmth, particularly when managed with NSAIDs.
    \item \textbf{Class-specific adverse profiles:} Emergence of predictable side effects, such as dyspepsia or fluid retention with NSAIDs; somnolence, constipation, or pupillary constriction with opioids; and anticholinergic signs (dry mouth, blurred vision) with tricyclic antidepressants.
    \item \textbf{Tolerance and withdrawal signs:} With prolonged opioid use, patients may present with diminished analgesic effect (tolerance) or physical withdrawal signs (tachycardia, diaphoresis, rhinorrhoea, anxiety) upon abrupt dose reduction.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before initiating or adjusting pain-relief medicines, the underlying pain state must be accurately classified to ensure the selection of an effective pharmacological class.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nociceptive pain:} Pain arising from actual or threatened damage to non-neural tissue, which can be somatic (e.g., osteoarthritis, fractures) or visceral (e.g., biliary colic, postoperative organ pain), responding well to traditional analgesics.
    \item \textbf{Neuropathic pain:} Pain caused by a primary lesion or dysfunction in the somatosensory nervous system (e.g., diabetic neuropathy, post-herpetic neuralgia, cervical radiculopathy), requiring adjuvant medications rather than standard opioids or NSAIDs.
    \item \textbf{Nociplastic pain:} Pain characterized by altered nociceptive processing despite no clear evidence of tissue damage or nerve lesions (e.g., fibromyalgia, chronic primary widespread pain), which is largely unresponsive to opioids and is best managed with non-pharmacological therapies and low-dose antidepressants.
    \item \textbf{Medication-overuse headache (MOH):} A secondary headache disorder caused by the frequent or daily use of acute headache medications (such as triptans, simple analgesics, or opioids) for more than 10 to 15 days per month.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should seek medical advice if their pain remains uncontrolled despite taking prescribed analgesics, if they experience significant drug side effects, or if they require guidance on how to safely stop their medications.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Suspected acute overdose of any pain-relief medicine, particularly paracetamol or opioids.
    \item Signs of opioid overdose, including extreme drowsiness, pinpoint pupils, slow or shallow breathing, and cyanosis (bluish lips or skin).
    \item Symptoms of a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis), such as facial swelling, difficulty breathing, and a widespread itchy rash.
    \item Severe abdominal pain, hematemesis (vomiting blood), or melaena (black, tarry stools) suggesting acute gastrointestinal bleeding from NSAIDs.
    \item Symptoms of serotonin syndrome, including high fever, tremors, muscle rigidity, sweating, and confusion, in patients taking multiple serotonergic agents.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Determining the appropriate pain-relief medicine requires a thorough diagnostic evaluation of the patient's pain etiology and organ function.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive pain assessment:} Utilising detailed history taking to identify pain character, location, onset, severity, and the impact on physical and emotional function.
    \item \textbf{Renal function assessment:} Calculating the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and serum creatinine, essential prior to initiating NSAIDs or gabapentinoids, which are renally cleared or can worsen renal perfusion.
    \item \textbf{Hepatic function tests (LFTs):} Assessing serum transaminases and bilirubin prior to prescribing high-dose paracetamol, particularly in patients with a history of alcohol misuse or liver disease.
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular risk profiling:} Evaluating history of ischaemic heart disease, hypertension, or stroke prior to prescribing non-aspirin NSAIDs, which can increase the risk of thrombotic events.
    \item \textbf{Opioid risk assessment:} Screening patients using tools such as the Opioid Risk Tool (ORT) or the Screener and Opioid Assessment for Patients with Pain (SOAPP) to evaluate the risk of opioid misuse.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The pharmacological management of pain should follow a structured, individualized, and multidisciplinary framework.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stepwise pharmacological approach:} Applying the principles of the WHO analgesic ladder (and its modern adaptations) to escalate therapy from simple non-opioids to weak and then strong opioids based on pain severity, while integrating adjuvants at any step.
    \item \textbf{Scheduled non-opioid dosing:} Implementing regular, around-the-clock dosing of paracetamol (up to 4 g daily in adults) and/or NSAIDs for inflammatory pain, rather than relying on prn (as-needed) administration, to maintain stable blood levels.
    \item \textbf{Judicious opioid prescribing:} Restricting the use of oral opioids (e.g., oxycodone, tramadol) to the management of severe acute pain (e.g., post-trauma or surgery) with a pre-planned tapering schedule and a duration limit of 3 to 7 days for non-cancer pain.
    \item \textbf{Neuropathic pain modulation:} Gradual dose titration of first-line agents such as amitriptyline (10 mg to 75 mg nocte) or pregabalin (75 mg to 300 mg daily in divided doses) to achieve therapeutic efficacy while minimizing side effects like dizziness or dry mouth.
    \item \textbf{Tapering and de-prescribing plans:} Formulating a collaborative plan with the patient to slowly reduce and discontinue high-risk analgesics as the underlying condition resolves or to prevent medication-induced complications.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The use of pain-relief medicines carries a high potential for adverse drug events, toxicity, and physiological dependence.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hepatotoxicity:} Acute liver injury or fulminant hepatic failure secondary to paracetamol overdose (either accidental or intentional) due to depletion of glutathione reserves and accumulation of the toxic metabolite NAPQI.
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal ulceration and haemorrhage:} Gastric or duodenal mucosal damage caused by NSAID-induced inhibition of COX-1, which depletion of cytoprotective prostaglandins.
    \item \textbf{Acute kidney injury (AKI):} Renal impairment resulting from NSAID-induced constriction of the afferent glomerular arteriole, particularly dangerous in patients taking the "triple therapy" combination of an ACE inhibitor, a diuretic, and an NSAID.
    \item \textbf{Opioid-induced respiratory depression (OIRD):} Life-threatening hypoventilation due to opioid-mediated suppression of respiratory drive in the brainstem medullary centres.
    \item \textbf{Opioid use disorder (OUD) and tolerance:} Psychological addiction, physical dependence, and the development of tolerance, where escalating doses are required to maintain analgesia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for pain control is highly favorable when the selected analgesics match the underlying pain pathophysiology. Acute pain is typically highly responsive to short-term, regular analgesic therapy. In chronic pain conditions, pain-relief medicines are rarely curative and are instead utilized as tools to facilitate physical rehabilitation, sleep, and overall quality of life. The integration of non-pharmacological, multidisciplinary therapies alongside a structured tapering of high-risk medications significantly improves long-term outcomes and reduces the risk of chronic drug toxicity and medication-overuse syndromes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Education on self-care and safe medication practices is essential to maximize analgesic efficacy and minimize drug-related harms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reviewing active ingredients:} Carefully reading labels of all over-the-counter preparations (such as cold and flu remedies) to avoid accidental duplication and toxic dosing of paracetamol or ibuprofen.
    \item \textbf{Strict adherence to dosing limits:} Observing the maximum daily limits (e.g., 4 g for paracetamol; 1200 mg for OTC ibuprofen) and the prescribed dosing intervals.
    \item \textbf{Using gastroprotection:} Co-prescribing a proton pump inhibitor (e.g., esomeprazole) in patients requiring long-term NSAID therapy who have risk factors for gastrointestinal bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Safe storage and disposal:} Storing all prescription analgesics (especially opioids) in a secure, locked cabinet and returning expired or unused medications to a community pharmacy for safe disposal.
    \item \textbf{Utilising non-drug strategies:} Combining pharmacological therapy with lifestyle measures, physical exercise, stretching, local heat/cold applications, and mindfulness-based stress reduction.
\end{itemize}
