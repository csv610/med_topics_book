\chapter{Acquired Brain Injury (ABI)}
\index{Acquired Brain Injury (ABI)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An acquired brain injury (ABI) is damage to the brain that occurs after birth, rather than being present from birth. It falls into two broad groups: traumatic brain injury (TBI), caused by an external blow or force to the head, and non-traumatic injury, caused by something within the body itself, such as a stroke, a lack of oxygen, an infection, or a tumour.

The term is distinct from intellectual disability present from birth and from progressive degenerative brain conditions. The effects vary enormously with the site and severity of the damage, but often combine physical, cognitive, emotional, and behavioural changes, some of which may be permanent. Care spans the emergency phase through to long-term rehabilitation and community support.

\section*{Epidemiology}
ABI is a leading cause of death and long-term disability worldwide. Traumatic brain injury is most common in young people, especially young men, and is often caused by road traffic accidents, falls, sport, and assault. Falls are the leading cause of head injury in young children and in older adults.

Non-traumatic causes such as stroke are most common in older adults and in people with high blood pressure, diabetes, or smoking histories. Because more people now survive serious brain injuries, the number living with long-term disability continues to grow each year, making rehabilitation and community services increasingly important.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Acquired brain injury has many possible causes.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Traumatic:} falls, road traffic accidents, assault, and sport; penetrating wounds can damage brain tissue directly
    \item \textbf{Stroke:} blockage (ischaemic) or bleeding (haemorrhagic) of a blood vessel in the brain
    \item \textbf{Lack of oxygen:} from cardiac arrest, choking, near-drowning, or severe asthma
    \item \textbf{Infections:} meningitis and encephalitis
    \item \textbf{Tumours:} growths in the brain and the effects of their treatment
    \item \textbf{Toxins and metabolic problems:} severe alcohol or drug use and dangerously low blood sugar
\end{itemize}

In traumatic injury, the brain is damaged at the moment of impact (primary injury, such as bruising, bleeding, and tearing of nerve fibres) and again afterwards (secondary injury), when swelling, bleeding, and reduced oxygen supply cause further damage. Preventing secondary injury is a major goal of emergency care. In non-traumatic causes, nerve cells die from loss of blood flow, oxygen, infection, or pressure.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features depend on the location and severity of the injury.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Consciousness and memory:} confusion, memory loss, and loss of consciousness around the time of injury
    \item \textbf{Physical symptoms:} headache, dizziness, and sensitivity to light or noise
    \item \textbf{Cognitive changes:} difficulty concentrating, thinking clearly, or finding words
    \item \textbf{Mood and behaviour:} irritability, depression, anxiety, or personality change
    \item \textbf{Fatigue and movement:} tiredness, poor sleep, weakness, and balance or walking problems
    \item \textbf{Other features:} seizures, speech difficulties, and impaired judgement
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of ABI can be confused with several other conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Dementia and other progressive brain conditions
    \item Delirium or an acute confusional state from infection or medicines
    \item Depression, anxiety, or post-traumatic stress disorder
    \item Functional neurological disorder
    \item Chronic pain, fatigue, or sleep disorders
    \item Vestibular (balance system) problems causing dizziness
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any significant head injury needs medical assessment, especially in children, older adults, or people taking blood-thinning medicines. Seek urgent care after a head injury with any loss of consciousness, confusion, vomiting, severe headache, seizure, or difficulty remembering the event. New weakness, speech problems, or a sudden severe headache without a head injury can be signs of stroke and also need urgent help.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Loss of consciousness or a declining level of alertness after a head injury
    \item A fit or seizure
    \item Sudden weakness or numbness of the face, arm, or leg, difficulty speaking, or a drooping face (stroke signs)
    \item Repeated vomiting, or clear fluid leaking from the nose or ears after a head injury
    \item Unequal pupils or difficulty waking the person
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} how the injury or illness occurred, the level of consciousness (such as the Glasgow Coma Scale), and a full neurological examination
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} the main emergency scan for bleeding, fractures, swelling, or stroke
    \item \textbf{MRI:} more detailed images of the extent and nature of the damage
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} for infection, bleeding problems, blood sugar, and other causes
    \item \textbf{EEG:} if seizures are suspected
    \item \textbf{Neuropsychological assessment:} detailed testing of memory, attention, and other thinking skills during rehabilitation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Emergency and intensive care:} stabilising breathing, circulation, and blood pressure, with urgent surgery for bleeding or raised pressure when needed, and measures to reduce brain swelling
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} for seizures, pain, and mood problems, and for stroke as appropriate
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} a coordinated programme involving physiotherapists, occupational therapists, speech pathologists, and neuropsychologists
    \item \textbf{Cognitive and psychological support:} strategies for memory and thinking, plus counselling for the person and family
    \item \textbf{Community and vocational support:} help with work, study, driving, and daily life
    \item \textbf{Long-term follow-up:} management of late effects and prevention of complications
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seizures, which may develop weeks, months, or years after the injury
    \item Hydrocephalus (a buildup of fluid in the brain)
    \item Persistent cognitive problems affecting work, study, and relationships
    \item Depression, anxiety, personality changes, and post-traumatic stress
    \item Physical disability, including paralysis, balance problems, and speech difficulties
    \item Blood clots and pressure sores during prolonged bed rest, and, in severe cases, prolonged unconsciousness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Outcomes after ABI are highly variable and depend on the cause, severity, and quality of care. Many people with a mild injury (concussion) recover fully, although symptoms such as headache, fatigue, and poor concentration can last for weeks or months. Recovery from more severe injury is often slow and continues for months to years, with the most rapid gains in the first year.

Rehabilitation is the most important factor in improving long-term outcomes, and even people with severe injuries may make significant progress. Lasting changes in personality, memory, or emotion are common, and many people need support to return to work and independent living.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Always wear a seatbelt and an appropriately fitted helmet for cycling, motorcycling, and contact sports
    \item Follow road safety rules and never drive after alcohol or drugs
    \item Make the home safe for older adults, and supervise young children around heights, water, and roads
    \item Reduce stroke risk by managing blood pressure, not smoking, and controlling diabetes
    \item Vaccinate against illnesses such as meningitis where recommended
    \item After any brain injury, follow advice on rest and on when it is safe to drive, work, or play sport again
\end{itemize}
