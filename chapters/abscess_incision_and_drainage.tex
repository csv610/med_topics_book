\chapter{Abscess Incision and Drainage}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Incision and drainage (I\&D) is the standard procedure used to treat an abscess, a painful pocket of pus caused by infection. The doctor makes a small incision over the abscess, allows the pus to escape, gently explores the cavity, and often packs the wound so that it continues to drain and heal from the inside out. Incision and drainage is one of the most common minor procedures performed in emergency departments, general practice, and outpatient clinics, and it is normally carried out under local anaesthetic. In some situations, such as a deep, large, or anatomically complex abscess, drainage is performed in an operating theatre, sometimes under general anaesthesia.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Skin abscesses are very common, particularly among younger adults, and affect men somewhat more often than women. Certain groups are at higher risk, including people with diabetes, people who inject drugs, and those with weakened immune systems. The emergence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria, particularly methicillin-resistant \textit{Staphylococcus aureus} (MRSA), has made some abscesses more difficult to treat, and abscesses can recur in some individuals. Abscesses also occur at particular sites, such as around the anus (anal abscess), under a tooth (dental abscess), or in a gland at the opening of the vagina (Bartholin abscess).

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
An abscess forms when bacteria enter the skin or soft tissue, usually through a hair follicle, a small wound, an insect bite, or an injection site, and the immune response walls off the infection. White blood cells, dead tissue, and bacteria accumulate to form pus under pressure within a fibrous cavity. Because pus is enclosed in this cavity, antibiotics penetrate poorly, which is why drainage, rather than medicine alone, is the definitive treatment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Common bacteria:} \textit{Staphylococcus aureus}, including MRSA, and streptococci
    \item \textbf{Anaerobes:} important in abscesses of the mouth, perianal region, and gastrointestinal tract
    \item \textbf{Foreign bodies:} a splinter, seed, or injected material can initiate or perpetuate an abscess
    \item \textbf{Blocked glands or hair follicles:} the basis of boils and Bartholin abscesses
    \item \textbf{Underlying disease:} diabetes, immunosuppression, and injecting drug use predispose to formation and recurrence
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The presentation of an abscess is characteristic, with site-specific features where the anatomy differs.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A tender, red, swollen, warm lump beneath the skin
    \item Fluctuance: the lump becomes soft and yielding, like a water-filled balloon, as it matures
    \item Pain that builds as pressure within the cavity increases
    \item Pointing, in which a white or yellow centre appears, sometimes discharging pus
    \item Fever, chills, or malaise if the infection is spreading or systemic
    \item Site-specific features: toothache with facial swelling in dental abscess; a tender anal lump with pain on sitting; or a painful vulval swelling in Bartholin abscess
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A range of lumps and infections can be mistaken for an abscess.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cellulitis without an abscess, in which no drainable pocket of pus is present
    \item An infected epidermoid (sebaceous) cyst
    \item A boil (furuncle) or carbuncle, which are smaller or multi-headed abscesses
    \item Hidradenitis suppurativa, causing recurrent abscesses in the armpits and groin
    \item A thrombosed haemorrhoid or anal fissure, for lumps at the anus
    \item An enlarged lymph node, including from lymphoma or tuberculosis
    \item A deep abscess not visible on the surface, such as retropharyngeal or intra-abdominal collections
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any lump that may be an abscess should be assessed by a doctor. A suspected abscess should never be squeezed or drained at home, as this can push infection deeper into the tissues.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rapidly spreading redness, heat, or swelling around the lump
    \item High fever, chills, or confusion, which may indicate sepsis
    \item Difficulty swallowing, breathing, or speaking with a throat or neck abscess
    \item An abscess near the eye, or facial swelling that affects vision
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Most abscesses are diagnosed clinically, with investigation reserved for deep, complex, or recurrent cases.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} inspection and gentle palpation to detect fluctuance and define the extent
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} helpful for deep or early abscesses when the diagnosis is uncertain
    \item \textbf{Microbiological culture:} pus may be swabbed or sent for microscopy and culture to guide antibiotics in severe or recurrent infection
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count and inflammatory markers when infection is extensive or the patient is unwell
    \item \textbf{Blood glucose testing:} to detect undiagnosed diabetes in people with recurrent or severe infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The essential treatment is drainage; antibiotics play a supporting role in selected circumstances.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Incision and drainage:} the abscess is opened with a small incision, the pus is released, and the cavity is gently explored to break loculations
    \item \textbf{Local anaesthetic:} numbs the area before the incision is made; deeper or larger abscesses may require sedation or general anaesthesia
    \item \textbf{Packing:} the wound is frequently packed with sterile gauze, which is removed or changed over the following days so it heals from the inside out
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} not usually required after simple drainage, but appropriate for spreading cellulitis, systemic illness, diabetes, immunosuppression, and abscesses at certain sites such as the face or perianal region
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} paracetamol and anti-inflammatory medicines as needed
    \item \textbf{Warm compresses:} may help a small early abscess come to a head, though most will still require drainage
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} wound review with dressing and packing changes, and treatment of the underlying cause, such as tooth extraction for a dental abscess
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Recurrence of the abscess, especially if drainage was incomplete or the cause persists
    \item Spreading cellulitis around the site
    \item Bacteraemia or sepsis in severe cases
    \item Fistula formation, particularly after perianal abscesses
    \item Scarring at the drainage site
    \item Rarely, necrotising fasciitis, a rapidly spreading deep soft-tissue infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most abscesses heal completely within one to two weeks of drainage, and most people require little more than simple analgesia afterwards. Success depends on complete drainage, and a small proportion of abscesses recur or require a second procedure. Antibiotics are not a substitute for drainage, since a pocket of pus rarely resolves with medicines alone. Outcomes are generally excellent when the underlying cause is also addressed, such as removing a foreign body or treating a dental problem.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep cuts, scratches, and injection sites clean and covered
    \item Wash hands and skin before and after touching wounds
    \item Manage diabetes and other conditions that weaken immunity
    \item Do not squeeze, prick, or attempt to drain a suspected abscess yourself
    \item For recurrent boils, ask a doctor about skin-care routines and treatment of staphylococcal carriage
    \item Seek early medical care for any lump that is red, painful, or growing
\end{itemize}
