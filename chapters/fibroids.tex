\chapter{Fibroids}
\index{Fibroids}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Uterine fibroids, also called leiomyomas or myomas, are non-cancerous growths of muscle and fibrous tissue that develop in the wall of the uterus. They are extremely common and can range from the size of a pea to the size of a melon, occurring singly or in clusters. Many women with fibroids have no symptoms at all, but others experience heavy or painful periods, pelvic pressure, and effects on fertility and pregnancy. Fibroids are not cancer, do not spread to other organs, and rarely transform into cancer. Treatment is needed only when symptoms cause problems, and the options range from monitoring and medicines to procedures that remove or destroy the fibroids, and surgery on the uterus itself. This chapter explains fibroids, their symptoms, and the management options.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Fibroids are the most common benign tumour of the female reproductive tract, affecting around 70--80\% of women by the age of 50, with higher rates and earlier presentation in women of African ancestry. They are most commonly diagnosed in women aged 30--50, during the reproductive years, and they tend to shrink after menopause as oestrogen levels fall. Around a quarter to a half of women with fibroids have symptoms significant enough to seek care. Fibroids are a leading cause of heavy menstrual bleeding and a common reason for hysterectomy, although less invasive treatments are increasingly used.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Oestrogen and progesterone:} fibroids grow in response to ovarian hormones, enlarging during reproductive years and pregnancy and shrinking after menopause
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} fibroids arise from a single abnormal muscle cell that multiplies; specific chromosomal changes are found in many fibroids, and family history increases risk
    \item \textbf{Location:} fibroids are classified by position: submucosal (bulging into the uterine cavity), intramural (within the wall), and subserosal (bulging outward)
    \item \textbf{Effects of location:} submucosal fibroids most often cause heavy bleeding and fertility problems, while large or subserosal fibroids cause pressure on the bladder, bowel, and other organs
    \item \textbf{Blood supply:} fibroids are sensitive to blood supply; treatments such as embolisation work by cutting their blood supply
    \item \textbf{Growth factors and inflammation:} local growth factors and inflammatory signals promote fibroid cell multiplication
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} early menstruation, nulliparity, and obesity increase risk, while pregnancy and oral contraceptives may have protective effects
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heavy menstrual bleeding, prolonged periods, and clots, the most common symptom
    \item Pelvic pain, pressure, or a sense of fullness
    \item A palpable mass in the lower abdomen with large fibroids
    \item Pressure symptoms: frequent urination, difficulty emptying the bladder, or constipation
    \item Backache and pain during intercourse in some women
    \item Effects on fertility: submucosal fibroids can impair implantation and are associated with miscarriage
    \item Pregnancy effects: fibroids can cause pain, and rarely complications such as placental problems or preterm birth
    \item No symptoms at all in many women, with fibroids found incidentally on imaging
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions can cause similar symptoms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Adenomyosis, endometriosis within the uterine muscle
    \item Endometriosis and pelvic inflammatory disease
    \item Polycystic ovary syndrome and other hormonal causes of heavy bleeding
    \item Endometrial polyps
    \item Pregnancy complications, including miscarriage and ectopic pregnancy
    \item Ovarian cysts and masses, which can mimic the pelvic mass of large fibroids
    \item Uterine sarcoma, a rare cancer, which is considered when fibroids grow rapidly or after menopause
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Women should see a doctor for heavy, prolonged, or painful periods, pelvic pain or pressure, or concerns about fertility. Fibroids are common and treatable, and symptoms should not be endured.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Very heavy vaginal bleeding that soaks pads or tampons within an hour, especially with faintness
    \item Sudden severe pelvic or abdominal pain
    \item Bleeding in pregnancy with pain or collapse
    \item Fever with pelvic pain, which may indicate infection
    \item Collapse or severe dizziness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} symptoms, period pattern, and a pelvic examination that may reveal an enlarged, irregular uterus
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} the first-line imaging test, showing the size, number, and position of fibroids
    \item \textbf{Hysteroscopy:} direct visualisation of the uterine cavity for submucosal fibroids
    \item \textbf{MRI:} detailed imaging for complex fibroids and when planning surgery or embolisation
    \item \textbf{Full blood count:} to detect anaemia from heavy bleeding
    \item \textbf{Endometrial biopsy:} to exclude other causes of bleeding, especially in women over 45
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} asymptomatic fibroids need no treatment, only surveillance with ultrasound
    \item \textbf{Symptom medicines:} anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and tranexamic acid reduce bleeding; the hormonal IUD controls heavy bleeding effectively
    \item \textbf{Hormonal treatment:} combined contraceptives, progestogens, and GnRH analogues can reduce symptoms, though GnRH analogues are used short term because of menopausal side effects
    \item \textbf{Newer medicines:} oral GnRH antagonists and selective progesterone receptor modulators reduce bleeding and fibroid size
    \item \textbf{Myomectomy:} surgical removal of fibroids, preserving the uterus, for women wishing to conceive; approaches include hysteroscopic, laparoscopic, and open
    \item \textbf{Uterine artery embolisation:} a radiologist blocks the fibroids' blood supply, shrinking them
    \item \textbf{Radiofrequency ablation and focused ultrasound:} newer techniques that destroy fibroids without surgery
    \item \textbf{Hysterectomy:} removal of the uterus, the definitive treatment, for women who have completed their family
    \item \textbf{Treating anaemia:} iron replacement for heavy bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Iron-deficiency anaemia from chronic heavy bleeding
    \item Reduced quality of life from pain, bleeding, and pressure symptoms
    \item Infertility and pregnancy complications with certain fibroids
    \item Torsion of a pedunculated fibroid, causing acute pain
    \item Degeneration of a fibroid, causing pain, especially in pregnancy
    \item Rarely, sarcomatous change in a fibroid, usually presenting with rapid growth after menopause
    \item Complications of treatment, including surgical risks and effects on fertility
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Fibroids are benign and do not threaten life, and most women with symptoms can be managed successfully. Hormonal treatments control bleeding in most women, and procedures such as myomectomy, embolisation, and hysterectomy provide durable relief for those who need them. Fibroids typically stop growing and shrink after menopause. For women planning pregnancy, myomectomy offers good fertility outcomes for suitable fibroids. With the wide range of options available, almost every woman with symptomatic fibroids can find an effective treatment that fits her goals.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Report heavy or painful periods early rather than accepting them as normal
    \item Maintain a healthy weight, as obesity increases fibroid risk
    \item Treat anaemia with iron if bleeding is heavy
    \item Discuss treatment goals, including fertility plans, with a doctor before choosing an option
    \item Attend follow-up imaging as advised to monitor size and growth
    \item Seek a second opinion if considering major surgery, and explore all options
    \item Manage pain with NSAIDs under guidance, and seek review if symptoms change
\end{itemize}
