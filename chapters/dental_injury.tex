\chapter{Dental Injury}
\index{Dental Injury}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dental injuries are damage to the teeth, gums, or mouth caused by trauma, such as falls, sporting accidents, fights, or motor vehicle crashes. They range from chipped teeth and loosened teeth to knocked-out (avulsed) teeth and fractures of the jaw. Dental injuries are common in children and young adults and can affect appearance, speech, eating, and the long-term health of the teeth. Prompt, correct first aid significantly improves the chance of saving an injured tooth. This chapter covers the types of dental injury, immediate first aid, and ongoing treatment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Dental trauma is one of the most common injuries in children, affecting up to one in four school-age children, with the front teeth most frequently involved. The peak age is around 2--3 years (from falls as toddlers learn to walk) and 8--12 years (from sport and play). In many countries, sporting injuries are a major cause, and mouthguards prevent a large proportion of them. Knocked-out teeth are a dental emergency, and immediate re-implantation gives the best chance of saving the tooth. Adults experience dental injuries from falls, assaults, and road crashes.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Falls:} the most common cause in young children
    \item \textbf{Sport:} contact sports without mouthguards cause many injuries, especially to the front teeth
    \item \textbf{Assaults and accidents:} blows to the face from fights, bicycle accidents, and motor vehicle crashes
    \item \textbf{Types of injury:} enamel fractures, dentine fractures, root fractures, loosening (luxation), displacement, and avulsion (knock-out)
    \item \textbf{Avulsion:} when a tooth is knocked out, the ligament around the root is torn; the tooth can often be replanted if handled correctly
    \item \textbf{Effects:} injuries can damage the tooth pulp, root, and supporting bone, affecting the tooth's long-term survival
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A chipped, cracked, or broken tooth
    \item A loose or displaced tooth
    \item A tooth completely knocked out of the mouth
    \item Bleeding from the gums or mouth
    \item Pain, swelling, and bruising of the lip, gum, or face
    \item Difficulty biting or chewing
    \item Sensitivity to hot, cold, or air
    \item Delayed effects: the tooth may discolour or the pulp may die weeks to months later
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Soft tissue injury without tooth damage, such as a cut lip or gum
    \item Fracture of the jaw or facial bones, which requires imaging
    \item Tooth loosening from gum disease versus from trauma
    \item Concussion or head injury, which can accompany dental trauma
    \item Displacement of a baby tooth versus a permanent tooth, which are managed differently
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any significant dental injury should be assessed by a dentist, ideally within 30--60 minutes for a knocked-out permanent tooth. See a dentist for chipped, cracked, or loosened teeth, and for bleeding or swelling. Children with any injury to baby teeth should also be assessed, as trauma can affect the developing permanent teeth.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A knocked-out tooth: handle it by the crown, rinse if dirty, and replace it in the socket immediately, or keep it in milk or saliva, and go to a dentist urgently
    \item Significant head injury, loss of consciousness, vomiting, or confusion after the trauma
    \item Difficulty breathing, swallowing, or opening the mouth
    \item Severe bleeding that does not stop
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} how and when the injury occurred, and whether the tooth was knocked out
    \item \textbf{Dental examination:} assessment of the teeth, gums, and soft tissues
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} dental X-rays detect root fractures, tooth displacement, and damage to the bone and developing teeth
    \item \textbf{Panoramic X-ray or CT:} for suspected jaw fractures or complex injuries
    \item \textbf{Vitality testing:} assessment of the tooth pulp after injury
    \item \textbf{Head injury assessment:} where relevant, assessment for concussion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First aid for avulsion:} pick up a knocked-out permanent tooth by the crown, rinse it briefly in milk or saline if dirty, and re-implant it in the socket immediately, holding it in place; if it cannot be replaced, keep it in milk and see a dentist within 30 minutes
    \item \textbf{Fractured teeth:} the dentist restores the tooth with a filling, bonding, or crown depending on the extent of the fracture
    \item \textbf{Loosened teeth:} the tooth is stabilised with a splint while the ligament heals
    \item \textbf{Pulp damage:} root canal treatment is needed if the pulp is exposed or dies
    \item \textbf{Baby teeth:} avulsed baby teeth are not re-implanted, but the child should still see a dentist
    \item \textbf{Soft tissue injuries:} cleaning, suturing, and wound care for cuts
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular review for months to years to monitor the injured teeth
    \item \textbf{Facial fractures:} referral to a maxillofacial surgeon
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Loss of the tooth despite prompt treatment
    \item Pulp death requiring root canal treatment
    \item Root resorption, in which the body gradually reabsorbs the tooth root
    \item Discolouration of the injured tooth
    \item Damage to developing permanent teeth after injury to baby teeth
    \item Infection of the injured tooth or socket
    \item Fracture of the jaw or facial bones
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outcome of dental injuries depends on the type and severity of the injury and the speed of treatment. Knocked-out permanent teeth have a survival rate of over 80--90\% when re-implanted within 30 minutes, falling sharply with delay. Chipped teeth are usually restored simply and successfully. With splinting, root canal treatment, and follow-up, most injured teeth can be saved, although long-term monitoring is needed. For teeth that cannot be saved, implants, bridges, and other restorations replace them successfully.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wear a custom-fitted mouthguard for all contact sport and any activity with fall risk
    \item Supervise young children to prevent falls from furniture, stairs, and playground equipment
    \item Use seatbelts and appropriate child restraints in vehicles
    \item Avoid walking or running with objects in the mouth
    \item Know first aid for a knocked-out tooth
    \item Attend follow-up dental visits after any injury
    \item Keep the injured area clean and eat soft foods while healing
\end{itemize}
