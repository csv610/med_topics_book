\chapter{Dietary Fibre}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dietary fibre is the part of plant foods that the body cannot digest. It passes through the digestive system, adding bulk to stools, feeding the healthy bacteria in the gut, and helping regulate blood sugar and cholesterol. Fibre is found in vegetables, fruit, wholegrains, legumes, nuts, and seeds. Most Australians do not eat enough fibre, despite its strong links to gut health, heart health, weight management, and a lower risk of bowel cancer. This chapter explains the types of fibre, why it matters, and how to eat more of it.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The recommended daily fibre intake is around 25--30 grams for adults, but most Australians eat only about half of this. Low fibre intake is linked to constipation, which affects a large proportion of the population, and to higher rates of bowel cancer, heart disease, and type 2 diabetes. Increasing population fibre intake is a recognised public health priority, and most people can achieve the target with simple dietary changes.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Soluble fibre:} dissolves in water to form a gel; found in oats, barley, legumes, and fruit; helps lower cholesterol and steady blood sugar
    \item \textbf{Insoluble fibre:} adds bulk and speeds the passage of food; found in wholegrains, nuts, and vegetable skins; prevents constipation
    \item \textbf{Fermentation:} fibre is fermented by gut bacteria, producing short-chain fatty acids that feed the bowel lining and support gut health
    \item \textbf{Prebiotic effect:} fibre nourishes beneficial gut bacteria
    \item \textbf{Satiety:} fibre adds volume and slows digestion, helping with appetite and weight control
    \item \textbf{Bowel health:} fibre softens and bulks stool, reducing constipation and straining
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Adequate fibre supports regular, soft bowel motions
    \item It lowers cholesterol and improves blood glucose control
    \item It reduces the risk of bowel cancer, diverticular disease, and haemorrhoids
    \item It helps with satiety and healthy weight
    \item Increasing fibre too quickly can cause bloating and wind
    \item Inadequate fibre causes constipation and hard stools
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Soluble versus insoluble fibre, which have different effects
    \item Fibre deficiency versus other causes of constipation, such as dehydration, medicines, or thyroid disease
    \item Irritable bowel syndrome, where some people tolerate certain fibres poorly
    \item Fibre sources versus supplements, with food generally preferred
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for persistent constipation, bloating, or bowel changes, or if you plan to make major dietary changes and have a medical condition. People with irritable bowel syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, or swallowing problems should seek specific advice.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain, vomiting, or inability to pass wind or stool (possible bowel obstruction)
    \item Blood in the stool with severe symptoms
    \item Any medical emergency
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary assessment:} review of fibre intake against recommendations
    \item \textbf{Assessment of symptoms:} bowel habit, bloating, and discomfort
    \item \textbf{Medical review:} for persistent or concerning bowel symptoms
    \item \textbf{Individualised advice:} a dietitian can help with gradual fibre increase and specific conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Eat more vegetables and fruit:} aim for at least five serves of vegetables and two of fruit daily
    \item \textbf{Choose wholegrains:} wholegrain bread, brown rice, oats, and wholemeal pasta
    \item \textbf{Include legumes:} lentils, chickpeas, and beans several times a week
    \item \textbf{Snack on nuts and seeds:} as part of a balanced diet
    \item \textbf{Increase gradually:} add fibre slowly and drink plenty of water to avoid bloating
    \item \textbf{Keep skins on:} fruit and vegetable skins are rich in fibre
    \item \textbf{Read labels:} check the fibre content of packaged foods
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Constipation and haemorrhoids from low fibre intake
    \item Diverticular disease and bowel cancer risk with long-term low intake
    \item High cholesterol and blood sugar from a low-fibre diet
    \item Bloating and wind if fibre is increased too quickly
    \item Reduced satiety, contributing to weight gain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Increasing dietary fibre improves bowel regularity within days to weeks and produces longer-term benefits for heart health, blood sugar control, weight, and cancer prevention. Most people can comfortably reach the recommended intake with gradual changes and adequate fluids. The benefits of a high-fibre diet are substantial, and they accumulate over a lifetime.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Aim for 25--30 grams of fibre daily
    \item Start the day with wholegrain cereal or oats
    \item Add legumes to soups, salads, and stews
    \item Snack on fruit, nuts, and seeds
    \item Drink water throughout the day as fibre increases
    \item Increase fibre gradually to avoid digestive discomfort
    \item Choose whole fruit over juice
\end{itemize}
