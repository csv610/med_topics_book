\section*{Definition and Overview}
Tourette syndrome is a neurodevelopmental condition defined by multiple motor tics and at least one vocal (phonic) tic continuing for more than one year, with onset before eighteen years of age. Tics are sudden, rapid, recurrent, non-rhythmic movements or sounds experienced as only partly voluntary. Simple tics include eye blinking, facial grimacing, shoulder shrugging, sniffing, and throat clearing. Complex tics include touching, tapping, repeating words, or performing short sequences of movements. Coprolalia (involuntary swearing) occurs in only a minority and is not required for diagnosis, despite frequent media exaggeration. Tourette syndrome sits within a spectrum that includes provisional tic disorder and persistent motor or vocal tic disorder. Co-occurring ADHD, obsessive--compulsive symptoms, anxiety, sleep problems, and learning difficulties are common and often determine overall impairment more than tic counts alone. Severity commonly peaks in early adolescence; many people improve in adulthood, although some remain significantly affected. \section*{Epidemiology}
International estimates place prevalence at about 0.3--1 percent of school-aged children, with boys diagnosed roughly three to four times more often than girls. Onset is typically between four and eight years, often with facial or head motor tics. Peak severity is frequent between ten and twelve years. Longitudinal studies show that a large proportion experience meaningful improvement by early adulthood, while a smaller group continues to have moderate or severe tics. local care is delivered through general practice, paediatrics, child and adolescent mental health services, neurology, and specialised tic clinics in major centres. Under-diagnosis persists because mild tics are labelled habits and because stigma delays help-seeking. Clinic cohorts show high rates of ADHD and OCD-spectrum comorbidity. Family studies demonstrate aggregation of tics, OCD, and ADHD, consistent with shared genetic vulnerability. Incidence is not strongly socioeconomic, but access to CBIT, psychology, and school adjustments varies by region and clearly influences educational participation, bullying exposure, and caregiver stress. Teachers and sporting coaches are often important observers of functional impact outside the clinic.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Aetiology is polygenic and developmental rather than caused by a single mutation or by parenting style. Risk reflects many genetic variants of small effect, occasional rare variants, and maturation differences in motor control networks. First-degree relatives have elevated rates of tics and related neurodevelopmental conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuitry:} impaired filtering of motor and vocal programmes within basal ganglia--thalamo--cortical loops is a leading model
    \item \textbf{Dopamine signalling:} pharmacological response to dopamine-modulating medicines supports dopaminergic dysregulation as one contributor
    \item \textbf{Premonitory urge physiology:} sensory phenomena preceding tics suggest involvement of sensorimotor integration, not purely random muscle jerks
    \item \textbf{Neurodevelopmental timing:} frontal network maturation through adolescence aligns with the common natural improvement after the teenage years
    \item \textbf{Modifiers:} stress, excitement, fatigue, illness, and sometimes medicines can amplify tics without being root causes
    \item \textbf{Post-infectious hypotheses:} discussed in selected abrupt paediatric presentations; not the explanation for typical hereditary Tourette syndrome
\end{itemize}

Understanding biology helps families abandon blame-based explanations and engage with behavioural and medical therapies on realistic terms.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Motor tics:} blinking, grimacing, head jerks, shrugs, limb jerks, abdominal tensing, complex touching or tapping sequences, echopraxia
    \item \textbf{Vocal tics:} sniffing, coughing, throat clearing, grunting, squeaks, echolalia, palilalia, infrequently coprolalia
    \item \textbf{Temporal pattern:} waxing and waning over weeks to months with situation-specific flares during stress or fatigue
    \item \textbf{Suppressibility:} short-term suppression is common in classrooms but accumulates tension and rebound at home
    \item \textbf{Premonitory sensations:} itch, pressure, or a localised need to complete a particular movement or sound
    \item \textbf{Associated features:} ADHD traits, obsessive thoughts, compulsions, anxiety, sleep disruption, rage episodes, learning difficulties
    \item \textbf{Functional consequences:} pain, social embarrassment, bullying, academic friction, avoidance of public places, family conflict
\end{itemize}

Some adolescents become expert at camouflaging tics in public at high cognitive cost. Pain from repetitive neck or shoulder tics can become a primary complaint. Sleep fragmentation both worsens tics and compounds daytime irritability.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Other primary tic disorders:} provisional tics under one year; chronic motor-only or vocal-only tics
    \item \textbf{Stereotypies:} more rhythmic and self-soothing, especially in autism spectrum disorder
    \item \textbf{Other hyperkinetic disorders:} myoclonus, chorea, dystonia, tremor with different phenomenology
    \item \textbf{OCD rituals:} performed to neutralise anxiety rather than discharge a premonitory urge
    \item \textbf{Respiratory habit cough:} may mimic phonic tics; consider ENT or respiratory review if isolated
    \item \textbf{Medication effects:} stimulants, antipsychotics, anticonvulsants, and others may induce or worsen movements
    \item \textbf{Functional tics:} often abrupt onset, high variability, and different suggestibility patterns
    \item \textbf{Secondary disease:} Wilson disease, Sydenham chorea, neurodegenerative disease if progressive or systemic signs exist
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek assessment when tics are frequent, painful, socially impairing, or linked with ADHD, OCD, anxiety, low mood, self-harm thoughts, or school refusal. Early psychoeducation is worthwhile even if medication is not indicated. Prompt review is needed for sudden behavioural change, disabling rituals, suspected seizures, progressive neurology, or rapid academic collapse.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services} for:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Suicide risk or inability to stay safe:}
    \item \textbf{Major trauma from tics:} head injury, suspected cervical injury, uncontrolled self-injury
    \item \textbf{Acute behavioural emergency:} immediate danger to self or others
    \item \textbf{Airway or breathing threat:} rare choking or respiratory compromise related to tics
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is clinical using history and observation against criteria such as DSM-5-TR. Capture onset age, tic inventory, suppressibility, urges, course, family history, medications, and comorbidities. Severity scales (for example Yale Global Tic Severity Scale) assist specialist monitoring.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Examination:} neurological exam is usually otherwise normal
    \item \textbf{Comorbidity screening:} ADHD, OCD, autism traits, anxiety, depression, learning assessment
    \item \textbf{Investigations:} bloods and MRI only if atypical or secondary disease is suspected
    \item \textbf{School collateral:} teacher reports clarify frequency and classroom impact
    \item \textbf{Hearing and vision:} consider if academic struggle might have additional contributors
    \item \textbf{Multidisciplinary formulation:} integrate medical, psychological, and educational findings into one plan
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychoeducation:} cornerstone for all severities; reduces blame and catastrophic expectations
    \item \textbf{CBIT and habit reversal:} first-line behavioural treatments when tics impair function
    \item \textbf{School plans:} breaks, seating, non-punitive responses, exam adjustments, anti-bullying strategies
    \item \textbf{Treat ADHD and OCD:} often yields larger functional gains than tic-focused medication alone
    \item \textbf{Pharmacotherapy:} alpha-2 agonists (clonidine, guanfacine) commonly trialled; antipsychotics for severe cases under specialist care
    \item \textbf{Botulinum toxin:} selected focal disabling tics
    \item \textbf{Neuromodulation:} deep brain stimulation rarely for refractory severe adult disease
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} sleep protection, exercise, stress reduction, paced schedules
\end{itemize}
Review benefits versus sedation, mood change, metabolic effects, and blood-pressure effects before and during medication. Combine approaches rather than relying on pills alone.

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physical injury and pain:} especially cervical and shoulder strain
    \item \textbf{Bullying and stigma:} school avoidance and social withdrawal
    \item \textbf{Educational underachievement:} commonly mediated by ADHD, anxiety, and sleep loss
    \item \textbf{Mental health disorders:} depression and heightened suicide risk in some cohorts
    \item \textbf{Drug adverse effects:} metabolic syndrome risk with some antipsychotics; sedation with several agents
    \item \textbf{Family stress:} misunderstandings about discipline and chronic caregiver load
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Many people improve after adolescence, and productive adult lives are the norm with appropriate supports. Residual mild tics may persist without major limitation. Prognosis for quality of life tracks comorbidity treatment and school or workplace inclusion as much as raw tic severity. Early access to CBIT, fair classroom adjustments, and treatment of ADHD or OCD improve long-term educational attainment. Adults with ongoing severe tics benefit from specialist clinics, peer support, and flexible work design. Stigma reduction remains a public-health goal. \section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Non-punitive responses:} never punish involuntary tics
    \item \textbf{Sleep hygiene:} consistent schedules reduce flares
    \item \textbf{Stress planning:} anticipate exams, performances, and transitions
    \item \textbf{School collaboration:} shared written plans
    \item \textbf{Selective peer education:} only with the young person's consent
    \item \textbf{Limit tic spotlighting:} constant comments increase tension
    \item \textbf{Early help-seeking:} start behavioural therapy when impairment appears
    \item \textbf{Secure medicine storage:} and adherence to monitoring blood tests if prescribed
