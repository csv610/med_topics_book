\chapter{EpiPen: How to Use It}
\index{EpiPen: How to Use It}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An EpiPen is an autoinjector device that delivers a single, fixed dose of adrenaline into the outer thigh muscle. It is used to treat anaphylaxis, a severe and potentially life-threatening allergic reaction. Adrenaline works quickly to reverse the dangerous effects of anaphylaxis, including swelling of the airway, low blood pressure, and breathing difficulty. Anyone who has been prescribed an EpiPen, and their family, friends, and carers, must know how to use it confidently and quickly. Using an EpiPen in anaphylaxis is safe and can be life-saving, even if the diagnosis is uncertain.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Anaphylaxis is a significant public health issue in many countries, which has some of the highest rates of food allergy and anaphylaxis in the world. Peanut, tree nuts, insect stings, and some medicines are common triggers. Anaphylaxis can occur at any age and can happen anywhere, which is why people at risk carry adrenaline and why everyone around them should know how to use the device.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Anaphylaxis is a severe allergic reaction that requires immediate treatment.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The reaction:} the immune system releases large amounts of histamine and other chemicals, causing swelling, airway narrowing, and a dangerous drop in blood pressure
    \item \textbf{Adrenaline:} reverses these effects by narrowing blood vessels, opening the airways, and reducing swelling
    \item \textbf{Timing:} adrenaline must be given promptly, as anaphylaxis can progress rapidly
    \item \textbf{The EpiPen:} a spring-loaded device that automatically injects a pre-measured dose
    \item \textbf{Dose:} children's EpiPens (150 micrograms) are used for younger children, and adult EpiPens (300 micrograms) for larger children and adults
    \item \textbf{Safety:} giving adrenaline in anaphylaxis is far safer than withholding it
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Recognising anaphylaxis is the first step.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, wheezing, and throat tightness
    \item Swelling of the lips, tongue, face, and throat
    \item Hives and widespread skin redness
    \item Vomiting, abdominal pain, and diarrhoea
    \item Dizziness, collapse, and loss of consciousness
    \item A feeling of doom or panic
    \item Symptoms usually begin within minutes of exposure to the trigger
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Not every allergic reaction is anaphylaxis.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Mild allergic reactions with hives and swelling but no breathing or circulation problems
    \item Asthma attacks without an allergic trigger
    \item Fainting and panic attacks
    \item Other causes of collapse and breathing difficulty
    \item When in doubt, adrenaline is given because anaphylaxis is life-threatening
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Use the EpiPen immediately if anaphylaxis is suspected, and call for an ambulance. Even if the person improves, they must be observed in hospital because symptoms can return, sometimes hours later.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, throat tightness, or stridor
    \item Swelling of the tongue or face
    \item Collapse, faintness, or confusion
    \item Vomiting with other allergy symptoms
    \item Any suspected anaphylaxis, even if adrenaline has been given
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Using the EpiPen correctly is a simple sequence.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Preparation:} hold the EpiPen firmly, with the orange tip pointing down; remove the blue safety cap by pulling straight up
    \item \textbf{Position:} place the orange tip against the outer thigh, at right angles
    \item \textbf{Inject:} push firmly until you hear a click, and hold for three seconds
    \item \textbf{Remove:} pull the EpiPen away, and massage the injection site
    \item \textbf{Note the time:} the dose was given at this time
    \item \textbf{Seek help:} call for an ambulance and give a second dose after five minutes if symptoms continue
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management continues after the injection.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Contact your local emergency services:} an ambulance is always needed after using an EpiPen
    \item \textbf{Lie the person flat:} with legs raised, unless they are having difficulty breathing, in which case they may sit
    \item \textbf{Second dose:} given five minutes later if there is no improvement
    \item \textbf{Hospital observation:} everyone who has had anaphylaxis is observed in hospital for at least several hours
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} referral to an allergy specialist for testing and a plan
    \item \textbf{Replace the device:} a used EpiPen is given to the ambulance crew and a new one is prescribed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Death from anaphylaxis if adrenaline is not given promptly
    \item The "second phase" of anaphylaxis, when symptoms return hours later
    \item Side effects of adrenaline, including a fast heartbeat and tremor, which are harmless compared with the reaction
    \item Injury if the EpiPen is used incorrectly, such as injecting into a thumb
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Prompt adrenaline injection dramatically improves the outcome of anaphylaxis, and most people recover fully once treated. Deaths from anaphylaxis in many countries are rare and are often associated with delayed or absent adrenaline. Knowing how to use the EpiPen, and using it early and without hesitation, saves lives.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Carry two EpiPens at all times and check the expiry dates regularly
    \item Practise with a trainer device so using the real one is automatic
    \item Teach family, friends, child care, and school staff how to use it
    \item Follow the allergy action plan
    \item Avoid known triggers and read food labels carefully
    \item Wear medical alert identification
    \item Review the allergy plan with an allergy specialist regularly
\end{itemize}
