\chapter{Verrucas}
\index{Verrucas}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Verrucas, clinically designated as verrucae plantares and colloquially referred to as plantar warts, represent benign, intraepidermal epithelial proliferations. These lesions are caused by a localized cutaneous infection with specific strains of the Human Papillomavirus (HPV), a double-stranded DNA virus. Verrucas are anatomically restricted to the plantar aspect (sole) or the lateral margins of the foot. Unlike common warts (verrucae vulgares) that present as exophytic, dome-shaped papules on non-weight-bearing surfaces such as the hands, verrucas are subject to the persistent mechanical forces of gravity, weight-bearing, and locomotion. Consequently, these lesions are forced inward beneath the skin surface, growing endophytically. This unique structural configuration makes them clinically distinct, highly painful, and notoriously challenging to treat.

The medical significance of verrucas extends beyond simple cosmetic concerns. Because of their location on the weight-bearing surfaces of the heel and metatarsal heads, they frequently cause severe physical discomfort and functional impairment. The deep-seated hyperkeratotic plug that characterizes a chronic verruca stimulates nociceptors in the dermis during ambulation. To avoid this pain, patients often adopt compensatory gait alterations. Over time, these antalgic gait patterns can cause secondary biomechanical strain, leading to musculoskeletal pain in the ankle, knee, hip, and lumbar spine.

Furthermore, the persistent nature of HPV infections in the thick stratum corneum of the foot makes verrucas a therapeutic challenge. The thick, cornified layer of plantar skin shields the virus from topically applied agents and limits the visibility of the host's immune surveillance. A comprehensive understanding of the etiology, pathophysiology, clinical presentation, and management of verrucas is essential for clinicians to provide effective patient education and selection of appropriate therapeutic interventions. This chapter serves as an authoritative reference on the subject, aiming to guide clinical decision-making and improve patient outcomes.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Plantar warts are among the most prevalent dermatological conditions observed in primary care, dermatology clinics, and podiatric practices. Because many individuals manage their symptoms using over-the-counter remedies or do not seek medical attention for asymptomatic lesions, the true global incidence is difficult to measure. However, epidemiological surveys estimate that the point prevalence of verrucas in the general population ranges from approximately 10\% to 12\%.

The demographic distribution of verrucas reveals distinct patterns:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Age:} The prevalence of verrucas peaks significantly in school-aged children and adolescents, with the highest incidence observed between 12 and 16 years of age. In this cohort, prevalence rates as high as 20\% to 30\% have been documented. The incidence declines sharply in adults, particularly after the third decade of life. This decline is attributed to cumulative exposure and the subsequent development of adaptive cellular immunity against common HPV genotypes.
    \item \textbf{Gender:} Most epidemiological studies indicate a relatively equal gender distribution, although some clinics report a slightly higher presentation rate among females, which may reflect health-seeking behaviors rather than biological differences.
    \item \textbf{High-Risk Populations:} Transmission is heavily influenced by environmental and host factors. Individuals who frequently walk barefoot in warm, damp, communal environments—such as swimming pool surrounds, public showers, gymnasium locker rooms, and communal bathhouses—exhibit a substantially higher risk of infection. Similarly, athletes, swimmers, and military personnel are disproportionately affected.
    \item \textbf{Immunological Status:} The extent, persistence, and recurrence rate of verrucas are directly linked to host immune competency. Patients with primary or secondary immunodeficiencies, such as individuals living with HIV/AIDS, patients undergoing chemotherapy, or organ transplant recipients on long-term immunosuppressive regimens, are highly susceptible to extensive, recalcitrant, and confluent ``mosaic'' warts that are resistant to standard therapies.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The development of a verruca is a multi-step pathological process that involves viral inoculation, target cell infection, evasion of host immunity, and the subsequent benign proliferation of epidermal cells.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Causative Organism:} Plantar warts are caused by non-enveloped, double-stranded DNA viruses of the family Papillomaviridae, specifically \textbf{Human Papillomavirus (HPV)}. The most common genotypes isolated from plantar lesions include \textbf{HPV types 1, 2, 4, 27, and 57}. HPV-1 is classically associated with deep, painful, solitary lesions (myrmecia), whereas HPV-2, 4, 27, and 57 are more frequently isolated from superficial, cluster-type mosaic warts.
    \item \textbf{Transmission Pathways:} The primary mode of transmission is indirect contact with exfoliated, virus-laden keratinocytes deposited on contaminated surfaces. The physical properties of HPV, including its non-enveloped capsid, make it highly stable and resistant to environmental desiccation, heat, and standard detergents, allowing it to persist for long periods on damp surfaces like swimming pool decks. Direct skin-to-skin contact is also possible but less common for plantar lesions.
    \item \textbf{Micro-abrasion and Portal of Entry:} The virus cannot penetrate intact, healthy skin. Exposure of the basal layer of the epidermis requires a disruption in the epithelial barrier, such as a micro-abrasion, minor laceration, puncture wound, or maceration caused by prolonged exposure to moisture (hyperhidrosis). The mechanical shear forces exerted on the foot during walking provide the perfect conditions for these micro-tears.
    \item \textbf{Target Cell Infection:} HPV selectively infects the dividing basal keratinocytes of the cutaneous epithelium. The viral capsid binds to host cell receptors (primarily heparan sulfate proteoglycans), prompting internalization via receptor-mediated endocytosis. Once inside, the viral DNA is transported to the nucleus, where it establishes itself as a low-copy-number extrachromosomal element (episome).
    \item \textbf{Dysregulation of Cell Proliferation:} The expression of early viral genes, specifically E5, E6, and E7, plays a critical role in pathogenesis. These viral proteins alter the host cell cycle by interacting with tumor suppressor proteins (such as p53 and retinoblastoma protein pRb), promoting cellular replication and delaying normal differentiation. This results in the rapid proliferation of basal and spinous cells.
    \item \textbf{Histopathological Changes:} As the infected keratinocytes differentiate and migrate toward the stratum corneum, viral replication and assembly increase. This leads to characteristic histopathological features:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Hyperkeratosis:} Extreme thickening of the stratum corneum.
        \item \textit{Acanthosis:} Thickening of the stratum spinosum.
        \item \textit{Papillomatosis:} Finger-like projections of the dermal papillae extending upward.
        \item \textit{Koilocytosis:} The presence of vacuolated keratinocytes with shrunken, pyknotic nuclei in the upper epidermal layers, which is diagnostic for HPV infection.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neovascularization:} The metabolic demands of the proliferating epidermis stimulate the elongation and dilation of dermal capillary loops. These capillaries grow vertically into the elongated dermal papillae. Under mechanical pressure, micro-thromboses occur within these vessels, producing the small black dots pathognomonic of verrucas.
    \item \textbf{Immune Evasion Mechanisms:} HPV is highly adapted to evade the host immune system. The viral life cycle is entirely intraepidermal; it does not cause cell lysis, inflammation, or viremia. Consequently, there is minimal release of pro-inflammatory cytokines to recruit antigen-presenting cells (dendritic cells). This lack of inflammatory signal delays the activation of cell-mediated immunity (CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes), allowing the virus to persist in the skin for months or years.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of a verruca is shaped by the mechanical stresses of its plantar environment, distinguishing it from warts found on other anatomical sites.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Morphology:} Verrucas present as firm, hyperkeratotic, well-demarcated papules or plaques with a rough, verrucous (cauliflower-like) surface. Due to weight-bearing pressure, they are typically flush with the surrounding skin or grow endophytically, presenting as a hard, depressed core surrounded by a raised keratosic ring.
    \item \textbf{Thrombosed Capillaries (Black Dots):} When the superficial, dry layers of keratin are pared down, tiny black or dark-red dots become visible. These represent thrombosed capillary loops. Patients frequently misidentify these dots as ``roots'' or ``seeds.''
    \item \textbf{Disruption of Skin Striations:} A classic diagnostic feature of verrucas is the path of the normal epidermal ridge lines (dermatoglyphics). These lines run around the periphery of the lesion rather than passing through it, a feature that distinguishes it from a simple callus.
    \item \textbf{Anatomical Distribution:} Verrucas are most frequently located over areas of maximum mechanical pressure, such as the plantar aspects of the metatarsal heads, the heel, and the plantar surface of the hallux (great toe). However, they can occur anywhere on the sole, the lateral margins of the foot, or the interdigital spaces.
    \item \textbf{Clinical Subtypes:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Myrmecia Warts:} Typically caused by HPV-1, these are solitary, deep-seated, dome-shaped, and highly painful lesions. They are characterized by a central keratosic plug and are often surrounded by an inflammatory ring.
        \item \textit{Mosaic Warts:} Typically caused by HPV-2 or HPV-4, these are large, flat, superficial plaques formed by the coalescence of dozens of small, closely packed warts. They are generally less painful than myrmecia but are exceptionally resistant to therapy.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Symptomatology:} Pain is a variable feature. While some verrucas are entirely asymptomatic, others cause severe, sharp pain during weight-bearing. The pain of a verruca is characteristically elicited by lateral compression (pinching the lesion side-to-side), whereas a mechanical callus is more painful under direct perpendicular pressure (pressing directly onto the lesion).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because incorrect treatment of plantar lesions can cause chronic pain or delay the diagnosis of malignancies, differential diagnosis is essential. The following conditions must be distinguished from verrucas:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Calluses (Tylomata):} Broad areas of hyperkeratotic skin caused by chronic friction or abnormal biomechanics. Unlike verrucas, calluses preserve normal dermatoglyphic lines, lack thrombosed capillaries, and are painful primarily under direct downward pressure.
    \item \textbf{Corns (Helomata):} Localized, deep-seated plugs of keratin, often on the toes or metatarsal heads, caused by pressure from ill-fitting shoes. While they can be highly painful and mimic myrmecia warts, corns preserve dermatoglyphic lines, lack black dots, and reveal a translucent, hard central core upon paring, rather than pinpoint bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Verrucous Carcinoma (Epithelioma Cuniculatum):} A low-grade, slow-growing squamous cell carcinoma that arises on the sole of the foot. It closely mimics a large, recalcitrant mosaic wart and can present as a vegetating, exophytic mass with multiple sinus tracts discharging foul-smelling keratinous debris. Any chronic, atypical, or treatment-resistant plantar lesion must undergo a biopsy to rule this out.
    \item \textbf{Amelanotic Melanoma:} A rare, highly aggressive malignant melanoma that lacks melanin pigmentation. It can present as a flesh-colored, pink, or reddish nodule on the plantar surface, easily mistaken for an atypical wart. Delayed diagnosis carries a high mortality rate.
    \item \textbf{Foreign Body Granuloma:} A chronic inflammatory reaction to a foreign object (e.g., a splinter, glass shard, or hair) embedded in the dermis of the sole. It can present as a painful, hyperkeratotic papule, but history of trauma and the absence of HPV characteristics (like pinpoint bleeding upon paring) help differentiate it.
    \item \textbf{Lichen Planus:} Hypertrophic lichen planus on the feet can present as hyperkeratotic, purplish plaques. It is differentiated by intense pruritus, Wickham's striae, and potential involvement of the oral mucosa, nails, or other skin areas.
    \item \textbf{Arsenic Keratosis:} Hard, hyperkeratotic papules on the palms and soles resulting from chronic arsenic ingestion. These lesions are premalignant, typically bilateral and symmetric, and present in a patient with a history of exposure.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most verrucas are benign and can be managed conservatively. However, certain patient populations and clinical presentations require professional medical evaluation by a general practitioner, podiatrist, or dermatologist.

Patients must seek medical assessment in the following scenarios:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The patient has \textbf{diabetes mellitus}, peripheral arterial disease, or peripheral neuropathy. In these individuals, the impaired circulation and sensory loss increase the risk of minor lesions developing into chronic foot ulcers, cellulitis, osteomyelitis, or gangrene. Self-treatment with over-the-counter keratolytics is strictly contraindicated.
    \item The patient is \textbf{immunocompromised} due to conditions such as HIV/AIDS, active oncology treatment, or immunosuppressive therapy following organ transplantation. These patients are prone to extensive, aggressive, and highly treatment-resistant viral infections that require systemic or specialized therapies.
    \item The lesion exhibits signs of secondary bacterial infection, such as spreading erythema (redness), localized warmth, significant edema (swelling), severe throbbing pain, purulent discharge (pus), or red streaks ascending the foot or ankle (lymphangitis).
    \item The lesion changes in appearance, showing rapid growth, spontaneous bleeding, unexplained ulceration, or irregular pigmentation. Such features raise suspicion of a malignant lesion, such as verrucous carcinoma or amelanotic melanoma.
    \item The pain is severe enough to alter the patient's normal walking gait, leading to secondary pain in the joints of the lower extremity or back.
\end{itemize}

In cases of rapidly spreading soft-tissue infections, high fever, or signs of systemic sepsis (such as confusion, rapid heart rate, or difficulty breathing), immediate emergency services must be contacted. For life-threatening emergencies, call:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{local emergency services} in many countries
    \item \textbf{911} in the United States and Canada
    \item \textbf{999} in the United Kingdom
    \item \textbf{112} in the European Union and globally on mobile networks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of a verruca is primarily clinical, relying on visual inspection, patient history, and physical examination of the lesion. Special laboratory tests or imaging studies are rarely needed for routine cases.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical Examination:} Visual assessment of the lesion's borders, texture, and location on the foot. The clinician will look for the hallmark disruption of dermatoglyphic lines.
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic Paring:} The most common diagnostic procedure. The clinician uses a sterile scalpel blade (typically a No. 15 blade) to gently shave away the superficial, non-viable hyperkeratotic layers of the lesion.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item In a verruca, paring reveals small, pinpoint bleeding points or black dots (thrombosed capillaries) and exposes the boundaries of the viral tissue.
        \item In a callus or corn, paring reveals concentric, translucent layers of hard keratin without bleeding, and the skin lines remain intact.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dermoscopy:} A non-invasive diagnostic technique using a dermatoscope (a magnifying lens with polarized light). Under dermoscopy, the clinician can clearly visualize the glomerular or dotted vascular pattern characteristic of verrucas, distinguishing them from the structured keratin of corns and calluses.
    \item \textbf{Skin Biopsy:} A punch or shave biopsy is indicated for atypical, chronic, or treatment-resistant lesions, particularly in older adults or immunocompromised individuals, to rule out verrucous carcinoma or amelanotic melanoma. Histopathological analysis of a biopsy shows hyperkeratosis, parakeratosis, papillomatosis, and koilocytes in the upper epidermis.
    \item \textbf{Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR):} While not used in routine clinical practice, PCR testing of skin scrapings can identify and genotype specific HPV strains. This is primarily used in clinical research and academic dermatology settings.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Because many verrucas are self-limiting and resolve spontaneously through natural immunity, the option of \textbf{benign neglect} (no active treatment) is appropriate for asymptomatic lesions. When treatment is requested due to pain, functional limitation, or cosmetic concerns, the goal is to destroy the infected tissue or stimulate an immune response against HPV.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conservative and Topical Therapies:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Salicylic Acid:} The gold-standard, first-line topical treatment. High-concentration salicylic acid (15\% to 50\% in collodion, plaster, or ointment) acts as a keratolytic, slowly chemically digesting the infected epidermal cells. Daily application, combined with gentle debridement of dead tissue using a file or pumice stone, and occlusion with adhesive tape, is required. It is highly effective but demands strict patient compliance over several weeks or months.
        \item \textit{Duct Tape Occlusion:} A simple, low-cost home remedy where the lesion is covered with silver duct tape for six days, followed by debridement and re-application. The mechanism is thought to involve localized skin maceration and irritation, which may trigger a local immune response.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physical and Ablative Interventions:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Cryotherapy:} The application of liquid nitrogen (at -196 degrees Celsius) using a spray or cotton bud to freeze the lesion. This induces cell death and epidermal-dermal separation, forming a blister that lifts the viral tissue. It is typically repeated every 2 to 4 weeks for a maximum of 3 to 6 sessions. Cryotherapy can be painful and may cause temporary blistering, local sensory nerve damage, or hypopigmentation.
        \item \textit{Electrosurgery and Curettage:} Surgical removal under local anesthesia. The lesion is scraped out using a sharp curette, and the base is electrocauterized to control bleeding and destroy any residual viral cells. Because this procedure carries a high risk of painful plantar scarring, it is reserved for refractory cases.
        \item \textit{Laser Therapy:} Pulsed dye lasers (PDL) target the microvasculature supplying the wart, leading to tissue ischemia and necrosis. Carbon dioxide (CO2) lasers can also be used to vaporize the infected tissue. These treatments are costly and typically reserved for specialist clinics.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chemical and Cytotoxic Agents:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Cantharidin:} A blistering agent derived from the beetle family Meloidae. It is applied by a clinician, covered with tape, and causes intraepidermal blistering that lifts the wart off the basement membrane.
        \item \textit{Fluorouracil (5-FU):} A topical antimetabolite that blocks DNA synthesis, halting the replication of the virus-infected cells. It is applied under occlusion for several weeks.
        \item \textit{Intralesional Bleomycin:} Direct injection of the cytotoxic antibiotic bleomycin into the lesion. It is highly effective for resistant warts but is painful and requires specialized clinical handling.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immunotherapy:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Topical Imiquimod:} A prescription cream that stimulates local production of interferon-alpha and other cytokines, helping the host immune system identify and target the HPV infection.
        \item \textit{Intralesional Antigen Injections:} Injection of common antigens (such as Candida or mumps antigens) directly into the wart. This stimulates a cell-mediated hypersensitivity reaction, prompting the immune system to recognize and clear the HPV infection both locally and systemically.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While verrucas are benign lesions, they can lead to complications arising from the infection itself, the physical location on the foot, or the side effects of various treatments.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chronic Pain and Gait Alterations:} Severe, persistent pain during weight-bearing can cause patients to alter their walking mechanics. This antalgic gait can lead to secondary strain on the joints of the lower limb, causing pain in the ankle, knee, hip, or lower back.
    \item \textbf{Painful Plantar Scarring:} Aggressive ablative treatments, such as deep cryotherapy, electrosurgery, or laser ablation, can damage the dermis. Healing on the plantar surface of the foot can result in dense, fibrous scar tissue. These scars can cause chronic, localized pressure pain during walking that mimics a verruca but is highly resistant to treatment.
    \item \textbf{Secondary Bacterial Infection:} The skin barrier is compromised by the HPV infection, regular debridement, and chemical treatments (such as salicylic acid or cantharidin). This increases the risk of secondary bacterial invasion, potentially leading to localized cellulitis, abscesses, or in high-risk patients (like those with diabetes), chronic foot ulcers and osteomyelitis.
    \item \textbf{Koebnerization (Viral Spread):} Physical trauma, scratching, or aggressive scrubbing of a verruca can spread the virus to surrounding healthy skin, leading to the development of new, satellite warts.
    \item \textbf{Psychological Distress and Activity Limitation:} Chronic, painful, or aesthetically unpleasing warts can cause emotional distress, anxiety, and limit a patient's participation in social, athletic, or recreational activities, especially those involving bare feet.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals with verrucas is generally excellent, as the condition is benign and self-limiting. The natural history of HPV infections is characterized by eventual clearance by the host's immune system:
\begin{itemize}
    \item In children, spontaneous resolution occurs in approximately 50\% of cases within one year, and up to 60\% to 70\% within two years without any intervention.
    \item In adults, spontaneous resolution is much slower and less predictable, often taking several years. This difference is due to the chronic nature of the infection and the differing cellular immune response in older individuals.
    \item Poor prognostic indicators include older age, long-standing duration of the lesion (greater than two years), large or multiple mosaic-type warts, and underlying immunocompromised states. In immunocompromised individuals, warts can become a persistent, life-long management issue rather than a curable condition.
    \item Recurrence rates after successful clinical treatment are significant, ranging from 10\% to 30\%. Recurrence is typically caused by the reactivation of latent viral DNA persisting in clinically normal skin surrounding the treated area, or by re-exposure to the virus.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventing the acquisition and transmission of HPV is key to managing the spread of verrucas. Since the virus is resilient in the environment, personal hygiene and behavioral measures are essential.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Footwear in Communal Areas:} Wear protective footwear, such as flip-flops, sandals, or water shoes, when walking in public showers, locker rooms, swimming pool surrounds, and communal changing areas. Avoid walking barefoot on damp, high-traffic surfaces.
    \item \textbf{Hygiene Practices:} Wash feet daily with soap and water, drying them thoroughly, particularly between the toes. Do not share personal items such as towels, socks, shoes, nail files, or pumice stones, as these can act as vectors for viral transmission.
    \item \textbf{Skin Barrier Integrity:} Keep the skin of the feet well-moisturized to prevent cracking, scaling, or micro-abrasions, which serve as entry points for the virus. If the skin is macerated due to excessive sweating (hyperhidrosis), use drying powders or moisture-wicking socks.
    \item \textbf{Avoid Direct Contact:} Do not touch, scratch, or pick at your own warts or those of others. Scratching can cause autoinoculation, spreading the virus to other areas of the body or exacerbating existing lesions.
    \item \textbf{Safe Home Care:} If using home treatments like salicylic acid or pumice stones, ensure these tools are kept clean and are not shared. Do not use the same file or pumice stone on healthy skin that has been used on a wart, as this can spread the infection.
    \item \textbf{Wart Protection:} Cover active warts with a waterproof plaster or tape, especially when swimming or participating in sports, to reduce the shed of virus-containing cells into the environment.
    \item \textbf{Vaccination:} Although the recombinant quadrivalent and nonavalent human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccines (e.g., Gardasil 9) are designed to target mucosal HPV strains associated with cervical and anogenital cancers (types 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58), several clinical reports and observational studies suggest that HPV vaccination can induce cross-reactive immunity, leading to the rapid clearance of recalcitrant cutaneous warts in some patients.
\end{itemize}
