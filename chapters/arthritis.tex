\chapter{Arthritis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Arthritis is a general term for inflammation or degeneration affecting one or more joints. It produces pain, swelling, stiffness, and reduced movement and encompasses more than 100 distinct conditions. The most common are \textbf{osteoarthritis}, a wear-and-tear condition of the joint surface, and \textbf{rheumatoid arthritis}, a chronic inflammatory autoimmune disease. Other important forms include gout, psoriatic arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, and septic arthritis. Some types are sudden and short-lived, while others are lifelong and progressive. Because the treatments differ substantially between types, an accurate diagnosis is the essential first step in management.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Arthritis is among the most common chronic health problems in the world. Around one in ten people has some form of arthritis, and the prevalence rises sharply with age, with osteoarthritis affecting a large proportion of people over 60. Rheumatoid arthritis affects approximately one in 100 adults and is two to three times more common in women. Gout affects roughly one in 20 men and fewer women, with rates rising after the menopause. Arthritis is a leading cause of disability, reduced work capacity, and health-care expenditure worldwide, and its burden is expected to grow as populations age and obesity becomes more common.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The causes differ between the types of arthritis, but all involve damage or inflammation of joint structures, including the articular cartilage, the synovium, the underlying bone, and the joint fluid.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Osteoarthritis:} age-related wear, previous joint injury, obesity, and genetic predisposition cause cartilage thinning and secondary changes in the underlying bone
    \item \textbf{Rheumatoid arthritis:} the immune system attacks the synovial lining; smoking and genetic susceptibility are strong risk factors, and autoantibodies such as anti-CCP are characteristic
    \item \textbf{Gout:} crystals of uric acid deposit in the joint and trigger intense inflammation; genetics, diet, alcohol, and kidney disease all influence uric acid levels
    \item \textbf{Psoriatic arthritis:} an inflammatory arthritis associated with the skin condition psoriasis, affecting joints, entheses, and sometimes the spine
    \item \textbf{Ankylosing spondylitis:} inflammation of the spine and sacroiliac joints, strongly associated with the genetic marker HLA-B27
    \item \textbf{Septic arthritis:} bacteria, usually via the bloodstream, enter the joint and can destroy the articular cartilage within days
    \item \textbf{Other types:} systemic lupus erythematosus, viral arthritis, and arthritis associated with inflammatory bowel disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The hallmark of arthritis is joint pain, swelling, and stiffness, but the pattern differs importantly between types and helps to identify the cause.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Joint pain that is typically worse with use in osteoarthritis and eased by rest
    \item Morning stiffness; stiffness lasting more than an hour suggests inflammatory arthritis
    \item Swelling, warmth, and overlying redness of affected joints
    \item Reduced range of movement and difficulty with daily activities
    \item Osteoarthritis: usually affects the knees, hips, hands, and spine, often asymmetrically
    \item Rheumatoid arthritis: symmetrical involvement, classically of the small joints of the hands and feet
    \item Gout: sudden, severe attacks of a single joint, most often the big toe
    \item Inflammatory types may be accompanied by fatigue, low-grade fever, and malaise
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The types of arthritis must be distinguished from one another and from conditions that mimic joint disease.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Osteoarthritis versus inflammatory arthritis, such as rheumatoid and psoriatic arthritis
    \item Gout and other crystal arthropathies, such as pseudogout (CPPD disease)
    \item Septic arthritis, which requires urgent treatment
    \item Fibromyalgia and other causes of widespread musculoskeletal pain
    \item Bursitis, tendinopathy, and other periarticular soft-tissue problems
    \item Trauma, fracture, or ligament injury
    \item Systemic diseases such as lupus, polymyalgia rheumatica, and haemochromatosis
    \item Infection in or around a joint replacement
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if joint pain, swelling, or stiffness lasts for more than a few weeks, limits daily activities, or is accompanied by fatigue or a feeling of being unwell. Early referral is especially important when an inflammatory arthritis is suspected, because treatment is most effective before joint damage develops. A single hot, swollen joint is a medical emergency, because it may be infected.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A single hot, red, exquisitely painful joint accompanied by fever, which may indicate septic arthritis
    \item Chest pain or breathlessness in someone with inflammatory arthritis, which may indicate heart or lung involvement
    \item Sudden inability to bear weight or to use a joint after an injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A careful history of which joints are affected, the pattern of stiffness, and any skin, eye, or systemic features guides the choice of investigations.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} joint distribution, the duration of morning stiffness, and any associated psoriasis, eye inflammation, or family history
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} inflammatory markers (ESR and C-reactive protein), full blood count, and autoantibodies including rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP
    \item \textbf{Uric acid level:} elevated in gout, although it may be normal during an acute attack
    \item \textbf{Joint fluid analysis:} synovial fluid drawn from a swollen joint is examined for crystals and cultured for infection
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} demonstrate joint-space narrowing, osteophytes, and erosions
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound and MRI:} detect synovitis, joint effusion, and early structural damage before it is visible on plain films
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a rheumatologist when the diagnosis is uncertain or an inflammatory arthritis is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment aims to relieve pain, preserve joint movement, and prevent structural damage. Most people manage arthritis with a combination of non-drug measures, medicines, and, where needed, surgery.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Exercise and physiotherapy:} strengthening and stretching keep joints moving; low-impact activity such as walking, swimming, and cycling suits most people
    \item \textbf{Weight loss:} reduces load on weight-bearing joints and meaningfully improves symptoms in knee and hip osteoarthritis
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} paracetamol, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and topical preparations applied to the affected joint
    \item \textbf{Disease-modifying drugs:} methotrexate and biological therapies slow joint damage in rheumatoid and psoriatic arthritis
    \item \textbf{Gout treatment:} medicines to settle acute attacks and urate-lowering drugs such as allopurinol to prevent recurrence
    \item \textbf{Joint injections:} corticosteroid injections for inflammatory flares
    \item \textbf{Joint replacement surgery:} highly effective for advanced osteoarthritis of the hip and knee
    \item \textbf{Occupational therapy and aids:} splints, grips, and home adaptations make daily tasks easier
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} pain-management programmes and counselling for people with chronic pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive joint damage, deformity, and disability
    \item Loss of independence and reduced quality of life
    \item Inflammatory arthritis can involve other organs, raising the risk of heart and lung disease
    \item Side effects of medicines, including gastrointestinal bleeding from NSAIDs and increased infection risk from immunosuppressants
    \item Gout attacks can lead to joint damage, tophi, and kidney stones
    \item Chronic pain, poor sleep, anxiety, and depression
    \item Septic arthritis can destroy a joint rapidly if not treated urgently
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most types of arthritis are not curable, but they are highly manageable. Many people with osteoarthritis remain active for years with exercise, weight control, and simple analgesia. Rheumatoid arthritis responds much better to early treatment, and modern disease-modifying medicines can often suppress inflammation and prevent deformity. Gout can usually be well controlled with urate-lowering therapy, and joint replacement gives excellent long-term results in advanced osteoarthritis. Overall, outcomes are better with early diagnosis, an accurate classification, and an active role in self-management.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a healthy weight to reduce stress on the knees, hips, and feet
    \item Stay active with regular low-impact exercise, and move each joint gently through its full range
    \item Protect joints from injury with good technique, warm-ups, and appropriate footwear
    \item Do not smoke, as smoking is a strong risk factor for rheumatoid arthritis
    \item For gout, limit alcohol and high-purine foods, and drink plenty of fluids
    \item Use heat and cold packs, and rest joints during flares before gently resuming activity
    \item See a doctor early for persistent or unusual joint symptoms, and attend regular reviews
\end{itemize}
