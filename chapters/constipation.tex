\section*{Definition and Overview}
Constipation is not a disease but a common symptom defined by difficult, infrequent, or incomplete bowel movements. It is usually described as fewer than three bowel movements a week, passing hard or lumpy stools, straining, or feeling that the bowel has not fully emptied. Almost everyone experiences constipation at some time, and it is usually temporary and easily corrected. Occasionally it signals an underlying medical problem that needs investigation.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Constipation is very common worldwide. Roughly 1 in 7 adults has it at any given time, and it is more common in women, older people, and during pregnancy. About 1 in 3 adults over 65 experiences constipation, partly due to medicines and reduced activity. It is also common in children, affecting up to 1 in 10 at some point. It accounts for a large number of visits to general practitioners and for a substantial share of laxative use.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diet:} low fibre intake and inadequate fluids
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} inactivity, ignoring the urge to go, and long periods of immobility
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} opioids, some antidepressants, iron and calcium supplements, antacids, and some blood pressure medicines
    \item \textbf{Functional bowel disorders:} including irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)
    \item \textbf{Structural problems:} haemorrhoids, anal fissures, or narrowing of the bowel
    \item \textbf{Endocrine and metabolic causes:} underactive thyroid, diabetes, and high blood calcium
    \item \textbf{Neurological causes:} spinal cord injury, Parkinson's disease, and multiple sclerosis
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} hormonal changes and pressure from the growing uterus
\end{itemize}

In most cases, waste moves too slowly through the large bowel, so too much water is absorbed and the stool becomes hard and dry. Slow colonic transit, difficulty relaxing the pelvic floor muscles, or ignoring the urge to defecate can all contribute.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fewer than three bowel movements per week
    \item Hard, dry, or lumpy stools
    \item Straining to pass a stool
    \item A feeling that the bowel has not emptied completely
    \item Bloating, abdominal discomfort, and wind
    \item Needing to use fingers or manual pressure to help pass stools
    \item In severe cases, overflow soiling when liquid stool leaks around a blockage
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Irritable bowel syndrome, which combines constipation with pain and bloating
    \item Irritable bowel syndrome with alternating constipation and diarrhoea
    \item Intestinal obstruction or blockage, a surgical emergency
    \item Bowel cancer, especially with new constipation in an older person
    \item Hypothyroidism or other metabolic disorders
    \item Medication-induced constipation
    \item Anorectal conditions such as anal fissure or obstructed defecation
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if constipation is severe, persists for more than two to three weeks, alternates with diarrhoea, or is accompanied by concerning symptoms.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain with vomiting or a swollen abdomen
    \item Inability to pass wind or stool with worsening pain (possible blockage)
    \item Blood in the stool with faintness or collapse
    \item Confusion, fever, or signs of dehydration with constipation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} bowel habit, stool consistency, duration, medicines, diet, and family history of bowel conditions
    \item \textbf{Examination:} abdominal examination and a rectal examination (digital rectal examination) to check the anus and rectum
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} thyroid function, calcium, blood count, and glucose if metabolic causes are suspected
    \item \textbf{Stool tests:} checking for blood (faecal occult blood) where screening is indicated
    \item \textbf{Colonoscopy or flexible sigmoidoscopy:} recommended for new or persistent constipation in older people or with warning features such as bleeding
    \item \textbf{Specialist tests:} colonic transit studies or anorectal physiology tests if the cause remains unclear
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary change:} increase fibre gradually with fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, and drink plenty of fluids
    \item \textbf{Physical activity:} regular exercise encourages normal bowel movement
    \item \textbf{Bowel retraining:} set a regular time to sit on the toilet, usually after meals, and do not ignore the urge
    \item \textbf{Bulk-forming laxatives:} fibre supplements such as psyllium or ispaghula are the usual first choice
    \item \textbf{Other laxatives:} osmotic (e.g.\ lactulose, polyethylene glycol) or stimulant (e.g.\ bisacodyl, senna) laxatives for short-term or resistant cases
    \item \textbf{Review medicines:} a doctor may adjust medications that cause constipation
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} for persistent or severe cases, including pelvic floor physiotherapy and occasionally prescribed newer agents
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Haemorrhoids and anal fissures from straining
    \item Faecal impaction, where hard stool lodges in the rectum and cannot be passed
    \item Overflow soiling, particularly in children and older people
    \item Rectal bleeding and discomfort
    \item Rarely, a bowel obstruction or perforation in severe cases
    \item Reduced quality of life and avoidance of social or work activities
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people improve with dietary and lifestyle changes, and episodes of simple constipation settle within days to weeks. Chronic constipation may require long-term management, including regular laxatives under medical supervision, but generally responds well to treatment. The outlook depends on the underlying cause: functional constipation is usually well controlled, while constipation from structural, neurological, or endocrine causes improves as the cause is treated. Any sudden change in bowel habit or blood in the stool should always be evaluated.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Eat a high-fibre diet with fruits, vegetables, legumes, and whole grains
    \item Drink enough fluids each day
    \item Stay physically active most days
    \item Use the toilet when the urge arises and allow unhurried time
    \item Limit constipating medicines where possible and discuss alternatives with a doctor
    \item Avoid prolonged use of stimulant laxatives without medical advice
    \item Keep a record of changes in bowel habit to discuss with your doctor
\end{itemize}
