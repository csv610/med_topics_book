\chapter{Malabsorption Syndromes}
\index{Malabsorption Syndromes}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Malabsorption syndromes encompass a group of disorders in which the small intestine is unable to adequately absorb nutrients---including macronutrients (proteins, carbohydrates, fats) and micronutrients (vitamins and minerals)---into the bloodstream. This impairment can result from defects in intraluminal digestion, mucosal absorption, or lymphatic transport. Common underlying conditions include coeliac disease, chronic pancreatitis, Crohn's disease, and short bowel syndrome. Clinical consequences range from mild gastrointestinal discomfort to severe malnutrition, weight loss, and systemic deficiency states.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of malabsorption syndromes is highly dependent on the specific underlying etiology and geographical region. Coeliac disease affects approximately 1\% of the the population, though a large percentage remains undiagnosed. Lactose intolerance is highly prevalent globally, particularly in Asian, African, and Indigenous local populations. Chronic pancreatitis and alcohol-related malabsorption are more common in middle-aged men. Globally, infectious causes like tropical sprue and parasitic infections (such as \textit{Giardia duodenalis}) are significant causes of malabsorption in developing nations.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Malabsorption arises from structural or functional abnormalities that disrupt the digestive and absorptive process at different stages of transport.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mucosal damage:} loss of absorptive surface area due to enteropathy, villous atrophy, or inflammation, as seen in coeliac disease, Crohn's disease, or tropical sprue.
    \item \textbf{Pancreatic exocrine insufficiency:} deficient secretion of digestive enzymes (lipase, amylase, proteases) due to chronic pancreatitis, cystic fibrosis, or pancreatic cancer, preventing macronutrient breakdown.
    \item \textbf{Bile acid deficiency:} impaired fat emulsification due to cholestatic liver disease, bacterial overgrowth (which deconjugates bile salts), or ileal resection (preventing enterohepatic circulation).
    \item \textbf{Lymphatic obstruction:} impaired transport of absorbed lipids and chylomicrons due to conditions like Whipple's disease, intestinal lymphangiectasia, or lymphoma.
    \item \textbf{Surgical resection:} reduced mucosal surface area and rapid transit time following bowel resection (short bowel syndrome).
    \item \textbf{Enzyme deficiencies:} congenital or acquired lack of brush-border disaccharidases, such as lactase deficiency leading to carbohydrate malabsorption.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The presentation varies from subclinical micronutrient deficiencies to classic malabsorptive features.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic diarrhoea, often characterized by pale, voluminous, foul-smelling, and greasy stools that float (steatorrhoea)
    \item Unexplained weight loss, muscle wasting, and failure to thrive in paediatric patients
    \item Abdominal bloating, distension, flatulence, and crampy pain
    \item Fatigue, weakness, and lethargy secondary to anaemia (iron, B12, or folate deficiency)
    \item Peripheral neuropathy, ataxia, or night blindness due to fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies (vitamins A, D, E, K)
    \item Easy bruising, petechiae, or bleeding gums due to vitamin K deficiency (hypoprothrombinaemia)
    \item Bone pain, tetany, or pathological fractures due to calcium and vitamin D malabsorption
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
It is crucial to identify the specific cause of malabsorption and differentiate it from non-malabsorptive diarrheal conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS):} a functional bowel disorder presenting with abdominal pain and altered bowel habits, but without malnutrition, steatorrhoea, or nutrient deficiencies.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD):} Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis, which can cause both inflammatory diarrhoea and malabsorption.
    \item \textbf{Microscopic colitis:} lymphocytic or collagenous colitis causing chronic watery diarrhoea, but typically without significant nutrient malabsorption.
    \item \textbf{Coeliac disease:} an autoimmune enteropathy triggered by dietary gluten, causing classic villous atrophy.
    \item \textbf{Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO):} excessive bacteria in the small intestine that deconjugate bile acids and consume vitamin B12.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients experiencing chronic diarrhoea, unexplained weight loss, or symptoms of nutrient deficiencies should undergo medical evaluation.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Collapse, severe dizziness, or confusion associated with severe dehydration or electrolyte imbalance
    \item Severe, acute abdominal pain with abdominal rigidity or rebound tenderness
    \item Active, massive gastrointestinal bleeding (melaena or haematochezia)
    \item Sudden weakness or confusion in a severely malnourished patient
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Investigations aim to confirm malabsorption, identify nutrient deficiencies, and diagnose the underlying cause.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Faecal elastase test:} a reliable non-invasive test to assess pancreatic exocrine function (low levels indicate pancreatic insufficiency).
    \item \textbf{Faecal fat estimation (72-hour collection):} the gold standard for diagnosing and quantifying steatorrhoea.
    \item \textbf{Coeliac serology:} testing for tissue transglutaminase IgA (tTG-IgA) and deamidated gliadin peptide (DGP) antibodies.
    \item \textbf{Upper endoscopy with duodenal biopsy:} essential for diagnosing mucosal disorders like coeliac disease, Whipple's disease, or giardiasis through histological examination.
    \item \textbf{Breath tests:} hydrogen or methane breath tests to evaluate for lactose intolerance or SIBO.
    \item \textbf{Nutritional blood profile:} assessing full blood count, iron studies, folate, vitamin B12, vitamin D, calcium, and zinc levels.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment involves managing the underlying cause and replacing deficient nutrients.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gluten-free diet:} strict, lifelong avoidance of gluten for patients diagnosed with coeliac disease.
    \item \textbf{Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT):} oral enzymes taken with meals for patients with pancreatic exocrine insufficiency.
    \item \textbf{Nutritional supplementation:} oral, enteral, or parenteral administration of vitamins (especially A, D, E, K, B12) and minerals (iron, calcium, magnesium, zinc).
    \item \textbf{Dietary modifications:} low-fat diets, lactose-free diets, or medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) oil supplementation as appropriate.
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} specific courses (e.g., rifaximin) for small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, or long-term antibiotics for Whipple's disease.
    \item \textbf{Immunomodulators or biologics:} for Crohn's disease to control mucosal inflammation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe protein-energy malnutrition and cachexia
    \item Osteopenia, osteoporosis, and increased risk of fragility fractures due to calcium and vitamin D deficiency
    \item Megaloblastic or iron-deficiency anaemia, causing cardiovascular strain
    \item Neurological deficits, including subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord (due to vitamin B12 deficiency)
    \item Growth retardation and developmental delay in children
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis depends entirely on the underlying cause. Conditions like coeliac disease or giardiasis have an excellent prognosis with complete resolution of symptoms and mucosal healing following appropriate dietary or antimicrobial treatment. In contrast, progressive diseases such as chronic pancreatitis or advanced Crohn's disease require lifelong multidisciplinary management to control symptoms and prevent severe nutritional deficits.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Adhere strictly to prescribed dietary restrictions (e.g., gluten-free or lactose-free diets).
    \item Take vitamin and mineral supplements consistently as directed by health professionals.
    \item Monitor weight regularly and report any unexplained weight loss or return of diarrheal symptoms to a doctor.
    \item Keep a food and symptom diary to help identify dietary triggers.
    \item Attend regular medical check-ups to monitor nutritional levels and bone density.
\end{itemize}
