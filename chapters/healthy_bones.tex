\chapter{Healthy Bones}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Healthy bones are strong bones that resist fracture and support the body throughout life. Bone is living tissue that is constantly being broken down and rebuilt, and bone health depends on genetics, nutrition, physical activity, hormones, and lifestyle. Peak bone mass is reached in the late 20s, after which bone density slowly declines, with accelerated loss in women after menopause. Key measures for healthy bones are adequate calcium and vitamin D, regular weight-bearing and resistance exercise, not smoking, and limiting alcohol. These measures build bone in youth, maintain it in adulthood, and slow loss in older age, reducing the risk of osteoporosis and fractures.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Osteoporosis and fractures are major health problems. Around 1 in 4 women and 1 in 6 men over 50 in Australia will experience an osteoporotic fracture, and around a million Australians have osteoporosis or osteopenia. The most serious fracture, the hip fracture, is associated with significant disability and mortality. Many people with osteoporosis do not know they have it until a fracture occurs. Bone health is influenced throughout life, and while genetics play a role, diet, exercise, and lifestyle have a large impact on fracture risk.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Bone health reflects the balance between bone formation and bone resorption.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bone remodelling:} bone is continuously remodelled, with osteoblasts building bone and osteoclasts breaking it down
    \item \textbf{Peak bone mass:} bone density increases through childhood and adolescence, peaking around age 25--30
    \item \textbf{Calcium:} the main mineral in bone, obtained from the diet
    \item \textbf{Vitamin D:} essential for calcium absorption; deficiency causes soft, weak bones
    \item \textbf{Exercise:} weight-bearing and resistance activity stimulates bone formation
    \item \textbf{Hormones:} oestrogen and testosterone protect bone; their decline after menopause and with ageing accelerates bone loss
    \item \textbf{Osteoporosis:} low bone mass with deterioration of bone structure, increasing fracture risk
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} age, female sex, family history, low body weight, smoking, alcohol, and some medicines
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Unhealthy bones usually cause no symptoms until a fracture occurs.

\begin{itemize}
    \item No symptoms in early bone loss
    \item Fractures from minor falls or injuries, most often of the wrist, hip, and spine
    \item Back pain and height loss from vertebral fractures
    \item A stooped posture (kyphosis)
    \item Reduced mobility and independence after fractures
    \item Risk factors identified at a bone density test or health check
    \item Bone pain in some conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Conditions affecting bone health and fracture risk should be considered.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Osteoporosis and osteopenia (low bone density)
    \item Vitamin D deficiency and osteomalacia (softened bone)
    \item Hyperparathyroidism and other hormonal conditions
    \item Coeliac disease and other causes of malabsorption
    \item Chronic kidney disease affecting bone
    \item Medicines, including long-term corticosteroids
    \item Inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis
    \item Multiple myeloma and other cancers affecting bone
    \item Genetic bone conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Talk to your doctor about bone health if you have risk factors, have had a fracture from a minor injury, or are approaching menopause. A bone density test (DEXA) is recommended for people at increased risk.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A suspected fracture, especially of the hip, with severe pain and inability to bear weight
    \item Sudden severe back pain with numbness or weakness of the legs, which may indicate a vertebral fracture with cord compression
    \item Falls with head injury
    \item Any serious injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Bone health is assessed with risk factors, blood tests, and bone density testing.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Risk factor assessment:} age, sex, fracture history, family history, and lifestyle
    \item \textbf{Bone density test (DEXA):} measures bone density at the hip and spine and gives a T-score
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} calcium, vitamin D, kidney function, and thyroid function
    \item \textbf{Fracture risk calculators:} tools such as FRAX estimate the risk of fracture
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} repeat bone density testing to monitor change and treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Bone health is managed with nutrition, exercise, and, where appropriate, medicines.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Calcium:} 1,000--1,300 mg daily from dairy, fortified foods, or supplements if intake is inadequate
    \item \textbf{Vitamin D:} sun exposure and, when deficient, supplements
    \item \textbf{Weight-bearing exercise:} walking, jogging, dancing, and stair climbing
    \item \textbf{Resistance exercise:} strength training builds and maintains bone
    \item \textbf{Falls prevention:} balance training, home safety, and vision checks
    \item \textbf{Smoking cessation and limited alcohol}
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} bisphosphonates and other treatments for osteoporosis
    \item \textbf{Hormone therapy:} considered in some women around menopause
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} repeat bone density tests track progress
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Osteoporotic fractures, including hip and vertebral fractures
    \item Chronic pain and disability after fractures
    \item Loss of independence and the need for aged care
    \item Increased mortality after hip fracture
    \item Fear of falling and reduced activity
    \item Complications of osteoporosis medicines (rare)
    \item Missed diagnosis of underlying conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Bone health can be improved at any age. Adequate calcium and vitamin D, and regular exercise, build bone in youth and slow loss in older age. Treatment of osteoporosis reduces fracture risk by around half, and falls prevention further reduces fractures. People who look after their bones from childhood onwards have the lowest risk of fractures. Even people diagnosed late benefit from treatment, which substantially reduces the risk of the hip and spine fractures that cause the most disability.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Eat a calcium-rich diet, including dairy, leafy greens, and fortified foods
    \item Get adequate vitamin D from safe sun exposure and, if needed, supplements
    \item Do weight-bearing and resistance exercise most days
    \item Do not smoke, and limit alcohol
    \item Maintain a healthy body weight
    \item Prevent falls with home safety, good lighting, and balance exercise
    \item Ask your doctor about a bone density test if you have risk factors
    \item Take osteoporosis medicines as prescribed
    \item Ensure children and teenagers build bone with calcium and activity
\end{itemize}
