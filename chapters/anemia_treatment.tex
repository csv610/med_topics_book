\chapter{Anemia Treatment}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Anemia treatment is the range of measures used to raise the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity and correct the underlying cause of the low red blood cell count. Because anemia has many causes, treatment is tailored to the type: iron deficiency is treated with iron, vitamin deficiencies with supplements, and blood loss with stopping the bleeding, while anemia of chronic disease is managed by treating the underlying illness.

Severe or life-threatening anemia may need a blood transfusion. The aim of treatment is to relieve symptoms such as fatigue and breathlessness, restore a normal blood count, and prevent complications, including the risk posed by the underlying cause itself.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Treatment needs follow the pattern of the condition itself. Iron-deficiency anemia is the most commonly treated type, especially in women of childbearing age, and most cases respond to oral iron. Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency are common in older people and in people with certain dietary or absorption problems.

Anemia of chronic disease is common in people with long-term illnesses such as kidney disease and arthritis, and its treatment is often part of managing those conditions. Blood transfusion is used much less often now than in the past, and is reserved mainly for severe or symptomatic anemia.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Iron deficiency:} caused by blood loss or low iron intake; treatment replaces iron and corrects the cause of the loss
    \item \textbf{Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency:} from poor diet, absorption problems, or certain medicines; treatment replaces the missing vitamin
    \item \textbf{Chronic disease and kidney failure:} inflammation or reduced erythropoietin slows red cell production; treatment targets the underlying condition
    \item \textbf{Hemolysis:} red cells are destroyed too quickly; treatment addresses the cause of destruction and supports red cell production
    \item \textbf{Bone marrow failure:} the marrow cannot produce red cells, which may need specialist treatment
    \item \textbf{Mechanism of treatment:} providing the missing nutrient or hormone, stopping losses, or replacing blood restores the red cell supply and eases symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Symptoms that signal treatment is needed: fatigue, weakness, pallor, and shortness of breath on exertion
    \item A fall in hemoglobin confirmed on blood testing
    \item Signs that a specific deficiency is present, such as a sore tongue with B12 deficiency or unusual food cravings with iron deficiency
    \item Symptoms of the underlying cause, such as heavy periods, black stools, or abdominal discomfort
    \item Improvement in energy and breathlessness expected within weeks once effective treatment starts
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Distinguishing iron-deficiency from anemia of chronic disease, which is essential because iron treatment is not helpful in the latter unless deficiency also exists
    \item Vitamin B12 deficiency versus folate deficiency, which need different supplements
    \item Anemia from bleeding versus anemia from reduced production
    \item Hidden bowel bleeding requiring investigation, such as endoscopy or colonoscopy
    \item Bone marrow or blood disorders requiring a hematology opinion
    \item Other causes of fatigue that are not due to anemia, such as thyroid disease or depression
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor before starting any anemia treatment on your own if the cause is not known, because taking the wrong supplement can delay the correct diagnosis. Seek review if treatment is not improving your symptoms.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, severe breathlessness, or fainting
    \item Very heavy bleeding that you cannot control
    \item Coughing up blood, or passing large amounts of blood in the stools or vomit
    \item Any severe symptom that develops suddenly during treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} symptoms, diet, menstrual and pregnancy history, medicines, family history, and any signs of bleeding
    \item \textbf{Full blood count:} confirms the anemia and shows how severe it is
    \item \textbf{Iron studies and ferritin:} establish whether iron deficiency is present
    \item \textbf{Vitamin B12 and folate levels:} guide vitamin replacement
    \item \textbf{Reticulocyte count:} shows whether the marrow is responding to treatment
    \item \textbf{Blood film:} checks for abnormal cells that suggest a particular cause
    \item \textbf{Investigations for the underlying cause:} such as endoscopy or colonoscopy for bowel bleeding, gynecologic assessment for heavy periods, or kidney function tests
    \item \textbf{Monitoring during treatment:} repeat blood counts to confirm a response and guide the length of treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Oral iron:} the main treatment for iron deficiency, taken daily as prescribed; taking it with vitamin C and on an empty stomach helps absorption
    \item \textbf{Managing iron side effects:} constipation, nausea, and dark stools are common; the doctor may adjust the dose or switch to a gentler preparation
    \item \textbf{Intravenous iron:} used when tablets are not absorbed, not tolerated, or unable to keep up with ongoing losses
    \item \textbf{Vitamin B12 and folate:} B12 may need to be given by injection if absorption is the problem; folate is usually taken as a tablet
    \item \textbf{Treating the cause:} stopping the source of bleeding, treating the underlying illness, or adjusting medicines
    \item \textbf{Blood transfusion:} for severe, symptomatic, or rapidly worsening anemia
    \item \textbf{Specialist treatments:} in kidney disease or bone marrow disorders, including stimulating agents or other therapy under a hematologist
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} repeat blood tests to confirm the count has recovered and iron stores are rebuilt, usually over several months
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Side effects of iron, especially constipation and stomach upset, that make people stop treatment too early
    \item Iron overload if iron is taken when the anemia is not caused by deficiency
    \item Recurrence of anemia if the underlying cause is not corrected
    \item Risks of transfusion, including reactions and fluid overload, though these are rare with modern screening
    \item Risks of intravenous iron, such as allergic-type reactions, which are uncommon
    \item A missed diagnosis of a serious underlying cause, such as bowel cancer, if treatment is given without investigation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With the right treatment, most anemias improve substantially. Iron-deficiency anemia usually lifts hemoglobin within weeks, although iron stores take longer to rebuild, so treatment is often continued for several months. Vitamin deficiency anemias respond well to replacement, though nerve symptoms from B12 deficiency may not fully resolve. Anemia of chronic disease improves when the underlying illness improves. Long-term success depends on finding and treating the cause, and on completing the full course of treatment even when symptoms have gone.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Follow the full course of prescribed iron or vitamins, even after you feel better
    \item Eat iron-rich foods and combine them with vitamin C; avoid tea or coffee with meals
    \item Do not take iron or other supplements for anemia unless a doctor has confirmed the cause
    \item Treat heavy periods and report any unexpected bleeding to a doctor
    \item Attend follow-up blood tests to confirm the anemia has fully resolved
    \item Seek prompt review if symptoms return after treatment has finished
\end{itemize}
