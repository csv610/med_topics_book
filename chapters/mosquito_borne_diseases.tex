\chapter{Mosquito-borne diseases}
\index{Mosquito-borne diseases}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Mosquito-borne diseases are infectious diseases transmitted to humans and animals through the bite of infected mosquitoes, which act as biological vectors. Mosquitoes transmit pathogens such as viruses (arboviruses) and parasites during blood feeding. Major global diseases include malaria, dengue fever, Zika virus, yellow fever, lymphatic filariasis, and West Nile virus. In many countries, endemic mosquito-borne diseases include Ross River virus, Barmah Forest virus, and Murray Valley encephalitis. These diseases represent a major public health challenge globally and locally, causing clinical manifestations ranging from self-limiting arthritic syndromes to fatal haemorrhagic fevers or encephalitis.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Globally, mosquito-borne diseases cause millions of deaths and constitute a major socioeconomic burden, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions. Malaria alone accounts for over 240 million cases and 600,000 deaths annually, primarily in sub-Saharan Africa. Dengue incidence has grown dramatically, with over 3.9 billion people at risk in more than 129 countries. In many countries, Ross River virus is the most common mosquito-borne infection, with approximately 5,000 cases reported annually. Transmission risk in many countries is highly seasonal (peaking in spring and summer) and associated with high rainfall, warm temperatures, and high tides that expand breeding habitats.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Pathogens are introduced into the host skin during mosquito probing and saliva injection, which contains vasodilators and immunosuppressive proteins:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral pathogens (Arboviruses):} Flaviviruses (dengue, Zika, Japanese encephalitis, Murray Valley encephalitis) and alphaviruses (Ross River, Barmah Forest, chikungunya) replicate in local dendritic cells before hematogenous dissemination.
    \item \textbf{Parasitic pathogens (Plasmodium):} Transmitted by female \textit{Anopheles} mosquitoes, sporozoites infect hepatocytes (hepatic phase) before invading erythrocytes (erythrocytic phase), causing clinical malaria.
    \item \textbf{Vector specificity:} \textit{Aedes} mosquitoes (primarily \textit{Aedes aegypti}) transmit dengue, Zika, and yellow fever. \textit{Culex} mosquitoes transmit Japanese encephalitis and Murray Valley encephalitis. \textit{Anopheles} transmit malaria.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory response:} Viral replication in joint tissue (e.g. Ross River virus) or endothelial cells (dengue) triggers immune activation, causing severe arthralgia, capillary leakage, or neuroinflammation.
    \item \textbf{Environmental drivers:} Climate change, urbanisation, and flooding alter vector distribution and transmission dynamics.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms vary depending on the specific pathogen involved, but many share common early clinical presentations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute febrile illness:} High fever, chills, and rigors (characteristically cyclical in malaria).
    \item \textbf{Severe arthralgia and myalgia:} Joint pain (often symmetric, affecting wrists, ankles, and fingers) and muscle aches, which can persist for months in Ross River or chikungunya infections.
    \item \textbf{Cutaneous rash:} A maculopapular rash appearing on the trunk and limbs, common in dengue, Zika, and Barmah Forest virus.
    \item \textbf{Retro-orbital headache:} Intense headache located behind the eyes, a hallmark feature of dengue fever.
    \item \textbf{Neurological signs (Encephalitic syndromes):} Stiff neck, photophobia, confusion, seizures, tremor, or focal paralysis (suggestive of Japanese encephalitis or Murray Valley encephalitis).
    \item \textbf{Haemorrhagic manifestations:} Spontaneous mucosal bleeding, petechiae, or severe bleeding secondary to thrombocytopenia in severe dengue (dengue haemorrhagic fever).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because early presentations are similar, clinicians must distinguish between specific mosquito-borne diseases and other febrile illnesses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Influenza or COVID-19:} Viral respiratory tract infections presenting with fever, headache, and myalgia, but distinguished by respiratory symptoms and diagnostic swabs.
    \item \textbf{Sepsis or Meningococcal disease:} Severe bacterial infections presenting with fever and rash, requiring urgent blood cultures and antibiotics.
    \item \textbf{Rheumatoid arthritis:} Can be confused with Ross River virus arthralgia, but presents with chronic joint destruction, rheumatoid factor, and lack of viral serology.
    \item \textbf{Leptospirosis or Rickettsial infections:} Non-mosquito zoonoses presenting with fever, rash, and muscle aches, with distinct occupational or animal exposure history.
    \item \textbf{Travel-related vs. Endemic infections:} Distinguish imported malaria or dengue from locally acquired Ross River virus in returning travellers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone returning from tropical areas or who has sustained mosquito bites and develops a sudden high fever, joint pain, or severe headache should consult a doctor.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Signs of severe malaria or encephalitis:} Sudden confusion, loss of consciousness, seizures, or severe breathing difficulties.
    \item \textbf{Severe bleeding:} Spontaneous bleeding from the gums, nose, or blood in stools or vomit (suspected haemorrhagic fever).
    \item \textbf{Signs of circulatory shock:} Extreme dizziness, cold and clammy skin, or rapid heart rate.
\end{itemize}
Other reasons to see a doctor include persistent joint pain or fatigue lasting more than a month, or a spreading maculopapular rash with a high fever.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Accurate diagnosis requires combining travel and exposure history with specific laboratory testing:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood film (thick and thin smears):} The gold standard for diagnosing malaria, allowing identification and quantification of \textit{Plasmodium} parasites.
    \item \textbf{Serological testing (ELISA):} Detects IgM and IgG antibodies to Ross River, Barmah Forest, dengue, or Zika viruses.
    \item \textbf{Polymerase chain reaction (PCR):} Detects viral RNA or parasitic DNA in blood during the early acute phase of infection before antibodies develop.
    \item \textbf{Full blood count (FBC):} Often shows thrombocytopenia (low platelets) and leukopenia (low white blood cells) in dengue and malaria.
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture and CSF PCR:} Indicated for patients presenting with encephalitic symptoms to identify Japanese encephalitis or Murray Valley encephalitis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is primarily supportive for viral infections, whereas parasitic infections require specific antimicrobial therapy:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antimalarial pharmacotherapy:} Specific regimens (e.g. artemisinin-based combination therapies, chloroquine, or primaquine to eradicate hypnozoites) depending on the \textit{Plasmodium} species and drug resistance patterns.
    \item \textbf{Supportive management for viral diseases:} Rest, hydration, and paracetamol for fever and pain relief. NSAIDs (e.g. ibuprofen) must be avoided in suspected dengue until haemorrhagic fever is ruled out due to bleeding risks.
    \item \textbf{Symptomatic support and physiotherapy:} For chronic Ross River virus arthralgia and fatigue to maintain joint mobility and muscle strength.
    \item \textbf{Hospitalisation and intensive care:} Indicated for severe malaria, severe dengue with plasma leakage, or encephalitic cases requiring airway support and seizure control.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Potential complications of mosquito-borne diseases include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cerebral malaria:} Seizures, coma, and brain damage due to parasitised erythrocytes sequestering in cerebral capillaries.
    \item \textbf{Dengue shock syndrome:} Severe circulatory collapse secondary to systemic capillary leakage and plasma volume depletion.
    \item \textbf{Chronic arthropathy:} Debilitating joint pain and fatigue lasting for months or years in Ross River virus or chikungunya.
    \item \textbf{Congenital Zika syndrome:} Microcephaly and severe neurodevelopmental defects in infants born to mothers infected with Zika virus during pregnancy.
    \item \textbf{Permanent neurological deficits:} Cognitive decline, motor weakness, or speech deficits following viral encephalitis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis depends on the pathogen and host factors. Ross River and Barmah Forest virus infections are rarely fatal, but symptoms like arthralgia and fatigue can persist for months, with most patients recovering fully within a year. Malaria and dengue have an excellent prognosis if diagnosed early and treated appropriately, but mortality exceeds 20\% in severe, untreated cases. Encephalitic syndromes (like Murray Valley encephalitis) have a guarded prognosis, with a significant mortality rate (up to 20\%) and high rates of permanent neurological sequelae in survivors.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventing mosquito bites and controlling breeding sites are the main strategies for prevention:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Use insect repellents:} Apply repellents containing DEET, picaridin, or oil of lemon eucalyptus to exposed skin.
    \item \textbf{Wear protective clothing:} Wear light-coloured, loose-fitting, long-sleeved shirts and long trousers, especially during peak mosquito activity (dawn and dusk).
    \item \textbf{Control vector breeding sites:} Empty standing water from flowerpots, buckets, tyres, and gutters around the home to prevent mosquito breeding.
    \item \textbf{Install fly screens:} Ensure windows and doors are fitted with intact mesh screens, and use mosquito nets if sleeping outdoors.
    \item \textbf{Vaccination and prophylaxis:} Receive recommended vaccines (e.g. Japanese encephalitis, yellow fever, dengue vaccines) and take prescribed chemoprophylaxis when travelling to malaria-endemic regions.
\end{itemize}
