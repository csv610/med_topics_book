\chapter{Zika}
\index{Zika}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Zika virus (ZIKV) is an enveloped, single-stranded RNA virus belonging to the family \textit{Flaviviridae} and the genus \textit{Flavivirus}. It is primarily transmitted to humans through the bites of infected mosquitoes, particularly those of the \textit{Aedes} genus, which are also responsible for transmitting other major tropical pathogens such as dengue, chikungunya, and yellow fever viruses. First isolated in 1947 from a sentinel rhesus monkey in the Zika Forest near Entebbe, Uganda, the virus was subsequently detected in humans in Uganda and the United Republic of Tanzania in 1952. For several decades, Zika virus infection was considered a minor medical curiosity, characterized by sporadic, mild, and self-limiting febrile illnesses in Africa and parts of Asia. However, the epidemiology of Zika shifted dramatically in the twenty-first century. A major outbreak occurred in the Federated States of Micronesia (Yap Island) in 2007, followed by a massive epidemic in French Polynesia and other Pacific islands during 2013--2014. The virus subsequently reached the Americas, culminating in an explosive pandemic in 2015--2016, with Brazil serving as the epicentre. During this pandemic, clinicians and researchers identified a strong causal link between maternal Zika virus infection during pregnancy and severe congenital malformations, most notably microcephaly, which is now classified under the umbrella term Congenital Zika Syndrome (CZS). Additionally, Zika virus was linked to an increased incidence of Guillain-Barr\'e syndrome, an acute, progressive demyelinating polyneuropathy in adults. These associations led the World Health Organization (WHO) to declare Zika virus a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) in February 2016. Today, although transmission has declined from the peak of the pandemic, Zika remains a significant global health threat, particularly for pregnant individuals and couples planning families, emphasizing the critical need for continued vigilance, diagnostic precision, and vector control.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiological landscape of Zika virus has evolved from localized, sporadic outbreaks to a widespread, global distribution. The primary vectors, \textit{Aedes aegypti} and \textit{Aedes albopictus}, thrive in tropical, subtropical, and temperate regions, placing billions of people worldwide at risk of exposure. Historically, serological evidence and sporadic viral isolations indicated that the virus was endemic to a narrow equatorial belt across Africa and Asia. The first documented outbreak outside these regions occurred in 2007 on Yap Island, Micronesia, where an estimated 73\% of the population aged three years and older was infected, although only a minority displayed clinical symptoms. This outbreak demonstrated the potential of the virus to cause high-attack-rate epidemics in immunologically naive populations.

Between 2013 and 2014, a much larger epidemic swept through French Polynesia, affecting approximately 30,000 individuals. This outbreak was clinically significant as it provided the first epidemiological signal linking Zika to neurological complications, specifically Guillain-Barr\'e syndrome. The virus then spread rapidly across the Pacific to South America. In early 2015, local transmission was confirmed in northeastern Brazil, initiating an unprecedented pandemic. By the end of 2016, active transmission was reported in over 80 countries and territories across the Americas, the Caribbean, Southeast Asia, and the Western Pacific.

In the Americas alone, hundreds of thousands of suspected and confirmed cases were reported, alongside thousands of cases of Congenital Zika Syndrome. The incidence was highest in urban areas characterized by high population density, inadequate sanitation, and abundant breeding sites for \textit{Aedes aegypti} mosquitoes. While the epidemic has subsided due to the development of widespread herd immunity in previously affected populations, Zika virus remains endemic in many regions, and the risk of localized outbreaks persists. The demographic groups most vulnerable to the devastating consequences of Zika virus are pregnant women and their fetuses, due to the high rate of vertical transmission and teratogenic outcomes. Consequently, epidemiological surveillance remains focused on monitoring viral activity in mosquito populations, tracking congenital anomalies, and providing targeted travel warnings for reproductive-aged individuals.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Zika virus is a member of the \textit{Flaviviridae} family, sharing structural and genetic similarities with other human pathogens such as dengue, West Nile, Japanese encephalitis, and yellow fever viruses. The virion is spherical, approximately 50 nanometres in diameter, and possesses a lipid bilayer envelope enclosing an icosahedral nucleocapsid. The genome consists of a single-stranded, positive-sense RNA molecule of approximately 10.8 kilobases, which encodes a single polyprotein. This polyprotein is cleaved by viral and host proteases into three structural proteins (capsid [C], membrane [M], and envelope [E]) and seven non-structural proteins (NS1, NS2A, NS2B, NS3, NS4A, NS4B, and NS5). The E protein is the primary target for neutralizing antibodies and facilitates binding to host cell receptors.

The transmission and pathophysiology of Zika virus involve several distinct mechanisms and cellular pathways:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vector-Borne Transmission}: The virus is transmitted to humans through the saliva of infected female mosquitoes, primarily \textit{Aedes aegypti}, during blood feeding. \textit{Aedes albopictus} is also a competent vector and has contributed to transmission, particularly in temperate regions. Once introduced into the skin dermis, the virus infects permissive cells, including epidermal keratinocytes, dermal fibroblasts, and immature dendritic cells (Langerhans cells).
    \item \textbf{Viral Entry and Cellular Receptors}: Zika virus utilizes specific host cell surface molecules for entry. Cellular receptors such as DC-SIGN, AXL, Tyro3, and TIM-1 facilitate receptor-mediated endocytosis. Among these, the AXL receptor tyrosine kinase is highly expressed on neural progenitor cells, radial glial cells, microglia, and brain endothelial cells, explaining the neurotropic nature of the virus.
    \item \textbf{Viraemia and Dissemination}: Following replication in dermal cells and local lymph nodes, the virus enters the bloodstream (viraemia), allowing dissemination to various organs, including the brain, eyes, joints, and reproductive tract. Viraemia is typically transient in immunocompetent adults, lasting three to seven days, but can be prolonged in pregnant women and immunocompromised individuals.
    \item \textbf{Vertical Transmission and Placental Pathophysiology}: The virus can cross the placental barrier during any trimester of pregnancy. It infects maternal decidual cells, placental trophoblasts, and fetal macrophages (Hofbauer cells). This leads to placental inflammation, compromised vascular integrity, and direct viral entry into the fetal circulation, allowing the virus to target the developing fetal brain.
    \item \textbf{Neurotropism and Impaired Neurogenesis}: Once in the fetal brain, Zika virus selectively infects neural progenitor cells (NPCs). The infection leads to cell death via apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, and impaired differentiation of NPCs. This disruption in neurogenesis halts the normal expansion of the cerebral cortex, resulting in microcephaly, ventriculomegaly, and subcortical calcifications.
    \item \textbf{Sexual Transmission}: Zika virus exhibits a unique persistent replication in the male reproductive tract, particularly in the testes, epididymis, and seminal vesicles. The virus can be detected in semen for several months after initial infection, often at concentrations significantly higher than in blood or urine. Sexual transmission can occur from symptomatic or asymptomatic males to their partners, as well as between female-to-male and male-to-male partners.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
In the majority of individuals (approximately 50\% to 80\% of cases), Zika virus infection is asymptomatic or causes only subclinical, mild symptoms that go unnoticed. When symptoms do occur, they typically manifest after an incubation period of 3 to 14 days and are characterized by a mild, self-limiting febrile illness lasting 2 to 7 days.

The classic clinical presentation in symptomatic adults and children involves a combination of the following signs and symptoms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fever}: Typically low-grade, ranging from 37.5 degrees Celsius to 38.5 degrees Celsius (99.5 degrees Fahrenheit to 101.3 degrees Fahrenheit). Unlike dengue or chikungunya, high fevers are uncommon.
    \item \textbf{Maculopapular Rash}: This is often the most prominent and earliest sign. The rash is typically erythematous, pruritic (itchy), and maculopapular (consisting of flat red spots and small bumps). It usually begins on the face and spreads to the trunk and extremities, often involving the palms of the hands and soles of the feet.
    \item \textbf{Arthralgia and Myalgia}: Patients frequently experience joint pain (arthralgia), which is usually bilateral and symmetric, primarily affecting the small joints of the hands and feet. This is often accompanied by swelling of the joints and generalized muscle pain (myalgia).
    \item \textbf{Non-purulent Conjunctivitis}: Bilateral conjunctival injection (redness of the eyes) without purulent discharge is a key distinguishing feature of Zika. Patients may also complain of retro-orbital pain, photophobia (sensitivity to light), and mild lacrimation.
    \item \textbf{Constitutional Symptoms}: Generalized headache, asthenia (weakness), fatigue, retro-orbital headache, and minor gastrointestinal disturbances (such as nausea, vomiting, or abdominal pain) may also be present.
\end{itemize}

In contrast to the mild course in adults, intrauterine exposure to the virus can lead to Congenital Zika Syndrome (CZS), which represents a severe, devastating clinical entity. Infants born with CZS exhibit a distinct pattern of physical and neurological abnormalities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Severe Microcephaly}: Characterized by a head circumference that is more than three standard deviations below the mean for gestational age and sex, often accompanied by a collapsed skull structure and redundant, folded scalp skin.
    \item \textbf{Brain Abnormalities}: Ventriculomegaly (enlarged ventricles), subcortical calcifications, thin cerebral cortex, abnormal gyral patterns (such as polymicrogyria or lissencephaly), and cerebellar hypoplasia.
    \item \textbf{Ocular Damage}: Macular scarring, focal pigmentary retinal lesions, optic nerve atrophy, microphthalmia, and chorioretinal atrophy, which can lead to severe visual impairment or blindness.
    \item \textbf{Neuromuscular and Skeletal Defects}: Severe hypertonia (increased muscle tone), spasticity, hyperreflexia, congenital contractures (such as arthrogryposis or clubfoot), and difficulty swallowing (dysphagia).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Given that the clinical presentation of Zika virus infection closely resembles that of several other acute febrile illnesses, particularly those endemic to the same geographic regions, a careful differential diagnosis is essential. Clinicians must consider both infectious and non-infectious causes when evaluating patients with acute fever, rash, and joint pain.

The primary differential diagnoses include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dengue Fever}: Dengue is a flavivirus transmitted by the same mosquito vectors. While it shares symptoms like fever, rash, and myalgia, dengue typically presents with a sudden onset of much higher fever, severe headache, intense retro-orbital pain, and severe muscle and bone pain (hence the name ``breakbone fever''). Lab findings in dengue often reveal marked leukopenia and thrombocytopenia, and there is a risk of severe dengue characterized by plasma leakage, severe haemorrhage, and organ impairment.
    \item \textbf{Chikungunya Fever}: Chikungunya is an alphavirus that co-circulates in many Zika-endemic regions. The hallmark of chikungunya is sudden, high fever and extremely severe, debilitating polyarthralgia that is often bilateral and symmetric. While the joint pain associated with Zika is mild and transient, the joint pain of chikungunya can be chronic, persisting for months or even years, leading to significant morbidity.
    \item \textbf{Malaria}: An infection caused by \textit{Plasmodium} parasites, malaria must be ruled out in travelers returning from endemic areas. It typically presents with cyclical high fevers, chills, diaphoresis (sweating), splenomegaly, and laboratory abnormalities like anaemia and thrombocytopenia. Unlike Zika, untreated malaria (particularly \textit{Plasmodium falciparum}) can be rapidly fatal.
    \item \textbf{Measles (Rubeola)}: A highly contagious viral infection that presents with high fever, cough, coryza (runny nose), and conjunctivitis (the ``three Cs''), along with Koplik spots on the buccal mucosa. The rash in measles begins at the hairline and spreads downwards, and is typically confluent, unlike the itchy, scattered maculopapular rash of Zika.
    \item \textbf{Rubella (German Measles)}: Rubella presents with a mild maculopapular rash and low-grade fever, similar to Zika. However, rubella is classically associated with prominent post-auricular, suboccipital, and posterior cervical lymphadenopathy. Congenital Rubella Syndrome must also be differentiated from Congenital Zika Syndrome, though the former more commonly features cataracts, congenital heart defects, and sensorineural deafness without the severe cranial collapse seen in Zika.
    \item \textbf{Parvovirus B19}: In adults, Parvovirus B19 can cause acute, symmetric polyarthritis mimicking the arthralgia of Zika. In children, it causes erythema infectiosum (``slapped cheek'' disease). It can also cause transient aplastic crisis in patients with underlying haemoglobinopathies.
    \item \textbf{Leptospirosis}: A bacterial zoonotic infection transmitted through contact with water or soil contaminated with animal urine. It presents with high fever, severe headache, myalgia (particularly in the calves), and conjunctival suffusion (redness without inflammatory exudate). Severe cases (Weil's disease) can progress to renal failure, jaundice, and haemorrhagic manifestations.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most cases of Zika virus infection are mild and do not require hospitalization. However, certain situations warrant prompt medical consultation, and some symptoms represent medical emergencies that require immediate intervention.

\textbf{Consult a Doctor or Healthcare Provider if:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item You are pregnant and have traveled to or reside in an area with active Zika virus transmission, regardless of whether you have experienced symptoms. Regular prenatal monitoring and diagnostic testing are essential.
    \item You develop a fever, rash, joint pain, or red eyes within two weeks of traveling to a Zika-affected region.
    \item You or your partner have traveled to an endemic area and are planning a pregnancy, to discuss safe timelines for conception and appropriate screening.
    \item You experience symptoms that do not improve after a week, or if symptoms worsen significantly.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Seek Immediate Emergency Medical Care if:}
Some individuals may develop rare but life-threatening neurological complications, such as Guillain-Barr\'e syndrome, following a viral illness. Immediate emergency services should be contacted if any of the following symptoms occur:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rapidly progressive, ascending muscle weakness, beginning in the legs and spreading to the arms and upper body.
    \item Difficulty breathing, shortness of breath, or a feeling of suffocation.
    \item Difficulty swallowing, speaking, chewing, or moving facial muscles.
    \item Severe, unexplained numbness, tingling, or pain in the hands and feet.
    \item Loss of coordination, inability to stand, or sudden paralysis.
\end{itemize}
If you or someone in your care exhibits these emergency warning signs, call emergency services immediately. Contact \textbf{local emergency services} in many countries, \textbf{911} in the United States, \textbf{112} in Europe, or your local emergency number. Inform the dispatcher and the arriving medical team of any recent travel history to tropical or subtropical regions.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Accurate diagnosis of Zika virus infection relies on a combination of clinical evaluation, travel history, and laboratory investigations. Because the symptoms of Zika are non-specific and overlap with other flaviviruses, laboratory confirmation is critical, particularly for pregnant women.

The primary diagnostic investigations include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Molecular Testing (Reverse Transcription-Polymerase Chain Reaction - RT-PCR)}: This is the gold standard for diagnosing acute Zika virus infection. RT-PCR is used to detect viral RNA in clinical specimens.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Serum Testing}: Zika RNA is typically detectable in blood during the viraemic phase, which is generally within the first 7 days after symptom onset.
        \item \textbf{Urine Testing}: Zika RNA is often detectable in urine for a longer period than in blood, frequently up to 14 days or more after symptom onset. Combining serum and urine RT-PCR testing increases diagnostic sensitivity.
        \item \textbf{Other Fluids}: In specific cases, RT-PCR can be performed on semen, saliva, amniotic fluid, or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Serological Testing (MAC-ELISA)}: IgM antibodies against Zika virus typically become detectable from day 4 of the illness and can persist for several months. Serum is tested using the Zika immunoglobulin M (IgM) antibody capture enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (MAC-ELISA). However, serology has a major limitation: high cross-reactivity with antibodies from other flaviviruses, such as dengue or yellow fever, and in individuals who have received flavivirus vaccinations (e.g., yellow fever or Japanese encephalitis vaccines).
    \item \textbf{Plaque Reduction Neutralization Test (PRNT)}: PRNT is a highly specific confirmatory test used to measure virus-specific neutralizing antibodies. It is employed to resolve false-positive or cross-reactive ELISA results. A PRNT titre of 10 or greater for Zika, along with a titre at least four-fold higher than the titre against other co-circulating flaviviruses (like dengue), confirms a recent Zika infection.
    \item \textbf{Prenatal and Fetal Investigations}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Fetal Ultrasonography}: For pregnant women with suspected or confirmed Zika infection, serial fetal ultrasounds (every 3 to 4 weeks) are recommended to monitor fetal growth, assess amniotic fluid volume, and detect structural brain abnormalities, microcephaly, ventriculomegaly, and intracranial calcifications.
        \item \textbf{Amniocentesis}: Amniocentesis to perform RT-PCR on amniotic fluid may be considered in consultation with maternal-fetal medicine specialists. However, it carries risks, and a negative result does not exclude fetal infection, while a positive result cannot predict the severity of birth defects.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Postnatal Newborn Evaluation}: Infants born to mothers with confirmed or suspected Zika virus infection should undergo a comprehensive evaluation, including:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Testing of umbilical cord blood or infant serum/urine for Zika RNA (RT-PCR) and Zika IgM antibodies.
        \item Physical examination, including precise measurement of head circumference, length, and weight.
        \item Cranial ultrasound to detect structural brain abnormalities or calcifications. CT or MRI may be indicated if the ultrasound is abnormal.
        \item Comprehensive ophthalmologic examination within the first month of life to check for retinal lesions or optic nerve abnormalities.
        \item Auditory brainstem response (ABR) testing to screen for sensorineural hearing loss.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Currently, there is no specific antiviral therapy or vaccine available to treat or prevent Zika virus infection. The primary goal of medical management is supportive care, aimed at relieving symptoms and preventing complications.

The management strategy involves the following components:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Supportive Symptomatic Care}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Rest}: Adequate bed rest is recommended during the acute phase of the illness to help the body fight the infection.
        \item \textbf{Hydration}: Patients should drink plenty of fluids (water, oral rehydration solutions, clear broths) to prevent dehydration, especially if fever or vomiting is present.
        \item \textbf{Pharmacotherapy for Fever and Pain}: Paracetamol (acetaminophen) is the preferred medication for reducing fever and managing joint and muscle pain.
        \item \textbf{Avoidance of NSAIDs and Aspirin}: It is critical to avoid non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen, naproxen, and aspirin until dengue fever has been laboratory-excluded. This precaution is necessary because NSAIDs and aspirin can increase the risk of severe bleeding and haemorrhage if the patient actually has dengue fever, and aspirin carries a risk of Reye's syndrome in children.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Management in Pregnancy}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Pregnant women with laboratory-confirmed or suspected Zika infection require close monitoring by a multidisciplinary team, including obstetricians, maternal-fetal medicine specialists, and pediatric infectious disease experts.
        \item Serial fetal ultrasounds are performed to monitor fetal anatomy and growth.
        \item Psychosocial support and counseling should be provided to help families navigate the uncertainty and potential outcomes of congenital infection.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Treatment of Neurological Complications}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Patients who develop Guillain-Barr\'e syndrome (GBS) require immediate admission to a hospital, typically in an intensive care unit (ICU) to monitor respiratory function closely.
        \item Management of GBS includes supportive care (respiratory support, physical therapy) and specific treatments such as intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) therapy or plasmapheresis (plasma exchange) to reduce the severity and duration of the autoimmune attack.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Care for Infants with Congenital Zika Syndrome}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Infants diagnosed with CZS require lifelong, comprehensive, multidisciplinary care.
        \item Management includes developmental pediatric monitoring, physical and occupational therapy to manage hypertonia and contractures, speech and feeding therapy for dysphagia, anticonvulsant medications to manage seizures, and regular ophthalmologic and audiological follow-ups.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While the acute illness caused by Zika virus is generally mild, the virus is associated with severe, life-altering complications, primarily affecting developing fetuses and the nervous system.

The most significant complications associated with Zika virus infection include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Congenital Zika Syndrome (CZS)}: This is the most devastating complication, occurring when the virus is transmitted vertically from mother to fetus during pregnancy. CZS comprises a distinct pattern of severe birth defects:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Severe Microcephaly}: Underdevelopment of the brain, leading to a significantly small head.
        \item \textbf{Brain Abnormalities}: Cerebral atrophy, ventriculomegaly, subcortical calcifications, cortical malformations, and hypoplasia of the cerebellum and brainstem.
        \item \textbf{Congenital Contractures}: Joint deformities such as arthrogryposis (multiple joint contractures) or clubfoot, resulting from abnormal muscle tone and neurological damage during fetal development.
        \item \textbf{Ocular Damage}: Macular scarring, focal pigmentary retinopathy, optic nerve hypoplasia, and chorioretinal lesions, which can cause permanent vision loss.
        \item \textbf{Neurological Impairment}: Severe hypertonia, spasticity, feeding difficulties (dysphagia), irritability, developmental delays, intellectual disability, and refractory epilepsy.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Guillain-Barr\'e Syndrome (GBS)}: Zika virus is an established trigger for GBS, an autoimmune disorder in which the body's immune system mistakenly attacks its own peripheral nerves. This results in rapid-onset muscle weakness, loss of reflexes, and sensory disturbances (tingling, numbness). In severe cases, GBS can paralyze the respiratory muscles, requiring mechanical ventilation in an ICU, and can lead to permanent neurological deficits or death.
    \item \textbf{Other Neurological Disorders}: Rare reports have linked Zika virus infection to other acute neurological conditions, including encephalitis (inflammation of the brain), meningitis (inflammation of the membranes covering the brain and spinal cord), myelitis (inflammation of the spinal cord), and acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM).
    \item \textbf{Adverse Pregnancy Outcomes}: Maternal Zika infection is associated with an increased risk of miscarriage (spontaneous abortion), stillbirth, fetal growth restriction, and placental insufficiency, even in the absence of obvious structural birth defects.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals infected with Zika virus varies dramatically depending on the patient's demographic group, particularly whether the infection occurred during fetal development or in postnatal life.

For the vast majority of immunocompetent adults, adolescents, and children, the prognosis is excellent. The acute phase of Zika fever is a mild, self-limiting illness. Symptoms typically resolve fully within 2 to 7 days, and long-term sequelae are rare. Hospitalization is infrequently required, and deaths directly attributed to acute Zika virus infection in adults are extremely rare, usually occurring only in individuals with severe pre-existing comorbidities, immune suppression, or those who develop severe neurological complications.

For individuals who develop Guillain-Barr\'e syndrome, the prognosis is generally favorable, but recovery can be slow and challenging. Approximately 80\% of patients with GBS recover completely or walk independently within six months to a year, although some may experience persistent fatigue, muscle weakness, or pain. The mortality rate for GBS is approximately 3\% to 5\%, primarily due to respiratory failure, cardiac arrhythmias, or secondary infections.

In stark contrast, the prognosis for fetuses infected with Zika virus in utero is guarded and frequently poor. The risk of congenital anomalies is highest when infection occurs during the first trimester, with studies indicating that approximately 5\% to 15\% of infants born to mothers infected during pregnancy develop CZS or other Zika-related complications. The long-term outcomes for children born with Congenital Zika Syndrome are marked by chronic, lifelong disability. These children require continuous, multidisciplinary medical, developmental, and social support. They face severe neurodevelopmental delays, persistent motor deficits, intellectual disabilities, intractable seizures, and sensory impairments (hearing and vision loss), which severely limit their independence and overall quality of life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
In the absence of a licensed vaccine or specific antiviral treatment, prevention is the primary strategy for controlling the spread of Zika virus and protecting vulnerable populations. Prevention efforts focus on vector control, personal protection against mosquito bites, and preventing sexual transmission.

\textbf{Vector Control and Personal Protection Against Mosquitoes:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Use of Insect Repellents}: Apply Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)-registered insect repellents containing active ingredients such as DEET, picaridin, IR3535, oil of lemon eucalyptus (OLE), or para-menthane-diol (PMD). Always follow the product label instructions, especially when applying to children.
    \item \textbf{Wear Protective Clothing}: Wear long-sleeved shirts, long pants, socks, and a hat when outdoors. Treating clothing and gear with permethrin provides an additional layer of protection.
    \item \textbf{Eliminate Breeding Sites}: Aedes mosquitoes breed in standing water. Regularly empty, clean, turn over, cover, or discard containers that can hold water, such as flowerpots, buckets, animal dishes, birdbaths, and discarded tires, both inside and outside the home.
    \item \textbf{Secure Living Spaces}: Install or repair window and door screens to keep mosquitoes outside. Use air conditioning when available. If sleeping in unscreened rooms or outdoors, sleep under an insecticide-treated bed net.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Preventing Sexual Transmission:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Use Condoms}: Consistent and correct use of condoms (male or female) or abstinence from sexual activity can prevent the sexual transmission of Zika virus.
    \item \textbf{Guidelines for Travelers}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Men}: Men returning from areas with active Zika transmission should use condoms or abstain from sex for at least 3 months after return (or from the start of symptoms if they were infected).
        \item \textbf{Women}: Women returning from affected areas should use condoms or abstain from sex for at least 2 months after return (or from the start of symptoms).
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Pregnancy and Travel Restrictions:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pregnant women should avoid travel to any area with an active Zika virus outbreak or high risk of transmission.
    \item If travel is unavoidable, pregnant women must strictly follow mosquito bite prevention measures and discuss testing options with their healthcare provider upon return.
    \item Couples planning a pregnancy should consult guidelines regarding wait times before attempting conception after potential exposure (at least 3 months for men and 2 months for women).
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Self-Care for Infected Individuals:}
If you are diagnosed with or suspect you have Zika virus infection, practice the following self-care measures to promote recovery and prevent further transmission:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Get plenty of rest and stay well-hydrated by drinking water and electrolyte solutions.
    \item Take paracetamol (acetaminophen) to manage fever and pain. Do not take aspirin or other NSAIDs (such as ibuprofen or naproxen) until dengue has been ruled out.
    \item Prevent mosquito bites during the first week of illness. During viraemia, mosquitoes biting an infected person can acquire the virus and transmit it to others, accelerating local spread.
\end{itemize}
