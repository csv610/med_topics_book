\chapter{ACL Injury}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An ACL injury is a sprain or tear of the anterior cruciate ligament, one of the main ligaments that stabilise the knee joint. The ACL runs diagonally through the middle of the knee and prevents the shin bone (tibia) from sliding too far forward on the thigh bone (femur). It is essential for sports that involve sudden stopping, landing, twisting, and changing direction.

Injuries range from mild stretching (a partial sprain) to a complete tear in which the ligament is torn in two. Because a completely torn ACL does not heal on its own, these injuries typically require a lengthy rehabilitation programme and sometimes surgery. They are frequently accompanied by damage to other knee structures, especially the menisci (shock-absorbing cartilages).

\section*{Epidemiology}
ACL injuries are among the most common serious knee injuries in sport, particularly football, netball, basketball, Australian rules football, rugby, and skiing. They occur most often in adolescents and young adults. In sports such as netball, football, and basketball, women have a significantly higher rate of ACL injury than men who play the same sport, owing to a combination of anatomical, hormonal, and biomechanical factors.

The majority of injuries are non-contact, occurring during landing or sudden changes of direction rather than from a direct blow, and around half are accompanied by a meniscal tear. The incidence has risen in many countries alongside increasing participation in high-intensity sport.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Most ACL injuries happen without direct contact. A common mechanism is landing awkwardly from a jump with the knee nearly straight and collapsing inward (valgus collapse), or suddenly stopping while changing direction. Twisting injuries, where the foot is planted and the body rotates over the knee, can also tear the ligament, and direct blows to the side of the knee account for the remainder.

Risk factors include fatigue, weak quadriceps, hamstring, and hip muscles, poor landing technique, and a history of previous knee injury. When the ligament tears, blood vessels within the joint bleed, causing rapid swelling, and the knee becomes less stable because the tibia can now shift forward relative to the femur. In the absence of repair, a complete tear leaves permanent laxity that must be compensated for by muscles and, in selected cases, surgery.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A ``pop'' sensation:} a sudden popping or snapping feeling heard or felt at the time of injury
    \item \textbf{Severe pain:} often preventing the person from continuing the activity
    \item \textbf{Rapid swelling:} the knee swells noticeably within a few hours as the joint fills with blood
    \item \textbf{Instability:} a feeling of the knee ``giving way'' or shifting under load
    \item \textbf{Reduced function:} difficulty bearing weight or fully straightening the knee
    \item \textbf{Delayed features:} tenderness, stiffness, and loss of full movement in the days after injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Several other knee injuries can look similar to an ACL tear, particularly in the first few days.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Meniscal tear (a tear of the knee's shock-absorbing cartilage)
    \item Medial or lateral collateral ligament sprain
    \item Patellar dislocation or subluxation
    \item Patellofemoral pain syndrome
    \item Tibial plateau fracture or other bone injuries around the knee
    \item Posterior cruciate ligament injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor promptly after any injury that produces a popping sensation, significant swelling, or a feeling that the knee is unstable, especially if the knee cannot be straightened or weight cannot be borne. Early assessment helps plan rehabilitation and detect associated injuries such as meniscal tears.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A major knee injury with a visibly deformed or dislocated joint
    \item The foot or lower leg feeling numb, cold, or turning pale (possible nerve or blood-vessel damage)
    \item An open wound or bone visible through the skin
    \item An inability to bear any weight with severe, worsening pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} how the injury happened, whether a ``pop'' was felt, and how quickly the knee swelled
    \item \textbf{Examination:} assessment of effusion and knee stability using the Lachman test, anterior drawer test, and pivot-shift test
    \item \textbf{X-ray:} used to exclude fractures around the knee, including avulsion fractures
    \item \textbf{MRI:} the best test for confirming an ACL tear and detecting meniscal, cartilage, and other ligament damage
    \item \textbf{Arthroscopy:} keyhole inspection of the joint, mainly used during surgery rather than for diagnosis alone
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immediate first aid:} rest, ice, compression, and elevation to control swelling, with crutches if needed
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple medicines such as paracetamol or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs in the short term
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} essential whether or not surgery is performed, restoring movement and strength and progressing to sport-specific training
    \item \textbf{Bracing:} may help some people with instability, although it is not a substitute for rehabilitation
    \item \textbf{Surgery (ACL reconstruction):} considered mainly for young, active people, for those with persistent giving way, and where there are additional meniscal tears requiring repair
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular review to guide a graduated return to sport, which usually takes 9 to 12 months after surgery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic knee instability, with the knee giving way during daily activities or sport
    \item Meniscal tears, which may require further treatment
    \item Cartilage damage and the early development of osteoarthritis of the knee
    \item Reduced range of motion or persistent stiffness (arthrofibrosis)
    \item Re-injury, including to the same knee or the opposite knee
    \item Complications of reconstruction, including graft failure, infection, and nerve injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the severity of the injury, the person's activity level, and the quality of rehabilitation. People who do not participate in cutting and pivoting sports often do well without surgery once the knee has recovered its strength and stability.

For those who need a stable, active knee, surgery combined with rehabilitation gives good results in most cases, with the majority returning to sport. Full recovery takes time: most people return to sport 9 to 12 months after reconstruction, and returning too early increases the risk of re-injury. In the long term, a history of ACL injury raises the chance of knee osteoarthritis whatever treatment is chosen, underlining the importance of ongoing muscle conditioning.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Participate in neuromuscular training programmes that teach safe landing, balance, and cutting technique
    \item Strengthen the muscles around the knee, including the quadriceps, hamstring, and hip muscles
    \item Warm up properly and avoid training while fatigued
    \item Use correct technique and appropriate footwear for the sport and surface
    \item After any knee injury, complete a supervised rehabilitation programme before returning to sport
\end{itemize}
