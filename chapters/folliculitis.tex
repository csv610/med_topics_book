\chapter{Folliculitis}
\index{Folliculitis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Folliculitis is inflammation or infection of the hair follicles, the small openings in the skin from which hairs grow. It appears as small red bumps or pus-filled pimples around hair openings, and it can occur anywhere on the body that has hair, but is most common on the scalp, face, neck, arms, legs, and buttocks. Folliculitis is usually caused by bacteria, most often Staphylococcus aureus, but it can also be caused by fungi, viruses, or irritation from friction, shaving, heat, and chemicals. Most cases are mild and clear on their own or with simple self-care, but recurrent or deep folliculitis can be more troublesome, and severe infections can spread. This chapter explains the causes, types, and treatment of folliculitis.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Folliculitis is one of the most common skin conditions, affecting people of all ages. It is particularly common in hot, humid climates and in people who sweat heavily, wear tight clothing, shave, or use communal pools, spas, and gyms. Barber's itch (of the beard area) and hot tub folliculitis (from Pseudomonas in spas) are specific common forms. Recurrent folliculitis affects a significant minority, especially people who are overweight, diabetic, or carry staphylococcal bacteria on the skin. While most cases are minor, deep forms such as boils (furuncles) and carbuncles can require medical treatment.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bacterial:} Staphylococcus aureus is the most common cause; it enters the follicle through minor breaks in the skin from shaving, friction, or scratching
    \item \textbf{Hot tub folliculitis:} Pseudomonas aeruginosa from inadequately chlorinated spas and pools causes a rash that appears hours to days after exposure
    \item \textbf{Fungal:} pityrosporum folliculitis, caused by a yeast that normally lives on the skin, produces itchy bumps on the chest, back, and shoulders
    \item \textbf{Viral:} herpes simplex and molluscum can infect follicles
    \item \textbf{Irritant:} friction from tight clothing, heat, sweat, occlusion, and chemicals (including oils and some hair products) irritate follicles
    \item \textbf{Shaving:} causes pseudofolliculitis barbae, where hairs curl back into the skin, most commonly in people with curly hair
    \item \textbf{Predisposing factors:} obesity, diabetes, reduced immunity, antibiotics that alter skin bacteria, and nasal carriage of staphylococci
    \item \textbf{Progression:} superficial infection involves only the follicle opening; deep infection (furuncle, carbuncle) involves the whole follicle and surrounding tissue, forming boils
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Small red bumps or pustules, each centred on a hair follicle
    \item Itching, burning, or tenderness of the affected skin
    \item Crusting and healing within days to a week in mild cases
    \item Distribution related to the cause: beard area after shaving, hot tub exposure sites, or the scalp, chest, and back with fungal forms
    \item Recurrent crops in chronic folliculitis
    \item Boils: larger, painful, deep lumps that come to a head and drain pus
    \item Carbuncles: a cluster of boils with fever and feeling unwell
    \item Scarring and hair loss with deep or repeated infections
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Rashes with small bumps have several causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acne, which involves sebaceous glands rather than follicles alone and appears on the face, chest, and back
    \item Keratosis pilaris, harmless rough bumps on the upper arms
    \item Insect bites and contact dermatitis
    \item Impetigo, a superficial skin infection with honey-coloured crusts
    \item Herpes simplex and shingles, which have clustered blisters
    \item Hidradenitis suppurativa, a chronic condition affecting the groin and armpits
    \item The depth, distribution, and history distinguish these
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most folliculitis is managed at home, but medical care is needed for extensive, spreading, or recurrent infection, boils that do not drain or are large, fever, or infection in people with diabetes or reduced immunity.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever, chills, or feeling very unwell with a skin infection
    \item Rapidly spreading redness, pain, or swelling, which may indicate cellulitis or sepsis
    \item Red streaks extending from the affected area
    \item Severe pain or an infected area on the face, which is higher risk
    \item Collapse or confusion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} distribution, triggers such as shaving and hot tubs, and risk factors
    \item \textbf{Swabs:} bacterial swabs of pus to identify the organism and antibiotic sensitivity, especially in recurrent cases
    \item \textbf{Skin scrapings:} for fungal folliculitis
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} for diabetes or immune problems in recurrent or severe infection
    \item \textbf{Nasal swabs:} to detect staphylococcal carriage in recurrent cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Self-care:} warm compresses, avoiding shaving the area, wearing loose clothing, and keeping the skin clean and dry
    \item \textbf{Antiseptics:} antibacterial washes and topical antiseptics reduce bacterial load
    \item \textbf{Topical antibiotics:} for bacterial folliculitis, such as fusidic acid or clindamycin
    \item \textbf{Oral antibiotics:} for extensive, deep, or recurrent infection, with courses guided by swab results
    \item \textbf{Fungal treatment:} antifungal washes or creams for pityrosporum folliculitis
    \item \textbf{Boils:} warm compresses and drainage by a doctor when needed; antibiotics for surrounding infection
    \item \textbf{Shaving adjustments:} shave in the direction of hair growth, use a clean sharp razor, and consider alternative methods for pseudofolliculitis
    \item \textbf{Decolonisation:} in recurrent staphylococcal infection, a short course of nasal ointment and antibacterial body wash
    \item \textbf{Address predisposing factors:} weight management, diabetes control, and avoiding hot tubs with poor chlorination
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Boils and carbuncles, which can recur and cause scarring
    \item Cellulitis, a deeper spreading skin infection
    \item Abscess formation requiring drainage
    \item Scarring and permanent hair loss in severe or chronic cases
    \item Spreading infection and sepsis in people with diabetes or weakened immunity
    \item The social and cosmetic impact of persistent folliculitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Mild folliculitis resolves within days with simple measures, and even recurrent cases are usually controlled with hygiene changes, antiseptics, and targeted treatment of the underlying cause. Most boils drain and heal without complication, and deep infections respond to antibiotics and drainage. For people with recurrent staphylococcal infection, decolonisation programs and attention to shaving and skin care produce lasting improvement. With appropriate treatment, folliculitis rarely causes permanent harm.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep skin clean and dry, and change out of sweaty or tight clothing promptly
    \item Shave in the direction of hair growth with a clean, sharp razor, and moisturise afterwards
    \item Avoid sharing razors, towels, and clothing
    \item Shower promptly after swimming, and check spa pools are properly chlorinated
    \item Use antibacterial washes if prone to folliculitis
    \item Apply warm compresses to early boils to encourage drainage
    \item Avoid squeezing or picking lesions, which deepens infection
    \item See a doctor for recurrent, extensive, or painful infection
\end{itemize}
