\chapter{Lightheadedness}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Lightheadedness is a common, non-specific symptom characterised by a sensation of feeling faint, woozy, unsteady, or close to losing consciousness. It is distinct from vertigo, which is defined as a false sensation of spinning or rotational movement of either the patient or their environment. Lightheadedness is a subtype of dizziness that typically arises from a transient reduction in blood flow to the brain (cerebral hypoperfusion), systemic metabolic disturbances, or psychological factors. While often benign and self-limiting, such as when arising from mild dehydration or standing up too quickly, persistent or recurrent lightheadedness can be a sign of significant underlying cardiovascular, neurological, or endocrine pathology, and it represents a major contributor to the risk of falls and injury, particularly in older individuals.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Lightheadedness and related forms of dizziness are among the most frequent complaints in primary care and emergency departments, affecting up to 15\% to 30\% of the general population at some point in their lives. The prevalence increases significantly with age, exceeding 30\% in individuals over the age of 65. In older adults, lightheadedness is frequently multifactorial, stemming from a combination of age-related physiological changes, polypharmacy, and chronic health conditions. It is more commonly reported in females than in males. In Australia, lightheadedness is a leading cause of presentations in general practice among older adults, often serving as a key risk factor for fall-related hospitalisations, which impose a substantial burden on the healthcare system.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The primary pathophysiological mechanism underlying lightheadedness is a temporary drop in systemic blood pressure or cerebral blood flow, which deprives the brain of oxygen and glucose.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Orthostatic hypotension:} A sudden drop in blood pressure occurring within three minutes of standing up from a sitting or lying position, commonly caused by autonomic dysfunction, volume depletion, or medications.
    \item \textbf{Dehydration and hypovolaemia:} Insufficient fluid intake, excessive sweating, vomiting, or diarrhoea reduces circulating blood volume, lowering blood pressure and cerebral perfusion.
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular conditions:} Cardiac arrhythmias (such as bradycardia, heart block, or tachyarrhythmias), structural heart disease (such as aortic stenosis or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy), and heart failure can impair cardiac output and trigger lightheadedness.
    \item \textbf{Medication side effects:} Antihypertensives, diuretics, beta-blockers, vasodilators, tricyclic antidepressants, and sedatives can lower blood pressure or cause orthostatic instability.
    \item \textbf{Hypoglycaemia:} Low blood glucose levels, most commonly occurring in patients with diabetes mellitus taking insulin or oral hypoglycaemic agents, deprives brain cells of their primary energy source.
    \item \textbf{Anaemia:} A reduction in red blood cells or haemoglobin concentration limits the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood, leading to cerebral hypoxia, especially during physical exertion.
    \item \textbf{Anxiety and hyperventilation:} Panic attacks and generalised anxiety disorder can induce hyperventilation, which lowers blood carbon dioxide levels (hypocapnia), causing cerebral vasoconstriction and lightheadedness.
    \item \textbf{Vasovagal syncope:} An exaggerated autonomic response to triggers such as pain, fear, prolonged standing, or sight of blood, leading to sudden bradycardia and vasodilation (neurocardiogenic reaction).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The description of lightheadedness is highly subjective, and patients may use a variety of terms to describe their symptoms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Presyncope:} A feeling of impending blackout or fainting, often accompanied by tunnel vision, dimmed vision, or a graying out of the visual field.
    \item \textbf{Wooziness and unsteadiness:} A feeling of instability or being "spaced out," without a subjective or objective spinning sensation.
    \item \textbf{Autonomic signs:} Diaphoresis (cold sweating), pallor, nausea, clamminess, and a cold feeling in the extremities during acute episodes.
    \item \textbf{Sensory disturbances:} Blurred or double vision, muffled hearing, or a ringing in the ears (tinnitus).
    \item \textbf{Positional exacerbation:} Symptoms that worsen immediately upon standing or sitting up, and improve upon lying flat.
    \item \textbf{Cognitive slowing:} Temporary confusion, difficulty concentrating, or a feeling of detachment from the environment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
It is essential to differentiate lightheadedness from other forms of dizziness and systemic conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vertigo:} Distinguished by a clear sensation of motion or spinning, typically indicating vestibular pathology such as BPPV, vestibular neuritis, or M\textsuperscript{e}ni\textsuperscript{e}re's disease.
    \item \textbf{Disequilibrium:} A feeling of imbalance or instability when walking, typically caused by sensory deficits, peripheral neuropathy, cerebellar disease, or musculoskeletal weakness, rather than cerebral hypoperfusion.
    \item \textbf{Syncope:} Represents a complete, transient loss of consciousness due to global cerebral hypoperfusion, characterised by a rapid onset, short duration, and spontaneous complete recovery.
    \item \textbf{Hypoglycaemic episode:} Presents with trembling, sweating, intense hunger, and confusion, which is rapidly reversed by carbohydrate ingestion.
    \item \textbf{Transient ischaemic attack (TIA):} Characterised by focal neurological deficits (such as unilateral weakness, numbness, or dysphasia) that resolve within 24 hours, rather than generalised lightheadedness alone.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients who experience frequent, unexplained, or worsening episodes of lightheadedness should consult a doctor, particularly if they have a history of heart disease, stroke, or are taking multiple medications.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Lightheadedness accompanied by chest pain, pressure, or a rapid, irregular heartbeat (palpitations)
    \item Sudden shortness of breath, difficulty breathing, or wheezing
    \item Sudden numbness, weakness, or paralysis of the face, arm, or leg, especially on one side of the body
    \item Difficulty speaking, slurred speech, confusion, or difficulty understanding others
    \item A fainting episode (syncope) or loss of consciousness, even if brief
    \item Lightheadedness following a head injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A thorough evaluation is necessary to identify the specific physiological or psychological driver of the symptoms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical history and physical examination:} Detailed assessment of the onset, triggers, medication history, and associated symptoms, alongside a comprehensive cardiovascular and neurological examination.
    \item \textbf{Orthostatic vitals:} Measuring blood pressure and heart rate while the patient is supine, and repeating the measurements after standing for one and three minutes. A drop of $\ge 20$~mmHg in systolic blood pressure or $\ge 10$~mmHg in diastolic blood pressure confirms orthostatic hypotension.
    \item \textbf{Electrocardiogram (ECG):} Performed in all patients presenting with unexplained lightheadedness to screen for cardiac conduction blocks, arrhythmias, or ischaemic changes.
    \item \textbf{Pathology tests:} Full blood count (to check for anaemia), blood glucose levels, serum electrolytes, renal function tests, and thyroid function tests.
    \item \textbf{Cardiac monitoring:} A Holter monitor or event recorder for patients suspected of having intermittent cardiac arrhythmias.
    \item \textbf{Echocardiography:} Indicated if a cardiac murmur, heart failure, or structural heart disease is suspected.
    \item \textbf{Tilt table testing:} Considered for patients with recurrent, unexplained episodes of presyncope or syncope to evaluate for vasovagal susceptibility.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is directed at correcting the underlying cause, adjusting contributing medications, and implementing lifestyle measures to maintain adequate cerebral blood flow.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fluid and salt management:} Increasing daily water intake (2 to 2.5 litres) and, if appropriate, increasing dietary sodium to expand blood volume, provided there are no contraindications such as hypertension or heart failure.
    \item \textbf{Medication review:} Reducing the dosage or discontinuing drugs that contribute to hypotension or orthostatic instability, such as diuretics, vasodilators, or certain antidepressants, under medical supervision.
    \item \textbf{Physical counterpressure manoeuvres:} Teaching patients to cross their legs, tense their thigh and abdominal muscles, or clench their fists when feeling lightheaded to raise systemic blood pressure.
    \item \textbf{Orthostatic behavior modification:} Instructing patients to sit on the edge of the bed for a few minutes before standing, and to avoid prolonged standing or sudden changes in posture.
    \item \textbf{Compression garments:} Using waist-high compression stockings or abdominal binders to reduce venous pooling in the lower extremities.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological therapy:} For severe, refractory orthostatic hypotension, medications such as midodrine (an alpha-1 agonist) or fludrocortisone (a mineralocorticoid) may be prescribed to increase vascular tone and blood volume.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Falls and skeletal trauma:} Increased risk of falls, leading to fractures (e.g., neck of femur), joint dislocations, and soft-tissue injuries, especially in the elderly.
    \item \textbf{Traumatic brain injury:} Head trauma, concussions, or subdural haematomas resulting from falls during a presyncopal or syncopal episode.
    \item \textbf{Loss of independence:} Reduced mobility and fear of falling, leading to social isolation, anxiety, and a decline in activities of daily living.
    \item \textbf{Accidents:} Enhanced risk of motor vehicle collisions or workplace injuries if lightheadedness occurs while driving or operating machinery.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for lightheadedness is generally excellent when it is related to benign causes such as dehydration, hyperventilation, or mild orthostatic hypotension, as these respond well to simple lifestyle and dietary interventions. When lightheadedness is secondary to chronic conditions like heart failure, severe cardiac arrhythmias, or progressive autonomic neuropathies (such as in advanced Parkinson's disease or diabetes), the outlook depends on the successful management of the primary disorder. In older adults, a multi-disciplinary approach addressing vision, strength, medication review, and home safety is essential to mitigate the risk of falls and maintain overall quality of life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stay adequately hydrated:} Drink plenty of fluids throughout the day, especially in hot weather or during physical exercise.
    \item \textbf{Change positions slowly:} When waking up, sit on the edge of the bed for a minute before standing, and avoid jumping up quickly from a seated position.
    \item \textbf{Limit alcohol and caffeine:} Restrict substances that promote dehydration and vasodilation, which can exacerbate lightheadedness.
    \item \textbf{Eat small, frequent meals:} Avoid large, carbohydrate-heavy meals that can lead to postprandial hypotension (blood pooling in the digestive tract).
    \item \textbf{Avoid prolonged standing:} If you must stand for long periods, shift your weight, clench your leg muscles, or sit down periodically.
    \item \textbf{Create a safe home environment:} Remove tripping hazards, ensure adequate lighting, and install grab bars in the bathroom to prevent fall-related injuries.
\end{itemize}
