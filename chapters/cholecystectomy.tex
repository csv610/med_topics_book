\section*{Definition and Overview}
A cholecystectomy is an operation to remove the gallbladder, a small pear-shaped organ tucked beneath the liver that stores bile. It is one of the most commonly performed abdominal operations worldwide and is usually carried out to treat gallstones and their complications, such as attacks of biliary pain, inflammation of the gallbladder, or blockage of the bile duct. Today most cholecystectomies are done with keyhole (laparoscopic) surgery, which means small incisions, less pain, and a quicker recovery. The gallbladder is not essential for life, and most people notice no long-term problems after it is removed.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Gallbladder disease is common, and cholecystectomy is one of the most frequently performed elective operations in many countries, with large numbers performed each year. Gallstones are found in around 1 in 10 adults and become more common with age, especially after 40. They affect women roughly twice as often as men, particularly during the reproductive years. Most operations are performed as planned (elective) procedures for symptomatic gallstones, while a smaller proportion are done as emergencies for acute cholecystitis or blocked bile ducts.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The operation is performed because the gallbladder has become diseased or is causing problems.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gallstones:} hard deposits that form in bile; they are the reason for most cholecystectomies
    \item \textbf{Biliary colic:} intermittent pain when a stone temporarily blocks the gallbladder outlet
    \item \textbf{Acute cholecystitis:} inflammation of the gallbladder, often when a stone blocks the cystic duct
    \item \textbf{Blocked bile duct:} a stone passing into the common bile duct can cause jaundice or pancreatitis
    \item \textbf{Gallbladder polyps} or, rarely, gallbladder cancer
    \item \textbf{Diseased, non-functioning gallbladder:} sometimes called "porcelain" or sludge-filled gallbladder
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms that lead to surgery relate to the underlying gallbladder disease, and recovery after the operation follows a typical pattern.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Before surgery:} attacks of pain in the upper right or centre of the abdomen, often after fatty meals, sometimes radiating to the back or right shoulder
    \item \textbf{Nausea, vomiting, and bloating} during pain attacks
    \item \textbf{In acute cholecystitis:} constant pain, fever, and tenderness over the liver area
    \item \textbf{After surgery:} soreness around the keyhole incisions or the wound
    \item \textbf{Shoulder-tip pain:} common for a few days after keyhole surgery, caused by gas used during the operation
    \item \textbf{Reduced appetite} and some tiredness in the first week or two
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The pain of gallbladder disease can be mistaken for several other conditions, which the surgeon will consider.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Peptic ulcer disease or gastro-oesophageal reflux
    \item Pancreatitis
    \item Irritable bowel syndrome and other functional gut disorders
    \item Appendicitis (when pain is lower or atypical)
    \item Kidney stones or urinary tract infection
    \item Liver disease or hepatitis
    \item Heart conditions (angina), pneumonia, or referred pain from the chest
    \item Hiatus hernia
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if you have recurring upper abdominal pain, especially after meals, or pain that worries you. Sudden severe pain with fever needs urgent assessment.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services or attend an emergency department for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain that is not settling
    \item Fever, chills, or rigors with abdominal pain
    \item Yellowing of the skin or eyes (jaundice)
    \item Vomiting that prevents keeping fluids down
    \item Signs of peritonitis: a rigid, tender abdomen with severe pain
    \item After surgery: heavy bleeding, a wound that is red, hot, or leaking pus, or a temperature
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Before surgery, the diagnosis and the plan are confirmed with tests.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound of the abdomen:} the main test; it reliably shows gallstones and signs of inflammation
    \item \textbf{Liver function tests:} blood tests that can signal a stone blocking the bile duct
    \item \textbf{Full blood count and inflammatory markers:} to look for infection or inflammation
    \item \textbf{MRCP or CT scan:} used to examine the bile ducts in more detail if a duct stone is suspected
    \item \textbf{ERCP:} a camera-and-X-ray test that can both diagnose and treat a bile duct stone before or after surgery
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative assessment:} including fitness for anaesthesia and review of medications (such as blood thinners)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Cholecystectomy is the definitive treatment for symptomatic gallstones and is usually performed as keyhole surgery.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Laparoscopic (keyhole) cholecystectomy:} 3--4 small incisions, most people go home the same day or next day
    \item \textbf{Open cholecystectomy:} a single larger incision, needed in some cases where keyhole surgery is difficult or unsafe
    \item \textbf{Urgent surgery:} performed during the same admission for acute cholecystitis where feasible
    \item \textbf{Bile duct stones:} may be cleared with ERCP before or after the gallbladder is removed
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple analgesics after surgery, with stronger options if needed
    \item \textbf{Return to activity:} gradual return to normal eating within a day or two and to full activity over a few weeks
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review of the wound and any pathology (a gallbladder removed is routinely examined under the microscope)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Cholecystectomy is generally very safe, but as with any operation there are risks.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bleeding} or infection at the incision sites
    \item \textbf{Bile leak:} bile escaping from the divided bile ducts, sometimes requiring a drainage procedure
    \item \textbf{Injury to the bile duct:} a rare but serious complication that may need further surgery
    \item \textbf{Damage to nearby organs:} the bowel, liver, or blood vessels
    \item \textbf{Blood clots} in the legs or lungs (deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism)
    \item \textbf{Anaesthetic complications}
    \item \textbf{Post-cholecystectomy syndrome:} ongoing or new abdominal symptoms after removal of the gallbladder
    \item \textbf{Loose stools:} diarrhoea in the first weeks or months after surgery, which usually settles
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Outcomes after cholecystectomy are excellent in the vast majority of cases. Most people are pain-free from their biliary colic and return to normal activities within 1--3 weeks after keyhole surgery, or a little longer after an open operation. Bile duct injury, though rare, is the most serious complication and is associated with a worse outcome if it occurs. Some people continue to have mild digestive symptoms such as loose stools, but these usually improve. In most cases the operation resolves the problem permanently and no special diet is required long term.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Eat a balanced diet with plenty of fibre and a moderate fat intake
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and avoid rapid weight-loss diets, which can promote stone formation
    \item Stay physically active
    \item Stop smoking, as smoking increases gallstone risk
    \item After surgery, eat small regular meals and gradually reintroduce fatty foods
    \item Follow the surgeon's advice about lifting, driving, and returning to work
    \item Attend the follow-up appointment and report any wound or abdominal concerns promptly
\end{itemize}
