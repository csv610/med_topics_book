\chapter{Epigastric Hernia Repair (Child)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An epigastric hernia in a child is a small lump that appears in the upper middle of the abdomen, between the breastbone and the belly button, caused by a weakness in the abdominal wall. It is usually present from birth and most often contains a small pad of fat. Epigastric hernias in children are common, are usually harmless, and often need no treatment, but surgery is offered if the hernia causes pain, enlarges, or does not close on its own. The repair is a short, simple operation with an excellent outcome.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Epigastric hernias are among the common hernias of childhood and can be present at birth or appear in early childhood. They occur in boys and girls, and many are found incidentally during routine examinations or by parents noticing a lump. Unlike umbilical hernias, which often close on their own, epigastric hernias are more likely to persist and may eventually require repair.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Epigastric hernias develop through natural weak points in the abdominal wall.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Development:} the midline of the abdominal wall has gaps where blood vessels pass, and a hernia can push through these
    \item \textbf{Contents:} usually a small pad of fat; bowel is rare in children
    \item \textbf{Pressure:} crying, coughing, or straining can make the lump more visible
    \item \textbf{Multiple hernias:} some children have more than one
    \item \textbf{Closure:} small hernias may close as the abdominal wall develops, but many persist
    \item \textbf{Complications:} trapping of the fat (incarceration) is uncommon but requires urgent care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Epigastric hernias in children have a characteristic appearance.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A small lump in the upper midline of the abdomen
    \item The lump may appear when the child cries, strains, or stands
    \item It may be tender to the touch
    \item Most cause no symptoms and are noticed by parents or doctors
    \item Pain at the site is uncommon but can occur
    \item Signs of a complication: a firm, tender lump that does not flatten, with vomiting or severe pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other lumps in a child's abdomen should be considered.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Umbilical hernia, a similar lump at the belly button
    \item Diastasis recti, separation of the abdominal muscles
    \item Lipoma and other benign lumps
    \item Enlarged lymph nodes
    \item Other rare abdominal wall masses
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents who notice a lump in their child's upper abdomen should have it assessed by a GP or paediatric surgeon. Most hernias need no urgent action, but a painful, firm lump that cannot be pushed back requires urgent review.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A hard, tender lump that does not flatten, with the child in pain
    \item Vomiting, especially bile-stained vomiting
    \item A very unsettled, crying child with a firm lump
    \item Fever with a painful lump
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is usually made by examination.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Examination:} the lump is felt, and the child is examined lying and standing
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} used if the diagnosis is unclear
    \item \textbf{Surgical review:} referral to a paediatric surgeon to decide on treatment
    \item \textbf{No routine tests:} healthy children need no blood tests before this small operation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Repair is offered when needed.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observation:} small hernias that cause no symptoms may simply be watched, as some close with growth
    \item \textbf{Repair:} a small incision over the hernia, with the gap closed with stitches
    \item \textbf{Multiple hernias:} the area is checked for others at the same time
    \item \textbf{General anaesthetic:} the child is asleep for the short procedure
    \item \textbf{Day surgery:} most children go home the same day
    \item \textbf{Recovery:} quick, with simple pain relief and a return to normal activity within days
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Recurrence, which is uncommon
    \item Bruising and swelling at the site
    \item Wound infection, which is rare
    \item Numbness around the scar
    \item The rare risk of incarceration before repair
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for children with epigastric hernia repair is excellent. The operation is short and safe, complications are uncommon, and recurrence is rare. Children recover quickly and are soon back to normal activities, with only a small scar.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Have the lump assessed by a doctor
    \item Follow the post-operative advice on bathing, wound care, and activity
    \item Keep the wound clean and dry
    \item Give simple pain relief as advised
    \item Seek care if the wound becomes red or the child develops a fever
    \item Attend follow-up appointments
\end{itemize}
