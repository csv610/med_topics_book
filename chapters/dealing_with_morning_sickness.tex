\chapter{Dealing with Morning Sickness}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Morning sickness is nausea and vomiting experienced during pregnancy, typically in the first trimester. Despite its name, it can occur at any time of day. Most pregnant women experience some nausea, which is generally unpleasant but harmless and settles by around 12--16 weeks. In a smaller proportion of women, vomiting is severe and persistent, a condition called hyperemesis gravidarum that requires medical treatment. This chapter explains the causes, symptoms, and safe management options for morning sickness.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Nausea and vomiting of pregnancy is extremely common, affecting around 70--80\% of pregnant women, with about half experiencing vomiting. Symptoms typically begin around weeks 4--6, peak at weeks 8--10, and resolve by weeks 12--16, although some women have symptoms throughout pregnancy. Hyperemesis gravidarum, the severe form, affects 0.5--2\% of pregnancies and is the most common reason for hospital admission in early pregnancy. Risk factors include a history of morning sickness in previous pregnancies, multiple pregnancy, a family history, and a personal history of migraine or motion sickness.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hormonal changes:} rising levels of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) and oestrogen are strongly associated with nausea; symptoms peak when hCG levels are highest
    \item \textbf{Thyroid interactions:} hCG can stimulate the thyroid gland, and transient biochemical changes may contribute
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal changes:} pregnancy slows stomach emptying, which can worsen nausea and reflux
    \item \textbf{Sensory sensitivity:} heightened sensitivity to smells and tastes triggers nausea
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} susceptibility to severe morning sickness runs in families
    \item \textbf{Evolutionary theories:} nausea may protect the developing embryo from potentially harmful foods and toxins
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Nausea that may be present constantly or triggered by smells, foods, or times of day
    \item Vomiting of variable frequency
    \item Food aversions, particularly to strong-smelling or fatty foods
    \item Excessive salivation in some women
    \item Fatigue and reduced appetite
    \item In hyperemesis gravidarum: persistent vomiting preventing food and fluid intake, with weight loss, dehydration, and weakness
    \item Symptoms resolve for most women by the second trimester
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Gastroenteritis or food poisoning
    \item Urinary tract infection or kidney infection
    \item Thyroid disorders, including hyperthyroidism
    \item Gallbladder disease, pancreatitis, or peptic ulcer disease
    \item Migraine with nausea
    \item Molar pregnancy or other pregnancy complications
    \item Hyperemesis gravidarum, when vomiting is severe, persistent, and causes dehydration or weight loss
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if vomiting is severe or persistent, if fluids cannot be kept down, if there is weight loss, or if symptoms continue beyond the first trimester. Women who cannot keep down fluids or are passing only small amounts of dark urine need assessment, as dehydration can require hospital treatment. Any abdominal pain, fever, or unusual symptoms should also be reviewed.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Inability to keep down any fluids with signs of severe dehydration, faintness, or confusion
    \item Severe abdominal pain, especially with vomiting
    \item Vomiting blood
    \item Signs of a seizure or loss of consciousness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} morning sickness is diagnosed from the typical pattern of symptoms in early pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} to confirm the pregnancy, gestation, and that it is a single healthy pregnancy where relevant
    \item \textbf{Urine tests:} to check for ketones and exclude urinary infection
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} electrolytes, kidney function, and liver function in severe cases; thyroid function where indicated
    \item \textbf{Weight monitoring:} serial weights to identify significant loss
    \item \textbf{Assessment of severity:} tools such as the PUQE score help grade nausea and vomiting severity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary measures:} eat small, frequent meals; choose bland, carbohydrate-rich foods such as dry biscuits and toast; avoid greasy, spicy, or strong-smelling foods
    \item \textbf{Hydration:} sip fluids frequently, including cold drinks, ice blocks, or electrolyte solutions
    \item \textbf{Ginger:} ginger products, such as ginger tea or crystallised ginger, are safe and may reduce nausea
    \item \textbf{Acupressure:} wristbands at the P6 point help some women
    \item \textbf{Pyridoxine (vitamin B6):} safe and effective for mild to moderate nausea, alone or with an antihistamine such as doxylamine
    \item \textbf{Prescription antiemetics:} medicines such as ondansetron, metoclopramide, and prochlorperazine are used when symptoms are severe, after discussion of risks and benefits
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} intravenous fluids, electrolyte correction, and antiemetics for hyperemesis gravidarum
    \item \textbf{Nutritional support:} dietitian input and, rarely, feeding tubes in the most severe cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dehydration, electrolyte disturbance, and kidney injury in severe cases
    \item Weight loss and nutritional deficiency
    \item Reduced quality of life, work absence, and emotional distress
    \item Psychological effects, including anxiety and low mood, and an increased risk of pregnancy-related depression
    \item Rare complications such as Wernicke's encephalopathy from severe prolonged vomiting with vitamin B1 deficiency
    \item Impact on the ability to take antenatal vitamins and eat well
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Morning sickness is self-limiting in the vast majority of cases, settling by the second trimester, and is not harmful to the baby when mild to moderate. Even moderate symptoms can be greatly improved with lifestyle measures and safe treatments. Hyperemesis gravidarum, while very distressing, responds to medical treatment, and with appropriate management most women recover and have healthy pregnancies and babies. Symptoms usually recur in subsequent pregnancies, so planning early treatment is helpful.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Eat something bland before getting out of bed in the morning, such as a dry biscuit
    \item Eat small, frequent meals throughout the day and avoid an empty stomach
    \item Avoid triggers such as strong smells, cooking odours, and stuffy rooms
    \item Drink fluids between meals rather than with them
    \item Rest when tired, as fatigue worsens nausea
    \item Take antenatal vitamins with food or at a time of day when nausea is least
    \item Seek advice early if symptoms are severe or not settling
\end{itemize}
