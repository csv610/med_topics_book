\chapter{Iron-Rich Foods}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Iron-rich foods are foods that provide meaningful amounts of the mineral iron, which the body needs to carry oxygen in the blood. Iron in food comes in two forms: haem iron, found in meat, poultry, and fish, which is absorbed efficiently, and non-haem iron, found in plants, eggs, and fortified foods, which is absorbed less well. Eating a variety of iron-rich foods, and combining plant iron with vitamin C, keeps iron stores healthy and prevents deficiency. This chapter describes the best sources of iron, how to improve absorption, and how to meet your needs through food, whatever your diet.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Iron deficiency affects around one in eight Australian women of reproductive age and is the most common nutritional deficiency in the world. Meat-eaters generally meet their iron needs more easily than vegetarians and vegans, because haem iron is absorbed two to three times more efficiently than non-haem iron. In Australia, breakfast cereals and bread are fortified with iron, which contributes significantly to population intake. Women of reproductive age, teenagers, pregnant women, and people on plant-based diets are the groups most likely to fall short of their needs and benefit most from understanding iron-rich foods.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The body absorbs two types of dietary iron differently. Haem iron, from animal foods, is taken up intact through a dedicated pathway and is largely unaffected by other foods. Non-haem iron, from plants, must be reduced and released from food compounds before absorption, a process that is enhanced by vitamin C and some organic acids and inhibited by phytates in grains and legumes, polyphenols in tea and coffee, calcium in dairy, and tannins. Because of these interactions, a meal of lentils with tomato or capsicum provides more usable iron than the same lentils with tea. Understanding these principles lets people maximise the iron available from their food, which matters most for those at risk of deficiency.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Best haem sources: lean red meat, liver (in moderation), lamb, beef, pork, chicken, and fish
    \item Good non-haem sources: legumes, lentils, chickpeas, tofu, eggs, nuts, seeds, and dried fruit
    \item Fortified foods: breakfast cereals, bread, and infant formulas and cereals
    \item Green leafy vegetables such as spinach and silverbeet contain iron, though absorption is limited
    \item Vitamin C from fruit and vegetables increases iron absorption from the same meal
    \item Tea, coffee, and dairy at the same time as meals reduce absorption
    \item Cooking in an iron pan can add small amounts of iron to food
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Even with an iron-rich diet, deficiency can occur, and other factors should be considered.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood loss:} heavy periods, gut bleeding, and donation outstrip dietary intake
    \item \textbf{Malabsorption:} coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and gastric surgery
    \item \textbf{Increased needs:} pregnancy, growth, and endurance training
    \item \textbf{Poor absorption:} regular tea, coffee, or calcium with iron-rich meals
    \item \textbf{Plant-based diets:} adequate with planning, but non-haem iron needs boosting
    \item \textbf{Other causes of anaemia:} B12 or folate deficiency, and thalassaemia
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with persistent fatigue, paleness, or other symptoms of anaemia should have their iron checked even if their diet seems good. Pregnant women, vegetarians and vegans, and people with heavy periods should discuss their iron intake with a doctor or dietitian. A diet that is rich in iron should be part of any plan to prevent or correct deficiency.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe breathlessness, chest pain, or collapse
    \item Black, tarry, or bloody stools, or vomiting blood
    \item Heavy bleeding with faintness or dizziness
    \item Sudden severe weakness or confusion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary review:} assessment of iron intake against needs
    \item \textbf{Full blood count:} checking for anaemia
    \item \textbf{Ferritin:} assessment of iron stores
    \item \textbf{Review of inhibitors and enhancers:} tea, coffee, dairy, and vitamin C around meals
    \item \textbf{Assessment of losses:} menstrual history and gut symptoms
    \item \textbf{Screening in at-risk groups:} pregnancy and infancy
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} confirming that dietary change restores iron status
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Including iron foods at most meals:} a mix of haem and non-haem sources
    \item \textbf{Combining plant iron with vitamin C:} tomatoes, citrus, berries, or capsicum
    \item \textbf{Separating inhibitors:} tea and coffee between meals rather than with them
    \item \textbf{Choosing fortified foods:} cereals and bread for a reliable baseline
    \item \textbf{Planning plant-based diets:} with dietetic support where needed
    \item \textbf{Meeting special needs:} pregnancy, growth, and sport
    \item \textbf{Supplements:} when diet alone cannot meet needs
    \item \textbf{Treating underlying causes:} bleeding and malabsorption
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Iron deficiency and anaemia despite a diet that seems adequate
    \item Fatigue, impaired concentration, and reduced performance
    \item Impaired development in infants and children
    \item Adverse outcomes in pregnancy
    \item Iron overload is not caused by food alone in healthy people
    \item Missed underlying disease when deficiency is attributed only to diet
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For most people, an iron-rich diet meets their needs and prevents deficiency. Those who combine haem and non-haem sources, add vitamin C, and avoid inhibitors at meals can maximise absorption, and fortified foods provide a reliable baseline. Plant-based diets can fully meet iron needs with planning. Where deficiency is already present, dietary change combined with supplements corrects it, and good eating habits prevent recurrence.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Eat a serving of iron-rich food at most meals
    \item Include lean meat, poultry, or fish regularly if you eat them
    \item Pair plant iron with vitamin C: add tomato, capsicum, or citrus
    \item Have tea and coffee between meals rather than with them
    \item Choose iron-fortified cereals and bread
    \item Include legumes, tofu, eggs, and nuts in a varied diet
    \item Feed babies iron-rich foods from around six months
    \item Get a dietary review if you are vegetarian, vegan, or pregnant
    \item Have blood tests if you have fatigue or other symptoms
\end{itemize}
