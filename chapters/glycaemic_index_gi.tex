\chapter{Glycaemic Index (GI)}
\index{Glycaemic Index (GI)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The glycaemic index (GI) is a ranking system that describes how quickly a carbohydrate-containing food raises blood glucose levels compared with a reference food, usually pure glucose or white bread. Foods with a low GI are digested and absorbed slowly, causing a gradual rise in blood glucose, while high-GI foods cause a rapid spike. The GI is a useful tool for people with diabetes, people trying to manage their weight, and athletes, because choosing lower-GI foods can improve blood glucose control, prolong satiety, and provide steadier energy. However, GI is only part of the picture: portion size, food combinations, and overall nutrition also matter greatly.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Interest in the glycaemic index has grown steadily since the 1980s, and low-GI eating is now recommended in many national dietary guidelines, including local guidelines, for managing diabetes and promoting general health. Around 1 in 10 people have diabetes, and dietary management, including attention to carbohydrate quality, is central to diabetes care. Low-GI diets have been studied in large clinical trials and are associated with improved blood glucose control, lower insulin levels, and a reduced risk of developing type 2 diabetes in some populations.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The GI reflects the physiological effects of different carbohydrates on blood glucose.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Carbohydrate structure:} slowly digested starches, fibre, and certain sugars are absorbed gradually, giving a low GI
    \item \textbf{Food factors:} processing, cooking method, ripeness, particle size, and fat or protein content affect how quickly carbohydrate is digested
    \item \textbf{Digestion and absorption:} low-GI foods release glucose into the bloodstream slowly, requiring less insulin and avoiding sharp spikes
    \item \textbf{Insulin response:} rapid glucose spikes trigger a large insulin release, which can lead to a subsequent drop in blood glucose and hunger
    \item \textbf{GI scale:} foods are ranked low (55 or less), medium (56--69), or high (70 or more)
    \item \textbf{Glycaemic load:} combines the GI with the amount of carbohydrate in a serving, giving a more practical measure of overall glucose effect
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The GI is relevant to people with, or at risk of, conditions affected by blood glucose.

\begin{itemize}
    \item People with type 1 or type 2 diabetes managing blood glucose levels
    \item People with gestational diabetes during pregnancy
    \item People with prediabetes aiming to prevent progression to diabetes
    \item People trying to manage weight, as low-GI foods can increase fullness
    \item Athletes seeking sustained energy for endurance
    \item People with reactive hypoglycaemia, where high-GI meals cause blood glucose crashes
    \item General healthy eating, as dietary guidelines favour whole, low-GI staple foods
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The GI is one of several factors in carbohydrate quality, and it should not be used in isolation.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Glycaemic load:} a better measure than GI alone because it accounts for portion size
    \item \textbf{Total carbohydrate:} for people with diabetes, the amount of carbohydrate matters more than GI in determining insulin needs
    \item \textbf{Overall diet quality:} a low-GI food can still be processed or low in nutrients, while some high-GI foods, like watermelon, are nutritious
    \item \textbf{Individual responses:} blood glucose responses vary between people and between foods eaten together
    \item \textbf{Other nutrients:} fibre, fat, and protein content modify the glycaemic response
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with diabetes should seek advice from their doctor and an accredited practising dietitian before changing their diet or carbohydrate counting methods. The GI is a tool, not a prescription, and individualised advice is important.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item This chapter concerns nutrition rather than emergencies; however, people with diabetes should seek urgent care for severe symptoms of high or low blood glucose, including confusion, seizures, unconsciousness, or vomiting with ketones
    \item Any medical emergency related to diabetes should be treated immediately
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The GI is used as part of dietary management, informed by monitoring.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood glucose monitoring:} self-monitoring shows how individual meals affect glucose levels
    \item \textbf{HbA1c:} the long-term measure of blood glucose control used to evaluate overall dietary management
    \item \textbf{Food diaries:} tracking foods and blood glucose levels identifies patterns
    \item \textbf{Published GI tables:} reliable GI values for thousands of foods are available from accredited sources
    \item \textbf{Dietitian assessment:} individualised advice on portion sizes, food swaps, and balanced eating
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Using the GI effectively means applying it within an overall healthy eating pattern.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Choose low-GI staples:} wholegrain and sourdough breads, rolled oats, pasta, basmati rice, legumes, and most fruits
    \item \textbf{Limit high-GI foods:} white bread, processed breakfast cereals, rice crackers, and sugary drinks
    \item \textbf{Combine foods:} pairing carbohydrate with protein, healthy fat, or fibre slows digestion and lowers the glycaemic response
    \item \textbf{Watch portions:} use glycaemic load to account for how much carbohydrate is actually eaten
    \item \textbf{For diabetes:} use GI alongside carbohydrate counting and blood glucose monitoring, not instead of them
    \item \textbf{For athletes:} high-GI foods are useful during and after exercise for rapid energy replacement
    \item \textbf{Gradual change:} swap foods gradually and monitor the effects on glucose and wellbeing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Overemphasis on GI at the expense of overall nutrition, such as eating foods high in saturated fat or salt because they are low GI
    \item Confusion between GI and carbohydrate amount, leading to inappropriate portions
    \item Neglect of fibre, micronutrients, and variety in a rigid low-GI approach
    \item Poor blood glucose control if GI is used without monitoring
    \item Unnecessary dietary restrictions in people without a medical need
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For people with diabetes, consistently choosing lower-GI foods, combined with appropriate portions and medication, improves HbA1c and reduces glucose fluctuations. Studies show that low-GI diets lower blood glucose levels by a small but clinically meaningful amount, and they may improve insulin sensitivity and satiety. The glycaemic index is most effective as part of a broader healthy eating pattern rather than as a standalone rule, and its benefits are greatest when applied consistently with professional guidance.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Base meals on low-GI whole foods: vegetables, legumes, fruits, and whole grains
    \item Limit sugary drinks and highly processed foods, which are usually high GI
    \item Include protein, healthy fats, or fibre at meals to slow glucose absorption
    \item Watch portion sizes, as large servings of low-GI foods still raise glucose
    \item Monitor your own blood glucose responses if you have diabetes
    \item Use glycaemic load, which accounts for both GI and portion size
    \item Seek advice from a dietitian before making significant dietary changes
    \item Keep variety and enjoyment central to your eating pattern
\end{itemize}
