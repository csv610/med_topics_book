\section*{Definition and Overview}
Liver Infection is an infectious disease caused by a pathogenic microorganism -- virus, bacterium, fungus, or parasite -- that invades the host, replicates, and triggers an immune response. Infections may be localised or systemic, acute or chronic, and range from subclinical to life-threatening. Transmission occurs via person-to-person contact, respiratory droplets, faecal-oral route, vector-borne spread, or environmental exposure.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Infectious diseases remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, particularly in children, older adults, and immunocompromised individuals. Burden is highest in resource-limited settings, though globalisation, travel, and antimicrobial resistance have increased relevance in all populations. Seasonal epidemics (influenza) and periodic pandemics (COVID-19) can overwhelm health systems. Vaccination and sanitation have dramatically reduced the burden of many vaccine-preventable diseases.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Infectious diseases are caused by four main classes of pathogen:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viruses:} intracellular pathogens that hijack host cell machinery to replicate (e.g.\ influenza, HIV, SARS-CoV-2)
    \item \textbf{Bacteria:} single-celled organisms that cause disease through direct tissue damage and toxin production (e.g.\ \textit{Staphylococcus}, \textit{Streptococcus}, \textit{Mycobacterium tuberculosis})
    \item \textbf{Fungi:} eukaryotic organisms causing disease primarily in immunocompromised hosts or through superficial skin/nail infection
    \item \textbf{Parasites:} protozoa or helminths acquired through contaminated food, water, or vector bites (e.g.\ malaria, schistosomiasis)
\end{itemize}

Pathogenesis involves pathogen entry, evasion of host defences, replication, tissue damage, and host inflammatory response. The severity of disease reflects the balance between pathogen virulence and host immunity.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever, chills, or rigors
    \item Malaise, fatigue, and myalgia
    \item Localising symptoms reflecting the affected organ system (e.g.\ cough for respiratory, dysuria for urinary, diarrhoea for gastrointestinal)
    \item Lymphadenopathy
    \item Rash or skin eruption
    \item Headache, photophobia, or neck stiffness (meningeal irritation)
    \item Confusion or altered consciousness in severe systemic infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Non-infectious inflammatory conditions (e.g.\ autoimmune disorders, vasculitis)
    \item Malignancy (e.g.\ lymphoma presenting with fever and lymphadenopathy)
    \item Drug fever or hypersensitivity reactions
    \item Thromboembolic disease (e.g.\ DVT/PE presenting with fever)
    \item Other infectious agents producing the same syndrome (e.g.\ viral vs.\ bacterial pneumonia)
    \item Heat illness or endocrine causes of fever (thyrotoxic crisis, adrenal crisis)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most mild infections improve with supportive care within a few days. Seek medical assessment if symptoms are severe, progressive, or not improving.

\textbf{Call 000 or attend an emergency department for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing or chest pain
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or neck stiffness with a rash
    \item Persistent high fever not responding to treatment, especially in infants, older adults, or immunocompromised patients
    \item Signs of sepsis: rapid breathing, rapid heart rate, mottled skin, or reduced urine output
    \item Continuous vomiting or signs of dehydration
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} exposure, travel, vaccination status, immune status, and localising features
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count (leukocytosis or leukopenia), CRP, inflammatory markers
    \item \textbf{Microbiology:} blood cultures, urine culture, throat swab, stool culture, or wound swab
    \item \textbf{Molecular testing:} PCR for viral and atypical bacterial pathogens
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} chest X-ray, CT, or ultrasound to identify the focus of infection
    \item \textbf{Serology:} antibody testing for certain infections (e.g.\ hepatitis, HIV, syphilis)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antiviral therapy:} for specific viral infections (e.g.\ oseltamivir for influenza, antiretrovirals for HIV)
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} directed against the suspected or confirmed bacterial pathogen; empirical therapy may be needed before culture results
    \item \textbf{Antifungal agents:} for confirmed or suspected fungal infection (e.g.\ fluconazole, amphotericin B)
    \item \textbf{Anti-parasitic therapy:} for parasitic infections (e.g.\ antimalarials, anthelmintics)
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} rest, hydration, antipyretics (paracetamol, ibuprofen), and monitoring
    \item \textbf{Source control:} drainage of abscesses, removal of infected devices, or surgical debridement where necessary
    \item \textbf{Hospitalisation:} for severe infections requiring intravenous therapy, oxygen, or intensive care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sepsis and septic shock -- a life-threatening systemic inflammatory response
    \item Organ failure: respiratory, renal, hepatic, or multi-organ dysfunction
    \item Secondary infections (e.g.\ bacterial pneumonia following viral influenza)
    \item Chronic infection or carrier state (e.g.\ chronic hepatitis, latent tuberculosis)
    \item Post-infectious syndromes (e.g.\ Guillain-Barre syndrome after Campylobacter; rheumatic fever after streptococcal pharyngitis)
    \item Antimicrobial resistance and recurrent infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most common infections resolve with appropriate treatment within days to weeks. Uncomplicated viral upper respiratory infections typically resolve in 7--10 days. Bacterial infections usually improve within 48--72 hours of starting effective antibiotics. Prognosis is worse at extremes of age, in immunocompromised patients, and when treatment is delayed. Sepsis carries a mortality of 10--30\% depending on severity and timeliness of intervention.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain up-to-date vaccinations for age, risk group, and travel destination
    \item Practise regular hand hygiene with soap and water or alcohol-based sanitiser
    \item Cover coughs and sneezes; dispose of tissues safely
    \item Ensure safe food preparation, storage, and water supply
    \item Use insect repellent and protective clothing in vector-borne disease areas
    \item Practice safe sex to reduce sexually transmitted infections
    \item Stay home when unwell to avoid transmitting infection
    \item Complete prescribed courses of antibiotics to reduce resistance
\end{itemize}
