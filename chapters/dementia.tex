\chapter{Dementia}
\index{Dementia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dementia is not a single disease but a term for a group of progressive brain disorders that cause decline in memory, thinking, language, judgement, and the ability to perform daily activities. The most common cause is Alzheimer's disease, followed by vascular dementia and Lewy body dementia. Dementia is a major cause of disability and dependency in older people and has profound effects on families and carers. While there is currently no cure for most dementias, much can be done to support people and their families, and some treatments slow progression or improve symptoms. This chapter covers the causes, features, diagnosis, and care of dementia.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Dementia is one of the fastest-growing health challenges of ageing populations. Around 400,000--500,000 people currently live with dementia, and this number is expected to more than double by 2050 as the population ages. It affects about 1 in 10 people over 65 and 3 in 10 over 85. Alzheimer's disease accounts for roughly two-thirds of cases. Women are affected more often than men, partly because they live longer. Dementia is the second leading cause of death in many countries and the leading cause of disability in people over 75.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Alzheimer's disease:} abnormal clumps of beta-amyloid protein and tangled tau protein damage and destroy nerve cells, beginning in areas controlling memory
    \item \textbf{Vascular dementia:} reduced blood flow to the brain from stroke or small vessel disease causes areas of brain damage
    \item \textbf{Lewy body dementia:} abnormal protein deposits (Lewy bodies) cause fluctuating cognition, visual hallucinations, and Parkinson-like movement problems
    \item \textbf{Frontotemporal dementia:} degeneration of the frontal and temporal lobes causes personality, behaviour, and language changes, often at younger ages
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} age, genetics, and modifiable factors including hypertension, diabetes, smoking, physical inactivity, hearing loss, and social isolation
    \item \textbf{Other causes:} Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, and rarely reversible causes such as thyroid disease or vitamin deficiency
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive memory loss, especially for recent events and new information
    \item Difficulty with language, including finding words and following conversations
    \item Impaired judgement, planning, and problem-solving
    \item Disorientation to time and place, and getting lost in familiar settings
    \item Difficulty with everyday tasks such as cooking, finances, and medication
    \item Personality and mood changes, irritability, apathy, or withdrawal
    \item Behavioural changes, including agitation, wandering, and sleep disturbance
    \item In later stages: difficulty walking, swallowing, and communicating, and full dependence on others
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Mild cognitive impairment, in which changes are noticeable but daily function is preserved
    \item Depression, which can mimic dementia with poor concentration and memory
    \item Delirium, an acute reversible confusion that can coexist with dementia
    \item Medication side effects and alcohol-related brain damage
    \item Thyroid disease, vitamin B12 deficiency, and other reversible causes
    \item Normal ageing changes, which are milder and do not affect daily function
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with concerns about their own or a relative's memory and thinking should see a GP for assessment. Early diagnosis allows access to treatment, planning, and support services, and excludes reversible causes. Seek urgent care for sudden confusion, neurological symptoms, or rapid decline.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden confusion, weakness, facial droop, or difficulty speaking (possible stroke)
    \item A sudden, marked change in behaviour or consciousness, which may indicate delirium from infection or other illness
    \item Seizures, falls with head injury, or inability to wake the person
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cognitive assessment:} standardised tests such as the Mini-Mental State Examination, Montreal Cognitive Assessment, or GPCOG
    \item \textbf{History:} information from the person and a close family member about onset, progression, and impact on daily life
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} thyroid function, vitamin B12, full blood count, glucose, and kidney and liver function to exclude reversible causes
    \item \textbf{Brain imaging:} CT or MRI to detect stroke, tumour, or atrophy patterns
    \item \textbf{Specialist assessment:} memory clinic or geriatrician review for complex cases
    \item \textbf{PET scans and cerebrospinal fluid analysis:} used in research settings or complex diagnostic questions for Alzheimer's disease
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} rarely indicated, mainly in early-onset familial dementia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Medication:} acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) and memantine can improve symptoms or slow decline in Alzheimer's disease
    \item \textbf{Non-drug strategies:} structured routines, meaningful activities, cognitive stimulation, and environmental adaptations
    \item \textbf{Behavioural management:} person-centred approaches to agitation, with medication used sparingly for severe symptoms
    \item \textbf{Carer support:} education, support groups, respite care, and counselling for family carers
    \item \textbf{Community services:} home care packages, day centres, and residential aged care as needs progress
    \item \textbf{Planning:} advance care directives, legal and financial planning, and discussion of future preferences
    \item \textbf{Managing other health:} treatment of cardiovascular risk factors, hearing and vision correction, and good nutrition and exercise
    \item \textbf{Safety:} falls prevention, home modifications, and review of driving
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive dependence and need for residential care
    \item Falls, fractures, and injuries
    \item Malnutrition, dehydration, and pressure injuries in advanced disease
    \item Swallowing problems leading to aspiration pneumonia
    \item Behavioural and psychological symptoms that distress the person and carers
    \item Carer stress, burnout, and poor physical and mental health in family members
    \item Complications of medication and hospitalisation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Dementia is progressive, but the rate of decline varies greatly between individuals and types. Some people live with mild symptoms for many years, while others decline more rapidly. The average survival after diagnosis is around 4--8 years, but ranges from 2 to 20 years depending on age, health, and dementia type. While there is no cure for most dementias, good care dramatically improves quality of life, and some treatments slow progression. Early diagnosis, management of other health conditions, strong carer support, and advance planning improve outcomes for people with dementia and their families.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Protect brain health: control blood pressure, cholesterol, and diabetes; do not smoke; limit alcohol
    \item Stay physically, mentally, and socially active throughout life
    \item Protect hearing and treat hearing loss, which is a modifiable risk factor
    \item Eat a balanced diet, including fruits, vegetables, fish, and healthy fats
    \item Seek early assessment for memory concerns
    \item Carers: use support services, arrange respite, and look after your own health
    \item Plan ahead: make legal and care arrangements while the person can participate
\end{itemize}
