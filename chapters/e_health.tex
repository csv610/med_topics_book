\chapter{E-health}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
E-health refers to the use of digital technologies to deliver, support, and manage health care. It includes electronic health records, telehealth consultations, health apps, wearable devices, online appointment booking, and the secure sharing of health information between patients and providers. In Australia, the national My Health Record system and Medicare telehealth services are central to e-health, and the field is expanding rapidly. E-health has the potential to make care more convenient, accessible, and efficient, while also raising important questions about privacy, equity, and the quality of online health information.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The use of digital health technology has grown rapidly in Australia. Telehealth consultations expanded dramatically during the COVID-19 pandemic and remain widely used, and a large proportion of Australians have a My Health Record. Most adults use the internet to look up health information, and health and fitness apps are among the most downloaded app categories. However, access to digital health varies, with older people, people in rural areas, and those with lower incomes or digital skills using these services less.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
E-health is not a disease but a set of tools, and its "mechanisms" are the digital systems that support care.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Electronic health records:} secure digital records that compile health information, including My Health Record, which is available to clinicians across Australia
    \item \textbf{Telehealth:} video and phone consultations that allow people to see clinicians without travelling
    \item \textbf{Health apps and wearables:} smartphone applications and devices that track activity, sleep, heart rate, and symptoms
    \item \textbf{Online services:} appointment booking, prescription requests, test result viewing, and online pharmacies
    \item \textbf{Clinical decision support:} digital tools that help clinicians make safer, evidence-based decisions
    \item \textbf{Artificial intelligence:} emerging uses in diagnosis, imaging, and personal health advice
    \item \textbf{Data sharing and interoperability:} the secure movement of health information between systems, which makes coordinated care possible
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The benefits and potential problems of e-health can be thought of as its "symptoms."
\begin{itemize}
    \item Convenience: consultations and services available from home, saving travel and waiting time
    \item Accessibility: easier access to care for people in rural areas, with mobility problems, or with busy schedules
    \item Better coordination: shared records allow different providers to see the same information
    \item Self-management: apps and devices support people to track and manage chronic conditions
    \item Privacy concerns: worries about who can see health information and how it is protected
    \item Information quality: online health advice ranges from excellent to misleading
    \item Digital divide: unequal access for people without digital skills, devices, or reliable internet
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Not all digital health tools are appropriate for all situations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item In-person care is still necessary for physical examination, procedures, and emergencies
    \item The quality of health apps varies widely, and many are not evidence-based or regulated
    \item Online diagnosis tools cannot replace clinical assessment
    \item Wearable data may be inaccurate or misinterpreted
    \item Privacy and security risks differ between platforms
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People should see a doctor in person for new or serious symptoms, emergencies, physical examinations, and tests that require equipment. Telehealth is best suited to follow-up appointments, medication reviews, results discussions, and advice for minor concerns. Anyone with worrying symptoms should not rely on online information instead of seeking care.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, severe breathlessness, or stroke symptoms
    \item Severe bleeding or major injuries
    \item Sudden collapse or loss of consciousness
    \item Any life-threatening emergency, where an ambulance is required
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
For people using e-health services, understanding the tools is part of good care.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{My Health Record:} Australians can view, correct, and control their digital health record
    \item \textbf{Telehealth suitability:} clinicians assess whether a consultation is suitable for telehealth or needs to be in person
    \item \textbf{App evaluation:} checking the source, regulation, privacy policy, and evidence base of health apps
    \item \textbf{Data accuracy:} understanding the limitations of wearable measurements
    \item \textbf{Privacy settings:} reviewing who has access to personal health information
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
E-health is best used as part of a comprehensive approach to health.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Using telehealth wisely:} prepare for telehealth appointments with a list of questions and any relevant data
    \item \textbf{Managing records:} review My Health Record regularly and flag any errors
    \item \textbf{Choosing apps:} prefer apps from reputable organisations, with clear privacy policies and clinical oversight
    \item \textbf{Combining with in-person care:} use digital tools to support, not replace, regular medical care
    \item \textbf{Building digital skills:} seek help from family, libraries, or community programs
    \item \textbf{Protecting privacy:} use strong passwords, update software, and be cautious about sharing health data
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis if online tools replace appropriate assessment
    \item Harmful effects of poor-quality or unsafe health information
    \item Privacy breaches and misuse of health data
    \item The digital divide, worsening inequities in access to care
    \item Anxiety caused by misinterpreted wearable data
    \item Overload of health information from multiple uncoordinated sources
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
E-health is a rapidly evolving field with the potential to improve access, quality, and efficiency of care. Used well, it supports people to manage their health and stay connected to providers. The outcomes depend on how the tools are designed, regulated, and used, and the greatest benefits come when digital health is integrated thoughtfully with face-to-face care and is accessible to everyone.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Learn to use the digital health services available in your area, including telehealth and My Health Record
    \item Keep a list of your medicines and conditions in a format you can share with providers
    \item Check the source and evidence base of any health information or app
    \item Protect your health information with strong passwords and privacy awareness
    \item Attend regular in-person reviews for chronic conditions
    \item Ask your GP how to make the best use of digital tools for your health
\end{itemize}
