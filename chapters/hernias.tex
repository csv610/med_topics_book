\chapter{Hernias}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A hernia occurs when an organ or tissue protrudes through a weak spot in the muscle or connective tissue that normally contains it. The most common types are inguinal hernias in the groin, umbilical hernias at the belly button, incisional hernias through surgical scars, and hiatal hernias of the stomach through the diaphragm. Hernias develop when abdominal pressure pushes tissue through a weakness in the abdominal wall. Most cause a visible bulge and discomfort, and many eventually require surgical repair, particularly if they become painful or difficult to reduce.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hernias are among the most common surgical conditions. Inguinal hernias affect approximately 25--40\% of men at some point and around 3--4\% of women, making them the most common type. Umbilical hernias are present in up to 20\% of newborns and close spontaneously in most. Incisional hernias complicate about 10--20\% of abdominal operations. Around 80,000 hernia repairs are performed in Australia each year, and the lifetime risk of needing surgery for an inguinal hernia is about 27\% for men.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Congenital weakness:} the inguinal canal and umbilical ring are natural weak points
    \item \textbf{Increased abdominal pressure:} lifting, coughing, straining with constipation, obesity, and pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Previous surgery:} wounds weaken the abdominal wall, leading to incisional hernias
    \item \textbf{Ageing and connective tissue:} tissues weaken with age
    \item \textbf{Inguinal hernia:} bowel or fat protrudes through the inguinal canal into the groin
    \item \textbf{Femoral hernia:} protrusion through the femoral canal, more common in women, higher strangulation risk
    \item \textbf{Umbilical hernia:} protrusion at the navel
    \item \textbf{Hiatal hernia:} the stomach slides into the chest through the diaphragm
    \item \textbf{Complications:} incarceration (stuck) and strangulation (blood supply cut off) are emergencies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A soft bulge that appears with straining, coughing, or standing
    \item Aching or dragging discomfort, worse with lifting or at the end of the day
    \item The bulge usually reduces when lying down or with gentle pressure
    \item Groin hernias may extend into the scrotum in men
    \item Umbilical hernias are most visible in babies when they cry
    \item Hiatal hernias cause heartburn, reflux, and chest discomfort
    \item A painful, tender, irreducible lump signals incarceration or strangulation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Groin lumps: hydrocoele, lymph nodes, cysts, and testicular conditions
    \item Muscle strain and "sports hernia" (athletic pubalgia)
    \item Femoral artery aneurysm and varicose veins in the groin
    \item Abdominal wall tumours and haematomas
    \item Hiatal hernia symptoms mimic GORD, angina, and gallbladder disease
    \item Ultrasound and examination usually distinguish hernias from these conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for any bulge, lump, or new discomfort in the groin, navel, or an old surgical scar. Seek urgent care if a hernia becomes painful, tender, or cannot be pushed back.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A painful, tender, red, or irreducible hernia
    \item Severe abdominal pain with nausea, vomiting, or fever
    \item Vomiting with abdominal distension and inability to pass wind or stool
    \item Sudden severe chest or abdominal pain with collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} most hernias are diagnosed by examination, with a cough impulse confirming the bulge
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} confirms uncertain diagnoses and assesses contents
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} for complex, recurrent, or occult hernias
    \item \textbf{Endoscopy:} diagnoses hiatal hernia
    \item \textbf{Urgent evaluation:} for incarcerated or strangulated hernias
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observation:} small, asymptomatic hernias may be monitored safely, but most adults eventually choose repair
    \item \textbf{Trusses and binders:} occasionally used for temporary support but do not cure hernias
    \item \textbf{Surgical repair:} open or laparoscopic techniques, usually with mesh reinforcement
    \item \textbf{Hiatal hernia:} managed with reflux treatment; surgery reserved for severe cases
    \item \textbf{Emergency surgery:} for strangulation, with removal of non-viable bowel if needed
    \item \textbf{Recovery:} most people return to normal activity within a few weeks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Incarceration: the hernia becomes trapped and cannot be reduced
    \item Strangulation: blood supply is cut off, causing bowel gangrene and life-threatening sepsis
    \item Bowel obstruction from trapped bowel
    \item Recurrence after repair, less common with mesh
    \item Chronic pain and numbness after surgery
    \item Missed strangulation carries a high mortality, so timely treatment is essential
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most hernias can be repaired safely with excellent long-term results. Untreated, however, hernias do not heal on their own and carry an increasing risk of incarceration and strangulation over time, which turn an elective procedure into an emergency. With planned repair, recurrence is uncommon and most people are fully active within weeks. Babies' umbilical hernias usually close by school age, and hiatal hernias are typically managed effectively with medication.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use safe lifting techniques and avoid straining with heavy loads
    \item Prevent constipation with fibre, fluids, and regular activity
    \item Manage chronic cough and maintain a healthy weight
    \item Seek early assessment of any bulge
    \item Reduce hernias gently when they occur and rest with them reduced
    \item Seek urgent care if a hernia becomes painful or irreducible
    \item After surgery, follow graduated return-to-activity advice
\end{itemize}
