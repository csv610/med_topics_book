\chapter{Itchy Eyes}
\index{Itchy Eyes}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Itchy eyes are a very common complaint, usually caused by allergies, including hay fever and allergic conjunctivitis, but also by dry eyes, irritants, infection, and contact lens problems. The itch is driven by histamine release in the conjunctiva, the thin membrane covering the white of the eye and inner eyelids, often accompanied by redness, watering, and a gritty feeling. Most cases settle with simple measures, including avoiding triggers, cold compresses, and antihistamine or lubricating eye drops. Persistent, severe, or painful symptoms, and any change in vision, require prompt assessment, because some eye conditions can threaten sight.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Allergic eye disease affects around 15 to 20 per cent of people and is most common in spring and summer, when pollen counts are high. It affects people of all ages, with a peak in children and young adults, and is strongly linked to other allergic conditions such as hay fever, asthma, and eczema. Dry eye is more common with age, screen use, and some medicines. Infective conjunctivitis is very common in children and spreads easily in schools and childcare. Contact lens wearers are at particular risk of more serious eye infections.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Itchy eyes occur when the conjunctiva is irritated. In allergic conjunctivitis, allergens such as pollen, dust mites, or pet dander trigger mast cells in the conjunctiva to release histamine and other chemicals, causing itching, redness, watering, and swelling. In dry eye, the tear film is inadequate or unstable, leaving the eye surface irritated, which paradoxically causes reflex watering and itching. Infective conjunctivitis, from viruses or bacteria, causes redness, discharge, and often itching. Contact lenses can cause itching through irritation, dryness, or deposits, and poor lens hygiene raises the risk of infection. The nose-eye connection means hay fever often produces itchy eyes and nose together.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Itching, which may be intense, in one or both eyes
    \item Redness and watering of the eyes
    \item A gritty or burning sensation
    \item Swelling of the eyelids or the conjunctiva
    \item A clear, watery discharge in allergy, or a sticky or yellow discharge in infection
    \item Crusting of the eyelids, especially on waking, in infective conjunctivitis
    \item Symptoms that come on in certain seasons, places, or around animals
    \item Associated sneezing, a runny nose, and an itchy nose or throat
    \item Blurred vision, pain, or light sensitivity are warning features
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The cause of itchy eyes must be distinguished because treatments differ.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Allergic conjunctivitis:} seasonal or perennial, with clear discharge
    \item \textbf{Dry eye:} grittiness, burning, and fluctuating vision
    \item \textbf{Viral conjunctivitis:} watery, red, and very contagious
    \item \textbf{Bacterial conjunctivitis:} sticky discharge and crusting
    \item \textbf{Contact lens problems:} irritation, deposits, or infection
    \item \textbf{Blepharitis:} inflammation of the eyelid margins
    \item \textbf{Serious eye conditions:} keratitis, uveitis, and acute glaucoma, which cause pain and vision loss
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek assessment for itchy eyes that are persistent, painful, or associated with discharge, swelling, or vision changes. Contact lens wearers should seek urgent care for red, painful, or light-sensitive eyes, and stop wearing lenses. Eye symptoms in a baby, or following an injury or chemical exposure, require urgent attention.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden loss of vision or a change in vision
    \item Severe eye pain, especially with headache, nausea, or vomiting
    \item An eye injury or chemical splash
    \item Light sensitivity with a red eye
    \item A red, painful eye in a contact lens wearer
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} triggers, contact lens use, and associated symptoms
    \item \textbf{Examination:} inspection of the eyes, eyelids, and conjunctiva with a torch or slit lamp
    \item \textbf{Fluorescein staining:} to check the surface of the eye for damage
    \item \textbf{Allergy assessment:} identifying triggers, sometimes with allergy testing
    \item \textbf{Tear film assessment:} for dry eye
    \item \textbf{Swabs:} for infection where discharge suggests bacterial or viral causes
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to an optometrist or ophthalmologist for persistent or serious symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Avoiding triggers:} pollen, dust, pets, and irritants such as smoke
    \item \textbf{Cold compresses:} applied to closed eyes to soothe itching
    \item \textbf{Antihistamine eye drops:} for allergic conjunctivitis, some available over the counter
    \item \textbf{Lubricating eye drops:} artificial tears for dry eye and to wash allergens away
    \item \textbf{Oral antihistamines:} for hay fever affecting the eyes and nose together
    \item \textbf{Mast cell stabiliser drops:} longer-term prevention for recurrent allergy
    \item \textbf{Treatment of infection:} antibiotic drops for bacterial conjunctivitis
    \item \textbf{Eyelid hygiene:} warm compresses and lid cleaning for blepharitis
    \item \textbf{Contact lens care:} breaks from lenses, proper hygiene, and replacement
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic irritation and reduced quality of life
    \item Corneal damage from severe allergy, infection, or rubbing
    \item Spread of infective conjunctivitis to others
    \item Complications of contact lens infections, including corneal ulcers and vision loss
    \item Chronic dry eye with surface damage
    \item Missed serious eye disease when red-eye symptoms are dismissed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most itchy eyes settle quickly with trigger avoidance and simple treatment. Allergic conjunctivitis responds well to antihistamine and mast cell stabiliser drops, dry eye improves with regular lubrication, and infective conjunctivitis clears within days to two weeks. Recurrent allergy can be managed with seasonal prevention. Warning features such as pain, vision change, and light sensitivity must never be ignored, as they may indicate sight-threatening conditions that respond well to urgent treatment.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Identify and avoid your eye allergy triggers
    \item Use cold compresses for relief
    \item Apply lubricating drops if you have dry eyes
    \item Wear sunglasses outdoors to reduce pollen and light exposure
    \item Wash hands before touching the eyes
    \item Do not rub the eyes, which worsens allergy and can damage the cornea
    \item Replace eye cosmetics regularly and never share them
    \item Follow good contact lens hygiene and replace lenses as advised
    \item Take breaks from screens and blink regularly
    \item Seek urgent care for pain, vision change, or a red eye with a contact lens
\end{itemize}
