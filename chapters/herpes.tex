\chapter{Herpes}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Herpes is a common infection caused by herpes simplex viruses (HSV), of which there are two types. HSV-1 usually causes cold sores around the mouth, while HSV-2 usually causes genital herpes, although either type can infect either site through oral or genital contact. Herpes is spread by skin-to-skin contact, is very common, and, once acquired, stays in the body for life, causing recurrent outbreaks. There is no cure, but antiviral medicines shorten and suppress outbreaks, and the infection rarely causes serious health problems in otherwise healthy people. It is important, however, to discuss the risks of transmission with partners, and pregnant women need special care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Herpes is extremely common worldwide. Around two-thirds of the world's population under 50 has HSV-1, and an estimated 13\% of people aged 15--49 have HSV-2. Most people with herpes have no or unrecognised symptoms, so many more are infected than know it. Genital herpes affects around one in eight Australian adults, with rates higher in women and men who have sex with men. Many people experience recurrent outbreaks, but the frequency tends to decrease over time.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HSV-1:} traditionally causes oral herpes (cold sores); increasingly causes genital herpes through oral sex
    \item \textbf{HSV-2:} the main cause of genital herpes
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} direct skin-to-skin contact with an infected area, including during viral shedding when no symptoms are present
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} the virus enters through small breaks in the skin or mucous membranes, multiplies, and causes blisters; it then travels along nerves to the dorsal root ganglia, where it lies dormant
    \item \textbf{Reactivation:} triggers such as stress, illness, sun exposure, menstruation, and immune suppression cause the virus to travel back to the skin and cause outbreaks
    \item \textbf{Shedding:} the virus is shed from the skin even without visible symptoms, allowing transmission
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Primary infection: painful blisters that burst to form sores, with itching, burning, and sometimes fever and swollen glands
    \item Oral herpes: cold sores on the lips, mouth, and face
    \item Genital herpes: sores on the genitals, buttocks, and thighs, with painful urination
    \item Flu-like symptoms with the first episode
    \item Recurrences: smaller, milder, and shorter outbreaks at the same site
    \item Prodrome: tingling or burning before blisters appear
    \item Many infections cause no symptoms at all
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Other genital ulcers: syphilis, chancroid, and lymphogranuloma venereum
    \item Other mouth ulcers: aphthous ulcers, hand, foot and mouth disease, and herpes zoster
    \item Shingles (herpes zoster), caused by the varicella-zoster virus, has a similar appearance
    \item Cystitis and other causes of painful urination
    \item Skin conditions such as contact dermatitis and folliculitis
    \item Swabs and blood tests distinguish herpes from these conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with suspected genital herpes should be tested and treated. Pregnant women with herpes, or whose partner has herpes, need specialist advice. Seek review for severe, persistent, or frequently recurring outbreaks.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Herpes with severe headache, confusion, or seizures (possible encephalitis, rare)
    \item Eye symptoms with a herpes sore near the eye
    \item Severe infection in someone with a weakened immune system
    \item A newborn with a fever, rash, or lethargy whose mother has active herpes
    \item Difficulty urinating or severe pelvic pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} the appearance and site of sores are often diagnostic
    \item \textbf{Viral swab (PCR):} the most accurate test, taken from a blister or sore
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} antibody tests can detect past HSV-1 and HSV-2 infection
    \item \textbf{Type-specific testing:} distinguishes HSV-1 from HSV-2, which matters for prognosis and counselling
    \item \textbf{STI screening:} recommended because herpes often coexists with other sexually transmitted infections
    \item \textbf{Partner notification:} informing sexual partners allows them to be tested and advised
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antiviral medicines:} aciclovir, valaciclovir, or famciclovir speed healing of outbreaks
    \item \textbf{First episode:} a 5--10 day course of antiviral treatment
    \item \textbf{Suppressive therapy:} daily antivirals reduce outbreaks by 70--80\% and reduce transmission
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple analgesics, numbing creams, and saline baths for comfort
    \item \textbf{Self-care:} keep sores clean and dry, avoid tight clothing, and avoid sex until healed
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} specialist management to prevent transmission to the baby, including caesarean delivery in some cases
    \item \textbf{Counselling:} accurate information reduces the anxiety and stigma that accompany diagnosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Recurrent outbreaks with physical discomfort and psychological distress
    \item Neonatal herpes: transmission to a newborn during birth can cause severe, life-threatening infection, though it is rare
    \item Eye infection (herpes keratitis), which can threaten vision
    \item Encephalitis, a rare but serious brain infection
    \item Severe or chronic infection in people with weakened immune systems
    \item Increased risk of HIV acquisition because genital sores provide an entry point
    \item Stigma, anxiety, and relationship difficulties
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For otherwise healthy people, herpes is a manageable, recurrent skin condition rather than a serious illness. Outbreaks become less frequent and milder over the first few years, and suppressive therapy controls them effectively. The main challenges are emotional and relational, and these respond well to accurate information and support. Serious complications are rare, and pregnancy outcomes are excellent with appropriate specialist care. There is ongoing research into vaccines and curative treatments.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Avoid skin-to-skin contact with active sores
    \item Use condoms, which reduce but do not eliminate transmission
    \item Consider daily suppressive antiviral therapy to reduce transmission
    \item Tell partners about the infection and discuss testing
    \item Avoid oral sex when cold sores are present
    \item Identify and manage personal triggers for outbreaks
    \item Pregnant women should discuss herpes with their maternity team
    \item Practise good hygiene and avoid touching sores and then the eyes
\end{itemize}
