\chapter{Buruli Ulcer}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Buruli ulcer is a bacterial skin infection caused by \textit{Mycobacterium ulcerans}. It typically begins as a painless lump or swelling under the skin, which over weeks may break down into a large, open ulcer. Without treatment, the ulcer can grow very large and cause permanent scarring and disability.

The disease is most common in tropical regions of Africa, but it also occurs in Australia, particularly in coastal Victoria, where case numbers have risen in recent years. Buruli ulcer is curable, especially when diagnosed early, and modern treatment combines oral antibiotics with careful wound care. As a result, surgery is needed far less often than in the past. The World Health Organization lists Buruli ulcer among the neglected tropical diseases.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Buruli ulcer occurs in more than 30 countries, mainly in West and Central Africa, where it predominantly affects children in rural communities. In Australia, the disease occurs along the coast of Victoria and in parts of Queensland, where it affects adults more often, with many cases reported around the Mornington and Bellarine peninsulas. Reported Australian cases have risen over the past decade, and the disease is now considered endemic in some coastal areas. The bacterium is found in the environment, and most affected people cannot recall a specific injury at the site of the lesion.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The infection is caused by \textit{Mycobacterium ulcerans}, a slow-growing relative of the bacteria responsible for tuberculosis and leprosy. The exact route of transmission to humans is not fully understood, but the bacterium is thought to live in water, soil, and possibly in animals such as possums in affected areas, with mosquitoes suggested as a possible vector in Australia:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} probably through breaks in the skin after contact with contaminated water, soil, or vegetation
    \item \textbf{Toxin:} the bacterium produces a toxin called mycolactone, which destroys skin and underlying fat tissue while suppressing the local immune response -- this explains why the early lump is painless and why the ulcer grows so quietly
    \item \textbf{Course:} the infection spreads slowly beneath intact skin, so the visible ulcer is often much smaller than the tissue damage below it
    \item \textbf{Incubation:} it may take weeks to months between exposure and the appearance of the first lump
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Early stage:} a painless lump or swelling under the skin, often mistaken for an insect bite, boil, or cyst
    \item \textbf{Oedematous form:} a larger area of firm swelling, usually on a limb
    \item \textbf{Ulcer form:} the lump breaks down into a shallow ulcer with rolled, undermined edges and a characteristic rim of dead skin
    \item \textbf{Painless:} the ulcer is typically painless in its early stages, which delays presentation
    \item \textbf{Location:} most commonly on the arms and legs
    \item \textbf{Systemic illness:} usually absent -- affected people otherwise feel well
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Insect bites, boils, and skin abscesses, which are usually painful and settle more quickly
    \item Cellulitis and infected skin wounds
    \item Other chronic skin ulcers, such as venous leg ulcers or diabetic foot ulcers
    \item Tropical ulcers and fungal skin infections
    \item Cutaneous tuberculosis, leprosy, and other mycobacterial infections
    \item Rarely, ulcerating skin cancers such as squamous cell carcinoma
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Medical attention should be sought promptly for any painless lump, swelling, or non-healing sore on the skin, especially on an arm or leg, and particularly for people who live in or have visited an area where Buruli ulcer occurs. Early diagnosis greatly improves the outcome and reduces scarring.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A rapidly spreading, painful, red, or hot area of skin with fever, which may indicate a spreading bacterial infection
    \item Signs of sepsis, such as high fever, confusion, or collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} exposure to affected areas and the duration of the lump or ulcer
    \item \textbf{Examination:} the site, appearance, and undermined edge of the lesion are characteristic
    \item \textbf{Polymerase chain reaction (PCR):} testing a swab or biopsy for the bacterium's DNA is the most reliable investigation
    \item \textbf{Swab culture:} culturing the slow-growing bacterium in the laboratory, which takes several weeks
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} a small tissue sample examined under the microscope and tested with special stains for the organism
    \item \textbf{Ziehl--Neelsen stain:} a laboratory stain that can demonstrate the acid-fast bacteria within tissue
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} a course of oral antibiotics, usually a combination such as rifampicin with clarithromycin, is the mainstay of treatment and is taken over several weeks
    \item \textbf{Wound care:} regular cleaning and dressing of the ulcer, with frequent dressing changes; some wounds benefit from negative-pressure (vacuum) dressings
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} previously the main treatment through wide excision, surgery is now mostly limited to removing dead tissue or reconstructing areas with poor healing
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular review to confirm healing and to detect side effects of antibiotics
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} care is usually coordinated by specialists in infectious diseases and wound care
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy and stretching when healing leads to tightness or joint stiffness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Large, destructive ulcers that cause extensive scarring
    \item Permanent disability, including loss of joint movement at sites such as the wrist or ankle
    \item Scarring and contractures after healing
    \item Cosmetic disfigurement and social stigma, particularly where the disease is common
    \item Secondary bacterial infection of the ulcer
    \item Side effects of antibiotic treatment
    \item Recurrence or worsening of the ulcer if treatment is incomplete
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt antibiotic treatment, most Buruli ulcers heal completely, and the earlier treatment begins, the better the result. Small early lumps often heal with little visible scarring. Larger or established ulcers may take months to heal and can leave permanent scarring and some restriction of movement. Without treatment, the ulcer usually continues to enlarge slowly over months to years and can become seriously disabling. The disease does not generally spread to other people and does not cause generalised illness even when extensive.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item In affected areas, cover the skin with long sleeves and trousers, especially during mosquito season
    \item Use insect repellent to reduce mosquito bites
    \item Clean cuts and scratches promptly and cover them with a dressing
    \item Avoid contact between open wounds and natural water, soil, or mud in affected areas
    \item Wash or shower after working or playing in affected coastal or swampy environments
    \item Report any painless lump or non-healing sore promptly for early diagnosis
    \item Complete the full course of antibiotics if treated, and attend all wound-care appointments
\end{itemize}
