\chapter{Gliomas in Children}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Gliomas in children are brain tumours that arise from glial cells, the supporting cells of the brain and spinal cord. They are the most common type of brain tumour in childhood. Childhood gliomas differ from adult gliomas: they are often located in different parts of the brain, have different genetic changes, and, particularly the low-grade types, have a much better prognosis. Around half of childhood gliomas are low-grade and slow-growing, and many can be cured with surgery alone. High-grade gliomas in children are more aggressive and require intensive treatment. Because brain tumours in children are uncommon, care is centralised in specialised paediatric oncology centres with multidisciplinary teams.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Brain tumours are the most common solid tumours of childhood, and gliomas are the most common type. In Australia, around 150--200 children are diagnosed with a brain tumour each year, and roughly half of these are low-grade gliomas. Low-grade gliomas, including pilocytic astrocytoma, are most common in young children, with a peak around 5--9 years. High-grade gliomas are less common and occur across childhood. Survival has improved substantially over recent decades, particularly for low-grade tumours, for which five-year survival exceeds 90\%.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Most childhood gliomas arise without a known cause, but they have distinct genetic features.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Origin:} gliomas arise from glial cells; in children, the most common type is the pilocytic astrocytoma, a low-grade tumour of the cerebellum and optic pathways
    \item \textbf{Genetic changes:} childhood gliomas often have single gene fusions (such as BRAF fusions) rather than the complex mutations seen in adult disease
    \item \textbf{Low-grade gliomas:} slow-growing, well-circumscribed tumours that are often curable with surgery
    \item \textbf{High-grade gliomas:} aggressive tumours that infiltrate brain tissue and require multimodal therapy
    \item \textbf{Associated conditions:} a small proportion of childhood gliomas occur in children with neurofibromatosis type 1
    \item \textbf{No known prevention:} there are no established environmental risk factors for childhood brain tumours
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on the tumour's location and the age of the child.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Headaches, especially in the morning or with vomiting
    \item Vomiting, particularly on waking, without obvious cause
    \item Seizures or fits
    \item Problems with balance, coordination, or walking
    \item Visual problems, including double vision, squint, or loss of vision
    \item Slurred speech or difficulty with fine movements
    \item Changes in behaviour, school performance, or personality
    \item In infants: a rapidly enlarging head, irritability, poor feeding, and regression of milestones
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Symptoms and imaging findings can be caused by other conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Other brain tumours of childhood, including medulloblastoma, ependymoma, and craniopharyngioma
    \item Hydrocephalus from other causes
    \item Brain abscess and encephalitis
    \item Migraine and other recurrent headache disorders
    \item Epilepsy without a structural cause
    \item Developmental or behavioural conditions mistaken for early tumour symptoms
    \item Neurofibromatosis-related brain lesions that are not tumours
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents should seek medical review for any persistent neurological symptoms in a child, including repeated vomiting on waking, new headaches, balance problems, seizures, squint, or behavioural change.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A child with a first seizure or fit
    \item Sudden severe headache with vomiting, drowsiness, or confusion
    \item Sudden loss of vision, weakness, or difficulty waking a child
    \item Any rapid neurological deterioration
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is made by imaging and confirmed by tissue sampling in most cases.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{MRI:} the main imaging test, with detailed views of the brain and spinal cord
    \item \textbf{CT:} sometimes used in emergencies
    \item \textbf{Biopsy or surgery:} tissue is examined to confirm the diagnosis and grade
    \item \textbf{Molecular testing:} testing for BRAF fusions and other markers guides treatment
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture:} may be performed for some tumour types to check for spread in the cerebrospinal fluid
    \item \textbf{Assessment by specialists:} children are evaluated by a paediatric neuro-oncology team
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is planned by a specialist team and depends on the tumour type, location, and the child's age.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} complete removal is the goal for low-grade gliomas and is often curative
    \item \textbf{Observation:} some low-grade tumours that cannot be fully removed may be monitored with regular MRI, especially in young children
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy:} used for tumours that grow or cannot be fully removed; in infants, chemotherapy is preferred to delay radiotherapy
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} used carefully in older children for high-grade or progressive tumours, as it carries long-term risks to the developing brain
    \item \textbf{Targeted therapies:} newer medicines that target BRAF and other genetic changes are increasingly used
    \item \textbf{Management of hydrocephalus:} a shunt or endoscopic third ventriculostomy may be needed if fluid accumulates
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation and support:} physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech therapy, and educational support
    \item \textbf{Long-term follow-up:} survivors are monitored for late effects of treatment on growth, hormones, learning, and health
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Neurological deficits from the tumour or surgery, including weakness, balance problems, and visual loss
    \item Hydrocephalus requiring a shunt
    \item Seizures
    \item Endocrine problems from tumours or radiotherapy near the pituitary, including growth hormone deficiency
    \item Learning and cognitive difficulties after treatment
    \item Late effects of radiotherapy, including second cancers
    \item Infertility and other effects of treatment in survivors
    \item Psychological impact on the child and family
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for childhood gliomas is much better than for adult gliomas. Low-grade gliomas, which make up half of cases, have a five-year survival above 90\%, and many children are cured with surgery alone. High-grade gliomas have a poorer prognosis, although outcomes are slowly improving with intensive treatment and new targeted therapies. Survivors of childhood gliomas may face long-term effects of treatment, so lifelong follow-up focuses on detecting and managing these, maximising independence, and supporting education and quality of life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item There is no known way to prevent childhood gliomas
    \item See a doctor promptly for persistent neurological symptoms in a child
    \item If a child is diagnosed, seek care at a specialised paediatric oncology centre
    \item Engage the full multidisciplinary team, including rehabilitation and education services
    \item Keep a record of treatments received, which is important for long-term follow-up
    \item Attend all scheduled follow-up visits and surveillance imaging
    \item Seek support for siblings and parents through support organisations and counselling
    \item Advocate for school support and learning plans as the child returns to education
\end{itemize}
