\chapter{Aplastic Anemia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Aplastic anemia is a rare but serious blood disorder in which the bone marrow fails to produce enough new blood cells. The marrow becomes empty, or \textbf{hypocellular}, and cannot generate adequate numbers of red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets. The result is a combination of anemia (from low red cells), an increased risk of infection (from low white cells), and bleeding problems (from low platelets) -- a pattern known as \textbf{pancytopenia}.

The condition may be acquired or inherited, and it can develop suddenly or gradually over several months. Without treatment, severe aplastic anemia is often fatal, but modern management with immunosuppressive therapy or stem cell transplantation has transformed the outlook, and many people now survive long term.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Aplastic anemia is uncommon, with roughly 2 new cases per million people each year in Western countries and somewhat higher rates in parts of Asia. It can occur at any age but shows two peaks: in young people in their teens and twenties, and in adults over 60. Men and women are affected about equally.

Inherited forms, such as Fanconi anemia and dyskeratosis congenita, are rare and usually present in childhood. In most acquired cases the cause is never identified; however, the condition has well-recognised links to certain drugs, chemicals, viruses, and radiation exposure.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
In most cases of acquired aplastic anemia no cause is identified, and the disease is termed \textbf{idiopathic}. The underlying mechanism is thought to be autoimmune: the body's own T-lymphocytes attack and suppress the hematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow that normally mature into all blood cell types. Recognised causes and associations include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immune attack:} activated T-lymphocytes destroy or suppress marrow stem cells, producing pancytopenia
    \item \textbf{Medications:} certain drugs can suppress the marrow, including some antibiotics (such as chloramphenicol), gold salts, anticonvulsants, and many chemotherapy agents
    \item \textbf{Chemicals:} benzene and other solvents used in some industries
    \item \textbf{Infections:} hepatitis viruses, Epstein--Barr virus, parvovirus B19, cytomegalovirus, and HIV
    \item \textbf{Radiation:} high-dose exposure, such as from radiotherapy or nuclear accidents
    \item \textbf{Inherited syndromes:} Fanconi anemia and dyskeratosis congenita cause marrow failure, usually presenting in childhood
    \item \textbf{Associated disorders:} paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) frequently coexists with aplastic anemia
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} rarely, marrow failure appears during pregnancy and may resolve after delivery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms reflect the shortage of each type of blood cell. Onset is usually gradual, over weeks to months, but it can occasionally be rapid:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anemia (low red cells):} fatigue, weakness, pallor, breathlessness on exertion, palpitations, and dizziness
    \item \textbf{Bleeding (low platelets):} easy bruising, pinpoint red spots (petechiae), nosebleeds, bleeding gums, and heavy or prolonged menstrual periods
    \item \textbf{Infection (low white cells):} recurrent or severe infections, fevers, mouth ulcers, and sore throat
    \item \textbf{General symptoms:} a persistent feeling of being unwell, poor appetite, and reduced exercise tolerance
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions can produce pancytopenia or similar symptoms and must be distinguished from aplastic anemia:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute leukemia:} infiltration of the marrow by malignant blast cells
    \item \textbf{Myelodysplastic syndromes:} ineffective blood production with abnormal marrow cells
    \item \textbf{Myelofibrosis:} scarring of the marrow with abnormal cell shapes on the blood film
    \item \textbf{Severe vitamin B12 or folate deficiency:} which can cause pancytopenia
    \item \textbf{Bone marrow infiltration:} by cancer, lymphoma, or fibrosis
    \item \textbf{Hypersplenism:} an overactive spleen destroying blood cells
    \item \textbf{Severe infections:} including HIV, Epstein--Barr virus, and some viral hepatitis
    \item \textbf{Drug-induced marrow suppression} and \textbf{autoimmune destruction} of blood cells
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor promptly if you develop unexplained bruising, pinpoint red spots, unusual bleeding, persistent fatigue, or repeated fevers and infections -- especially if several of these occur together.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A high fever or signs of serious infection in someone known to have a low white cell count
    \item Heavy or uncontrolled bleeding, including bleeding that will not stop
    \item A sudden severe headache, confusion, or weakness -- possible bleeding inside the brain
    \item Difficulty breathing, chest pain, or fainting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A full blood count showing low red cells, white cells, and platelets together raises the suspicion of aplastic anemia, and the diagnosis is confirmed by examination of the bone marrow:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full blood count and blood film:} shows pancytopenia with a low reticulocyte (young red cell) count and no abnormal cells
    \item \textbf{Bone marrow biopsy:} the key diagnostic test, showing an unusually empty (hypocellular) marrow with no leukaemic or dysplastic infiltration
    \item \textbf{Blood tests for causes:} vitamin B12, folate, iron studies, liver function, and viral serology for hepatitis, Epstein--Barr virus, parvovirus, cytomegalovirus, and HIV
    \item \textbf{Cytogenetics:} chromosome analysis of marrow cells to exclude leukemia and myelodysplasia
    \item \textbf{PNH testing:} flow cytometry to detect paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria clones
    \item \textbf{Chromosome fragility testing:} performed in younger patients to look for Fanconi anemia
    \item \textbf{Severity assessment:} blood counts and reticulocyte counts are used to grade the disease as moderate, severe, or very severe, which guides treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on the severity of the disease and the age, fitness, and donor availability for the individual patient:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} red cell and platelet transfusions, antibiotics and antifungal agents for infection, and strict precautions to reduce infection risk
    \item \textbf{Immunosuppressive therapy:} a combination of anti-thymocyte globulin and ciclosporin, often with eltrombopag, used when transplantation is not possible or not preferred
    \item \textbf{Stem cell transplantation:} a bone marrow transplant from a matched donor can cure the disease and is the preferred treatment for younger patients
    \item \textbf{Growth factors:} medicines that stimulate blood cell production, which may help selected patients
    \item \textbf{Avoidance of triggers:} stopping any offending medicine and avoiding further chemical or radiation exposure
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} long-term monitoring of blood counts, iron levels, and signs of relapse or transformation to other marrow disorders
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, life-threatening infections and sepsis
    \item Serious bleeding, including intracranial haemorrhage
    \item Iron overload from repeated blood transfusions, damaging the liver and heart over time
    \item Progression to myelodysplastic syndrome or acute leukemia in a minority of cases
    \item Complications of treatment, including graft-versus-host disease after transplantation
    \item Chronic fatigue and reduced quality of life
    \item Psychological and social effects of living with a serious chronic illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Untreated severe aplastic anemia is usually fatal within months to a few years, but modern treatment has greatly improved outcomes. Young patients who receive a successful stem cell transplant have a high chance of long-term survival and cure. Of those treated with immunosuppressive therapy, a substantial proportion respond, although some relapse, need further treatment, or develop late clonal complications.

Older patients and those with very severe disease or significant co-morbidities generally do less well. The outlook in individual cases depends on age, the severity of the disease, the response to first-line therapy, and the availability of a suitable donor, and should always be discussed in detail with the treating haematologist.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Aplastic anemia is usually not preventable, but some risks can be reduced:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Limit exposure to benzene and other industrial solvents, using appropriate protective equipment at work
    \item Take medicines only as prescribed, and avoid unproven or unnecessary drugs
    \item Report unusual bruising, bleeding, or persistent fatigue to a doctor early
    \item Follow infection-prevention advice if you have a low white cell count, including good hand hygiene and avoiding crowded places
    \item Keep vaccinations up to date as advised by the treating team
    \item Attend all follow-up appointments and blood tests
    \item Manage treatment side effects, including iron overload, and seek support for emotional wellbeing
\end{itemize}
