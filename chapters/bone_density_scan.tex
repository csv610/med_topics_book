\chapter{Bone Density Scan}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A bone density scan, or dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA or DEXA), is a painless imaging test that measures the mineral content of bone, most commonly at the hip, lumbar spine, and sometimes the forearm. It is the standard method for diagnosing osteoporosis, for estimating the risk of future fracture, and for monitoring the response to treatment. The scan uses two X-ray beams of different energies to calculate how much radiation is absorbed by bone, and it delivers only a very small dose -- typically less than a chest X-ray and far less than a CT scan. Results are reported as a T-score, which compares bone density with the average for a healthy young adult, and a Z-score, which compares it with people of the same age.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Osteoporosis is one of the most common diseases of later life. It is estimated to affect around one in three women and one in five men over the age of 50, and the number of affected people is rising as populations age. The risk of fracture climbs steeply with declining bone density: around half of all women will sustain an osteoporosis-related fracture at some point, with the wrist, hip, and vertebrae the most common sites. Hip fractures in particular are associated with substantial disability and increased mortality. Many people are scanned only after a fracture has occurred, but the best results come from identifying high-risk individuals earlier through appropriate risk assessment and timely scanning.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bone physiology:} bone is continuously remodelled; through young adulthood, formation exceeds resorption, but from about age 30 bone mass slowly declines, and this accelerates after the menopause and with age in men.
    \item \textbf{Menopause:} oestrogen deficiency after the menopause markedly increases osteoclast activity and accelerates bone loss.
    \item \textbf{The DXA measurement:} the scanner compares the absorption of two X-ray beams, one absorbed mainly by bone and one by soft tissue, allowing a precise calculation of bone mineral density per unit area.
    \item \textbf{Who is scanned:} the test is usually offered to postmenopausal women and older men with risk factors, to people with a fragility fracture, and to those taking long-term steroid therapy or other bone-depleting medicines.
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} low body weight, smoking, excess alcohol, family history, previous fractures, and conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, coeliac disease, and hyperthyroidism all lower bone density.
    \item \textbf{Safety:} the scan involves radiation exposure of roughly 1--10 microsieverts, but the procedure is quick, non-invasive, and requires no special preparation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
A bone density scan is performed in people who may have no symptoms at all, because osteoporosis is silent until a fracture occurs. It is offered on the basis of risk, and the following groups are commonly referred:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Women aged 65 and over, and men aged 70 and over, whether or not they have risk factors
    \item Younger postmenopausal women and men with risk factors such as low body weight, smoking, or a parental history of hip fracture
    \item People who have had a low-trauma or fragility fracture
    \item People taking glucocorticoid (steroid) therapy for three months or more
    \item People with diseases that affect bone, such as hyperparathyroidism, coeliac disease, or rheumatoid arthritis
    \item People whose treatment for osteoporosis needs monitoring
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The bone density scan itself does not have differential diagnoses in the usual sense, but the results need to be interpreted in context:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{T-score of -1.0 or above:} normal bone density.
    \item \textbf{T-score between -1.0 and -2.5:} osteopenia, or low bone density, which increases fracture risk and may warrant lifestyle measures and sometimes treatment.
    \item \textbf{T-score of -2.5 or below:} osteoporosis, which usually warrants treatment to reduce fracture risk.
    \item \textbf{Severe or "established" osteoporosis:} a T-score of -2.5 or below together with a fragility fracture.
    \item \textbf{Technical factors:} osteoarthritis, previous spinal surgery, or calcification of the aorta can artefactually raise measured density at the lumbar spine.
    \item \textbf{Secondary causes:} a low Z-score in a younger person should prompt investigation for an underlying cause of bone loss.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
A bone density scan is arranged by a doctor, so no direct emergency applies to the test itself. Discuss with your GP whether you are eligible for scanning if you have risk factors, have had a fracture after a minor fall, take long-term steroids, or are going through early menopause. Sudden severe back pain, loss of height, or a stooped posture can indicate a vertebral fracture and warrant medical review.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe pain, inability to bear weight, or deformity after a fall (possible fracture)
    \item Numbness, weakness, or loss of bladder or bowel control (possible spinal cord compression)
    \item Signs of a pulmonary embolism, such as sudden breathlessness or chest pain after a period of immobility
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} age, menopausal status, fracture history, medications, family history, and lifestyle risk factors.
    \item \textbf{DXA scan:} measurement at the hip and lumbar spine; the forearm is scanned when the hip or spine cannot be measured reliably, such as in severe obesity or previous spinal fusion.
    \item \textbf{T-score and Z-score:} the T-score compares bone density with young adult peak bone mass and is used for diagnosis; the Z-score compares with age-matched norms and flags secondary causes when it is low.
    \item \textbf{Fracture risk assessment (FRAX):} a validated tool that combines bone density with clinical risk factors to estimate ten-year probability of major osteoporotic fracture, guiding whether treatment is needed.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} serum calcium, vitamin D, renal and liver function, thyroid function, and sometimes coeliac antibodies or testosterone in men, to exclude secondary causes.
    \item \textbf{Vertebral imaging:} if a vertebral fracture is suspected, plain films or a lateral spine image from the DXA scan can confirm it.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} adequate calcium (about 1000--1200 mg daily) and vitamin D, weight-bearing and muscle-strengthening exercise, smoking cessation, and moderation of alcohol, for everyone with low bone density.
    \item \textbf{Vitamin D supplementation:} recommended for people who are deficient, housebound, or at risk, alongside antiresorptive treatment.
    \item \textbf{Bisphosphonates:} first-line drug therapy for most people with osteoporosis; alendronate, risedronate, and zoledronate reduce vertebral and hip fracture risk by roughly 40--60\%.
    \item \textbf{Denosumab:} an injectable monoclonal antibody given twice a year; an effective alternative for people who cannot take bisphosphonates.
    \item \textbf{Anabolic agents:} teriparatide and romosozumab build bone and are reserved for people at very high fracture risk or those who have failed other treatments.
    \item \textbf{Follow-up scanning:} repeat DXA after two to three years of treatment to confirm response; bone density usually stabilises or improves.
    \item \textbf{Falls prevention:} home safety assessment, vision and balance review, and targeted exercise to reduce the risk of falling.
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} for young people, unusual fractures, or suspected secondary osteoporosis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The scan itself is essentially risk-free, but the underlying condition being investigated carries important risks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hip, vertebral, and wrist fractures, which can cause pain, disability, and loss of independence
    \item Increased mortality in the year after a hip fracture, particularly in older people
    \item Vertebral compression fractures leading to height loss, kyphosis, and chronic back pain
    \item Psychosocial consequences of fracture, including fear of falling and social withdrawal
    \item Adverse effects of treatment, including gastrointestinal intolerance of oral bisphosphonates and rare atypical femoral fractures or osteonecrosis of the jaw with prolonged use
    \item Failure to identify and treat high-risk individuals, which leaves fractures preventable only after they occur
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Bone density is a strong predictor of fracture risk, and the T-score remains the single most useful measurement for guiding treatment decisions. Treatment with bisphosphonates or denosumab slows bone loss, stabilises or modestly increases density, and reduces fracture risk substantially within one to two years. People who start treatment after a first fracture reduce their risk of a second fracture by roughly half. With lifestyle measures and appropriate medication, most people with osteoporosis remain mobile and independent. The scan is also used to monitor treatment: a stable or improved density on follow-up DXA is an excellent indicator that therapy is working.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Build peak bone mass early in life through regular weight-bearing activity and adequate calcium and vitamin D.
    \item Continue weight-bearing exercise such as walking, running, tennis, or resistance training throughout adulthood.
    \item Meet daily calcium needs from dairy, leafy greens, and fortified foods, and take supplements only if advised.
    \item Get sensible sun exposure and consider a vitamin D supplement during winter or if housebound.
    \item Do not smoke, and limit alcohol intake.
    \item Discuss bone density testing with your doctor if you have risk factors, have had a fragility fracture, or take long-term steroids.
    \item Reduce fall risk at home with adequate lighting, grab rails, and removal of loose rugs.
\end{itemize}
