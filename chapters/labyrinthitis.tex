\chapter{Labyrinthitis}
\index{Labyrinthitis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Labyrinthitis is an inflammatory condition affecting the labyrinth, the delicate structure of fluid-filled channels within the inner ear that houses the organs of hearing (the cochlea) and balance (the vestibular system). This inflammation typically impacts both the vestibular and cochlear branches of the vestibulocochlear nerve (cranial nerve VIII), resulting in a sudden onset of severe vertigo, balance disruption, sensorineural hearing loss, and tinnitus. It is clinically distinguished from vestibular neuritis, which involves inflammation of the vestibular nerve alone and does not cause hearing impairment. Labyrinthitis can be acute or chronic, and while most cases are secondary to viral infections and resolve spontaneously, bacterial or autoimmune forms can occur, requiring urgent medical intervention to prevent permanent neurological and otological damage.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Labyrinthitis is a relatively common cause of peripheral vestibular disorders, though its exact global incidence remains difficult to quantify due to underreporting and frequent misdiagnosis. It most frequently affects adults aged between 30 and 60 years, with no significant predilection for either sex. Viral labyrinthitis is the most prevalent form and often occurs as a post-infectious sequela, frequently peak-matching the seasonal prevalence of upper respiratory tract infections in autumn and winter. Paediatric cases are rare and are more frequently associated with bacterial complications of acute otitis media or meningitis. In many countries and globally, the widespread implementation of childhood immunisation programmes against measles, mumps, and rubella has significantly reduced the incidence of post-viral and suppurative labyrinthitis in children.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The inflammation of the membranous labyrinth disrupts the transmission of auditory and vestibular signals to the brain, leading to sensory mismatch and the characteristic symptoms of vertigo and hearing loss.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral infection:} The most frequent cause, often arising from direct viral invasion or a post-infectious inflammatory response. Implicated agents include herpes simplex virus type 1, varicella zoster virus (associated with Ramsay Hunt syndrome), influenza viruses, measles, mumps, rubella, and cytomegalovirus.
    \item \textbf{Bacterial infection:} A less common but severe form known as suppurative labyrinthitis. It occurs when bacteria invade the inner ear, either from the middle ear cavity (secondary to chronic otitis media or cholesteatoma) or from the cerebrospinal fluid via the cochlear aqueduct (secondary to bacterial meningitis). Common pathogens include \textit{Streptococcus pneumoniae}, \textit{Haemophilus influenzae}, and \textit{Neisseria meningitidis}.
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune disorders:} Systemic autoimmune diseases, such as granulomatosis with polyangiitis, polyarteritis nodosa, or Cogan's syndrome, can trigger localised vasculitis and autoimmune labyrinthitis.
    \item \textbf{Vascular ischaemia:} Compromise of the labyrinthine artery, which lacks collateral circulation, can mimic labyrinthitis by causing sudden ischaemia to the inner ear, though this is pathophysiologically an infarctive rather than inflammatory process.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of labyrinthitis is typically acute and dramatic, characterised by a combination of vestibular and auditory symptoms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vertigo:} A sudden, severe, and continuous sensation of spinning or motion, which is characteristically exacerbated by head movement.
    \item \textbf{Sensorineural hearing loss:} Mild-to-profound hearing impairment, typically unilateral, affecting high frequencies or all frequencies.
    \item \textbf{Tinnitus:} A persistent ringing, buzzing, or roaring sound in the affected ear, reflecting cochlear irritation.
    \item \textbf{Nausea and vomiting:} Often severe during the initial acute phase, driven by vestibulomedullary connections.
    \item \textbf{Gait instability and ataxia:} Difficulty walking, with a tendency to veer or fall toward the side of the affected ear.
    \item \textbf{Nystagmus:} Spontaneous, horizontal-torsional nystagmus, where the fast phase beats away from the affected ear due to unilateral vestibular hypofunction.
    \item \textbf{Aural fullness:} A sensation of pressure or blockage in the affected ear.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Distinguishing labyrinthitis from other central and peripheral causes of vertigo and hearing loss is critical for directing appropriate therapy.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vestibular neuritis:} Characterised by acute vertigo and nystagmus, but distinguished from labyrinthitis by the complete absence of hearing loss and tinnitus.
    \item \textbf{M\textsuperscript{e}ni\textsuperscript{e}re's disease:} A chronic condition presenting with recurrent episodes of vertigo, fluctuating low-frequency hearing loss, tinnitus, and aural fullness, whereas labyrinthitis is typically a single, prolonged episode.
    \item \textbf{Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV):} Causes brief, transient episodes of vertigo triggered by specific head movements, lasting seconds to minutes, without associated hearing loss or continuous dizziness.
    \item \textbf{Cerebellar or brainstem stroke:} A critical central cause of acute vertigo that can mimic inner ear disease; it is often accompanied by focal neurological signs, central nystagmus (direction-changing or vertical), and risk factors for cardiovascular disease.
    \item \textbf{Acoustic neuroma (vestibular schwannoma):} A slow-growing benign tumour of the vestibulocochlear nerve that causes progressive, gradual unilateral hearing loss and mild imbalance, rather than acute-onset severe vertigo.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients experiencing persistent vertigo, imbalance, or any degree of sudden hearing loss should seek a prompt medical evaluation to determine the underlying cause and initiate treatment.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vertigo accompanied by focal neurological symptoms such as double vision, slurred speech, facial weakness, or numbness in the limbs
    \item Sudden, severe, or unexplained headache, neck stiffness, or confusion
    \item Inability to walk or stand independently, or new-onset severe incoordination
    \item Continuous, intractable vomiting leading to dehydration
    \item Vertigo presenting with chest pain, shortness of breath, or loss of consciousness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A structured diagnostic approach is required to differentiate labyrinthitis from central pathology and to identify the primary cause.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical history and physical examination:} Focused evaluation of the onset, duration, and nature of symptoms, including otoscopic inspection to rule out middle ear pathology.
    \item \textbf{HINTS examination:} A three-part bedside oculomotor assessment (Head Impulse, Nystagmus, Test of Skew) used to differentiate peripheral vestibular lesions from acute central stroke.
    \item \textbf{Audiometry:} Pure-tone audiometry to document the presence, severity, and configuration of sensorineural hearing loss.
    \item \textbf{Vestibular function testing:} Electronystagmography (ENG) or videonystagmography (VNG), and caloric testing to assess the responsiveness of the horizontal semicircular canals.
    \item \textbf{Neuroimaging:} Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain and internal auditory canals, with gadolinium contrast, is indicated to rule out acoustic neuroma, demyelinating disease, or cerebellar infarction.
    \item \textbf{Laboratory investigations:} Full blood count, inflammatory markers (CRP and ESR), and blood cultures if bacterial suppurative labyrinthitis or meningitis is suspected.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is multi-faceted, aiming to control acute vestibular symptoms, reduce inner ear inflammation, and treat any underlying infection.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vestibular suppressants:} Short-term use of medications such as prochlorperazine or promethazine to alleviate acute vertigo and nausea; these should be discontinued within 48 to 72 hours to avoid delaying central vestibular compensation.
    \item \textbf{Corticosteroids:} A course of oral prednisolone (e.g., 1 mg/kg/day, tapered over 10 to 14 days) is initiated early in viral cases to reduce labyrinthine inflammation and improve the recovery of vestibular and auditory function.
    \item \textbf{Antimicrobial therapy:} Intravenous antibiotics are urgently indicated for bacterial suppurative labyrinthitis, often combined with surgical drainage (myringotomy) or mastoidectomy if chronic otitis media is the source.
    \item \textbf{Antiviral agents:} May be considered in cases where a specific viral cause is suspected, such as herpes zoster oticus (Ramsay Hunt syndrome), where aciclovir or valaciclovir is prescribed.
    \item \textbf{Vestibular Rehabilitation Therapy (VRT):} A specialised programme of physical exercises designed to facilitate central nervous system compensation for unilateral vestibular loss, indicated for patients with persistent imbalance after the acute phase.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Permanent hearing loss:} Moderate-to-profound sensorineural deafness in the affected ear, particularly common in bacterial labyrinthitis.
    \item \textbf{Chronic vestibular dysfunction:} Ongoing imbalance, spatial disorientation, or motion sensitivity due to incomplete central compensation.
    \item \textbf{Persistent Postural-Perceptual Vertigo (PPPV):} A chronic functional vestibular disorder characterised by non-spinning vertigo and unsteadiness, triggered by labyrinthitis.
    \item \textbf{Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo (BPPV):} Secondary BPPV can develop due to inflammatory debris or otoconia displacing into the semicircular canals.
    \item \textbf{Intracranial spread:} In bacterial cases, infection can spread to the meninges, leading to meningitis, brain abscess, or cavernous sinus thrombosis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for labyrinthitis depends primarily on the aetiology. Most cases of viral labyrinthitis resolve within several weeks, with vertigo subsiding first, followed by gradual improvement in balance. Auditory recovery is more variable; while many patients regain normal or near-normal hearing, some are left with permanent sensorineural deficits. Bacterial labyrinthitis carries a high risk of permanent hearing loss and long-term vestibular hypofunction. Early initiation of corticosteroid therapy and vestibular rehabilitation exercises significantly improves outcomes, promoting faster and more complete central vestibular compensation.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Complete immunisation schedules:} Ensure up-to-date vaccination against measles, mumps, rubella, and pneumococcal disease to prevent secondary labyrinthine infections.
    \item \textbf{Treat middle ear infections early:} Seek prompt medical attention for acute otitis media or chronic ear discharge to minimise the risk of suppurative complications.
    \item \textbf{Minimise head movements:} During the acute phase of vertigo, rest in a quiet, dark room and avoid sudden changes in head position.
    \item \textbf{Avoid driving and operating machinery:} Refrain from activities requiring precise balance and coordination until vertigo has completely resolved.
    \item \textbf{Maintain hydration:} Sip small amounts of clear fluids or oral rehydration solutions during periods of severe nausea and vomiting.
    \item \textbf{Engage in visual fixation:} Focus on a stationary object to help reduce the sensation of spinning during mild episodes of vertigo.
\end{itemize}
