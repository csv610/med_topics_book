\chapter{Knee Injuries}
\index{Knee Injuries}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The knee is the largest and one of the most complex joints in the body, bearing considerable weight and enabling walking, running, squatting, and turning. Because of its size, complexity, and exposure to force, it is frequently injured. Knee injuries range from minor ligament sprains and bruises to serious damage to ligaments, cartilage, tendons, and bone that may require surgery. Common structures injured include the medial and lateral collateral ligaments, the anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments, and the menisci (cartilage pads). This chapter provides an overview of the types of knee injuries, how they present, and how they are diagnosed and managed.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Knee injuries are among the most common musculoskeletal presentations in general practice and emergency departments. They occur across all ages and activity levels, with peaks in young adults during sport and in older adults from falls. The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is one of the most frequently injured knee ligaments, particularly in sports involving sudden stops, pivots, and jumps such as football, netball, and skiing, and women sustain ACL injuries at higher rates than men in comparable sports. Meniscal injuries are also common, often accompanying ligament damage or occurring with twisting injuries in older people whose menisci have become more fragile with age.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Trauma:} direct blows to the knee from falls, collisions, or motor vehicle accidents
    \item \textbf{Twisting injuries:} sudden pivoting on a planted foot, common in sport, can tear ligaments and menisci
    \item \textbf{Hyperextension and hyperflexion:} forced straightening or bending can damage the cruciate ligaments
    \item \textbf{Collateral ligament injury:} a sideways force to the knee injures the medial or lateral collateral ligament
    \item \textbf{ACL injury:} often a non-contact pivoting injury, frequently accompanied by a popping sensation, rapid swelling, and giving way
    \item \textbf{Patellar injuries:} dislocations, fractures, or damage to the cartilage behind the kneecap
    \item \textbf{Overuse:} repetitive loading causes patellofemoral pain, tendinopathy, and stress reactions in runners and jumpers
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain in or around the knee, which may be immediate or gradual
    \item Swelling, which may develop rapidly (suggesting bleeding within the joint, as in ACL rupture) or over hours
    \item Difficulty bearing weight, walking, or fully straightening or bending the knee
    \item Instability or "giving way" of the knee, suggesting ligament damage
    \item Locking or catching, when a torn meniscus or loose fragment blocks movement
    \item Bruising, tenderness, and deformity in fractures or dislocations
    \item A popping or tearing sensation at the moment of injury is classically described in ACL rupture
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ligament sprains and tears: collateral and cruciate ligaments
    \item Meniscal tears: medial and lateral cartilage injuries
    \item Patellofemoral pain syndrome and patellar dislocation
    \item Fractures: patella, distal femur, tibial plateau, or avulsion fractures
    \item Tendon injuries: quadriceps or patellar tendon rupture
    \item Bursitis, such as prepatellar or pes anserine bursitis
    \item Inflammatory or degenerative conditions: osteoarthritis, gout, or septic arthritis must be considered, especially in older adults
    \item Referred pain from the hip or lumbar spine
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if the knee is severely painful, swollen, or unable to bear weight, if it locks or gives way repeatedly, or if symptoms do not settle within a few days of simple measures. Early assessment matters because some injuries, such as ligament tears and meniscal injuries, are best treated promptly.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item An obviously deformed or angulated knee or an inability to straighten the leg
    \item Signs of dislocation, especially with numbness, coldness, or pallor of the lower leg or foot
    \item Severe pain, swelling, or inability to move the joint after significant trauma
    \item Fever with a hot, swollen knee, which may indicate septic arthritis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the mechanism of injury is often diagnostic, such as a pivoting injury with a pop and rapid swelling pointing to an ACL tear
    \item \textbf{Examination:} assessment of swelling, tenderness, range of movement, and stability; specific tests such as the Lachman test for the ACL and McMurray test for the menisci
    \item \textbf{X-ray:} performed when a fracture is suspected or using clinical rules such as the Ottawa Knee Rules to avoid unnecessary imaging
    \item \textbf{Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):} the best investigation for soft tissue injuries, showing ligaments, menisci, and cartilage in detail
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} useful for superficial structures and tendon injuries
    \item \textbf{Arthroscopy:} a keyhole procedure that allows direct visualisation and can be used for both diagnosis and treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute first aid:} rest, ice, compression, and elevation (RICE), together with protection of the knee and simple analgesia
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} the cornerstone of recovery for most knee injuries, restoring range of movement, strength, balance, and sport-specific function
    \item \textbf{Bracing and supports:} temporary splints, crutches, or a knee brace may be used to protect healing structures
    \item \textbf{Anti-inflammatory medication:} short courses may help pain and swelling, used cautiously and under advice
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} indicated for some complete ligament tears, displaced fractures, and meniscal tears that cause locking; ACL reconstruction is often recommended for active people
    \item \textbf{Injections:} corticosteroid injections for inflammation and other injections for arthritis-related pain
    \item \textbf{Graduated return to activity:} a structured rehabilitation programme that avoids premature return to sport, which increases the risk of re-injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic instability and repeated giving way if ligament injuries are untreated
    \item Post-traumatic osteoarthritis, which develops over years after significant injuries, especially meniscal and ACL injuries
    \item Stiffness, weakness, and loss of movement after immobilisation or surgery
    \item Re-injury, particularly if rehabilitation is incomplete before returning to sport
    \item Deep vein thrombosis after surgery or prolonged immobility
    \item Chronic pain and reduced participation in sport and work
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis depends on the structures injured and the quality of rehabilitation. Minor sprains and contusions usually settle within days to weeks. Ligament and meniscal injuries respond well to structured physiotherapy, and many people avoid surgery entirely. When surgery is needed, outcomes are generally good, with most people returning to full function, although complete recovery after ACL reconstruction typically takes nine to twelve months. Long-term, preventing osteoarthritis through muscle strengthening and safe return to activity is an important goal.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Strengthen the muscles around the knee, especially the quadriceps and hamstrings
    \item Warm up properly before sport and include balance and agility training
    \item Use correct technique for landing, cutting, and changing direction, taught in injury-prevention programmes such as the FIFA 11+
    \item Choose appropriate footwear for the activity and replace worn shoes
    \item Maintain a healthy weight to reduce load on the knee
    \item Return to sport gradually and only when strength and movement have recovered
    \item Use protective equipment where appropriate, such as knee pads for high-risk activities
\end{itemize}
