\chapter{Labia Problems}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The labia are the folds of skin surrounding the opening of the vagina. The outer folds (labia majora) are covered with hair after puberty, while the inner folds (labia minora) are delicate, hairless skin that vary greatly in size, shape, and colour between women. Labia problems are common and include itching, soreness, swelling, changes in appearance, lumps, and pain. They can arise from infections, skin conditions, irritation, injury, or normal anatomical variation that causes concern. This chapter covers the common causes of labia problems, when to seek help, and how they are treated.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Problems affecting the vulva and labia are very common, though often under-reported because of embarrassment. Vulval itching and soreness affect women of all ages; in children they are usually due to irritation or non-specific vulvovaginitis, in reproductive-aged women to infections or dermatitis, and in older women to thinning of the skin after menopause. Labial asymmetry or prominence is a normal variation, but a growing number of women seek advice about it, driven partly by cosmetic concerns. Most labial conditions are benign, but some need specific treatment, and a small number are serious.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infections:} candidiasis (thrush), bacterial vaginosis, and sexually transmitted infections such as herpes simplex, syphilis, and human papillomavirus (genital warts)
    \item \textbf{Skin conditions:} eczema, psoriasis, and lichen sclerosus or lichen planus, which cause itching and can scar if untreated
    \item \textbf{Contact irritation:} reactions to soaps, perfumes, sanitary products, laundry detergents, or tight clothing
    \item \textbf{Trauma:} friction from exercise, sexual activity, or injury; also female genital mutilation
    \item \textbf{Cysts and benign growths:} Bartholin's cysts, sebaceous cysts, and skin tags
    \item \textbf{Normal variation:} labia that differ in size, length, or colour are a normal part of female anatomy
    \item \textbf{Hormonal changes:} thinning and dryness of vulval skin in menopause and during breastfeeding
    \item \textbf{Cancer:} vulval cancer is rare but must be considered, especially in older women with persistent lumps, ulcers, or itch
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Itching, soreness, burning, or irritation of the labia and surrounding skin
    \item Redness, swelling, or a rash
    \item Discharge or odour, which may accompany infection
    \item Sores, ulcers, blisters, or warts
    \item Lumps or cysts, which may be painful or painless
    \item Pain during sex, urination, or sitting
    \item Skin changes such as whitening, thickening, or thinning of the vulval skin
    \item Bleeding or a persistent non-healing sore, which always warrants review
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Thrush, bacterial vaginosis, and sexually transmitted infections
    \item Eczema, psoriasis, and lichen sclerosus
    \item Contact dermatitis from products or clothing
    \item Bartholin's cyst or abscess
    \item Genital warts and molluscum contagiosum
    \item Vulvodynia: chronic vulval pain without an identifiable cause
    \item Vulval intraepithelial neoplasia or vulval cancer, rare but important
    \item Normal anatomical variation mistaken for a problem
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Women should see a doctor for persistent itching, soreness, or pain; for new lumps, sores, or ulcers; for discharge or bleeding; for symptoms that do not settle with simple measures; or for any concern about the appearance of the labia. A GP, sexual health clinic, or gynaecologist can assess and treat most conditions.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe swelling of the vulva with difficulty passing urine
    \item A rapidly growing, very painful swelling that may be a Bartholin's abscess
    \item Heavy bleeding from the vulva or vagina
    \item Signs of anaphylaxis, such as facial swelling, breathing difficulty, or collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} symptoms, duration, associated discharge, sexual history, products used, and any skin conditions elsewhere
    \item \textbf{Examination:} careful inspection of the vulva and labia, sometimes with a magnifying instrument (colposcope)
    \item \textbf{Swabs:} for thrush, bacterial vaginosis, and sexually transmitted infections
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} a small skin sample taken under local anaesthetic to diagnose persistent rashes, lichen sclerosus, or suspected cancer
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} occasionally for underlying conditions
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a gynaecologist or dermatologist for complex, persistent, or unclear cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Simple measures:} avoid irritants, wear cotton underwear, use unscented products, and keep the area clean and dry
    \item \textbf{Antifungal or antibiotic treatment:} for thrush and bacterial infections
    \item \textbf{Antiviral treatment:} for herpes outbreaks and, as prevention, for recurrent cases
    \item \textbf{Topical steroids:} effective for eczema, psoriasis, and lichen sclerosus
    \item \textbf{Moisturisers and barrier creams:} to protect sensitive skin
    \item \textbf{Bartholin's cyst or abscess:} drainage or marsupialisation may be needed
    \item \textbf{Pain management:} for vulvodynia, including physiotherapy, topical treatments, and psychological support
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} removal of persistent cysts or growths, and treatment for vulval cancer or its precursors
    \item \textbf{Counselling and reassurance:} for normal anatomical variation, addressing body image concerns without unnecessary surgery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Untreated lichen sclerosus can lead to scarring, narrowing of the vaginal opening, and a small increased risk of vulval cancer
    \item Bartholin's abscess with spreading infection
    \item Recurrent thrush or herpes affecting quality of life
    \item Chronic vulval pain and its effects on sex, relationships, and mental health
    \item Missed diagnosis of vulval cancer if persistent lesions are not investigated
    \item Unnecessary surgery for normal anatomical variation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most labia problems are benign and respond well to treatment. Thrush, dermatitis, and infections settle with appropriate therapy; skin conditions such as lichen sclerosus are manageable with long-term topical treatment, though they may need ongoing review. Vulvodynia improves in most women with a multidisciplinary approach. Vulval cancer, when detected early, has a good outlook. Women who feel their labia are "abnormal" can be reassured that a wide range of appearances is normal and healthy.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wash the vulva with water or a gentle, unscented cleanser; avoid soaps, perfumes, and douches
    \item Wear cotton underwear and avoid tight, synthetic clothing
    \item Change sanitary pads and tampons regularly
    \item Practise safe sex and attend recommended STI screening
    \item Moisturise the area if it is dry, especially after menopause
    \item Check the vulva regularly in a mirror and report any persistent lump, sore, or skin change
    \item Seek help early for persistent itching or pain; most conditions are easily treated
\end{itemize}
