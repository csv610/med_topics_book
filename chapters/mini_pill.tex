\chapter{Mini pill}
\index{Mini pill}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The mini pill, clinically known as the progestogen-only contraceptive pill (POP), is an oral contraceptive agent containing only a synthetic progestogen and no oestrogen. Unlike the combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP), the mini pill is widely prescribed for women who have contraindications to oestrogen, such as those with a history of venous thromboembolism, severe hypertension, focal migraine, or those who are currently breastfeeding. Its primary mechanism of action relies on thickening the cervical mucus to prevent sperm penetration and thinning the endometrium to inhibit implantation, with some newer formulations (such as desogestrel) also reliably suppressing ovulation.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Globally and within the affected region, oral contraceptives are among the most common methods of reversible contraception. The mini pill is estimated to be used by approximately 5 to 10\% of women using oral contraception, though its utilisation has increased with the introduction of newer, more forgiving desogestrel-based formulations. It is particularly prevalent among postpartum and lactating women, as well as women over the age of 35 who smoke, and those with cardiovascular risk factors that preclude the use of combined hormonal contraceptives.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The contraceptive efficacy and physiological effects of the mini pill are mediated through continuous progestogen administration, which alters the female reproductive system.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cervical mucus thickening:} Synthetic progestogen increases the viscosity and acidity of cervical mucus, creating a hostile environment that prevents sperm migration into the uterine cavity.
    \item \textbf{Endometrial changes:} The drug induces secretory changes and subsequent atrophy of the endometrium, making the uterine lining unreceptive to the implantation of a blastocyst.
    \item \textbf{Ovulation suppression:} While traditional mini pills (e.g., norethisterone or levonorgestrel) suppress ovulation in only about 50 to 60\% of cycles, newer desogestrel-based mini pills consistently suppress ovulation in approximately 97 to 99\% of cycles.
    \item \textbf{Tubal motility reduction:} Progestogens decrease the ciliary activity and muscular contractility of the fallopian tubes, slowing the transport of the ovum.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of a patient using the mini pill is characterised by alterations in the menstrual cycle and potential minor systemic effects.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Altered bleeding patterns:} Irregular vaginal bleeding, spotting, amenorrhoea, or prolonged bleeding cycles are highly common, especially during the first few months of use.
    \item \textbf{Hormonal symptoms:} Minor systemic effects may include breast tenderness, mild nausea, headaches, and mood fluctuations.
    \item \textbf{Dermatological changes:} Mild acne flare-ups or, conversely, improvement in skin clear-cut severity in some individuals.
    \item \textbf{Weight and fluid retention:} Minimal fluctuations in weight or mild bloating due to transient fluid retention.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When managing patients experiencing side effects, irregular bleeding, or contraceptive failure on the mini pill, clinicians must distinguish these signs from other gynaecological and medical conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy or ectopic pregnancy:} Must be ruled out in cases of sudden amenorrhoea, severe lower abdominal pain, or atypical irregular bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP) use:} Distinguished by the presence of oestrogen and a strict 21/7 regimen, whereas the mini pill is taken continuously without a pill-free break.
    \item \textbf{Gynaecological pathology:} Uterine fibroids, endometrial polyps, pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), or cervical neoplasia must be considered if irregular bleeding is persistent or accompanied by pelvic pain.
    \item \textbf{Thyroid dysfunction:} Hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism can cause menstrual irregularities similar to those induced by the mini pill.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should consult a general practitioner or sexual health clinic if they experience persistent irregular bleeding, severe mood changes, or suspect they may be pregnant.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, sudden, or one-sided lower abdominal or pelvic pain (suggestive of an ectopic pregnancy).
    \item Sudden difficulty breathing, chest pain, or coughing up blood.
    \item Severe pain, swelling, or redness in one calf (indicative of deep vein thrombosis).
    \item A sudden, severe headache, weakness on one side of the body, or difficulty speaking.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Pre-prescribing evaluation and monitoring during mini pill use involve targeted clinical assessment and diagnostic investigations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detailed clinical history:} Reviewing cardiovascular risk, venous thromboembolism history, active liver disease, breast cancer history, and current medications (specifically enzyme-inducing drugs).
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure measurement:} Although the mini pill has minimal impact on blood pressure, baseline measurement is routine practice.
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test:} Urine or serum beta-hCG testing to exclude pregnancy before initiating therapy or if amenorrhoea or abnormal spotting occurs.
    \item \textbf{Sexually transmitted infection (STI) screening:} Offered routinely as part of sexual health consultations prior to starting oral contraception.
    \item \textbf{Cervical screening test:} Ensure cervical screening is up to date in accordance with national guidelines.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Successful contraception with the mini pill depends on precise patient education, strict adherence to daily administration, and understanding corrective actions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Daily administration guidelines:} The pill must be taken at the same time every day without a break. Traditional formulations (e.g., levonorgestrel) have a narrow 3-hour window, while desogestrel formulations have a 12-hour window.
    \item \textbf{Missed pill management:} If a pill is missed beyond the allowed window, emergency contraception may be required if unprotected sexual intercourse occurred within the previous 7 days, and barrier contraception (e.g., condoms) must be used for the next 48 hours.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological interactions:} Concomitant use of enzyme-inducing medications (such as rifampicin, phenytoin, carbamazepine, or St John's wort) reduces efficacy, necessitating alternative contraceptive methods.
    \item \textbf{Non-pharmacological counselling:} Reassurance regarding the high likelihood of irregular bleeding pattern changes and emphasizing that the mini pill does not protect against STIs.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Contraceptive failure:} Resulting in unplanned pregnancy, with a typical-use failure rate of approximately 9\%.
    \item \textbf{Ectopic pregnancy:} If pregnancy occurs, there is a proportionally higher risk that it will be ectopic, particularly with traditional mini pills.
    \item \textbf{Functional ovarian cysts:} The pill can lead to the development of benign fluid-filled ovarian cysts, which usually resolve spontaneously but may cause pelvic pain.
    \item \textbf{Persistent heavy or irregular bleeding:} May lead to anaemia or discontinuation of the contraceptive method.
    \item \textbf{Exacerbation of mood disorders:} Depression or mood disturbances can worsen in predisposed individuals, requiring therapy modification.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for women using the mini pill is excellent, with high contraceptive efficacy when taken correctly (perfect-use failure rate of less than 1\%). The return of fertility is immediate upon discontinuation of the medication, with normal ovulatory cycles resuming within a few weeks. Menstrual side effects, such as irregular spotting, generally improve or stabilise after three to six months of continuous use, and the method has no adverse long-term effects on future reproductive health.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Compliance strategies:} Incorporating pill-taking into a daily routine, such as brushing teeth, or using mobile alarms to ensure consistent timing.
    \item \textbf{Emergency supply management:} Keeping a spare packet of pills and keeping track of expiry dates to avoid running out.
    \item \textbf{Barrier protection:} Using condoms alongside the mini pill to prevent sexually transmitted infections.
    \item \textbf{Self-monitoring:} Keeping a menstrual diary to track bleeding patterns and help clinicians assess ongoing suitability.
    \item \textbf{Bowel upset protocol:} Understanding that severe vomiting or diarrhoea within 2 to 3 hours of taking a pill acts as a missed pill, requiring barrier precautions.
\end{itemize}
