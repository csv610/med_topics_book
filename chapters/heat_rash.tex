\chapter{Heat Rash}
\index{Heat Rash}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Heat rash, also known as prickly heat or miliaria, is a skin condition caused by blockage of the tiny sweat ducts in the skin. When sweat cannot escape to the surface, it is trapped beneath the skin, causing small, itchy red bumps or blisters. Heat rash is most common in hot, humid weather, in babies and young children, and in people who sweat heavily or wear clothing that traps heat. It is generally harmless and clears quickly when the skin is cooled, though in rare cases blocked ducts can become infected.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Heat rash is extremely common in tropical and hot climates and during summer heatwaves. It affects people of all ages but is particularly common in newborns and infants, whose sweat ducts are immature. Up to 9\% of newborns develop heat rash. Adults in hot, humid workplaces, athletes, and people confined to bed are also at higher risk. Most cases resolve spontaneously, and severe or complicated cases are uncommon, though they occur more frequently in hot, humid environments.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blocked sweat ducts:} sweat becomes trapped under the skin when ducts are obstructed by dead skin cells, bacteria, or swelling
    \item \textbf{Heat and humidity:} heavy sweating in hot weather overwhelms the ducts
    \item \textbf{Clothing:} tight, synthetic, or heavy clothing traps heat and sweat against the skin
    \item \textbf{Infant skin:} immature sweat ducts make babies particularly susceptible
    \item \textbf{Prolonged bed rest:} constant pressure and heat under blankets
    \item \textbf{Types:} miliaria crystallina (clear blisters), miliaria rubra (red, itchy bumps or prickly heat), and miliaria profunda (deeper, larger lesions)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Small, raised, red bumps or clear blisters on the skin
    \item Itching or a prickling, stinging sensation, giving rise to the name "prickly heat"
    \item Common sites: neck, chest, back, armpits, groin, and skin creases
    \item In babies, rash often appears on the neck, shoulders, and chest
    \item Symptoms worsen with heat, sweating, or tight clothing
    \item Bumps usually appear within days of exposure to heat and settle with cooling
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Allergic rashes, including contact dermatitis and eczema, which cause similar itchy bumps
    \item Folliculitis and bacterial skin infections, which may need antibiotic treatment
    \item Viral rashes and childhood exanthems
    \item Fungal infections such as candidiasis, especially in skin creases
    \item Hives (urticaria), which are typically larger and rapidly changing
    \item The distribution, timing, and response to cooling help distinguish heat rash from these conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Medical advice is usually unnecessary for heat rash, which settles with cooling. See a doctor if the rash is severe, spreads, becomes painful, develops pus, or does not improve within a few days, or if the affected person has a fever or is unwell.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A rash with high fever, confusion, or drowsiness (possible heat illness or serious infection)
    \item Signs of heat stroke: hot, dry skin, confusion, seizures, or collapse
    \item Difficulty breathing or signs of a severe allergic reaction
    \item A baby with a rash who is lethargic, not feeding, or unwell
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} heat rash is diagnosed by its typical appearance and distribution
    \item \textbf{History:} recent heat exposure, sweating, clothing, and any fever help confirm the diagnosis
    \item \textbf{No tests are usually needed:} investigations are reserved for suspected infection or other diagnoses
    \item \textbf{Skin swabs:} taken if secondary bacterial infection is suspected
    \item \textbf{Assessment for heat illness:} temperature and hydration status checked in hot conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cooling:} move to a cooler, air-conditioned, or shaded environment
    \item \textbf{Skin care:} keep the skin dry, wear loose, light, breathable cotton clothing, and avoid heavy ointments
    \item \textbf{Cool baths or compresses:} soothe itching and reduce inflammation
    \item \textbf{Calamine lotion and mild hydrocortisone:} relieve itching for short periods
    \item \textbf{Antihistamines:} may help with itch, particularly at night
    \item \textbf{Avoid further sweating:} rest in a cool place and avoid exercise and hot environments until the rash clears
    \item \textbf{Treatment of infection:} antibiotics or antifungals if the rash becomes infected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Secondary bacterial infection of broken, scratched skin, causing pain, pus, and fever
    \item In severe, widespread miliaria profunda, impaired sweating can reduce heat tolerance and predispose to heat illness
    \item Scratching can cause scarring and hyperpigmentation
    \item Heat rash can be confused with more serious conditions, delaying appropriate treatment
    \item Recurrence with every exposure to heat and humidity is common in susceptible people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Heat rash almost always resolves completely once the skin is cooled and kept dry, usually within a few days. Crystalline miliaria clears within 24 hours; prickly heat settles over several days as the ducts recover. Complications are uncommon, and recurrence is the main issue for people repeatedly exposed to hot, humid conditions. With simple preventive measures, most people avoid significant problems.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Stay in cool, air-conditioned or shaded environments during hot weather
    \item Wear loose, light-coloured, breathable cotton clothing
    \item Avoid tight, synthetic fabrics and heavy occlusive ointments and creams
    \item Shower after sweating and dry skin gently, particularly in skin creases
    \item Keep babies cool and lightly dressed in hot weather
    \item Take cool baths or showers and use fans in humid conditions
    \item Avoid vigorous activity during the hottest part of the day
    \item Drink plenty of fluids and stay hydrated
\end{itemize}
