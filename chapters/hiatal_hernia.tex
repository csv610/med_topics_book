\chapter{Hiatal Hernia}
\index{Hiatal Hernia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A hiatal hernia occurs when part of the stomach pushes up through the diaphragm into the chest cavity through the hiatus, the opening through which the oesophagus passes. It is a very common condition, particularly in older adults, and most people with one have no symptoms. When symptoms occur, they are those of gastro-oesophageal reflux: heartburn, regurgitation, and chest discomfort. Most hiatal hernias are managed effectively with lifestyle measures and acid-suppressing medication, and surgery is reserved for severe cases or complications.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hiatal hernias are extremely common and increase with age, found in up to half of people over 60 and in a majority of those over 70. They are more common in women, in people who are overweight, and during and after pregnancy. Most are small "sliding" hernias that cause little or no trouble. The condition is often diagnosed incidentally during endoscopy or imaging performed for other reasons. Only a minority of people with hiatal hernias need any treatment beyond managing reflux symptoms.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sliding hiatal hernia:} the junction of the stomach and oesophagus and part of the stomach slide up through the hiatus; the most common type
    \item \textbf{Para-oesophageal hernia:} part of the stomach herniates beside the oesophagus while the junction stays in place; less common but higher risk of complications
    \item \textbf{Causes:} age-related weakening of the diaphragm, increased abdominal pressure from obesity, pregnancy, lifting, straining, and chronic cough
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} displacement of the junction disrupts the anti-reflux barrier, allowing stomach acid into the oesophagus
    \item \textbf{Complications:} the herniated stomach can twist (volvulus) or strangulate, though this is rare
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Most people have no symptoms
    \item Heartburn and regurgitation, worse after meals and when lying down
    \item Chest pain or discomfort that may be mistaken for heart pain
    \item Difficulty swallowing, a feeling of food sticking, or a lump in the throat
    \item Belching, bloating, and a sour taste
    \item Chronic cough, hoarseness, and recurrent aspiration
    \item Large para-oesophageal hernias may cause chest pain, breathlessness, and fullness after eating
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease without a hernia, which is managed similarly
    \item Cardiac chest pain, which must always be considered
    \item Other causes of dysphagia: strictures, cancer, and motility disorders
    \item Gallbladder disease and peptic ulcer disease
    \item Lung conditions causing cough and breathlessness
    \item Endoscopy and imaging confirm the diagnosis and exclude alternatives
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for persistent reflux symptoms, difficulty swallowing, or chest discomfort. Urgent care is needed for severe pain, vomiting, or the inability to swallow, which may indicate strangulation.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe chest or upper abdominal pain, especially with vomiting
    \item Inability to swallow, severe retching, or vomiting blood
    \item Signs of strangulation: severe pain, fever, and collapse
    \item Chest pain with sweating, breathlessness, or radiation to the arm or jaw
    \item Black, tarry stools or vomiting blood
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Endoscopy (gastroscopy):} visualises the hernia and any oesophagitis
    \item \textbf{Barium swallow:} demonstrates the position of the stomach on X-ray
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} may show an air-fluid level behind the heart in large hernias
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} for large or complex hernias and preoperative planning
    \item \textbf{pH monitoring and manometry:} assess reflux severity and oesophageal function before surgery
    \item \textbf{Cardiac assessment:} for atypical chest pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} weight loss, smaller meals, avoiding lying down after eating, and elevating the head of the bed
    \item \textbf{Acid suppression:} proton pump inhibitors or H2 blockers control reflux symptoms
    \item \textbf{Avoid triggers:} fatty foods, alcohol, caffeine, and smoking worsen reflux
    \item \textbf{Observation:} asymptomatic hernias need no treatment
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} laparoscopic fundoplication, which returns the stomach to the abdomen and reinforces the sphincter
    \item \textbf{Emergency surgery:} for strangulation or volvulus
    \item \textbf{Indications for surgery:} severe symptoms despite medication, large para-oesophageal hernias, and complications
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Oesophagitis, strictures, and Barrett's oesophagus from long-standing reflux
    \item Para-oesophageal hernia complications: gastric volvulus and strangulation, which are surgical emergencies
    \item Aspiration pneumonia from reflux into the airways
    \item Iron-deficiency anaemia from chronic bleeding at the hernia site
    \item Symptoms confused with cardiac or lung disease, delaying diagnosis
    \item Surgical complications and symptom recurrence after repair
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most hiatal hernias cause no problems and require no treatment. When reflux symptoms occur, lifestyle measures and acid suppression control them in the great majority of people. Surgery is effective for those with severe or complicated disease, with most people free of symptoms for many years. The rare complications of volvulus and strangulation are life-threatening if untreated but are treated successfully when recognised promptly. Overall, the outlook is excellent.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and avoid heavy lifting and straining
    \item Eat smaller meals and avoid lying down within 2--3 hours of eating
    \item Elevate the head of the bed for night-time reflux
    \item Avoid alcohol, caffeine, spicy and fatty foods, and smoking
    \item Take acid-suppressing medication as prescribed
    \item See a doctor promptly for difficulty swallowing or new chest symptoms
    \item Attend follow-up endoscopy as recommended
    \item Seek urgent care for severe pain, vomiting, or inability to swallow
\end{itemize}
