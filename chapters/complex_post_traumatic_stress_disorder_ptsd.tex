\section*{Definition and Overview}
Complex post-traumatic stress disorder (complex PTSD, or CPTSD) is a mental health condition that can develop after repeated, prolonged, or inescapable exposure to extremely threatening or horrific events. It is commonly seen in survivors of childhood abuse, domestic violence, captivity, torture, or repeated sexual violence. Unlike simple PTSD, which usually follows a single traumatic event, complex PTSD also involves lasting problems with emotional control, a damaged sense of self, and difficulty trusting others. It is recognised as a separate diagnosis in the ICD-11 classification and shares many features with PTSD, but is generally more severe and harder to treat.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Post-traumatic stress disorder affects roughly 1 in 20 adults at some point in life, and complex PTSD is a substantial subset of these cases. It is more common in women and in people who experienced abuse or violence in childhood. Survivors of prolonged interpersonal violence, war, and institutional care are at particularly high risk. Because the condition often goes unrecognised, the true number of affected people is likely higher than official figures suggest.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Complex PTSD arises from the brain's response to prolonged trauma, often during a person's developmental years:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Repeated trauma:} events that are inescapable, long-lasting, or occur at the hands of a caregiver or partner are the strongest triggers
    \item \textbf{Disrupted development:} trauma in childhood can affect how the brain matures, especially regions involved in fear, memory, and emotion
    \item \textbf{Chronic stress response:} the body's threat system stays switched on, producing high levels of stress hormones that sensitise the brain to danger
    \item \textbf{Learned responses:} the person may come to see the world as unsafe and themselves as helpless or worthless
    \item \textbf{Dissociation:} the mind may learn to numb or detach from overwhelming experiences as a protective strategy
    \item \textbf{Protective and vulnerability factors:} later supportive relationships, and the absence of further harm, influence whether symptoms settle or persist
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
People with complex PTSD have the core symptoms of PTSD plus additional, long-standing difficulties:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Re-experiencing:} flashbacks, nightmares, and intrusive memories of the trauma
    \item \textbf{Avoidance:} steering clear of people, places, or reminders connected with the trauma
    \item \textbf{Hyperarousal:} being constantly on edge, easily startled, irritable, and having trouble sleeping
    \item \textbf{Emotional dysregulation:} intense, rapidly shifting moods, anger outbursts, or feeling emotionally numb
    \item \textbf{Negative self-concept:} persistent shame, guilt, or feeling worthless, defeated, or fundamentally different from others
    \item \textbf{Relationship difficulties:} difficulty trusting, feeling disconnected from others, or repeated patterns of troubled relationships
    \item \textbf{Dissociation:} feeling detached from the body, memory lapses, or a sense of unreality
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Simple post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) without the additional emotion and self-concept problems
    \item Major depression and other mood disorders
    \item Borderline personality disorder, which shares emotional instability and relationship difficulties
    \item Anxiety disorders, panic disorder, and dissociative disorders
    \item Psychotic disorders, when hallucinations or suspiciousness are present
    \item Substance use disorders, which can mimic or worsen symptoms
    \item Adjustment disorder, burnout, or normal grief after a difficult event
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek help from a GP or mental health professional if symptoms last longer than about four weeks, cause significant distress, or interfere with work, study, or relationships. A GP can arrange a mental health treatment plan and referral to a psychologist or psychiatrist experienced in trauma.

\textbf{Contact local emergency services or go to an emergency department immediately if there are thoughts of suicide or self-harm.} You can also contact a local crisis service. Severe flashbacks, dissociation that puts the person at risk, or an inability to keep the person safe all warrant urgent care.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
There is no blood test or scan for complex PTSD; it is diagnosed through careful clinical assessment:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical interview:} a detailed history of the trauma, current symptoms, and their impact on daily life
    \item \textbf{Assessment of co-morbidities:} screening for depression, anxiety, substance use, and dissociation
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} checking for suicidal thoughts, self-harm, and risk to others
    \item \textbf{Screening tools:} validated questionnaires such as the PCL-5 for PTSD symptoms, with clinical judgement about the complex features
    \item \textbf{Physical review:} a medical check to identify physical health problems that commonly accompany chronic stress and trauma
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Complex PTSD usually responds best to a structured, phase-based approach delivered by experienced clinicians:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stabilisation first:} developing safety plans, coping skills, and grounding techniques before trauma-focused work begins
    \item \textbf{Trauma-focused therapy:} trauma-focused cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT) or eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing (EMDR), delivered at a pace the person can manage
    \item \textbf{Medication:} antidepressants such as SSRIs may help with mood, anxiety, and sleep, prescribed and monitored by a doctor
    \item \textbf{Skills work:} help with managing anger, mood swings, and relationship patterns
    \item \textbf{Psychoeducation:} understanding the effects of trauma on the brain and body
    \item \textbf{Peer support and family involvement:} support groups and involving trusted family members where appropriate
    \item \textbf{Acute care:} crisis services or hospital admission if the person is at immediate risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Suicide attempts and completed suicide -- the most serious complication
    \item Substance use as a way of numbing distressing memories
    \item Chronic depression and anxiety
    \item Relationship breakdown, isolation, and social withdrawal
    \item Work and study impairment, unemployment, and financial stress
    \item Physical health problems linked to long-term stress, including heart and digestive conditions
    \item Repeated exposure to further harm or revictimisation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Many people with complex PTSD improve substantially with specialist trauma-focused treatment, although recovery is often gradual and can take months to years. Some people continue to have residual symptoms, especially during stress, but with treatment these are usually less intense and less disabling. Early recognition, a stable and safe environment, and good social support improve the outlook. Without treatment, symptoms may persist for many years.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek safety and practical support after a traumatic event as early as possible
    \item Establish routines for sleep, eating, and exercise to help stabilise the nervous system
    \item Learn and practise grounding techniques to manage flashbacks and dissociation
    \item Avoid using alcohol or drugs to cope with memories
    \item Build a small network of trusted people who understand the condition
    \item Engage in therapy consistently, and attend follow-up appointments
    \item Learn to recognise early warning signs of worsening symptoms and have a crisis plan in place
\end{itemize}
