\chapter{Echocardiogram}
\index{Echocardiogram}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An echocardiogram is an ultrasound scan of the heart that uses sound waves to create moving pictures of the heart's structure and function. It is sometimes called a cardiac ultrasound or "echo." The test allows doctors to see the heart's chambers and valves, measure how well the heart pumps, and detect conditions such as heart failure, valve disease, and damage from heart attacks. Echocardiography is safe, painless, and uses no radiation, and it is one of the most important tests in cardiology.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Echocardiography is performed many thousands of times each year in many countries for the investigation of heart disease, which remains the leading cause of death and disability. The test is used across all ages, from newborn babies with congenital heart conditions to older adults with heart failure and valve disease. Advances in portable ultrasound have also brought echo into general practice and emergency departments.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Ultrasound imaging of the heart works on the same principles as ultrasound used elsewhere in the body.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sound waves:} a handheld probe placed on the chest transmits high-frequency sound waves that reflect off the heart's structures
    \item \textbf{The image:} the returning echoes are converted into real-time moving images on a screen
    \item \textbf{Doppler:} special techniques measure the direction and speed of blood flow, detecting leaks or narrowings in the valves
    \item \textbf{What it shows:} chamber size and wall thickness, pumping strength, valve structure and function, blood flow, and fluid around the heart
    \item \textbf{Ejection fraction:} the percentage of blood pumped out of the main pumping chamber with each beat, a key measure of heart function
    \item \textbf{Types:} transthoracic echo (through the chest) is most common; transoesophageal echo uses a probe in the oesophagus for clearer images; stress echo records images during exercise or medication
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
An echocardiogram is requested for many reasons.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Breathlessness, fatigue, or swelling of the ankles, which may indicate heart failure
    \item Heart murmurs and suspected valve disease
    \item Chest pain, particularly after a heart attack
    \item Palpitations and abnormal heart rhythms
    \item Fainting episodes of unknown cause
    \item Monitoring of known heart conditions over time
    \item Checking heart function before chemotherapy or other treatments
    \item Investigation of congenital heart disease, including in newborns
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The echo is a diagnostic tool that answers specific questions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A normal echocardiogram does not exclude all heart problems, including coronary artery disease without heart failure
    \item Some conditions, such as abnormal rhythms, may require other tests alongside the echo
    \item Image quality varies with body size and build
    \item Results are interpreted alongside symptoms, examination, and other investigations
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
An echocardiogram is arranged by a doctor based on clinical assessment. Anyone with breathlessness, chest pain, palpitations, or fainting should seek medical advice, and those with known heart disease should attend scheduled reviews and scans.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe chest pain or pressure, especially with sweating, breathlessness, or nausea
    \item Sudden severe breathlessness or inability to breathe comfortably
    \item Fainting or collapse
    \item Rapid or irregular heartbeat with dizziness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Undergoing an echocardiogram is straightforward.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Preparation:} no special preparation is needed for a standard transthoracic echo; loose clothing is helpful
    \item \textbf{The test:} the person lies on their side while a sonographer moves a gel-covered probe over the chest; the test takes 20 to 40 minutes
    \item \textbf{Transoesophageal echo:} a thin probe is passed into the oesophagus under sedation for more detailed views
    \item \textbf{Stress echo:} images are taken at rest and after exercise or medication
    \item \textbf{Reporting:} a cardiologist reports the results, which are discussed with the patient and their doctor
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management follows from the findings of the echocardiogram.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal findings:} reassurance, with further investigation only if symptoms persist
    \item \textbf{Heart failure:} medicines, lifestyle measures, and monitoring
    \item \textbf{Valve disease:} surveillance, medicines, or valve repair or replacement depending on severity
    \item \textbf{After heart attack:} assessment of pumping function to guide treatment
    \item \textbf{Congenital conditions:} specialist cardiac care from childhood
    \item \textbf{Repeat studies:} regular echo to monitor conditions over time
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The test is very safe, with no radiation and no significant complications from the standard procedure
    \item Transoesophageal echo carries small risks from sedation and the probe, which are explained beforehand
    \item Stress echo carries the small risks of any exercise test, and is performed with monitoring
    \item The main limitation is that results depend on image quality and the skill of the operator
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The echocardiogram is a cornerstone of cardiac diagnosis and monitoring. It provides rapid, detailed information that guides treatment of heart failure, valve disease, and other conditions, improving outcomes across all ages. For many people, a normal echocardiogram provides valuable reassurance.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend cardiac checks if you have risk factors such as high blood pressure, diabetes, or a family history of heart disease
    \item Follow up on breathlessness, chest pain, or palpitations rather than waiting
    \item Bring a list of your medicines and previous results to appointments
    \item Keep a record of your ejection fraction and valve findings for future reference
    \item Follow your doctor's advice on heart-healthy living
\end{itemize}
