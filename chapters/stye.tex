\section*{Definition and Overview}
A stye, also called a hordeolum, is a small, painful lump that forms on the edge of the eyelid when a gland at the base of an eyelash becomes infected. It is one of the most common eye problems, and nearly everyone will experience one at some point. Styes are usually caused by bacteria, most commonly staphylococci, which live harmlessly on the skin but can enter a tiny gland opening and cause a localised infection. The result is a red, tender swelling that fills with pus, resembling a small pimple on the eyelid. A stye is different from a chalazion, which is a blocked, non-infected gland that forms a harder, usually painless lump further back on the lid. Most styes are minor, self-limiting problems that settle within a week or two with simple home care, particularly warm compresses. Occasionally they recur, spread, or become a more serious infection requiring medical treatment. This chapter explains the types of styes, what causes them, how to treat them safely at home, when to see a doctor, and how to prevent them. A key message is that styes should never be squeezed, like a pimple, because this can spread infection.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Styes are extremely common and affect people of all ages, from young children to older adults. They are slightly more common in adults than children, and they occur with equal frequency in men and women. Most people have one at some point, and a significant minority experience recurrent styes. They are more common in people who rub their eyes, wear contact lenses, have blepharitis (chronic inflammation of the eyelid margins), have oily skin, or have conditions that lower resistance to skin bacteria, such as diabetes. Poor hand hygiene and sharing towels or eye makeup can contribute, particularly in households and childcare settings. Because most styes resolve at home without a medical visit, true numbers are hard to measure, but they are among the most frequent reasons for an eye complaint in general practice. In many countries, styes are seen year-round with no strong seasonal pattern, and they do not spread from person to person like a cold, though the bacteria responsible can pass on shared items.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
A stye forms when bacteria enter a gland at the base of an eyelash and set up a small abscess. The eyelid is lined with tiny glands, the Zeis glands attached to each lash follicle and the deeper meibomian glands that produce the oily layer of tears. When a duct becomes blocked by debris, dried oil, or inflammation, bacteria trapped inside multiply and the body's immune response produces the redness, swelling, and pus that characterise the infection.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bacterial infection:} \textit{Staphylococcus aureus} causes most styes, entering through a gland opening at the lash base
    \item \textbf{Blocked glands:} clogged meibomian or Zeis glands trap bacteria and prevent drainage
    \item \textbf{Blepharitis:} chronic lid-margin inflammation thickens gland secretions, making blockages and styes more likely
    \item \textbf{Eye rubbing:} transferring bacteria from hands to the eye and irritating the lash follicles
    \item \textbf{Shared items:} sharing towels, face cloths, pillowcases, or eye makeup spreads the bacteria
    \item \textbf{Makeup and hygiene:} old or contaminated eye cosmetics, and leaving mascara or liner on overnight, block glands
    \item \textbf{Contact lens use:} handling lenses with unwashed hands or poor hygiene practices raises risk
    \item \textbf{Systemic factors:} diabetes, immune suppression, and skin conditions such as seborrhoeic dermatitis predispose to recurrent infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
A stye usually announces itself quickly, over a day or two, with a tender swelling at the eyelid margin.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Painful red lump:} a small, tender bump on the edge of the eyelid, often with a visible yellow point of pus
    \item \textbf{Swelling of the lid:} the eyelid around the lump may puff up, sometimes enough to narrow the eye
    \item \textbf{Discomfort and soreness:} a gritty, tender feeling, worse when blinking
    \item \textbf{Watering:} increased tearing of the affected eye
    \item \textbf{Light sensitivity:} mild photophobia can occur but is not typical
    \item \textbf{Pus drainage:} the stye may burst spontaneously after a few days, releasing pus and relieving the pain
    \item \textbf{Sensation of something in the eye:} a foreign-body feeling while the lid is swollen
    \item \textbf{Recurrent crops:} some people develop multiple styes, or repeated styes in the same eye over months
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other eyelid lumps and inflammations can be mistaken for a stye, and distinguishing them guides treatment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chalazion:} a painless, firm lump from a blocked meibomian gland, with no infection; grows slowly and does not form a pointed pus head
    \item \textbf{Blepharitis:} diffuse redness, crusting, and itching of the whole eyelid margin, often with recurrent styes and chalazia
    \item \textbf{Preseptal cellulitis:} infection of the tissue in front of the eye causing widespread lid swelling, redness, and fever, needing urgent antibiotic treatment
    \item \textbf{Orbital cellulitis:} a serious infection behind the eye with proptosis, painful eye movement, reduced vision, and fever; an emergency
    \item \textbf{Basal cell or sebaceous gland carcinoma:} rare eyelid tumours can look like a persistent, non-healing "stye"; any lump lasting more than a few weeks should be checked
    \item \textbf{Conjunctivitis:} red, watery, sticky eyes without a discrete lid lump
    \item \textbf{Molluscum contagiosum:} small dome-shaped skin bumps that can occur on the lid but are not tender or pus-filled
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most styes can be managed at home with warm compresses and will resolve on their own. Medical advice is appropriate if the lump has not begun to improve within a week, if it grows rapidly, if vision is affected, if the whole eyelid becomes very swollen, or if styes keep coming back. People with diabetes, weakened immune systems, or a history of recurrent eye infections should have persistent or frequent styes assessed. An optometrist or general practitioner can examine the eye, and occasionally the stye may need to be drained or treated with antibiotic ointment. It is important to never squeeze a stye, and to see a doctor promptly if a stye seems to be spreading.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe swelling of the eyelids and the surrounding face, especially with fever
    \item The eye itself protruding, painful on movement, or vision that is blurred or lost
    \item Rapidly worsening redness and pain spreading from the eye
    \item Confusion, high fever, or feeling very unwell, which can indicate a serious infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A stye is usually diagnosed simply by looking at it, and no tests are needed in the great majority of cases.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} the doctor or optometrist inspects the lid margin, identifies the lump, and distinguishes a stye from a chalazion
    \item \textbf{Slit-lamp examination:} a magnifying microscope is used to assess the lump and check the front of the eye
    \item \textbf{Visual acuity:} vision is checked if there is any concern that the eye itself is affected
    \item \textbf{Swabs and cultures:} rarely, the draining pus is cultured if infection is severe, recurrent, or not responding to treatment
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} occasionally ordered to check for diabetes or immune problems in people with very frequent or severe styes
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} if a lump persists for weeks or has unusual features, a small sample may be taken to exclude other conditions, including rare lid tumours
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Most styes are treated entirely at home, with simple measures that speed healing and relieve pain.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Warm compresses:} a clean cloth soaked in warm water is held against the closed eye for five to ten minutes, three to four times a day; the heat encourages the gland to drain
    \item \textbf{Gentle massage:} after a compress, gently massage the lump toward the lash line to encourage drainage, without squeezing
    \item \textbf{Lid hygiene:} cleaning the lid margin with diluted baby shampoo or a commercial lid scrub removes crusts and reduces further blockage
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} paracetamol or ibuprofen can be taken for discomfort
    \item \textbf{Antibiotic ointment:} a doctor may prescribe an antibiotic eye ointment or drops if there are signs of spreading infection or frequent recurrence
    \item \textbf{Incision and drainage:} a large or persistent stye that does not drain may be lanced by an eye care professional under local anaesthetic; this is a quick, minor procedure
    \item \textbf{Avoid squeezing:} pressing a stye can push infection into deeper tissues and cause orbital cellulitis, so the lump must be left alone
    \item \textbf{Stopping makeup and contacts:} avoid eye makeup and contact lenses until the stye has resolved
    \item \textbf{Treating blepharitis:} if recurrent styes are due to blepharitis, a regular warm-compress and lid-scrub routine is the mainstay of prevention
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Styes are minor, but they can occasionally cause problems, especially if squeezed or ignored.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spread of infection:} squeezing or rubbing can push bacteria into the surrounding lid, causing preseptal or, rarely, orbital cellulitis, a serious infection that needs hospital care
    \item \textbf{Recurrence:} some people get styes repeatedly, often in association with blepharitis, requiring ongoing lid hygiene
    \item \textbf{Chalazion formation:} a stye can leave a blocked gland that turns into a firm, chronic chalazion needing drainage or steroid injection
    \item \textbf{Corneal irritation:} prolonged lid swelling can rub on the cornea, causing irritation and tearing
    \item \textbf{Scarring:} severe or repeatedly squeezed styes can leave a small scar on the lid margin
    \item \textbf{Missed diagnosis:} a persistent "stye" can rarely turn out to be a tumour, which is why lumps that do not settle should be checked
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The great majority of styes resolve completely within about a week, with or without treatment, and leave no lasting effect on vision or eye health. Warm compresses speed the process by helping the gland drain naturally, and once the pus is released the pain settles quickly. Recurrences are common, especially in people with blepharitis, but a consistent routine of lid hygiene and prompt treatment of new lumps usually keeps them under control. Complications are very rare when the stye is left alone and simple home care is used. For the small number of styes that persist, professional drainage or antibiotic treatment is quick and effective. The outlook for people with recurrent styes is good, since the underlying blepharitis or hygiene issue can be managed, and with attention to hand washing and eye makeup hygiene most people can reduce the frequency of episodes substantially. Styes do not affect long-term eyesight, and normal activities can be resumed as soon as the eye is comfortable.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wash hands thoroughly before touching the eyes or face, and never rub the eyes with dirty hands
    \item Remove all eye makeup every night, and replace mascara and eyeliner every three to six months
    \item Never share towels, face cloths, or eye makeup with other people
    \item Clean contact lenses properly and handle them with dry, washed hands
    \item Practise lid hygiene with warm compresses and gentle lid scrubs if you are prone to styes or have blepharitis
    \item Treat the first sign of a stye promptly with warm compresses, several times a day
    \item Avoid squeezing the stye, and avoid wearing makeup or contact lenses until it has resolved
    \item See a doctor or optometrist if a lump lasts more than a week, recurs frequently, or is accompanied by vision changes
\end{itemize}
