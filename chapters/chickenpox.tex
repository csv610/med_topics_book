\chapter{Chickenpox}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Chickenpox is a highly contagious viral infection caused by the varicella-zoster virus, the same virus that later causes shingles. It produces an intensely itchy, blistering rash together with fever and a general feeling of being unwell. In healthy children it is usually a mild, self-limiting illness, but it can be severe in infants, pregnant women, and people with weakened immune systems. Once the primary infection resolves, the virus remains dormant in nerve cells and can reactivate years later as shingles. Vaccination has made chickenpox much less common in countries where it is routinely offered.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Before widespread vaccination, nearly everyone contracted chickenpox, usually in childhood, and it was one of the most common infectious diseases worldwide. In countries without routine vaccination it remains extremely common, with most cases occurring in children under ten years of age and outbreaks peaking in late winter and early spring. Where the varicella vaccine is part of the childhood immunisation schedule, the number of cases, hospitalisations, and deaths has fallen dramatically. Adults who never had chickenpox and were never vaccinated remain susceptible, and primary infection in adulthood is generally more severe than in childhood.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\medskip
\textbf{Transmission.} The varicella-zoster virus spreads through the air when an infected person coughs or sneezes, or by direct contact with the fluid within the blisters:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Entry and spread:} the virus enters through the respiratory tract, multiplies locally, and then spreads through the bloodstream to the skin, producing the characteristic rash
    \item \textbf{Latency:} after the illness resolves, the virus retreats into sensory nerve cells near the spine, where it remains dormant for life and may reactivate years later as shingles
    \item \textbf{Infectious period:} a person is contagious from about two days before the rash appears until all the blisters have crusted over
    \item \textbf{Secondary attack rate:} over 90\% of susceptible household contacts develop the infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever, tiredness, headache, and feeling generally unwell for a day or two before the rash appears
    \item A rash of small red spots that quickly develop into fluid-filled blisters
    \item Intense itching
    \item The rash usually begins on the face, scalp, and trunk before spreading to the limbs
    \item Crops of spots at different stages of development, with new spots continuing to appear for several days
    \item Blisters dry out and form crusts, which fall off within about a week
    \item Sores may also appear inside the mouth, throat, and on the genitals
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hand, foot, and mouth disease -- a different viral infection with blisters on the hands, feet, and mouth
    \item Impetigo -- a bacterial skin infection with crusty, honey-coloured sores
    \item Shingles -- a localised blistering rash that occurs in people who have previously had chickenpox
    \item Molluscum contagiosum -- small pearly bumps that occur without fever
    \item Measles -- a rash of flat red spots accompanied by cough, runny nose, and red eyes
    \item Allergic skin reactions, drug rashes, and insect bites
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor promptly if a pregnant woman, a newborn, or someone with a weakened immune system is exposed to, or develops, chickenpox, since they may need specific treatment or protection.

\medskip
\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing or chest pain
    \item Confusion, severe drowsiness, or a seizure
    \item A rash that becomes very red, hot, painful, or begins to ooze pus
    \item High fever that persists beyond the first few days
    \item Severe headache, neck stiffness, or sensitivity to light
    \item Persistent vomiting or an inability to drink fluids
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical diagnosis:} the rash is usually so characteristic that no tests are required
    \item \textbf{History:} contact with a known case, and the person's vaccination status
    \item \textbf{Swab testing (PCR):} a swab of a blister can confirm the virus when the diagnosis is uncertain
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} antibody testing may confirm past infection or immunity, and is sometimes used in pregnancy or in people with weakened immunity
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} if pneumonia is suspected
    \item \textbf{Neurological assessment:} including lumbar puncture in rare cases where brain or spinal cord involvement is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Self-care:} rest, plenty of fluids, and paracetamol for fever in the recommended dose
    \item \textbf{Itch relief:} cool baths, calamine lotion, loose clothing, and keeping nails short
    \item \textbf{Antivirals:} medicines such as aciclovir may be prescribed for adults, pregnant women, and people with weakened immunity to shorten the illness
    \item \textbf{Avoid aspirin:} aspirin should never be given to children or teenagers with chickenpox, because it is linked to Reye's syndrome, a rare but serious liver and brain condition
    \item \textbf{Secondary infection:} antibiotics if a blister becomes infected with bacteria
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} for severe disease, particularly in people with weakened immune systems or serious complications
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Secondary bacterial skin infection from scratching, which can sometimes be severe
    \item Pneumonia, which is more common in adults, smokers, and pregnant women
    \item Inflammation of the brain (encephalitis) or of the membranes around the brain
    \item Infection of a newborn if the mother develops chickenpox around the time of birth
    \item Congenital varicella syndrome if the mother is infected in early pregnancy
    \item Shingles later in life from reactivation of the dormant virus
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
In healthy children, chickenpox is usually a mild illness that resolves within one to two weeks, with the rash crusting over in about a week. Adults and people with weakened immune systems tend to be more severely affected and are more likely to develop complications. Most people recover completely with no lasting effects, and lifelong immunity is usual after infection, although the dormant virus can later reactivate as shingles. Severe complications are uncommon where vaccination and timely medical care are available.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vaccination with the varicella vaccine is the most effective prevention and is given as part of the childhood schedule in many countries
    \item Keep children home from school or childcare until all blisters have crusted
    \item Avoid contact with pregnant women, newborns, and people with weakened immunity while infectious
    \item Wash hands regularly, especially after contact with blisters or crusts
    \item Do not share towels, clothing, or bedding during the illness
    \item People at high risk who have been exposed may be offered the vaccine or protective antibodies (immunoglobulin) within a suitable timeframe
\end{itemize}
