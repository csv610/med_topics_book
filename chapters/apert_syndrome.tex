\chapter{Apert Syndrome}
\index{Apert Syndrome}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Apert syndrome is a rare genetic condition present from birth. It is a form of \textbf{craniosynostosis} -- the premature fusion of the growth plates (sutures) of the skull -- which prevents the skull from growing normally and produces a characteristic head and face shape. It also causes webbing or fusion of the fingers and toes (\textbf{syndactyly}), typically affecting several digits on both hands and feet.

The condition results from a change in the \textit{FGFR2} gene, which normally guides the growth and development of bone and other tissues. Most cases arise from a new mutation, meaning the parents are usually unaffected. With modern multidisciplinary care, including early skull surgery, most people with Apert syndrome live normal or near-normal life spans, and many have normal intelligence.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Apert syndrome is rare, affecting roughly one in 65,000 to 88,000 births. It occurs across all ethnic groups and affects boys and girls equally. In the majority of cases there is no family history: the gene change occurs for the first time in the affected child, and the risk of such new mutations increases with older paternal age.

When a person with Apert syndrome has children, each child has a 50 per cent chance of inheriting the condition, because it follows an autosomal dominant pattern. The diagnosis is usually made at birth or in early infancy on the basis of the characteristic skull, facial, and limb features.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Apert syndrome is caused by specific changes (mutations) in the \textit{FGFR2} gene, which provides the instructions for a protein involved in signalling between cells during early development:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{New mutations:} most cases occur spontaneously, without any family history
    \item \textbf{Inheritance:} an affected parent can pass the gene change on, with a 50 per cent chance in each pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Paternal age:} the risk of a new mutation rises with the father's age at conception
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} the altered gene makes FGFR2 signalling overactive, so the skull sutures fuse too early, the facial bones fail to grow forward normally, and the fingers and toes fail to separate completely during development
    \item \textbf{Consequences:} early fusion of the skull limits brain growth and raises intracranial pressure; underdeveloped midfacial bones narrow the airway; and fused digits restrict hand and foot function
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A high, prominent forehead and a pointed, tower-shaped skull (turribrachycephaly)
    \item A flattened, underdeveloped midface with a small, beaked nose
    \item Widely spaced, prominent eyes (hypertelorism), which may not close fully, leaving the corneas exposed
    \item A high-arched or cleft palate, crowded teeth, and a relatively prominent lower jaw with age
    \item Fused or webbed fingers and toes, often forming a ``mitten'' or ``sock'' appearance, with varying degrees of fusion
    \item Raised pressure inside the skull as the brain grows against the prematurely fused bones
    \item Noisy breathing at night and obstructive sleep apnoea from narrowing of the upper airway
    \item Hearing loss and frequent ear and chest infections
    \item Difficulty feeding in infancy, and variable learning difficulties -- though many children have normal intelligence
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Crouzon syndrome:} similar skull and facial changes without fused fingers and toes
    \item \textbf{Pfeiffer syndrome:} similar facial features with different hand and foot changes
    \item \textbf{Saethre--Chotzen and Muenke syndromes:} other forms of craniosynostosis with overlapping features
    \item \textbf{Isolated craniosynostosis:} early fusion of a single suture without facial or limb involvement
    \item \textbf{Other chromosomal or genetic syndromes:} with unusual facial features and intellectual disability
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any baby with an unusual head shape, premature fusion of the skull sutures, or fused fingers and toes should be assessed by a specialist craniofacial team. Seek urgent advice for signs of raised pressure inside the skull, including persistent headaches, vomiting, a bulging or tense fontanelle in a baby, squinting, or increasing drowsiness. Children with Apert syndrome also need prompt attention for breathing difficulties, eye problems, or feeding trouble.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe breathing difficulty, especially in a baby or young child
    \item Seizures or a sudden loss of consciousness
    \item A sudden change in vision or severe headache with vomiting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} the characteristic skull, facial, and limb features usually suggest the diagnosis at birth
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} a blood test to confirm the \textit{FGFR2} gene change
    \item \textbf{Skull imaging:} CT scans to map the fused sutures, assess the shape of the skull, and check for pressure on the brain
    \item \textbf{Hand and foot X-rays:} to plan separation of the fused digits and assess the bones
    \item \textbf{Eye and hearing assessment:} examination for vision problems and repeated audiology testing
    \item \textbf{Sleep studies:} polysomnography to diagnose obstructive sleep apnoea
    \item \textbf{Prenatal diagnosis:} ultrasound and genetic testing can identify the condition before birth, particularly where there is a family history
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
A child with Apert syndrome needs a coordinated, lifelong team of specialists, usually including a craniofacial surgeon, neurosurgeon, ear, nose and throat surgeon, ophthalmologist, clinical geneticist, speech pathologist, and occupational therapist:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cranial vault surgery:} an operation in infancy to release the fused sutures, reshape the skull, and relieve pressure on the brain
    \item \textbf{Midface advancement:} surgery in childhood to bring the flattened face forward, improving breathing, eye protection, and appearance
    \item \textbf{Digit separation:} surgery to separate the fused fingers and toes and improve hand function and appearance
    \item \textbf{Airway support:} treatment of sleep apnoea, sometimes including continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) or, in severe cases, tracheostomy
    \item \textbf{Ear and dental care:} grommets for middle-ear problems, hearing aids where needed, and long-term orthodontic treatment
    \item \textbf{Developmental therapy:} speech, occupational, and physiotherapy to support feeding, communication, and independence
    \item \textbf{Genetic counselling:} for affected individuals and their families to discuss inheritance and recurrence risks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Raised intracranial pressure, which can permanently damage vision if untreated
    \item Hydrocephalus, requiring monitoring and sometimes a shunt
    \item Upper airway obstruction and obstructive sleep apnoea, with implications for growth and development
    \item Eye problems: dryness and corneal damage from incomplete eyelid closure, and optic nerve compression
    \item Recurrent ear infections and conductive or sensorineural hearing loss
    \item Dental crowding, malocclusion, and cleft palate
    \item Variable learning difficulties and developmental delay
    \item Risks of the multiple surgeries themselves, including bleeding, infection, and anaesthetic complications
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With modern coordinated treatment, the outlook for people with Apert syndrome is far better than in the past, and most now live normal or near-normal life spans. Intelligence is highly variable: many children have normal intellect and attend mainstream school, while others have learning difficulties that require additional support.

Outcome depends on how early surgery relieves pressure on the brain, how well breathing, vision, and hearing are managed, and the extent of family and educational support. Most people require ongoing specialist care throughout life, but with good support many live independent and fulfilling lives, including careers, relationships, and families.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Apert syndrome cannot be prevented, because most cases arise from a new gene change
    \item Genetic counselling helps families understand the risks of recurrence in future pregnancies
    \item Prenatal screening and diagnosis are available where there is a family history
    \item Attend all specialist appointments to monitor pressure on the brain, breathing, vision, and hearing
    \item Protect the eyes if they do not close fully, using prescribed lubricants and moisture chambers
    \item Families benefit from connecting with support groups and community services
    \item Early therapy and schooling support help children reach their full potential
\end{itemize}
