\chapter{Venereal diseases}
\index{Venereal diseases}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The term "venereal diseases" represents one of the oldest clinical classifications in medicine, derived from Venus, the Roman goddess of love and beauty. Historically used to describe infections contracted and transmitted through sexual intercourse, contemporary clinical practice has largely transitioned to the terms "sexually transmitted diseases" (STDs) and, more accurately, "sexually transmitted infections" (STIs). This shift in terminology is significant; the term "infection" acknowledges that a pathogen can occupy and replicate within a host's body without necessarily manifesting overt clinical symptoms or progressing to a diseased state. Consequently, individuals with asymptomatic sexually transmitted infections can unknowingly transmit the pathogen to their sexual partners, driving transmission dynamics within populations.

Venereal diseases encompass a broad spectrum of clinical syndromes caused by diverse microbiological agents, including bacteria, viruses, protozoa, ectoparasites, and fungi. The principal mode of transmission is through direct mucosal contact during vaginal, anal, or oral intercourse. However, several of these pathogens can also be transmitted via non-sexual routes, such as vertically from mother to child during gestation, childbirth, or breastfeeding, or through contact with contaminated blood products, needles, and syringes.

The public health significance of venereal diseases cannot be overstated. They represent a major global health burden, causing acute illness, chronic disability, severe psychosocial distress, and significant economic strain on healthcare systems worldwide. Beyond their direct clinical manifestations, STIs play a critical role in facilitating the transmission of the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) by compromising mucosal barriers and recruiting immune cells to the genital tract. Furthermore, untreated infections can lead to severe reproductive health complications, including pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), ectopic pregnancy, tubal-factor infertility, chronic pelvic pain, and adverse pregnancy outcomes such as stillbirth and neonatal death. Understanding the pathophysiology, clinical presentation, and management of these infections is therefore vital for clinicians, epidemiologists, and public health officials working to mitigate their impact.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of sexually transmitted infections is characterized by high global prevalence, persistent incidence rates, and marked disparities across different populations and demographic groups. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), more than 1 million sexually transmitted infections are acquired globally every single day. Each year, there are an estimated 374 million new infections of four curable STIs: chlamydia (129 million), gonorrhoea (82 million), syphilis (7.1 million), and trichomoniasis (156 million). Additionally, viral STIs present an immense burden; it is estimated that upwards of 500 million people aged 15--49 have a genital infection with herpes simplex virus (HSV-1 or HSV-2), and over 300 million women are infected with human papillomavirus (HPV), which is the primary aetiological agent of cervical cancer.

The distribution of venereal diseases is heavily influenced by demographic factors, behaviour, and socioeconomic determinants. Adolescents and young adults (aged 15--24 years) are disproportionately affected, representing nearly half of all newly diagnosed STIs in many jurisdictions. This elevated risk is driven by biological vulnerability (such as cervical ectopy in young females), developmental factors, and behavioral patterns, including higher rates of partner change and inconsistent barrier contraceptive use.

Other high-burden populations include men who have sex with men (MSM), transgender individuals, sex workers, and individuals residing in resource-limited settings or areas with poor access to healthcare. In MSM networks, biological factors (such as the high transmissibility of pathogens during receptive anal intercourse) combine with dense sexual networks to sustain high transmission rates of syphilis, gonorrhoea, and HIV. Syphilis, in particular, has seen a dramatic resurgence globally in recent years, affecting both MSM and heterosexual populations, leading to a highly concerning rise in congenital syphilis cases. Socioeconomic barriers, systemic discrimination, healthcare mistrust, and the persistent social stigma associated with sexual health services further compound the epidemiological challenge by deterring individuals from seeking timely screening, diagnosis, and treatment.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The aetiology of venereal diseases is diverse, involving a wide array of pathogenic microorganisms that have evolved specialized mechanisms to colonize the human urogenital tract, evade the host immune system, and facilitate transmission. The primary pathogens and their pathophysiological mechanisms include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bacterial Pathogens}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{\textit{Chlamydia trachomatis}}: An obligate intracellular bacterium that exists in two distinct forms during its life cycle: the infectious, metabolically inactive elementary body (EB) and the non-infectious, metabolically active reticulate body (RB). EBs infect mucosal epithelial cells of the urethra, cervix, rectum, or conjunctiva. Once inside, they replicate as RBs within host cell vacuoles (inclusions), ultimately lysing the host cell to release new EBs. The resulting chronic inflammatory response can lead to tissue scarring, fibrosis, and anatomical damage.
        \item \textbf{\textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae}}: A Gram-negative, facultative intracellular diplococcus that targets non-ciliated columnar epithelial cells. It utilizes pili and outer membrane proteins (such as Opa and Opc) to adhere securely to host cells. The bacterium induces its own engulfment and replicates within subepithelial tissues, triggering a robust polymorphonuclear leukocyte (neutrophil) response. This intensive inflammatory reaction is responsible for the characteristic purulent discharge associated with gonococcal infections.
        \item \textbf{\textit{Treponema pallidum}}: A highly motile, spiral-shaped bacterium (spirochete) that causes syphilis. It penetrates intact mucous membranes or microscopic dermal abrasions. Once inside, it rapidly disseminates via the lymphatic system and bloodstream long before the appearance of a primary lesion. Its outer membrane lacks lipopolysaccharide and has low immunogenicity, allowing it to evade early detection by the host immune system. The pathophysiology involves endothelial cell proliferation and perivascular infiltration (endarteritis obliterans), leading to tissue ischemia and necrosis, which manifests as the classic primary chancre and subsequent systemic lesions.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral Pathogens}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV-1 and HSV-2)}: Double-stranded DNA viruses that infect mucosal surfaces or compromised skin. Following lytic replication in epithelial cells, the virus invades sensory nerve endings and undergoes retrograde axonal transport to the dorsal root ganglia (typically the sacral ganglia for genital herpes), where it establishes lifelong latent infection. Reactivation is triggered by factors such as stress, immunosuppression, or hormonal changes, causing anterograde transport back to the mucosa and resulting in recurrent vesicular lesions or asymptomatic viral shedding.
        \item \textbf{Human Papillomavirus (HPV)}: Non-enveloped, double-stranded DNA viruses that target the basal keratinocytes of the squamous epithelium. Low-risk genotypes (e.g., HPV 6 and 11) cause benign proliferative lesions such as condyloma acuminata (genital warts). High-risk genotypes (e.g., HPV 16 and 18) integrate their viral DNA into the host genome, leading to the overexpression of the oncogenic proteins E6 and E7. These proteins bind and promote the degradation of host tumor suppressor proteins p53 and retinoblastoma protein (pRb), respectively, leading to uncontrolled cell division, genomic instability, and malignant transformation.
        \item \textbf{Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV-1 and HIV-2)}: Retroviruses that target cells expressing the CD4 receptor, primarily CD4+ T lymphocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells. HIV utilizes its envelope glycoprotein gp120 to bind to CD4 and co-receptors (CCR5 or CXCR4), facilitating viral entry and reverse transcription of its RNA genome into DNA, which integrates into the host cell genome. Active viral replication leads to cytopathic effects, immune depletion, and eventually, profound immunodeficiency (Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome or AIDS).
        \item \textbf{Hepatitis B Virus (HBV)}: A partially double-stranded DNA virus that, while primarily hepatotropic, is highly transmissible through sexual contact. The virus replicates in hepatocytes, and the resulting liver damage is mediated not by direct cytopathic effects, but by the host's cytotoxic T-cell-mediated immune response against viral antigens presented on the hepatocyte membrane.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Protozoan Pathogens}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{\textit{Trichomonas vaginalis}}: A flagellated, motile protozoan that colonizes the squamous epithelium of the female vagina and ectocervix, and the male urethra and prostate. It adheres to epithelial cells using specific adhesion proteins, releasing cytotoxic substances, pore-forming proteins, and cysteine proteases that cause cell detaching and lysis. The resulting local inflammatory response causes mucosal capillary dilation, leading to the classic clinical sign of a colpitis macularis ("strawberry cervix").
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ectoparasitic Infestations}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{\textit{Phthirus pubis} (Pubic Lice)}: Obligate parasitic insects that infest the pubic hair and feed on human blood, causing localized hypersensitivity reactions and intense pruritus due to saliva injected during feeding.
        \item \textbf{\textit{Sarcoptes scabiei} (Scabies)}: A microscopic mite that burrows into the stratum corneum of the skin, depositing eggs and faeces, which trigger a severe delayed (Type IV) hypersensitivity reaction characterized by intense itching and cutaneous lesions.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of venereal diseases varies widely depending on the causative pathogen, the site of infection, and the host's immunological status. Many infections remain entirely asymptomatic, particularly in females, which facilitates transmission and delays necessary medical intervention. When clinical features do manifest, they may present as localized, regional, or systemic signs and symptoms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urogenital Discharge and Dysuria}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item In males, urethritis presents as urethral discharge, which may be copious, purulent, and yellowish-green (classically seen in \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae}) or mild, mucoid, and clear-to-white (characteristic of \textit{Chlamydia trachomatis} or \textit{Mycoplasma genitalium}). This is typically accompanied by dysuria (a burning sensation during urination) and urethral pruritus.
        \item In females, cervicitis or vaginitis manifests as increased or altered vaginal discharge. Gonococcal and chlamydial cervicitis often produce a mucopurulent endocervical discharge, contact bleeding of the cervix (ectopy), and pelvic discomfort. Trichomoniasis classically produces a profuse, frothy, yellowish-green, malodorous vaginal discharge associated with vulvovaginal irritation, dysuria, and dyspareunia (painful intercourse).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genital Ulceration}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Primary Syphilis}: Characterized by a single, painless, indurated ulcer (chancre) at the site of inoculation (genital, anal, or oral). The chancre emerges after an incubation period of 10 to 90 days and resolves spontaneously within 3 to 6 weeks, even without treatment, leading patients to believe the infection has cleared.
        \item \textbf{Genital Herpes}: Presents as prodromal symptoms (tingling, burning, or itching) followed by the eruption of painful, grouped vesicles on an erythematous base. These vesicles rupture to form shallow, exquisitely painful ulcers that crust over and heal. Primary outbreaks are often severe and accompanied by systemic features such as fever, headache, myalgia, and painful regional (inguinal) lymphadenopathy.
        \item \textbf{Chancroid} (caused by \textit{Haemophilus ducreyi}): Rare in developed nations, it presents as one or more painful, necrotizing genital ulcers with soft, irregular borders, frequently accompanied by painful, fluctuant inguinal lymphadenitis (buboes).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cutaneous and Mucosal Lesions}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Secondary Syphilis}: Occurs weeks to months after the resolution of the primary chancre, representing systemic spirochetemia. It presents with a diffuse, non-pruritic, maculopapular rash that characteristically involves the palms of the hands and soles of the feet. Other features include mucous patches in the oral cavity or genitals, alopecia, constitutional symptoms (fever, malaise, sore throat), generalized lymphadenopathy, and condyloma lata (flat, moist, highly infectious papules in intertriginous areas).
        \item \textbf{Genital Warts (Condyloma Acuminata)}: Cauliflower-like, flesh-coloured, or hyperpigmented exophytic growths on the vulva, penis, perianal skin, or scrotum, caused by HPV infection. They are generally painless but can cause itching, bleeding, or cosmetic distress.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Systemic and Extra-genital Manifestations}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID)}: In females, upper genital tract dissemination of pathogens causes lower abdominal pain, cervical motion tenderness, adnexal tenderness, dyspareunia, and fever.
        \item \textbf{Proctitis and Pharyngitis}: Can occur following receptive anal or oral sex, presenting as rectal pain, tenesmus, discharge, or sore throat.
        \item \textbf{Systemic Viral Syndromes}: Acute HIV infection can present as a mononucleosis-like illness (fever, rash, pharyngitis, lymphadenopathy). Chronic HBV may present with jaundice, right upper quadrant pain, dark urine, and fatigue.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Accurate diagnosis of venereal diseases requires distinguishing them from other infectious and non-infectious conditions that present with similar urogenital, ulcerative, or cutaneous features.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bacterial Vaginosis (BV)}: A non-sexually transmitted dysbiosis characterized by a reduction in lactobacilli and an overgrowth of anaerobic bacteria (e.g., \textit{Gardnerella vaginalis}). It presents with a thin, homogenous, greyish-white vaginal discharge and a characteristic fishy odour, particularly after intercourse, but lacks the mucosal inflammation seen in STIs.
    \item \textbf{Vulvovaginal Candidiasis}: A fungal infection caused primarily by \textit{Candida albicans}. It presents with severe vulvar pruritus, erythema, dysuria, and a thick, white, curd-like ("cottage cheese") vaginal discharge. It is not classified as an STI, though sexual activity can sometimes trigger episodes.
    \item \textbf{Beh\c{c}et's Disease}: A systemic, immune-mediated vasculitis characterized by recurrent, painful oral and genital aphthous ulcers. It can be distinguished from herpes by the absence of vesicular stages and negative viral swabs.
    \item \textbf{Fixed Drug Eruptions}: Genital ulcerations or erythematous plaques that occur in the exact same anatomical location following the ingestion of specific systemic medications (e.g., tetracyclines, NSAIDs, sulfonamides).
    \item \textbf{Non-specific Urethritis/Cervicitis}: Irritation caused by trauma, chemical exposure (soaps, douches, spermicides), or foreign bodies, presenting with discharge and dysuria in the absence of pathogenic micro-organisms.
    \item \textbf{Contact Dermatitis}: Pruritic, erythematous lesions on the external genitalia resulting from exposure to allergens or irritants such as latex condoms, lubricants, or personal hygiene products.
    \item \textbf{Lichen Sclerosus}: A chronic inflammatory dermatosis that primarily affects the anogenital region in women, causing ivory-white plaques, skin thinning, pruritus, and architectural distortion of the vulva, which can mimic or coexist with chronic infections.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Early medical evaluation is crucial for the diagnosis and management of sexually transmitted infections to prevent permanent reproductive damage and interrupt transmission. Individuals should contact a primary care physician, sexual health clinic, or gynecologist if they notice any potential symptoms, including unusual genital discharge, unexplained pelvic or testicular pain, genital sores, or a new rash. Seeking care is also necessary if an individual is notified that a sexual partner has tested positive for an STI, even if they themselves feel completely healthy.

Certain symptoms indicate a severe, ascending infection or systemic involvement and require urgent or emergency medical evaluation. Individuals should immediately go to the nearest emergency department or call emergency services (such as \textbf{local emergency services} in many countries, \textbf{911} in the United States, or \textbf{999} in the United Kingdom) if they experience:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, acute lower abdominal or pelvic pain, especially when accompanied by a high fever, chills, nausea, or vomiting (indicative of acute pelvic inflammatory disease or ruptured tubo-ovarian abscess).
    \item Severe, sudden-onset testicular pain and swelling, which may indicate acute epididymo-orchitis or testicular torsion (a surgical emergency).
    \item High fever, rigors, rapid heart rate, or confusion accompanying genital symptoms, which suggest systemic bacteremia or sepsis (e.g., disseminated gonococcal infection).
    \item Signs of meningeal involvement, such as a severe headache, stiff neck, photophobia, or altered mental status, which can occur during acute primary HSV infection, neurosyphilis, or acute HIV seroconversion.
    \item Difficulty breathing, severe swelling, or signs of anaphylaxis if undergoing treatment for an STI (e.g., severe allergic reaction to penicillin).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of venereal diseases relies on a detailed clinical history, physical examination, and appropriate laboratory investigations. Because many STIs coexist, identifying one infection should prompt comprehensive screening for others.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical History and Physical Examination}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item History-taking must be non-judgmental, confidential, and comprehensive, covering sexual practices (vaginal, anal, oral), gender of partners, condom use, history of previous STIs, and symptoms.
        \item A thorough physical examination includes visual inspection and palpation of the external genitalia, perineum, and perianal region to look for ulcers, discharge, warts, or rashes. In females, a speculum examination is performed to visualize the vaginal walls and cervix, looking for cervical motion tenderness, discharge, or lesions. Bimanual palpation is performed to assess for adnexal or cervical tenderness. In males, the urethra is examined for discharge, and the testes and epididymides are palpated for swelling or tenderness. Inguinal lymph nodes are evaluated for enlargement and tenderness in both sexes.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Laboratory Investigations}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Nucleic Acid Amplification Tests (NAATs)}: The modern gold standard for detecting \textit{Chlamydia trachomatis}, \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae}, and \textit{Trichomonas vaginalis}. These tests detect pathogen-specific DNA or RNA and have exceptionally high sensitivity and specificity. In males, first-catch urine is the preferred specimen. In females, self-collected or clinician-collected vaginal swabs are preferred over endocervical swabs. Rectal and pharyngeal swabs should be collected based on exposure history.
        \item \textbf{Microscopic Evaluation}:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Gram stain of urethral or endocervical exudate can provide rapid presumptive diagnosis of \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae} by demonstrating Gram-negative intracellular diplococci within polymorphonuclear leukocytes.
            \item Wet mount microscopy of vaginal discharge is used to identify motile, flagellated trichomonads in trichomoniasis, as well as clue cells in bacterial vaginosis and budding yeast/pseudohyphae in candidiasis.
            \item Darkfield microscopy can directly visualize the motile spirochete \textit{Treponema pallidum} from primary or secondary lesion exudates, though it requires specialized equipment and expertise.
        \end{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Serology}:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Syphilis screening involves a combination of non-treponemal tests (e.g., Rapid Plasma Reagin [RPR] or Venereal Disease Research Laboratory [VDRL]) to measure disease activity and monitor response to treatment, and treponemal tests (e.g., Treponema pallidum particle agglutination [TP-PA] or enzyme immunoassays [EIA]) which remain positive for life.
            \item HIV testing is performed using fourth-generation antigen/antibody combination immunoassays that detect both HIV-1/2 antibodies and the p24 antigen, significantly shortening the window period. Positive results must be confirmed with a western blot or HIV RNA PCR.
            \item Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), surface antibody (anti-HBs), and core antibody (anti-HBc) are measured to diagnose active infection, past infection, or vaccine-induced immunity.
            \item HSV-1 and HSV-2 type-specific IgG serology can be used to detect past exposure, but viral PCR of active lesions is preferred for diagnosing acute outbreaks.
        \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of venereal diseases requires a multi-faceted approach, incorporating appropriate antimicrobial therapy, patient education, partner notification, and clinical follow-up. Successful treatment resolves the acute infection, prevents long-term sequelae, and stops ongoing transmission.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antimicrobial Therapy for Bacterial Infections}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Syphilis}: The standard treatment remains parenteral Benzathine Penicillin G. Primary, secondary, and early latent syphilis are treated with a single intramuscular injection of 2.4 million units. Late latent or tertiary syphilis requires three weekly injections of 2.4 million units. Neurosyphilis requires continuous intravenous aqueous crystalline penicillin G for 10 to 14 days. For penicillin-allergic patients (except pregnant females, who must undergo penicillin desensitization), oral Doxycycline (100 mg twice daily for 14 to 28 days) is an alternative.
        \item \textbf{Gonorrhoea}: Due to widespread antimicrobial resistance, dual therapy is often recommended. Current guidelines suggest a single intramuscular dose of Ceftriaxone (typically 500 mg or 1 g depending on local guidelines) combined with oral Doxycycline (100 mg twice daily for 7 days) if chlamydia has not been excluded.
        \item \textbf{Chlamydia}: The standard treatment is oral Doxycycline (100 mg twice daily for 7 days). Oral Azithromycin (1 g in a single dose) is used as an alternative, particularly during pregnancy, though cure rates may be lower than with Doxycycline.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antiviral Therapy for Viral Infections}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV)}: There is no cure for HSV, but antiviral medications significantly reduce the severity and duration of outbreaks. First episodes are treated with oral Aciclovir, Valaciclovir, or Famciclovir for 7 to 10 days. Recurrent episodes can be managed with episodic therapy started at the first sign of prodrome, or daily suppressive therapy for individuals with frequent recurrences (six or more outbreaks per year), which also reduces the risk of transmission to partners.
        \item \textbf{Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV)}: Initiating Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) as soon as possible is recommended for all individuals with HIV. Modern regimens typically combine three antiretroviral agents from at least two drug classes (e.g., integrase inhibitors, nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors). Consistent adherence to ART suppresses viral load to undetectable levels, preserving immune function and eliminating the risk of sexual transmission (Undetectable = Untransmittable or U=U).
        \item \textbf{Hepatitis B Virus (HBV)}: Acute HBV is managed supportively. Chronic HBV may require long-term therapy with oral nucleoside/nucleotide analogues (such as Tenofovir or Entecavir) to suppress viral replication, slow the progression of liver fibrosis, and lower the risk of hepatocellular carcinoma.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Therapy for Protozoan and Parasitic Infections}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Trichomoniasis}: Treated with oral Metronidazole (500 mg twice daily for 7 days for females; a single 2 g oral dose can be considered for males). Alcohol must be avoided during treatment and for 24 to 72 hours afterward to prevent a disulfiram-like reaction.
        \item \textbf{Ectoparasites}: Pubic lice are treated with topical Permethrin 1\% cream applied to affected areas and washed off after 10 minutes. Scabies is treated with topical Permethrin 5\% cream applied from the neck down and washed off after 8 to 14 hours, or oral Ivermectin.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Partner Notification and Treatment}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item All sexual partners of an individual diagnosed with an STI must be notified, evaluated, and treated, even if they are asymptomatic.
        \item \textbf{Expedited Partner Therapy (EPT)}: In some jurisdictions, clinicians can provide prescriptions or medication to patients to give to their partners without the partner first undergoing a clinical evaluation, which has been shown to reduce reinfection rates.
        \item Patients must abstain from sexual intercourse (even with condoms) until both they and their partners have completed therapy and all symptoms have resolved (typically 7 days post-treatment).
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated or inadequately managed venereal diseases can lead to severe, irreversible systemic and reproductive complications, disproportionately affecting women and neonates.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reproductive and Fertility Complications}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item In women, ascending infection from \textit{Chlamydia trachomatis} or \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae} can cause Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID). PID leads to inflammation and scarring of the endometrium, fallopian tubes, and pelvic peritoneum. This scarring can cause tubal occlusion, increasing the risk of tubal-factor infertility and ectopic pregnancy (a life-threatening condition where a fertilized egg implants outside the uterus). PID is also a major cause of chronic pelvic pain.
        \item In men, ascending infection can cause epididymitis, orchitis, or prostatitis. While rarely causing absolute infertility, bilateral testicular involvement can impair spermatogenesis and reduce fertility potential.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Obstetric and Neonatal Complications}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Congenital Syphilis}: Vertical transmission of \textit{Treponema pallidum} can occur at any stage of pregnancy, leading to spontaneous abortion, stillbirth, neonatal death, or severe congenital anomalies (e.g., bone deformities, dental abnormalities like Hutchinson's teeth, sensorineural deafness, and neurodevelopmental delay).
        \item \textbf{Neonatal Conjunctivitis (Ophthalmia Neonatorum)}: Acquisition of chlamydia or gonorrhoea during passage through the birth canal can cause severe purulent conjunctivitis, which can lead to corneal scarring and blindness if untreated.
        \item \textbf{Neonatal Herpes}: Vertical transmission of HSV during delivery (especially during a primary maternal outbreak) can cause devastating neonatal infection, presenting as localized skin, eye, and mouth disease, central nervous system disease (meningoencephalitis), or disseminated multi-organ disease with high mortality.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Oncogenesis}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Persistent infection with high-risk genotypes of Human Papillomavirus (HPV) is the primary cause of cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) and cervical cancer. It is also strongly linked to other anogenital malignancies (anal, vulvar, vaginal, and penile cancers) and oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinoma.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular and Neurological Sequelae}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Tertiary Syphilis}: Manifests years after infection as cardiovascular syphilis (aortitis, aortic aneurysm, coronary ostial stenosis) or neurosyphilis (tabes dorsalis, general paresis, meningovascular syphilis). Tabes dorsalis involves progressive degeneration of the posterior columns of the spinal cord, leading to ataxia, paresthesia, and loss of pain sensation.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Enhanced Susceptibility to HIV}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Ulcerative STIs (such as HSV or syphilis) disrupt the integrity of the genital epithelium, creating a physical portal of entry for HIV. Non-ulcerative STIs (such as chlamydia or gonorrhoea) recruit CD4+ T cells and macrophages to the mucosa, increasing the concentration of target cells for HIV and making transmission up to three to five times more likely.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals diagnosed with venereal diseases depends heavily on the specific pathogen involved, the timing of diagnosis, and adherence to prescribed treatment regimens. For bacterial and protozoan STIs, such as chlamydia, gonorrhoea, syphilis, and trichomoniasis, the prognosis is excellent. These infections are highly curable with short courses of standard oral or parenteral antibiotics. Complete resolution of the infection and prevention of long-term sequelae are achieved in the vast majority of cases, provided therapy is completed before permanent tissue damage (such as fallopian tube scarring) has occurred.

For viral STIs, including HIV, HSV, and HBV, the prognosis has dramatically improved over the past several decades. While these infections are currently incurable and require lifelong management, antiviral therapies allow individuals to live long, healthy, and productive lives. For patients with HIV, early initiation of and strict adherence to Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) preserves immune function, prevents progression to AIDS, and achieves a life expectancy close to that of the general population. Suppressive therapy for HSV minimizes the frequency and severity of recurrences, significantly improving quality of life. The prognosis for HPV infections is also generally favorable; the majority of HPV infections are transient and cleared spontaneously by the host immune system within two years. However, the prognosis for individuals who develop HPV-associated malignancies depends on the stage of the cancer at diagnosis, emphasizing the importance of routine cervical cancer screening (Pap smears and HPV testing).

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventing the transmission of sexually transmitted infections requires a combination of behavioral strategies, biomedical interventions, and patient empowerment. Because many STIs are asymptomatic, prevention should be a proactive component of healthcare for all sexually active individuals.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Barrier Methods}: The consistent and correct use of male latex condoms and female condoms during vaginal, anal, and oral intercourse is highly effective at preventing the transmission of pathogens spread through fluids, such as HIV, chlamydia, and gonorrhoea. Condoms also reduce the risk of transmission of pathogens spread through skin-to-skin contact, such as HSV, syphilis, and HPV, though transmission can still occur if lesions are present on areas not covered by the condom. Dental dams should be used to provide a barrier during oral-anal or oral-vaginal contact.
    \item \textbf{Vaccination}: Vaccines are available to prevent several major viral STIs:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{HPV Vaccine}: Highly effective at preventing infection with the most common high-risk and low-risk HPV genotypes. Vaccination is ideally administered before the onset of sexual activity (typically recommended at age 11--12 years, but available up to age 45).
        \item \textbf{Hepatitis B Vaccine}: A multi-dose vaccine series that provides long-term immunity against HBV, recommended for all infants and unvaccinated sexually active adults.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Routine Screening and Early Detection}: Regular screening is essential for sexually active individuals, particularly those with new or multiple partners. Screening should be performed at least annually for active individuals under 25, men who have sex with men, and pregnant individuals to identify and treat asymptomatic infections.
    \item \textbf{Biomedical Prevention}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP)}: A daily oral medication or periodic injection for individuals at high risk for HIV, which reduces the risk of acquiring HIV through sex by more than 99\%.
        \item \textbf{Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP)}: A 28-day course of antiretroviral medications started within 72 hours of a potential high-risk exposure to HIV to prevent infection.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Relationship Strategies and Communication}: Mutual monogamy with an uninfected partner reduces the risk of STI exposure. Discussing sexual history, screening status, and condom use with new partners before initiating sexual contact is a critical self-care practice.
    \item \textbf{Hygiene and Skin Care}: Gentle washing of the genital area with water and mild, unscented soap is recommended. Avoiding douching is crucial, as it disrupts the natural vaginal flora and pH, increasing vulnerability to infections like bacterial vaginosis and STIs. Regular self-examination of the genitals for new sores, bumps, or unusual rashes can help detect infections early.
\end{itemize}
