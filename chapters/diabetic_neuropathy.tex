\chapter{Diabetic Neuropathy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Diabetic neuropathy is nerve damage caused by diabetes, most commonly affecting the nerves of the feet and legs. It results from long-term high blood glucose levels, which damage the small blood vessels that supply the nerves and impair the nerves themselves. The most common form is peripheral neuropathy, causing numbness, tingling, burning, or pain in the feet. Autonomic neuropathy can affect the heart, digestive system, bladder, and sexual function. Diabetic neuropathy is a major risk factor for foot ulcers and amputation, but it can be slowed with good glucose control and managed effectively with screening and treatment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Diabetic neuropathy is one of the most common complications of diabetes, affecting around half of people with diabetes at some point. The risk increases with the duration of diabetes, poor glucose control, high blood pressure, smoking, and high cholesterol. It is the leading cause of non-traumatic lower-limb amputation worldwide, and it significantly reduces quality of life through pain, loss of sensation, and disability. Neuropathy is frequently under-diagnosed because early changes cause no symptoms.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High blood glucose:} chronic hyperglycaemia damages nerves directly and through damage to their blood supply
    \item \textbf{Metabolic changes:} glucose-related pathways produce toxic products that injure nerve cells
    \item \textbf{Reduced blood flow:} disease of the small vessels (vasa nervorum) starves nerves of oxygen and nutrients
    \item \textbf{Peripheral neuropathy:} affects the longest nerves first, so symptoms start in the feet and spread upward
    \item \textbf{Autonomic neuropathy:} affects the nerves controlling the heart, gut, bladder, and sex organs
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} poor glucose control, long duration of diabetes, high blood pressure, obesity, and smoking
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Numbness, tingling, burning, or pins and needles in the feet and hands
    \item Sharp, stabbing, or electric-shock-like pain, often worse at night
    \item Loss of sensation, so injuries and ulcers are not noticed
    \item Muscle weakness, unsteadiness, and difficulty walking
    \item Dry, cracked skin and reduced sweating in the feet
    \item Autonomic symptoms: dizziness on standing, bloating, diarrhoea or constipation, bladder problems, and erectile dysfunction
    \item Foot deformity such as clawed toes in advanced cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Peripheral vascular disease, which causes leg pain on walking and poor healing
    \item Vitamin B12 deficiency and other nutritional neuropathies
    \item Alcohol-related nerve damage
    \item Spinal disorders such as lumbar disc disease and spinal stenosis
    \item Other causes of pain, including fibromyalgia and restless legs syndrome
    \item Other autonomic disorders
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with diabetes should have a foot examination at least annually, and should see a doctor promptly for any foot injury, ulcer, change in sensation, or pain in the feet. Dizziness, digestive problems, or sexual dysfunction in a person with diabetes should also be discussed with a doctor.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A foot ulcer with spreading redness, heat, fever, or signs of infection
    \item Sudden weakness or loss of function
    \item Severe dizziness, fainting, or collapse
    \item Chest pain, palpitations, or breathlessness, which may indicate autonomic nerve or heart problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Foot examination:} inspection of the feet, testing of sensation with a monofilament and tuning fork, and checking pulses
    \item \textbf{Nerve conduction studies:} measure how well the nerves transmit signals
    \item \textbf{Electromyography (EMG):} assesses nerve and muscle function
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} glucose, HbA1c, vitamin B12, thyroid function, and kidney function
    \item \textbf{Autonomic testing:} heart rate and blood pressure responses, and other specialised tests where autonomic neuropathy is suspected
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a neurologist or podiatrist as needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Glucose control:} the most important measure to slow progression
    \item \textbf{Foot care:} daily inspection, professional podiatry, appropriate footwear, and prompt treatment of injuries
    \item \textbf{Pain management:} medicines such as pregabalin, gabapentin, duloxetine, or amitriptyline
    \item \textbf{Other pain options:} topical treatments, physiotherapy, and in some cases other interventions for persistent pain
    \item \textbf{Management of autonomic symptoms:} treatment of dizziness, digestive problems, bladder issues, and erectile dysfunction
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular risk management:} blood pressure, cholesterol, and smoking control
    \item \textbf{Regular review:} monitoring of feet, symptoms, and glucose control
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Foot ulcers, infection, and amputation
    \item Charcot foot, a destructive joint condition from loss of sensation
    \item Chronic neuropathic pain affecting sleep, mood, and function
    \item Falls from unsteadiness and loss of sensation
    \item Autonomic complications affecting the heart, gut, and bladder
    \item Reduced quality of life and depression
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Diabetic neuropathy is progressive, but its course can be slowed substantially with tight glucose control and management of cardiovascular risk factors. Neuropathic pain responds to treatment in most people, and foot complications are largely preventable with daily foot care and regular podiatry. With good self-management, most people with diabetic neuropathy remain mobile and independent, and serious complications such as amputation can usually be avoided.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain tight blood glucose control from diagnosis
    \item Examine your feet daily for cuts, blisters, redness, and swelling
    \item Wash and dry feet carefully and moisturise dry skin
    \item Wear well-fitting shoes and socks, and never walk barefoot
    \item Have your feet professionally examined at least annually
    \item Report any foot injury immediately, even if it does not hurt
    \item Control blood pressure and cholesterol, and do not smoke
    \item Take pain medicines as prescribed and discuss side effects with your doctor
\end{itemize}
