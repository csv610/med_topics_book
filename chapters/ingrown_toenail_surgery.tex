\chapter{Ingrown Toenail Surgery}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Ingrown toenail surgery is a minor procedure to treat an ingrown toenail that is painful, recurrent, or infected. In the most common procedure, a wedge of the nail and the underlying nail bed on the affected side is removed under local anaesthetic, so that the remaining nail grows straight. Often the root of the nail on that side is destroyed, either surgically or with phenol, to prevent regrowth and recurrence. The procedure is performed as a day case, usually by a podiatrist, GP, or surgeon, and most people return to normal activity within days. Surgery is reserved for ingrown toenails that do not respond to conservative care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Ingrown toenails are one of the most common nail problems, affecting around one in fifty people each year, with the highest rates in adolescents and young adults. The big toe is affected in the vast majority of cases. Risk factors include cutting nails too short or rounding the corners, tight footwear, trauma, excessive sweating, and anatomical factors such as curved nail plates. Among people who see a doctor for ingrown toenails, a substantial proportion eventually have surgery because of recurrence or infection. Complications such as diabetes and poor circulation increase the risk of severe infection.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
An ingrown toenail occurs when the edge or corner of the nail pierces the skin of the nail fold, causing pain, inflammation, and often infection. This is encouraged by nail-cutting practices that leave a spike of nail to embed, by pressure from tight shoes forcing the flesh against the nail, and by naturally curved or thick nails. The body responds with inflammation and sometimes a build-up of tissue (granulation tissue) that bleeds easily. Bacteria, usually staphylococci, can infect the site and cause discharge. Surgery addresses the problem by removing the offending nail edge and, when the germinal matrix (the nail root) is treated, preventing that edge from regrowing.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain and tenderness at the corner of the big toenail
    \item Redness and swelling of the nail fold
    \item Discharge, crusting, or a foul smell when infected
    \item A spike of nail visibly embedded in the skin
    \item Overgrowth of soft tissue (granulation tissue) beside the nail
    \item Worsening pain with pressure from footwear
    \item Limping or difficulty wearing closed shoes
    \item Recurrent episodes after conservative treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions can affect the nail or toe and should be distinguished before surgery.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cellulitis:} deeper skin infection without an ingrown nail
    \item \textbf{Paronychia:} infection of the skin fold around the nail
    \item \textbf{Nail fungus:} thickening and discolouration of the nail plate
    \item \textbf{Subungual tumour:} rare growths under the nail
    \item \textbf{Psoriasis and other nail diseases:} affecting the nail plate
    \item \textbf{Peripheral vascular disease and diabetes complications:} foot problems in at-risk people
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a podiatrist or doctor for an ingrown toenail that is painful, recurrent, or showing signs of infection, and before it becomes severe. People with diabetes, poor circulation, or impaired healing should seek care early. Surgery is considered when conservative measures have failed, the problem is recurrent, or infection is established.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rapidly spreading redness, pain, and swelling up the foot or leg
    \item Fever, chills, or feeling unwell with a toe infection
    \item Severe pain with poor circulation, coldness, or numbness of the toe in people with diabetes
    \item Red streaks extending up the leg from the infected toe
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} assessment of the nail, surrounding skin, and signs of infection
    \item \textbf{Vascular assessment:} checking pulses and circulation, especially in older people and those with diabetes
    \item \textbf{Blood glucose testing:} for undiagnosed diabetes in severe or recurrent cases
    \item \textbf{Microbiological swab:} if infection is present, to guide antibiotics
    \item \textbf{X-ray:} rarely needed, to exclude bone infection or other problems
    \item \textbf{Pre-procedure review:} checking medicines, allergies, and fitness for local anaesthesia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Local anaesthetic:} numbing the toe with injections around the base
    \item \textbf{Partial nail avulsion:} removing the offending edge of the nail under the fold
    \item \textbf{Phenolisation:} applying phenol to destroy the nail root on that side, greatly reducing recurrence
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} a course for established infection, sometimes started before surgery
    \item \textbf{Dressings and aftercare:} keeping the toe clean and dry, with regular dressing changes
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple analgesics for the first day or two
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review of healing and nail regrowth over the following weeks
    \item \textbf{Total nail removal:} only rarely needed for severe, recurrent, or deformed nails
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding and infection after the procedure
    \item Recurrence of the ingrown nail, much less likely when the root is destroyed with phenol
    \item Pain and altered nail shape as the nail regrows
    \item Numbness or tingling from the local anaesthetic or nerve irritation
    \item Delayed healing in people with diabetes or poor circulation
    \item Rarely, damage to surrounding tissue or bone infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Ingrown toenail surgery is highly effective: with phenolisation of the nail root, recurrence rates are low, in the region of 2 to 10 per cent, compared with much higher rates when the nail edge alone is removed. Most people return to normal footwear and activity within a week, and complications are uncommon. The cosmetic result is good, with the remaining nail continuing to grow normally. Prevention of further episodes relies on correct nail care and appropriate footwear.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cut toenails straight across and do not round the corners
    \item Avoid cutting nails too short
    \item Wear shoes that fit well and do not compress the toes
    \item Keep feet clean and dry
    \item Treat fungal infections and excessive sweating
    \item Seek early treatment rather than waiting for severe pain or infection
    \item After surgery, keep the wound clean, change dressings as advised, and rest the foot
    \item People with diabetes should check their feet daily and see a podiatrist regularly
    \item Avoid soaking the toe excessively after surgery
\end{itemize}
