\chapter{Peripheral artery disease}
\index{Peripheral artery disease}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Educational information; seek professional advice for individual care.}

\section*{What it is}
Peripheral artery disease (PAD) occurs when narrowed or blocked arteries reduce blood flow, most often to the legs. It is associated with cardiovascular disease and may affect the heart and brain as well as the limbs.

\section*{Symptoms}
Some people have no symptoms. Others develop aching or cramping in the buttock, thigh, or calf when walking that improves with rest, cool or pale skin, numbness, weakness, slow-healing wounds, or pain at rest in severe disease. Nerve, joint, or vein problems can cause similar symptoms.

\section*{Diagnosis}
Assessment includes risk factors, pulses, skin and wound examination, and comparison of blood pressure at the ankle and arm using an ankle-brachial index. Ultrasound, CT angiography, or other imaging may be used for planning treatment.

\section*{Management}
Treatment aims to improve walking and reduce heart attack, stroke, and limb risk. It may include stopping tobacco, a supervised walking programme, management of blood pressure, cholesterol and diabetes, antiplatelet or other prescribed medicines, wound care, and procedures to restore blood flow when needed. Foot protection and regular review are important.

\section*{When to seek urgent care}
Seek emergency help for sudden severe leg pain, a cold or pale limb, new numbness or weakness, inability to move the limb, rapidly worsening pain, or a wound with spreading infection. Arrange review for exertional leg pain, persistent foot wounds, or reduced walking ability.
