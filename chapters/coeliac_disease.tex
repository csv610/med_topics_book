\chapter{Coeliac Disease}
 
\section*{Definition and Overview}
Coeliac disease is a chronic, systemic autoimmune disorder triggered by the consumption of dietary gluten (a protein found in wheat, rye, and barley) in genetically susceptible individuals. It is characterised by a permanent intolerance to gluten, leading to immune-mediated enteropathy of the small intestine. Specifically, exposure to gluten causes chronic inflammation, villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, and malabsorption of nutrients in the lamina propria of the small bowel. Coeliac disease is not a food allergy or simple sensitivity; it is a serious autoimmune condition that can affect multiple organ systems and requires lifelong management.
 
\section*{Epidemiology}
Coeliac disease affects approximately 1\% of the population in Australia and other Western countries, though it remains significantly underdiagnosed, with an estimated 80\% of affected individuals unaware of their condition. The disease can present at any age, from infancy (after the introduction of gluten-containing foods) to late adulthood. It is more common in females than in males (ratio of approximately 2:1) and is highly prevalent among first-degree relatives of affected individuals (about 10\% risk). Prevalence is also higher in people with other autoimmune conditions, such as type 1 diabetes and autoimmune thyroid disease, and in individuals with Down syndrome or Turner syndrome.
 
\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Coeliac disease results from a combination of genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers:
 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gluten exposure:} the dietary trigger, specifically the gliadin fraction of gluten.
    \item \textbf{Genetic predisposition:} strong association with human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class II molecules HLA-DQ2 (present in \textasciitilde{}95\% of patients) and HLA-DQ8. The absence of these genes makes coeliac disease highly unlikely.
    \item \textbf{Tissue transglutaminase (tTG):} the enzyme that deamidates gliadin peptides in the lamina propria. Deamidated gliadin has a higher affinity for HLA-DQ2/DQ8 on antigen-presenting cells.
    \item \textbf{Immune response:} activation of CD4+ T helper cells triggers an inflammatory cascade, releasing cytokines that cause enterocyte damage, villous atrophy (flattening of the lining), and crypt hyperplasia, severely reducing the absorptive surface area of the small intestine.
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms can be classical (gastrointestinal) or atypical (extraintestinal), and many patients are asymptomatic (silent coeliac disease):
 
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic diarrhoea, steatorrhea (pale, foul-smelling, floating stools), abdominal pain, bloating, flatulence, and weight loss
    \item In children: failure to thrive, growth retardation, delayed puberty, muscle wasting, and irritability
    \item Extraintestinal features: chronic fatigue, iron-deficiency anaemia (refractory to oral iron), vitamin deficiencies (folate, B12, D), and aphthous mouth ulcers
    \item Dermatological: dermatitis herpetiformis, a highly itchy, blistering skin rash typically appearing on elbows, knees, and buttocks
    \item Neurological: ataxia, peripheral neuropathy, and recurrent headaches
    \item Gynaecological: recurrent miscarriages and unexplained infertility
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Coeliac disease must be distinguished from other causes of malabsorption, chronic diarrhoea, and abdominal pain:
 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS):} presents with abdominal pain and altered bowel habits, but without mucosal damage, autoantibodies, or malabsorption.
    \item \textbf{Non-coeliac gluten sensitivity (NCGS):} patients report symptom improvement on a gluten-free diet, but have normal histology and negative serology.
    \item \textbf{Wheat allergy:} an IgE-mediated allergic reaction to wheat proteins, presenting with hives, wheezing, or anaphylaxis, distinct from the autoimmune pathology of coeliac disease.
    \item \textbf{Crohn's disease:} an inflammatory bowel disease that can affect the small intestine, distinguished by colonoscopy, imaging, and histology showing transmural inflammation.
    \item \textbf{Tropical sprue:} a chronic diarrheal illness found in tropical regions, presenting with malabsorption but responding to antibiotics and folate.
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Individuals with persistent unexplained gastrointestinal symptoms, chronic fatigue, unexplained weight loss, or difficulty absorbing nutrients should consult a doctor.
 
\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, sudden abdominal pain with a rigid or distended abdomen (suggesting bowel perforation or obstruction)
    \item Uncontrolled, heavy rectal bleeding or vomiting blood
    \item Signs of shock: severe dizziness, confusion, rapid heart rate, or cold, clammy skin
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gluten consumption:} crucial that the patient maintains a gluten-containing diet (equivalent to 2 slices of wheat bread daily for at least 6 weeks) before testing to avoid false negatives.
    \item \textbf{Serology:} screening with IgA anti-tissue transglutaminase (tTG) and IgA anti-endomysial antibodies (EMA). Total serum IgA must be measured to exclude IgA deficiency.
    \item \textbf{HLA genetic testing:} checking for HLA-DQ2/DQ8; a negative result virtually excludes coeliac disease.
    \item \textbf{Small bowel biopsy:} the gold standard, performed via upper endoscopy (gastroscopy), showing villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, and increased intraepithelial lymphocytes.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count (for anaemia), iron studies, folate, B12, vitamin D, and liver function tests.
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gluten-free diet (GFD):} the only effective treatment, requiring lifelong, strict avoidance of wheat, rye, barley, and contaminated oats.
    \item \textbf{Dietary education:} consultation with an accredited practicing dietitian specializing in coeliac disease.
    \item \textbf{Nutritional supplementation:} replacement of deficient iron, folate, calcium, and vitamins B12 and D.
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular follow-up with serology (to monitor diet adherence) and repeating small bowel biopsy in adults if symptoms persist.
    \item \textbf{Pneumococcal vaccination:} recommended for adults with coeliac disease due to potential functional hyposplenism.
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Osteoporosis and osteopenia due to chronic calcium and vitamin D malabsorption
    \item Refractory coeliac disease, where enteropathy persists despite a strict gluten-free diet
    \item Increased risk of gastrointestinal malignancies, specifically enteropathy-associated T-cell lymphoma (EATL) and small bowel adenocarcinoma
    \item Nutrient deficiencies leading to persistent anaemia, neuropathy, or dermatological issues
    \item Pregnancy complications, including low birth weight and preterm birth
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals with coeliac disease is excellent once a strict gluten-free diet is implemented. Most patients experience symptom improvement within weeks, and mucosal healing of the small intestine occurs over several months to years (faster in children). Adherence to a gluten-free diet dramatically reduces the long-term risk of osteoporosis, infertility, and lymphoma. However, accidental gluten exposure can cause temporary recurrence of symptoms, and continuous monitoring is essential for long-term health.
 
\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain strict, lifelong avoidance of gluten, checking all food labels and ingredients for hidden gluten sources
    \item Join support organisations like Coeliac Australia for resources, recipe ideas, and community support
    \item Prevent cross-contamination at home by using separate cooking utensils, toasters, and preparation surfaces
    \item Discuss your diagnosis with first-degree relatives, as they have a 1 in 10 chance of also having coeliac disease
    \item Attend annual follow-up appointments with your doctor and dietitian to check nutrient levels and dietary adherence
\end{itemize}
