\chapter{Oesophageal cancer}
\index{Oesophageal cancer}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Oesophageal cancer is a malignant neoplastic disease originating in the mucosal lining of the oesophagus, the muscular tube that transports food and liquids from the pharynx to the stomach. The disease primarily presents in two main histological forms: squamous cell carcinoma, which typically arises in the upper and middle thirds of the oesophagus, and adenocarcinoma, which occurs in the lower third, often at the gastro-oesophageal junction. Because early-stage disease is frequently asymptomatic or presents with non-specific symptoms such as mild indigestion, oesophageal cancer is commonly diagnosed at an advanced stage, making it one of the most lethal gastrointestinal malignancies globally.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Globally, oesophageal cancer is the eighth most common cancer and the sixth leading cause of cancer-related mortality. The epidemiology varies significantly by geographic region and histological subtype. Squamous cell carcinoma remains the predominant subtype worldwide, with high-incidence areas known as the ``oesophageal cancer belt'' stretching from Northern Iran through Central Asia to Northern China. In contrast, the incidence of adenocarcinoma has risen rapidly in Western nations, including some countries, over the past few decades, primarily driven by rising rates of gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) and obesity. It predominantly affects older males, with a male-to-female ratio exceeding 4:1 for adenocarcinoma, and most diagnoses occur in individuals over 60 years of age.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The pathophysiology of oesophageal cancer involves distinct cellular pathways for its two primary subtypes, driven by specific environmental exposures and genetic alterations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD):} Chronic acid and bile reflux damages the squamous epithelium of the lower oesophagus, promoting metaplastic changes into columnar epithelium (Barrett's oesophagus), a critical precursor to adenocarcinoma.
    \item \textbf{Obesity:} Excess adiposity increases intra-abdominal pressure, exacerbating acid reflux, and promotes systemic inflammation and altered adipokine levels that stimulate tumour growth.
    \item \textbf{Tobacco smoking and alcohol consumption:} These factors synergistically increase the risk of squamous cell carcinoma by causing direct mucosal irritation and DNA damage.
    \item \textbf{Dietary factors:} Diets low in fresh fruits and vegetables and high in red meat, processed foods, or very hot beverages are associated with an increased risk.
    \item \textbf{Achalasia and tylosis:} Rare structural and genetic conditions that cause chronic stasis of food or hyperkeratosis, elevating the risk of squamous cell carcinoma.
    \item \textbf{Genetic alterations:} Mutations in key cell cycle regulator genes, such as TP53 and CDKN2A, and amplification of oncogenes like HER2 and EGFR, drive progressive dysplasia and invasive carcinoma.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms of oesophageal cancer typically develop when the tumour lumen restricts the passage of food, indicating advanced disease.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dysphagia:} Progressive difficulty swallowing, starting with solids (such as meat and bread) and eventually progressing to soft foods and liquids.
    \item \textbf{Weight loss:} Significant, unexplained weight loss resulting from both reduced oral intake and cancer cachexia.
    \item \textbf{Odynophagia:} Pain on swallowing, which may be felt behind the breastbone or in the upper back.
    \item \textbf{Retrosternal chest pain:} Persistent dull ache or burning discomfort in the chest, which may be mistaken for indigestion or cardiac pain.
    \item \textbf{Regurgitation:} Backflow of undigested food or saliva, which can lead to coughing, choking, or aspiration pneumonia.
    \item \textbf{Hoarseness:} Chronic hoarseness or coughing, caused by the invasion of the tumour into the recurrent laryngeal nerve.
    \item \textbf{Anaemia:} Iron deficiency anaemia secondary to chronic, occult bleeding from the tumour, leading to fatigue and pallor.
    \item \textbf{Haematemesis:} Vomiting blood or passing dark, tarry stools (melaena), which is a sign of acute haemorrhage.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
It is vital to differentiate oesophageal cancer from other structural, inflammatory, or functional disorders of the upper gastrointestinal tract.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Oesophageal stricture:} Benign narrowing of the oesophagus, commonly caused by chronic acid reflux scarring (peptic stricture), ingestion of corrosive substances, or previous surgery.
    \item \textbf{Achalasia:} A primary motility disorder characterised by failure of the lower oesophageal sphincter to relax and impaired peristalsis, presenting with long-standing dysphagia for both solids and liquids.
    \item \textbf{Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD):} Common inflammatory condition presenting with heartburn and acid regurgitation without structural blockage.
    \item \textbf{Oesophagitis:} Inflammation of the oesophageal lining due to infection (such as Candida or Herpes simplex), medications, or eosinophilic infiltration (eosinophilic oesophagitis).
    \item \textbf{Oesophageal spasm:} Functional motility disorder causing sudden, severe chest pain and temporary swallowing difficulty.
    \item \textbf{Gastric cancer:} Malignancy of the stomach, which can present with similar symptoms of weight loss, early satiety, and dysphagia if located near the cardia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone experiencing persistent difficulty or pain when swallowing (dysphagia or odynophagia), unexplained weight loss, chronic chest pain, or recurrent vomiting should consult a doctor promptly. An urgent referral for an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy is required to exclude malignancy.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vomiting large amounts of blood or coffee-ground material
    \item Passing black, sticky, tarry stools (melaena) accompanied by dizziness, sweating, or fainting
    \item Sudden, severe chest pain that radiates to the neck, arm, or back (which may indicate myocardial infarction or oesophageal perforation)
    \item Acute airway obstruction or severe difficulty breathing due to aspiration
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A series of diagnostic procedures is required to confirm the presence of cancer, determine the histological subtype, and stage the extent of the disease.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (gastroscopy):} Visualisation of the oesophageal lumen using a flexible camera, allowing direct inspection and tissue biopsy of abnormal areas.
    \item \textbf{Biopsy and histopathology:} Microscopic evaluation of tissue samples to confirm cancer, classify the cell type (adenocarcinoma versus squamous cell), and determine HER2 status.
    \item \textbf{CT scan of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis:} Performed to evaluate local tumour size, regional lymph node involvement, and distant metastatic spread.
    \item \textbf{Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS):} High-resolution ultrasound performed from inside the oesophageal lumen to determine the depth of tumour invasion (T stage) and assess adjacent lymph nodes.
    \item \textbf{PET-CT scan:} Positron emission tomography combined with CT, essential for identifying occult distant metastases (M stage) and planning radiotherapy.
    \item \textbf{Staging laparoscopy:} Performed for lower oesophageal and junctional cancers to check for peritoneal metastases.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is determined by a multidisciplinary team and depends on the stage of the disease, the histological type, and the patient's general fitness.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} Surgical removal of the oesophagus (oesophagectomy) is the primary curative treatment for localised disease, often involving reconstruction using a stomach or colon pull-up.
    \item \textbf{Neoadjuvant therapy:} Chemotherapy or chemoradiotherapy administered prior to surgery to shrink the tumour and eliminate micrometastases, which improves survival rates.
    \item \textbf{Definitive chemoradiotherapy:} Combined chemotherapy and radiotherapy used as the primary treatment for patients with squamous cell carcinoma who are not candidates for surgery.
    \item \textbf{Palliative therapy:} Focuses on symptom relief for advanced, non-curable disease, including oesophageal stenting to relieve dysphagia, palliative radiotherapy, and systemic chemotherapy.
    \item \textbf{Targeted and immunotherapy:} Monoclonal antibodies (e.g., trastuzumab for HER2-positive tumours) and checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., pembrolizumab) for advanced or metastatic disease.
    \item \textbf{Nutritional support:} Assessment by a dietitian, insertion of feeding tubes (jejunostomy), or enteral nutrition to combat malnutrition and weight loss.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Complete obstruction of the oesophageal lumen, preventing any oral intake and leading to dehydration and malnutrition
    \item Tracheo-oesophageal fistula (a connection between the oesophagus and airway), causing severe aspiration pneumonia
    \item Anastomotic leak, infection, or stricture formation following oesophagectomy
    \item Severe dumping syndrome, reflux, or delayed gastric emptying post-surgery
    \item Toxicity from chemotherapy (neutropenic sepsis, peripheral neuropathy) and radiation-induced oesophagitis
    \item Severe, life-threatening haemorrhage from erosion into major blood vessels (such as the aorta)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Oesophageal cancer carries a guarded prognosis, primarily because most cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage. The overall five-year survival rate in many countries and other Western nations is approximately 20\% to 25\%. For patients with localised disease who can undergo successful surgery (with or without neoadjuvant therapy), the five-year survival rate increases to approximately 45\% to 50\%. However, for those with distant metastases (Stage IV), the disease is generally incurable, and the median survival is approximately 10 to 12 months with palliative therapy. Continuous clinical follow-up is necessary to manage long-term nutritional changes and monitor for recurrence.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Quit smoking and avoid all tobacco products to lower the risk of squamous cell carcinoma.
    \item Limit alcohol consumption to recommended guidelines, as alcohol synergistically increases cancer risk.
    \item Maintain a healthy body weight through a balanced diet and regular physical activity to reduce the risk of GORD and adenocarcinoma.
    \item Manage chronic acid reflux (GORD) effectively through lifestyle measures and medication as prescribed by a physician.
    \item Undergo regular endoscopic surveillance if diagnosed with Barrett's oesophagus to detect precancerous changes (dysplasia) early.
    \item Eat a diet rich in fresh fruits, vegetables, and dietary fibre while avoiding extremely hot foods and beverages.
\end{itemize}
