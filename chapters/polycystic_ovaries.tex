\chapter{Polycystic Ovaries and Polycystic Ovary Syndrome}
\index{Polycystic Ovaries and Polycystic Ovary Syndrome}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{This chapter is for general education. An ultrasound appearance of polycystic ovaries is not the same as polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS).}

\section*{What it means}
Polycystic ovaries describes ovaries with many small follicles on ultrasound. PCOS is a hormone and metabolic syndrome that may involve irregular ovulation, higher androgen activity, acne or increased hair growth, and metabolic risk. A person can have polycystic-appearing ovaries without PCOS, and PCOS can occur without that ultrasound appearance.

\section*{Symptoms and diagnosis}
Symptoms may include irregular or absent periods, difficulty becoming pregnant, acne, increased facial or body hair, scalp hair thinning, weight changes, or darkened skin folds. Diagnosis is clinical and usually involves assessment of menstrual pattern, signs or blood tests of androgen excess, exclusion of other causes, and sometimes ultrasound. No single test confirms PCOS.

\section*{Treatment}
Treatment depends on symptoms, pregnancy plans, and health risks. Options may include regular physical activity, a balanced eating pattern, support with weight management where relevant, hormonal contraception or cyclic progestogen for irregular bleeding, medicines for androgen-related symptoms, and fertility treatment. Metformin may be considered for selected metabolic or fertility indications. Treatment should be prescribed and reviewed individually.

\section*{Long-term health}
PCOS is associated with increased risk of impaired glucose regulation, type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, sleep apnoea, and mood difficulties. Regular review may include blood pressure, glucose, lipids, and mental-health assessment. Very infrequent periods should be discussed because protecting the uterine lining may be important.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek urgent care for severe pelvic pain, fainting, heavy bleeding, or possible pregnancy with pain or bleeding. Arrange review for new or rapidly increasing androgen-related symptoms, persistent abnormal bleeding, or distress affecting daily life.
