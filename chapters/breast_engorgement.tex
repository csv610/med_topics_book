\chapter{Breast Engorgement}
\index{Breast Engorgement}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Breast engorgement is the painful overfilling, swelling, and hardening of the breasts that commonly occurs in the first few days after childbirth, when the milk "comes in" -- usually around the third to fifth day. It is caused not only by an increased volume of milk but also by increased blood flow and accumulation of fluid within the breast tissue. Engorgement is a normal physiological event after birth, but when severe it can be intensely painful, make it difficult for a baby to attach and feed, and set the stage for blocked ducts or mastitis. It is distinct from ordinary breast fullness, which is a mild, temporary sense of heaviness that settles easily with feeding. Understanding the difference, and acting early, allows engorgement to resolve quickly and keeps breastfeeding on track.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Breast engorgement affects the majority of new mothers to some degree during the first week after giving birth. It is more common and more severe in women having their first baby, and in circumstances such as delayed or infrequent feeding, a poor latch, nipple problems, or periods of separation between mother and baby. It can also occur when breastfeeding is stopped abruptly or when a baby suddenly feeds much less than usual -- for example, after a long sleep or during illness. Although almost universal to some degree, clinically significant engorgement that interferes with feeding is far more common in the first few days of life, when the milk supply is still being established.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
When the placenta is delivered, the fall in progesterone removes the brake on milk secretion and the mammary glands begin to produce milk in earnest. At the same time, blood flow to the breasts increases substantially and lymph fluid accumulates. The combination of the arriving milk, increased blood flow, and interstitial fluid congestion makes the breasts feel heavy, firm, warm, and tender. The problem is compounded when milk is not removed regularly and effectively: retained milk and the raised pressure inside the breast can slow further production and make attachment harder, setting up a cycle of congestion, inefficient feeding, and -- in some women -- blocked ducts and mastitis.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Both breasts feel heavy, full, hard, and tender, usually affecting both sides together
    \item The breasts may look swollen, warm, reddened, or shiny over the affected areas
    \item Pain over the whole breast, which may be severe and continuous
    \item The nipples may become flattened or stretched by the swelling, making a good latch difficult
    \item A low-grade fever may occur with severe engorgement, sometimes called milk fever
    \item Difficulty getting the baby to attach and feed effectively, with the baby becoming frustrated or falling asleep at the breast
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Normal breast fullness in the early days of feeding, which is mild and settles with feeding
    \item Mastitis, which is usually one-sided and localised, with a wedge of redness and pain, often with fever and flu-like symptoms
    \item Breast abscess, which feels like a localised, fluctuant (soft and fluid-filled) lump
    \item A blocked duct or localised milk stasis in one area
    \item Inflammatory breast cancer, which is very rare, is persistent rather than settling with feeding, and does not resolve with breast drainage
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor, midwife, or lactation consultant if engorgement is not improving with frequent feeding and comfort measures, if it is severely painful, or if the baby is not feeding well or is not gaining weight. Early professional support with positioning and attachment can break the cycle before complications develop.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item High fever, chills, or severe flu-like symptoms -- possible mastitis or infection
    \item A red, hot, painful area that is spreading over one breast
    \item Severe pain that prevents any feeding or expressing
    \item A baby who is floppy, lethargic, or not feeding at all
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the timing after birth, feeding frequency and effectiveness, whether one or both breasts are affected, and any preceding nipple problems
    \item \textbf{Examination:} in engorgement the whole breast is typically firm, warm, and tender, whereas mastitis and abscess are usually localised to one area
    \item \textbf{Temperature check:} a mild fever can occur with severe engorgement, but a high fever points to infection
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} not usually needed, but may be used if an abscess is suspected or a localised swelling fails to settle
    \item \textbf{Review of feeding:} observation of the baby's attachment and feeding by a midwife or lactation consultant
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Frequent, effective feeding:} feed the baby on demand, including through the night, and wake the baby to feed if necessary
    \item \textbf{Hand expression or pumping:} express a little milk before a feed to soften the breast and improve the latch; avoid prolonged pumping, which stimulates more milk and worsens the problem
    \item \textbf{Gentle massage} of the breast towards the nipple while feeding or expressing
    \item \textbf{Warmth before feeding:} a warm shower or compress before a feed helps milk flow
    \item \textbf{Cold compresses between feeds:} applied for short periods, together with simple analgesia such as paracetamol or ibuprofen, reduces discomfort and swelling
    \item \textbf{A supportive bra or cloth} for comfort, ensuring nothing compresses the breast tightly
    \item \textbf{Cabbage leaves:} a traditional remedy applied cool between feeds; many women find it soothing, though the evidence for its effectiveness is limited
    \item \textbf{Early professional help:} a midwife or lactation consultant can assist with latch and feeding technique before problems escalate
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Blocked ducts and localised milk stasis
    \item Mastitis, which develops in some women with severe or unresolved engorgement
    \item Breast abscess if mastitis is left untreated
    \item Sore, cracked, or damaged nipples from difficulty latching onto a firm breast
    \item Difficulty establishing breastfeeding, with some women stopping because of the pain
    \item A temporary dip in milk supply if engorgement interferes with milk removal and suppresses further production
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Breast engorgement is temporary and, with frequent feeding and the measures described above, usually settles within a day or two as milk supply adjusts to the baby's demands. Once breastfeeding is established, significant engorgement is uncommon. When engorgement is severe, untreated, or accompanied by ineffective feeding, the risk of progression to mastitis is higher; however, with early and appropriate management, the great majority of mothers recover fully and continue breastfeeding successfully.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Begin feeding early and often after birth, and feed on demand, including at night
    \item Ensure a good latch, with help from a midwife or lactation consultant if needed
    \item Avoid long gaps between feeds and do not routinely skip night feeds in the early weeks
    \item Express a little milk by hand if the breast feels overfull and the baby is struggling to attach
    \item Treat sore or cracked nipples early rather than enduring them
    \item If weaning, do so gradually and express just enough to remain comfortable
\end{itemize}
