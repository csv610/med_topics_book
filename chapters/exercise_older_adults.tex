\chapter{Exercise for Older Adults}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Exercise is one of the most important things older adults can do to stay healthy, independent, and strong. Physical activity in later life preserves muscle and bone, improves balance and mobility, reduces the risk of falls, and helps manage heart disease, diabetes, and arthritis. The Australian guidelines recommend that older adults are active on most days, including aerobic activity, muscle strengthening, and balance activities. It is never too late to start, and even small amounts of activity bring real benefits.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Physical inactivity increases with age, and a large proportion of older Australians are less active than recommended. Inactivity contributes to muscle loss, frailty, falls, and chronic disease, while active older people maintain independence and a better quality of life. Falls are a leading cause of injury in older people, and balance and strengthening exercise is one of the most effective ways to prevent them.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Ageing brings changes that exercise counteracts.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Muscle loss:} muscle mass declines with age, but resistance exercise preserves and rebuilds it
    \item \textbf{Bone loss:} weight-bearing activity slows bone loss and reduces fracture risk
    \item \textbf{Balance:} balance declines with age, and specific exercises improve it
    \item \textbf{Heart and lungs:} aerobic activity maintains fitness and heart health
    \item \textbf{Metabolism:} activity improves blood sugar, blood pressure, and cholesterol
    \item \textbf{Mood and brain:} exercise reduces depression risk and supports memory
    \item \textbf{The message:} frailty is not inevitable; much of the decline once attributed to age is actually due to inactivity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The recommended exercise program for older adults has several parts.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Aerobic activity on most days, accumulating at least 30 minutes of moderate activity
    \item Muscle-strengthening activities on at least two days a week
    \item Balance exercises for those at risk of falls
    \item Activities tailored to ability, health, and enjoyment
    \item Examples: brisk walking, swimming, cycling, chair exercises, and resistance bands
    \item Breaking activity into shorter sessions is fine
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Exercise is adjusted for individual conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heart disease, high blood pressure, and diabetes may need specific guidance
    \item Arthritis and joint problems respond well to low-impact exercise
    \item People with balance problems should exercise safely, with support
    \item After stroke or other illness, exercise is part of rehabilitation
    \item A GP or exercise physiologist can tailor the program
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Older adults with significant health conditions, or who have been inactive, should discuss exercise with a GP before starting a program. Any chest pain, severe breathlessness, or dizziness during exercise requires assessment.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain or pressure during or after activity
    \item Collapse, fainting, or severe dizziness
    \item Sudden weakness or difficulty speaking
    \item Severe breathlessness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Before starting, the program is planned.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Health review:} a GP assessment of conditions, medicines, and fitness
    \item \textbf{Falls risk:} assessment of balance and any recent falls
    \item \textbf{Individual planning:} activities matched to ability and interests
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to an exercise physiologist or physiotherapist for specific programs
    \item \textbf{Safety:} understanding warning symptoms and how to exercise safely
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Exercise is managed as part of everyday life.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Start gently:} short sessions with gradual progression
    \item \textbf{Strength:} resistance bands, light weights, or body-weight exercises twice weekly
    \item \textbf{Balance:} activities such as tai chi, standing on one foot, or heel-toe walking
    \item \textbf{Aerobic:} walking, swimming, or cycling most days
    \item \textbf{Group programs:} community exercise classes provide motivation and social connection
    \item \textbf{Safety:} good footwear, warm-ups, and listening to the body
    \item \textbf{Consistency:} regular activity matters more than intensity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Falls during exercise, which are minimised by safe practice
    \item Muscle soreness, which settles with gradual progression
    \item Injuries from unsuitable exercises
    \item The greater risks of inactivity: frailty, falls, and chronic disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Exercise in later life is remarkably beneficial. Strength and mobility improve within weeks, falls risk falls substantially with balance and strengthening programs, and the risk of heart disease, diabetes, and cognitive decline is reduced. Older adults who exercise regularly maintain independence longer and enjoy a better quality of life, and it is never too late to start.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Move regularly throughout the day
    \item Include strength and balance activities in the weekly routine
    \item Start with activities you enjoy and can sustain
    \item Exercise with a friend or in a group
    \item Use safe shoes and surroundings
    \item Build up gradually and rest when needed
    \item Discuss any health concerns with a doctor
\end{itemize}
