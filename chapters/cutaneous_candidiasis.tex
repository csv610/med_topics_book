\chapter{Cutaneous Candidiasis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cutaneous candidiasis is a skin infection caused by the yeast \textit{Candida}, most commonly \textit{Candida albicans}. Candida is a normal part of the skin and gut flora, but it can overgrow when the skin is warm, moist, or damaged, or when the immune system is weakened. The infection typically affects skin folds, including under the breasts, in the groin, armpits, and nappy area, causing a red, itchy, sometimes sore rash with satellite spots. Cutaneous candidiasis is common, easily treated with antifungal creams, and usually clears without long-term consequences.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cutaneous candidiasis is one of the most common fungal skin infections. It is more common in warm, humid climates and in summer. Risk increases with obesity, diabetes, sweating, antibiotic use, and immune suppression. Nappy rash caused by candida affects many babies. The infection occurs in both sexes and at all ages, and it frequently recurs in people with ongoing risk factors. Although uncomfortable, it rarely causes serious illness in otherwise healthy people.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Cutaneous candidiasis develops when candida overgrows on the skin.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\textit{Candida albicans}:} the most common species, a yeast normally present on skin and in the gut
    \item \textbf{Moisture and warmth:} skin folds provide the ideal environment for overgrowth
    \item \textbf{Skin damage:} friction, maceration, and irritants allow the yeast to penetrate
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} kill competing bacteria, allowing candida to multiply
    \item \textbf{Diabetes:} high blood sugar promotes fungal growth
    \item \textbf{Immune suppression:} steroid treatment, chemotherapy, and immune disease increase risk
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} the yeast invades the outer layers of skin, causing inflammation and the characteristic rash
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The rash has a characteristic appearance and location.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Red, moist, itchy patches in skin folds: under the breasts, groin, armpits, between the buttocks, or in the navel
    \item A well-defined edge, sometimes with peeling skin
    \item Small red "satellite" spots just beyond the main rash
    \item Cracking, soreness, and a burning sensation
    \item White, cheesy material in the folds in some cases
    \item In babies: nappy rash that persists, with redness in the folds and satellite spots
    \item Foul odour if there is secondary bacterial infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other rashes in the same areas are considered.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Tinea (ringworm), a fungal infection with a different pattern
    \item Eczema, particularly flexural and allergic eczema
    \item Contact dermatitis from soaps, creams, or nappies
    \item Psoriasis, including inverse psoriasis in the folds
    \item Bacterial infection, such as impetigo or erythrasma
    \item Intertrigo, simple friction and moisture rash without infection
    \item Seborrhoeic dermatitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Mild cases can be treated with over-the-counter antifungal creams, but see a doctor if the rash is severe, spreading, recurrent, or not improving after a week or two of treatment, or if there are signs of infection.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Spreading redness, heat, swelling, and pain with fever, which may indicate a serious bacterial infection of the skin
    \item Difficulty breathing, facial swelling, or other signs of a severe allergic reaction
    \item Sudden severe illness in a person with weakened immunity
\end{itemize}

Seek medical care if the rash is severe, widespread, recurrent, or not improving after a week or two of treatment, or if you have diabetes, are immunosuppressed, or are pregnant.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is usually clinical, with tests for uncertain or recurrent cases.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the appearance, location, and risk factors
    \item \textbf{Skin scraping:} examined under the microscope for yeast and hyphae
    \item \textbf{Culture:} to confirm the organism and species
    \item \textbf{Blood sugar testing:} to check for undiagnosed diabetes in recurrent cases
    \item \textbf{Immune assessment:} if infections are frequent or severe
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is simple and effective.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antifungal creams:} clotrimazole, miconazole, or ketoconazole applied for one to two weeks
    \item \textbf{Combination products:} creams containing an antifungal with a mild steroid for inflammation
    \item \textbf{Oral antifungals:} fluconazole for severe, extensive, or recurrent infection
    \item \textbf{Keeping the area dry:} the most important self-care measure
    \item \textbf{Barrier products:} protective creams and barrier nappy creams for babies
    \item \textbf{Treatment of the cause:} control of diabetes, review of antibiotics, and reduction of moisture
    \item \textbf{Continued treatment:} creams should be continued for a few days after the rash clears
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Secondary bacterial infection of broken skin
    \item Chronic or recurrent infection, particularly with ongoing risk factors
    \item Spread to other sites, including the mouth, genital area, or nails
    \item Invasive infection in severely immunocompromised people, which can be serious
    \item Scarring or pigment change in chronic cases
    \item Discomfort, embarrassment, and reduced quality of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Cutaneous candidiasis responds well to treatment, and most rashes clear within one to two weeks of antifungal therapy. Recurrence is common when the underlying conditions persist, such as diabetes, obesity, or ongoing antibiotic use, and managing these reduces recurrence. In healthy people, the infection causes no lasting harm. In people with severe immune suppression, prompt treatment is important to prevent spread.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep skin folds clean and dry, and dry thoroughly after washing
    \item Use breathable clothing and cotton underwear
    \item Change nappies frequently and use barrier creams
    \item Avoid tight, occlusive clothing and excessive sweating
    \item Control blood sugar if you have diabetes
    \item Use antibiotics only when needed
    \item Treat the full course of antifungal cream, even after the rash improves
    \item See a doctor for recurrent or persistent rashes
\end{itemize}
