\chapter{Pneumothorax}
\index{Pneumothorax}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Educational information; seek urgent professional advice for chest symptoms.}

\section*{What it is}
A pneumothorax occurs when air enters the space between the lung and chest wall, causing part or all of the lung to collapse. It may happen spontaneously, after chest injury or a procedure, or because of underlying lung disease. A tension pneumothorax can rapidly impair breathing and circulation.

\section*{Symptoms}
Possible symptoms include sudden one-sided sharp chest pain, breathlessness, rapid breathing, cough, fast heartbeat, or light-headedness. Severity varies with the size of the pneumothorax and the person’s lung function.

\section*{Diagnosis}
Clinicians assess breathing and circulation and may use oxygen measurement, chest X-ray, ultrasound, or CT. The diagnosis and urgency are based on the examination and imaging; a normal early test may not exclude a small pneumothorax.

\section*{Treatment}
Small, stable cases may be observed with repeat assessment, while larger or symptomatic cases may need needle aspiration or a chest drain. Underlying lung disease and recurrence risk are considered. Follow advice about travel, diving, smoking, activity, and follow-up imaging; do not fly or dive until cleared.

\section*{When to seek emergency care}
Get emergency help for sudden or worsening chest pain, breathlessness, blue or grey lips, fainting, confusion, severe weakness, or rapid deterioration, especially after chest injury or a procedure.
