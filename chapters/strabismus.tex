\section*{Definition and Overview}
Strabismus, commonly called squint or crossed eyes, is a condition in which the eyes do not align properly: one eye looks straight ahead while the other turns inward, outward, upward, or downward. The two eyes therefore send slightly different images to the brain, and although this is normal for a very young baby, by around four to six months of age the eyes should be well coordinated. Strabismus is not simply a cosmetic issue. If a misaligned eye is left untreated in childhood, the brain learns to ignore the image from that eye to avoid double vision, and the eye can permanently lose the ability to see clearly, a state called amblyopia or lazy eye. For this reason, early detection and treatment are important. Strabismus is common, affecting around 2 to 4 percent of children, and it also affects adults, in whom it may be a longstanding childhood condition or the result of new disease affecting the eyes, nerves, or brain. This chapter covers the types of strabismus, why it develops, how it is detected, the treatments available for children and adults, and when a sudden squint signals an emergency.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Strabismus is one of the commonest eye problems of childhood. Internationally, around 2 to 4 percent of children are affected, and in many countries the condition is among the main reasons for referral to paediatric eye services. It is detected through routine child health surveillance and the vision screening programs run by states and territories. Two patterns predominate: an inward turning eye, called esotropia, and an outward turning eye, called exotropia. Esotropia is the more common form in young children, and the intermittent outward drift of exotropia commonly appears around the preschool years or later. Some forms run in families, and a child with a parent or sibling who has a squint is at higher risk. Children born prematurely, those with a family history, and those with certain refractive errors are overrepresented. In adults, strabismus is much less common but is not rare, and may date from childhood or arise from conditions such as thyroid eye disease, diabetes-related nerve damage, stroke, or trauma. Rates do not differ meaningfully between boys and girls.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Seeing a single, three-dimensional world requires both eyes to point at the same object and for the brain to fuse the two images. Six small muscles control the movement of each eye, directed by nerve signals from the brain. Strabismus occurs when this system is disrupted: the muscles themselves may be abnormal, the nerves that drive them may fail, or the brain may fail to coordinate the two eyes.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Refractive error (accommodative esotropia):} a child with significant long-sightedness works hard to focus, and the effort of accommodation causes the eyes to cross inward
    \item \textbf{Intermittent exotropia:} an outward drift that occurs when a child is tired, daydreaming, or looking at distant objects, controlled at other times
    \item \textbf{Infantile esotropia:} a large inward deviation present from early infancy, with no obvious refractive cause
    \item \textbf{Neurological causes:} cerebral palsy, hydrocephalus, or other brain conditions that impair eye control
    \item \textbf{Cranial nerve palsy:} damage to the nerves supplying the eye muscles, for example from diabetes, high blood pressure, stroke, or trauma, causes a sudden or gradual misalignment
    \item \textbf{Thyroid eye disease:} inflammation and swelling of the eye muscles, seen with overactive or underactive thyroid function, causing restriction and double vision
    \item \textbf{Eye trauma or surgery:} direct injury to the muscles or their attachments
    \item \textbf{Genetic syndromes:} Down syndrome, and various chromosomal and congenital conditions, are associated with a higher rate of strabismus
    \item \textbf{Heredity:} a family history increases risk without following a simple pattern
\end{itemize}

In childhood the crucial danger is amblyopia: if the brain suppresses the image from the deviating eye for long enough, the visual pathway for that eye never develops normally, and the vision cannot be fully recovered later.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Strabismus can be constant or intermittent, and may be present from infancy or appear later.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Misalignment of the eyes:} one eye turns inward, outward, upward, or downward while the other fixes on an object
    \item \textbf{Intermittent drift:} an eye that wanders only when tired, stressed, or looking into the distance
    \item \textbf{Double vision:} adults, and some older children, see two images; younger children usually suppress the second image instead
    \item \textbf{Closing or covering one eye:} a child may close one eye, tilt the head, or turn the face to avoid double vision or glare
    \item \textbf{Poor depth perception:} difficulty judging distance, catching balls, or climbing stairs safely
    \item \textbf{Head tilt or turn:} a habitual head posture that compensates for the eye position
    \item \textbf{Headaches and eye strain:} more common in older children and adults with certain types of strabismus
    \item \textbf{Light sensitivity:} some children with exotropia close one eye in bright sunlight
    \item \textbf{False appearance of a squint:} wide nasal skin folds can make a baby's eyes appear crossed when they are actually aligned, a normal finding that eye care professionals distinguish easily
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A suspicion of strabismus must be separated from conditions that mimic it or coexist with it.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pseudostrabismus:} wide epicanthal skin folds or a broad nasal bridge make the eyes look crossed; verified by the cover test, which shows normal alignment
    \item \textbf{Refractive errors:} uncorrected long-sightedness or short-sightedness can cause or mimic squint; measured with retinoscopy after eye drops
    \item \textbf{Amblyopia without strabismus:} one eye may be lazy because of a large difference in prescription between the eyes, without obvious misalignment
    \item \textbf{Retinoblastoma or other eye disease:} a white pupil, poor vision, or a turning eye in an infant can rarely signal a serious tumour and requires urgent ophthalmology review
    \item \textbf{Congenital cataract:} a cloudy lens from birth causes poor vision and a wandering eye, and needs urgent surgery
    \item \textbf{Nystagmus:} involuntary eye flickering can accompany or be mistaken for misalignment
    \item \textbf{Acquired neurological disease:} a suddenly appearing squint or double vision in an adult may be due to stroke, aneurysm, myasthenia, or tumour and requires urgent assessment
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any child who may have a squint should be assessed by a general practitioner, optometrist, or ophthalmologist, ideally before school age so that vision can be preserved. A parent who notices a turning eye, persistent head tilt, double vision, or one eye closing in sunlight should raise it with their doctor, who can arrange a specialist referral. In many countries, children can have eyesight and eye health checks through the government-funded scheme and free visits to local optometrists, and routine child health checks include looking for a squint. A child whose eye turns constantly from early infancy, or who is at high risk because of prematurity or family history, should be seen early.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A sudden new squint or double vision in an adult or older child, which may indicate stroke
    \item A squint with severe headache, weakness, difficulty speaking, or facial droop
    \item A squint after a blow to the head or eye
    \item A white glow or pupil change in the eye of an infant or child, seen in photographs or examination
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of strabismus is made by examination; the aim is to measure the deviation and check the vision of each eye.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and vision check:} the clinician asks about when the squint started, whether it is constant or intermittent, and tests each eye's vision
    \item \textbf{Cover test:} the patient fixes on an object while each eye is alternately covered; the movement of the uncovered eye reveals the deviation
    \item \textbf{Corneal light reflex test:} light reflections from the two corneas are compared to judge alignment
    \item \textbf{Refraction:} eye drops relax the focusing muscles and allow precise measurement of the prescription, detecting the long-sightedness that drives accommodative squint
    \item \textbf{Eye movement assessment:} checking the range and smoothness of movement in all directions identifies muscle or nerve problems
    \item \textbf{Retinal examination:} the back of each eye is examined to exclude cataracts, tumours, and other disease
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT or MRI scans are used when a brain, nerve, or orbit cause is suspected, especially in adults with sudden onset
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} thyroid function and other tests are ordered if systemic disease is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment aims to straighten the eyes, restore or preserve vision, and prevent amblyopia. It usually combines vision correction, exercises, and sometimes surgery.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Glasses:} correcting long-sightedness often straightens an accommodative squint without further treatment
    \item \textbf{Eye patching or atropine drops:} occluding the stronger eye forces the amblyopic eye to work and improves its vision; patching hours are carefully prescribed and reviewed
    \item \textbf{Orthoptic exercises:} eye coordination training, delivered by an orthoptist, strengthens the ability to control the squint and fuse images
    \item \textbf{Prism lenses:} prisms shift the image into alignment and can relieve double vision in adults, used alone or before surgery
    \item \textbf{Botulinum toxin injections:} injected into an overactive eye muscle, these temporarily weaken it and can straighten the eye; used particularly in certain forms of squint
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} tightening or loosening the eye muscles realigns the eyes; the commonest treatment for infantile and many other squints, usually performed in childhood
    \item \textbf{Adult treatment:} surgery, prisms, and exercises can also help adults, improving appearance, double vision, and the ability to use both eyes together
    \item \textbf{Treating the underlying cause:} managing thyroid disease, diabetes, or blood pressure is essential where these drive the problem
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated strabismus can have lasting effects, and treatment itself carries small risks.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Amblyopia:} permanent loss of vision in the deviating eye if suppression is not interrupted during the critical childhood years
    \item \textbf{Poor binocular vision:} loss of depth perception, affecting sporting performance and occupational tasks such as driving
    \item \textbf{Double vision:} persistent diplopia, more common in adult-onset strabismus, causing difficulty with everyday tasks
    \item \textbf{Psychosocial effects:} children and adults with a visible squint may experience teasing, reduced self-esteem, and social disadvantage
    \item \textbf{Treatment complications:} eye irritation from patching or drops, and surgical risks such as infection, over- or under-correction, and, rarely, a return of the squint
    \item \textbf{Missed underlying disease:} a delay in investigating an adult's sudden squint can miss a stroke or tumour
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for childhood strabismus is generally good when treatment begins early. Most children achieve straight or near-straight eyes, and with glasses, patching, and orthoptic therapy the majority regain good vision in both eyes. Some degree of residual misalignment or amblyopia persists in a minority, and surgery may need repeating because the eyes can drift again during growth. Vision that is preserved in childhood usually stays stable in adult life. In adults, the prognosis depends on the cause: squints from diabetic or vascular nerve damage often improve with time, while those from thyroid disease or trauma may need ongoing treatment. Even longstanding adult squints can be successfully straightened, and most people report improved confidence and function after treatment. Regular eye reviews are recommended throughout childhood to catch recurrence, and any new double vision in adult life warrants urgent assessment.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend routine child health checks, which include looking for squints and vision problems
    \item Take advantage of the free eye checks available for children through government schemes and community optometrists
    \item Mention any family history of squint, lazy eye, or glasses use at a young age to the child's doctor
    \item Watch for signs such as head tilting, eye closing, or one eye wandering, and report them early
    \item Follow the prescribed patching or drop schedule exactly, as it is time-limited and critical to vision
    \item Keep follow-up appointments with the orthoptist or ophthalmologist, even when the eye looks straight
    \item Protect the eyes with appropriate headwear and seatbelts to reduce the risk of traumatic squint
\end{itemize}
