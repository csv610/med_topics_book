\chapter{Delusions}
\index{Delusions}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A delusion is a firmly held belief that is not based in reality, is resistant to reason or contrary evidence, and is not explained by a person's culture or religion. Delusions are a symptom of several mental health conditions, most notably schizophrenia and delusional disorder, but they can also occur in severe depression, mania, dementia, and drug-induced states. Delusions may be bizarre or plausible, and they can cause significant distress and difficulty functioning. This chapter explains the types of delusions, their causes, and how they are assessed and treated.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Delusions occur across a wide range of conditions. Schizophrenia affects around 1\% of the population and commonly involves delusions, as do other psychotic disorders. Delusional disorder is rarer, affecting around 0.2\% of people. Delusions also occur in a minority of people with severe depression or bipolar disorder, in dementia, and with substance use such as stimulants. Persecutory delusions are the most common type. Delusions can develop at any age but are most frequent in young adults for schizophrenia and in older people for dementia-related psychosis.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Brain chemistry:} disturbances in dopamine and other neurotransmitter systems underlie psychotic symptoms
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} psychotic disorders run in families, and specific gene variations increase risk
    \item \textbf{Psychological mechanisms:} biases in reasoning, such as jumping to conclusions and difficulty considering alternatives, contribute to delusion formation
    \item \textbf{Stress and trauma:} stressful events and childhood adversity increase vulnerability
    \item \textbf{Substance use:} stimulants such as amphetamines, cannabis, and other drugs can trigger delusions
    \item \textbf{Brain disease:} dementia, stroke, Parkinson's disease, and brain injury can cause delusions
    \item \textbf{Sleep deprivation and sensory deprivation:} can precipitate psychotic symptoms in vulnerable people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A firmly held belief that is false, fixed, and resistant to evidence
    \item Persecutory delusions: believing one is being harmed, followed, or plotted against
    \item Grandiose delusions: believing one has special powers, wealth, or importance
    \item Somatic delusions: believing one has a serious illness or bodily abnormality
    \item Referential delusions: believing that events or comments are directed at oneself
    \item Delusions of guilt, jealousy, or being controlled by outside forces
    \item Associated symptoms may include hallucinations, disorganised thinking, anxiety, low mood, or agitation
    \item Impaired work, social, and self-care functioning
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Schizophrenia, when delusions occur with hallucinations, disorganised speech, and negative symptoms
    \item Delusional disorder, in which delusions are the main feature without other psychotic symptoms
    \item Mood disorders: severe depression with psychotic features, and mania with grandiose delusions
    \item Dementia and other brain disorders
    \item Substance-induced psychosis from stimulants, cannabis, or withdrawal states
    \item Cultural or religious beliefs, which must be distinguished from true delusions
    \item Obsessive-compulsive disorder, in which intrusive thoughts are recognised as unreasonable
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with persistent, distressing, or unusual beliefs that affect their wellbeing or behaviour should be assessed by a doctor, ideally a psychiatrist. Early treatment of psychosis improves outcomes. Family and friends should encourage a gentle, non-judgemental approach and seek help rather than arguing with the delusion.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Risk of harm to self or others, including suicidal thoughts or threats
    \item Severe agitation, aggression, or behaviour that is dangerous
    \item A person who cannot care for themselves or is severely disorganised
    \item Symptoms that develop suddenly with confusion, fever, or drug use (possible medical emergency)
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Support lines:} a local crisis service and the Mental Health Line (1800 011 511) provide support.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychiatric assessment:} detailed interview covering the beliefs, onset, mood, thoughts, and functional impact
    \item \textbf{Physical examination and tests:} to exclude medical causes such as infection, metabolic disturbance, or brain disease
    \item \textbf{Substance screening:} urine drug tests where substance use is possible
    \item \textbf{Brain imaging:} CT or MRI where dementia, stroke, or injury is suspected
    \item \textbf{Collateral information:} accounts from family and friends, which are valuable in assessing fixed beliefs
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} evaluation of danger to self or others
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antipsychotic medication:} the mainstay of treatment for psychotic disorders, reducing or eliminating delusions
    \item \textbf{Psychological therapy:} cognitive-behavioural therapy for psychosis helps people examine and cope with delusional beliefs
    \item \textbf{Family support and psychoeducation:} helping families understand the illness and respond helpfully
    \item \textbf{Treating the underlying condition:} mood stabilisers or antidepressants for delusions in mood disorders, and treatment of dementia or substance use
    \item \textbf{Community mental health care:} case management, crisis support, and rehabilitation services
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} for severe episodes or risk of harm
    \item \textbf{Recovery-focused care:} social support, employment assistance, and relapse prevention planning
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Social withdrawal, relationship breakdown, and stigma
    \item Difficulty holding employment or studying
    \item Self-harm or suicide, particularly with delusions of guilt or persecution
    \item Rarely, violence in response to persecutory beliefs
    \item Neglect of physical health and self-care
    \item Substance use as a coping strategy
    \item Side effects of antipsychotic medication
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With early and consistent treatment, most people with delusions improve substantially, and many achieve remission. Antipsychotic medication is effective for the majority, and psychological therapy adds further benefit. Delusional disorder has a more variable course but responds to treatment in many cases. Some people experience recurrent episodes requiring maintenance treatment. With good support, many people with psychotic disorders recover well, work, and live fulfilling lives, though outcomes are best with early intervention and adherence to treatment.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek help early for unusual beliefs, suspiciousness, or changes in thinking
    \item Take prescribed medication regularly and attend follow-up appointments
    \item Avoid stimulant drugs and heavy cannabis use, which can trigger psychosis
    \item Maintain regular sleep, activity, and social connection
    \item Manage stress and seek support during difficult periods
    \item Family members: respond calmly, do not argue with the delusion, and encourage professional help
    \item Have a relapse plan agreed with the treating team
\end{itemize}
