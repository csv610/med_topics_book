\chapter{Drinking Water and Your Health}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Water is essential for life: the body uses it to regulate temperature, transport nutrients, remove waste, and keep organs functioning. Drinking enough water supports health in every system, while dehydration impairs concentration, physical performance, and health. In Australia, tap water is safe, affordable, and the best choice for hydration, and most of it is fluoridated, which protects teeth. How much water a person needs depends on age, activity, weather, and health. This chapter explains why water matters, how much to drink, and how to stay well hydrated.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Most Australians drink some water daily, but many do not drink enough, especially older adults, whose thirst sensation declines, and children. Soft drinks and other sugary drinks contribute to obesity and tooth decay, and replacing them with water is a key public health message. Dehydration is common in heatwaves and among older people and people with chronic illness. Safe water supplies are a cornerstone of public health in Australia, with universal access to treated, fluoridated water in most areas.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Body water:} water makes up about 60\% of body weight and is vital for every cell and organ
    \item \textbf{Functions:} temperature regulation, digestion, circulation, kidney function, and joint lubrication
    \item \textbf{Fluid balance:} the body balances intake against losses through urine, sweat, and breathing
    \item \textbf{Thirst:} the sensation of thirst signals that fluid is needed
    \item \textbf{Hydration needs:} around 2.6 litres of fluid a day for men and 2.1 litres for women, more in heat and with exercise
    \item \textbf{Fluoride:} most Australian tap water contains fluoride, which prevents tooth decay
    \item \textbf{Dehydration:} occurs when losses exceed intake
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Adequate hydration supports energy, concentration, digestion, and kidney health
    \item Clear or pale urine is a good sign of adequate hydration
    \item Signs of mild dehydration: thirst, dry mouth, dark urine, headache, and fatigue
    \item Severe dehydration: dizziness, confusion, rapid heart rate, and reduced urine output
    \item Water is the best drink: no kilojoules, no sugar, and it quenches thirst effectively
    \item Needs increase with heat, exercise, pregnancy, breastfeeding, and illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dehydration versus overhydration, both of which can cause symptoms
    \item Thirst from dehydration versus from high blood sugar in diabetes
    \item Water versus sugary drinks, sports drinks, and energy drinks
    \item Tap water versus bottled water, which has no health advantage
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for persistent thirst, very dark urine, or symptoms of dehydration, and for medical advice on fluid intake in conditions such as kidney or heart disease, where fluid needs differ.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe dehydration: confusion, drowsiness, fainting, or no urine output
    \item An infant or older person with signs of serious dehydration
    \item Excessive thirst with weight loss, which may indicate diabetes
    \item Collapse or heat stroke in hot weather
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} hydration signs such as dry mouth, skin turgor, and urine output
    \item \textbf{Urine tests:} concentration and colour
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} electrolytes and kidney function where dehydration is significant
    \item \textbf{Assessment of fluid needs:} individualised advice for age, activity, and health conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Drink water regularly:} with meals and throughout the day
    \item \textbf{Carry a water bottle:} makes drinking easy
    \item \textbf{Drink more in heat and with exercise:} before, during, and after activity
    \item \textbf{Increase intake when unwell:} with fever, vomiting, or diarrhoea
    \item \textbf{Replace sugary drinks with water:} improves weight and dental health
    \item \textbf{Encourage children and older adults to drink:} their thirst cues are less reliable
    \item \textbf{Drink tap water:} it is safe, fluoridated, and free
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dehydration, with impaired concentration and physical performance
    \item Kidney stones and urinary tract infections from chronic low intake
    \item Constipation
    \item Heat illness in hot weather
    \item Overhydration in rare cases of excessive drinking
    \item Tooth decay and weight gain from sugary drink consumption
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Staying well hydrated is simple and powerfully protective of health. Most people can meet their needs by drinking water regularly and responding to thirst, and the benefits include better energy, kidney health, digestion, and concentration. Replacing sugary drinks with water improves weight and dental health. With sensible habits, hydration is easily maintained across all ages.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Drink water throughout the day, not only when thirsty
    \item Keep water accessible at home, work, and school
    \item Drink extra water in hot weather and during exercise
    \item Offer drinks to children and older adults regularly
    \item Check urine colour as a simple guide
    \item Choose water over soft drinks, sports drinks, and juices
    \item Drink fluoridated tap water for dental health
    \item Follow medical advice on fluid limits if you have heart or kidney disease
\end{itemize}
