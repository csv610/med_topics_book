\chapter{Blood Tests}
\index{Blood Tests}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A blood test is a laboratory examination of a sample of blood. It is one of the most commonly used tools in medicine, providing information about many different aspects of health. Blood tests can measure the cells in the blood, the chemicals and salts dissolved in it, how well organs such as the liver and kidneys are working, and whether the blood is able to clot properly. They are used to screen for disease, diagnose illness, monitor chronic conditions, and check for the effects of medicines. Because blood circulates to every organ, it reflects much of what is happening throughout the body, making blood tests a uniquely versatile investigation.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Blood tests are among the most frequent investigations performed in healthcare worldwide, with billions of tests carried out every year. They are used across all age groups, from newborn screening to monitoring frailty in older adults. Routine blood tests are a standard part of antenatal care, pre-operative assessment, and annual health checks. Conditions commonly detected or monitored through blood tests, such as diabetes, high cholesterol, anaemia, and chronic kidney disease, affect a large proportion of adults, which makes these investigations a cornerstone of both primary care and hospital medicine.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Blood is composed of cells and plasma, and tests examine different parts of it:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full blood count:} measures red cells, white cells, and platelets -- helps detect anaemia, infection, and bleeding or clotting disorders
    \item \textbf{Electrolytes and renal function:} measures sodium, potassium, creatinine, and urea -- reflects kidney health and body fluid balance
    \item \textbf{Liver function tests:} enzymes and proteins affected by liver disease, alcohol, or medicines
    \item \textbf{Glucose and HbA1c:} used to diagnose and monitor diabetes
    \item \textbf{Lipids:} cholesterol and triglycerides -- assess cardiovascular risk
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory markers:} such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) -- indicate the presence and severity of inflammation or infection
    \item \textbf{Coagulation studies:} measure how quickly blood clots, used before surgery and during anticoagulant therapy
    \item \textbf{Thyroid tests:} thyroid-stimulating hormone and thyroid hormones, used to investigate energy, weight, and mood symptoms
    \item \textbf{Iron studies:} ferritin and related measures that help diagnose iron deficiency and overload
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The experience of having a blood test is similar regardless of which tests are ordered:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A brief stinging sensation as the needle enters the vein
    \item A small bruise or tender spot at the site afterwards
    \item Possible light-headedness, particularly in those who are anxious about needles
    \item Some tests require fasting beforehand, which can cause hunger or mild weakness
    \item There are usually no other symptoms; the results themselves are often the only notable feature
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because a single blood test can be affected by many things, results are interpreted cautiously:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A mildly abnormal value may be a normal variation or the result of diet, exercise, or recent illness rather than disease
    \item Several conditions can produce the same pattern of results, so tests are combined and read in context
    \item Medications, herbal supplements, alcohol, and pregnancy can all alter results
    \item Errors in collection, handling, or laboratory analysis can occur, though they are uncommon
    \item Borderline results often need to be repeated or interpreted with the full clinical picture
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
You should speak with a doctor if:

\begin{itemize}
    \item You have symptoms that have not been explained, and a blood test may be appropriate
    \item You have received results but do not understand what they mean or what to do next
    \item A result was abnormal and you have been advised to follow up, but have not yet done so
    \item You experience new symptoms after a test, such as worsening pain, swelling, or bleeding at the site
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fainting or collapse that does not recover quickly after a blood test
    \item Difficulty breathing, chest pain, or any sudden severe symptom
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Choosing and interpreting blood tests is a clinical skill:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} guide which tests are needed and in what order
    \item \textbf{Test selection:} chosen for their sensitivity and specificity for the suspected condition
    \item \textbf{Timing and preparation:} fasting, time of day, and medication adjustments where required
    \item \textbf{Combined panels:} groups of tests, such as liver or thyroid panels, are often more informative than single tests
    \item \textbf{Repeat testing:} confirms results that are borderline or unexpected
    \item \textbf{Specialist review:} complex or persistently abnormal results may be referred to a pathologist or other specialist
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A blood test is a diagnostic aid, not a treatment -- results guide decisions about further investigation or therapy
    \item Abnormal results may lead to lifestyle advice, medication, specialist referral, or imaging
    \item Normal results may be reassuring, but if symptoms persist, further testing may still be needed
    \item Chronic conditions such as diabetes or kidney disease are monitored with scheduled repeat blood tests
    \item Results are shared with the patient, and the plan for follow-up is made clear
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bruising, haematoma, or bleeding at the puncture site
    \item Infection at the puncture site, which is uncommon
    \item Fainting or feeling faint during or after collection
    \item False reassurance from a normal result, which may delay diagnosis of a condition that blood tests cannot reliably detect
    \item Unnecessary procedures and anxiety resulting from false-positive results
    \item Cumulative blood loss, which is rarely significant except in very ill or anaemic patients
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most blood tests are completed safely in minutes and have no lasting effects. Their value depends on how the results are used: timely action on abnormal results can lead to earlier treatment and better outcomes in conditions such as anaemia, diabetes, and kidney disease. Because every test has limits, results are most reliable when combined with a clinician's assessment. In most cases a single round of testing answers the clinical question, while chronic disease requires ongoing monitoring over months or years.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend recommended health checks and screening programs that include blood tests
    \item Ask what each test is for and how to prepare, including any fasting requirements
    \item Keep a personal record of your results so changes over time can be seen
    \item Tell your doctor about all medicines and supplements you take
    \item Ask for results to be explained in plain language
    \item Raise any concerns about needle anxiety so the test can be made more comfortable
\end{itemize}
