\chapter{Avian Influenza}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Avian influenza, sometimes called bird flu, is an infectious disease caused by influenza A viruses that occur naturally in birds. Wild waterfowl carry many of these viruses without becoming sick, but certain subtypes, especially H5N1, H5N6, H7N9 and H9N2, can cause severe disease and large outbreaks in domestic poultry such as chickens and ducks.

Most avian influenza viruses do not infect people, and human cases remain rare and usually follow close contact with infected birds or contaminated environments. When infection does occur in people, it can range from mild conjunctivitis or a flu-like illness to severe pneumonia and respiratory failure. Avian influenza is monitored closely because the viruses mutate, and a strain that spreads easily between people could cause a pandemic.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Human cases of avian influenza occur sporadically, almost always in people with direct contact with sick or dead poultry, such as poultry workers and people living on farms or near live animal markets. Since the late 1990s, H5N1 has caused hundreds of reported human cases, mainly in parts of Asia and Africa, with occasional spread to other regions via wild birds and the poultry trade. H7N9 caused human infections mainly in China.

Among reported cases, H5N1 has caused severe disease and a high death rate, though mild and unnoticed infections may make the true death rate lower. Human-to-human transmission has so far been rare, limited and generally does not spread beyond close contacts. The main risk is to people exposed to infected birds.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Avian influenza is caused by influenza A viruses. Influenza A viruses are classified by two surface proteins, haemagglutinin (H) and neuraminidase (N), giving subtypes such as H5N1 and H7N9. Wild aquatic birds are the natural reservoir, and domestic poultry amplify the virus when outbreaks occur.

When a person is infected, the virus attaches to cells lining the respiratory tract and multiplies, triggering a strong immune response. Avian viruses prefer receptors in the deeper parts of the human lung, which may explain why they often cause severe lower respiratory disease. The main danger is mutation or genetic reassortment that allows the virus to spread efficiently between people.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms typically appear within days of exposure and initially resemble seasonal flu.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever and chills
    \item Cough and sore throat
    \item Muscle aches, headache and severe fatigue
    \item Conjunctivitis (red, sore, watery eyes), which is common with some subtypes such as H7
    \item Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea and abdominal pain in some cases
    \item Rapid progression to breathlessness, chest pain and a cough producing bloody sputum in severe disease
    \item Signs of pneumonia, respiratory failure or organ failure in the worst cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The early symptoms overlap with many common and more likely conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Seasonal influenza
    \item COVID-19 and other respiratory viruses (respiratory syncytial virus, rhinovirus)
    \item Bacterial pneumonia
    \item Other zoonotic infections, such as psittacosis, leptospirosis or Q fever
    \item Tuberculosis
    \item Sepsis from any cause in severe presentations
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who develops fever or respiratory symptoms after contact with sick or dead birds, or after visiting a poultry farm or live animal market, should seek medical assessment promptly and mention that exposure. Treatment is most effective when started early.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, shortness of breath or chest pain
    \item Coughing up blood
    \item Confusion, drowsiness or seizures
    \item Blue lips or skin, or a feeling of faintness
    \item Persistent high fever that is not improving
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis requires laboratory testing because avian influenza cannot be distinguished from other respiratory infections on symptoms alone.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and exposure assessment:} travel, contact with birds and symptoms
    \item \textbf{Respiratory sampling:} nose and throat swabs, or deeper samples such as sputum
    \item \textbf{Molecular testing:} PCR to detect influenza A and to identify the subtype
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, inflammatory markers and organ function
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} to look for pneumonia and assess its severity
    \item \textbf{Public health notification:} confirmed cases are reported to public health authorities for contact tracing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Most care is supportive, with antiviral medicines used where they can help.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antiviral medicines:} such as oseltamivir, which work best when started within 48 hours of symptoms
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} rest, fluids and medicines for fever and pain
    \item \textbf{Oxygen therapy:} for people with low oxygen levels
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} for those with pneumonia or breathing difficulty
    \item \textbf{Intensive care:} for severe respiratory failure, sometimes including breathing support
    \item \textbf{Treatment of complications:} such as antibiotics for secondary bacterial infection
    \item \textbf{Isolation precautions:} to reduce the risk of spread in healthcare settings
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe viral pneumonia and acute respiratory distress syndrome
    \item Sepsis and multi-organ failure
    \item Secondary bacterial pneumonia after the viral illness
    \item Conjunctivitis and eye complications
    \item Kidney or liver dysfunction in severe cases
    \item Death, which occurs in a substantial proportion of severe reported cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the virus subtype, the person's age and health, and how quickly care is started. H5N1 has caused severe disease in many of the people infected, though the death rate among all infected people is uncertain because mild cases may go unreported. Early antiviral treatment and intensive care improve the chances of recovery. For people with mild infection, symptoms typically settle like an ordinary flu over a week or two.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Because there is no widely available vaccine against avian influenza for humans, prevention centres on avoiding exposure and basic hygiene.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Avoid contact with sick or dead birds, and keep children away from poultry
    \item Wash hands thoroughly after handling birds, eggs or raw poultry
    \item Cook poultry and eggs thoroughly before eating
    \item Use gloves and protective equipment when handling birds in at-risk settings
    \item Report unusual bird deaths to local authorities so outbreaks can be controlled
    \item Keep up to date with seasonal influenza vaccination, which does not prevent avian flu but reduces the risk of dangerous co-infection
    \item See a doctor promptly and mention bird exposure if you develop flu symptoms
\end{itemize}
