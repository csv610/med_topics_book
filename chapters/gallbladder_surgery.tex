\chapter{Gallbladder Surgery}
\index{Gallbladder Surgery}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Gallbladder surgery, medically called cholecystectomy, is the surgical removal of the gallbladder. It is one of the most common operations in many countries and the standard treatment for symptomatic gallstones, gallbladder inflammation (cholecystitis), and complications such as pancreatitis caused by stones. The operation is almost always performed by keyhole (laparoscopic) surgery through three or four small incisions, which allows a quick recovery, and most people go home the same day or the next day. The gallbladder is not essential: bile flows directly from the liver into the intestine after removal, and most people notice no difference in digestion. This chapter explains why the surgery is needed, what it involves, and what recovery is like.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Gallbladder removal is performed more than 30,000 times a year in many countries, making it one of the most frequently performed operations. Around 10--15% of adults have gallstones, and while most never need surgery, symptomatic stones, repeated colic, cholecystitis, and gallstone pancreatitis account for the operations. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is now the standard, with open surgery used in only a minority of complex cases. The operation is safe: serious complications occur in well under 5% of elective cases, and day-case or overnight surgery is the norm.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Why remove the gallbladder:} it is the source of the problem; stones form in it, and leaving it in place allows recurrent colic, cholecystitis, pancreatitis, and cholangitis
    \item \textbf{Laparoscopic technique:} carbon dioxide gas inflates the abdomen, a camera and instruments are passed through small incisions, and the gallbladder is dissected free and removed through one of the incisions
    \item \textbf{Open technique:} a larger incision under the ribs is used when keyhole surgery is unsafe or fails, such as with severe inflammation or previous surgery
    \item \textbf{The cystic duct and artery:} the surgeon clips and divides these, detaching the gallbladder from the bile duct and liver
    \item \textbf{The biliary system after removal:} bile produced by the liver flows directly into the intestine; the gallbladder is not needed for survival or normal digestion
    \item \textbf{Timing:} elective surgery for symptomatic stones, early surgery for cholecystitis, and delayed surgery after pancreatitis has settled
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative assessment:} blood tests, imaging, and review of medicines and anaesthetic fitness
    \item \textbf{Anaesthesia:} general anaesthesia for all approaches
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features are those of the procedure and recovery.
\begin{itemize}
    \item The operation takes 1--2 hours under general anaesthesia
    \item Laparoscopic surgery uses 3--4 small incisions; open surgery uses one larger incision
    \item Most people go home the same day or the next morning
    \item Mild shoulder and abdominal pain for a few days from the gas and incisions
    \item Small dressings over the wounds, with dissolving or removable stitches
    \item A return to light activity within days and normal activity within 2--4 weeks
    \item Some people have looser stools or need to moderate fatty meals for a time
    \item The symptoms that prompted surgery, such as colic, resolve
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The decision for surgery is based on the diagnosis.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Symptomatic gallstones with biliary colic, which surgery cures
    \item Acute cholecystitis, where surgery is usually needed
    \item Gallstone pancreatitis, where the gallbladder is removed to prevent recurrence
    \item Bile duct stones, which may need ERCP before or instead of surgery
    \item Gallbladder polyps, with surgery for larger ones
    \item Other causes of upper abdominal pain, which should be excluded before surgery
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone considering gallbladder surgery should have the procedure explained fully by the surgeon, including the risks and the expected recovery. Pre-operative review is required for people with other medical conditions.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain, fever, or chills after surgery, which may indicate a complication such as a bile leak or infection
    \item Jaundice or dark urine after surgery
    \item Heavy bleeding from the wounds, or a wound that opens
    \item Difficulty breathing or chest pain
    \item Vomiting that prevents keeping fluids down
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative imaging:} ultrasound confirms stones and gallbladder inflammation; MRCP or ERCP assesses duct stones
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} liver function, inflammatory markers, and pancreatic enzymes
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative assessment:} general health review, including heart and lung checks and medicines
    \item \textbf{Intraoperative cholangiogram:} an X-ray during surgery to check the bile ducts for stones
    \item \textbf{Post-operative review:} wound checks and follow-up in the outpatient clinic
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Before surgery:} fasting as instructed, stopping some medicines, and arranging support at home
    \item \textbf{The operation:} laparoscopic in most cases; converted to open if needed for safety
    \item \textbf{After surgery:} pain relief, early eating and walking, and discharge when comfortable
    \item \textbf{Wound care:} keep dressings dry, shower as advised, and report redness or discharge
    \item \textbf{Activity:} light walking immediately, returning to work in 1--3 weeks depending on the job
    \item \textbf{Diet:} a light diet initially; most people return to a normal diet, moderating fatty meals if needed
    \item \textbf{When duct stones are present:} ERCP may remove them before, during, or after the operation
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} a review with the surgeon and pathology results of the removed gallbladder
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding, infection, and wound problems
    \item Bile leak, which usually settles with drainage but occasionally needs further surgery
    \item Injury to the bile duct, the most serious complication, occurring in well under 1% of operations and usually repaired at the time
    \item Retained stones in the duct, treated with ERCP
    \item Anaesthetic complications
    \item Post-cholecystectomy diarrhoea or digestive change in a minority
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Gallbladder surgery is safe and effective, and it cures the symptoms of gallstone disease in the vast majority of people. Recovery from laparoscopic surgery is quick, with most people back to normal life within weeks, and the long-term outlook is excellent: the gallbladder is not essential, and most people have no digestive problems at all. Serious complications are uncommon, and even open and emergency surgery have good outcomes. For most people, the operation is a one-off cure that prevents the recurrent pain and serious complications of gallstones.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discuss the operation, its risks, and recovery with the surgeon before consenting
    \item Tell the anaesthetist about all medicines, allergies, and health conditions
    \item Follow the fasting and medicine instructions before surgery
    \item Arrange help at home for the first days after discharge
    \item Follow wound care and activity advice
    \item Report fever, severe pain, jaundice, or bleeding promptly
    \item Return for scheduled follow-up
    \item If you have gallstones and choose not to have surgery, seek urgent care for severe or persistent pain
\end{itemize}
