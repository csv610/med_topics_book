\chapter{Haematuria}
\index{Haematuria}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Haematuria is the presence of blood in the urine. It can be visible to the naked eye (macroscopic or gross haematuria), giving the urine a pink, red, or cola colour, or it can be invisible and detected only by urine testing (microscopic haematuria). Blood in the urine is a common symptom with many possible causes, ranging from harmless ones such as vigorous exercise or a urinary tract infection to serious conditions such as kidney disease, kidney stones, and cancers of the bladder or kidney. Because haematuria can be a sign of significant disease, it should always be investigated, even when it is painless and even when it occurs only once.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Haematuria is common. Around 1 in 10 adults will have blood in the urine at some stage, and visible haematuria accounts for a significant proportion of urology referrals. In many countries, bladder and kidney cancers are among the leading cancers, and haematuria is the most common presenting symptom, particularly for bladder cancer. The risk of an underlying cancer increases with age, smoking, and male sex. Microscopic haematuria is found incidentally in around 2--5\% of routine urine tests, and most causes are benign, but investigation is still recommended.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Blood can enter the urine anywhere along the urinary tract, from the kidneys to the urethra.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urinary tract infection:} inflammation of the bladder (cystitis) or kidney (pyelonephritis) causes bleeding
    \item \textbf{Kidney stones:} stones irritate the lining of the kidney or ureter as they pass
    \item \textbf{Kidney disease:} glomerulonephritis and other kidney conditions cause blood and protein in the urine
    \item \textbf{Bladder and kidney cancer:} tumours bleed, often painlessly
    \item \textbf{Prostate conditions:} in men, an enlarged prostate or prostate cancer can cause blood in the urine
    \item \textbf{Trauma:} injury to the kidney, bladder, or urethra
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} blood thinners such as warfarin and some painkillers can cause bleeding
    \item \textbf{Exercise:} strenuous exercise can cause transient haematuria
    \item \textbf{Other:} benign causes such as vigorous sexual activity, menstruation contamination, and viral infections
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The presentation of haematuria varies with the cause.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Pink, red, or cola-coloured urine (visible haematuria)
    \item Painless blood in the urine, which is a key warning sign for cancer
    \item Pain or burning on urination, suggesting infection
    \item Flank or loin pain, suggesting a stone or kidney problem
    \item Frequent urination, urgency, or difficulty starting (prostate or bladder problems)
    \item Clots of blood in the urine
    \item Fever and back pain in pyelonephritis
    \item Swelling of the face or legs, which can accompany kidney disease
    \item No symptoms at all in microscopic haematuria
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because many conditions cause haematuria, a systematic approach is needed.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Urinary tract infection and cystitis
    \item Kidney stones and ureteric stones
    \item Kidney disease, including glomerulonephritis and IgA nephropathy
    \item Bladder, kidney, ureteric, and prostate cancer
    \item Benign prostatic hyperplasia
    \item Trauma and injury
    \item Medicines, including anticoagulants
    \item Exercise-induced haematuria
    \item Menstrual contamination in women
    \item Rare causes, including schistosomiasis in travellers and vascular malformations
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any episode of visible blood in the urine should be reported to a doctor promptly, as should microscopic blood found on testing. Blood in the urine is never normal and always warrants investigation.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Blood in the urine with severe pain, vomiting, or an inability to pass urine
    \item Blood in the urine after significant trauma
    \item Clots that obstruct the flow of urine
    \item Blood in the urine with high fever, chills, or signs of sepsis
    \item Very heavy bleeding with faintness or dizziness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Haematuria is investigated to find the source and exclude serious disease.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urine tests:} dipstick and microscopy confirm blood and check for infection, protein, and casts
    \item \textbf{Urine culture:} identifies bacterial infection
    \item \textbf{Urine cytology:} looks for abnormal cells suggesting cancer
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} kidney function, and other tests depending on the suspected cause
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} images the kidneys, bladder, and prostate
    \item \textbf{CT urogram:} detailed imaging of the whole urinary tract, used for visible haematuria and high-risk cases
    \item \textbf{Cystoscopy:} a camera inspection of the bladder, essential for excluding bladder cancer
    \item \textbf{Kidney biopsy:} when kidney disease is suspected
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a urologist or nephrologist as indicated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on the underlying cause.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infection:} antibiotics and fluids
    \item \textbf{Kidney stones:} pain relief, fluids, and management of the stone
    \item \textbf{Kidney disease:} blood pressure control, and specific treatment of the underlying condition
    \item \textbf{Cancer:} surgical removal and appropriate oncology treatment
    \item \textbf{Prostate conditions:} medical or surgical treatment
    \item \textbf{Trauma:} rest and, in severe cases, hospital care
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} adjusting anticoagulants under medical advice
    \item \textbf{Exercise-related haematuria:} reassurance and rest
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} repeat urine tests and imaging to confirm resolution
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Delayed diagnosis of bladder or kidney cancer when haematuria is ignored
    \item Anaemia from significant blood loss
    \item Clots blocking urine flow, causing pain and kidney damage
    \item Progression of undiagnosed kidney disease
    \item Complications of investigation, including contrast reactions and cystoscopy-related discomfort
    \item Anxiety about the cause of the blood
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis depends on the cause. Most haematuria in young people is due to infection or benign causes and resolves completely. In older people, especially smokers, haematuria must be taken seriously because of the risk of bladder and kidney cancer, which are much more treatable when caught early. Kidney diseases causing haematuria are managed with specialist care, and their outlook depends on the specific condition. The best outcomes come from prompt investigation of every episode of haematuria.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Do not ignore blood in the urine, even if it is painless or happens only once
    \item See a doctor promptly and complete the recommended investigations
    \item Drink plenty of water to stay hydrated
    \item Do not smoke, as smoking is a major risk factor for bladder cancer
    \item Practise good hygiene and treat urinary infections promptly
    \item Avoid prolonged use of medicines that can irritate the kidneys
    \item Attend follow-up tests to confirm the cause has resolved
    \item If you have recurring microscopic haematuria, attend regular monitoring as advised
\end{itemize}
