\chapter{Candidiasis}
\index{Candidiasis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Candidiasis is an infection caused by \textit{Candida} yeasts, most commonly \textit{Candida albicans}. These organisms live harmlessly as part of the normal microbial flora on the skin, in the mouth, and in the gut and vagina of most healthy people, but they can cause infection when conditions allow them to multiply excessively. Candidiasis ranges from common, minor surface infections such as oral thrush, vaginal thrush, and nappy rash, through to rare but serious invasive disease of the bloodstream and internal organs. The type and severity of disease depend on where the infection occurs and on the strength of the person's immune defences, and the term encompasses both trivial self-limiting conditions and life-threatening hospital-acquired illness.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Candidiasis is extremely common: most women will experience at least one episode of vaginal thrush during their lifetime, and most infants develop oral thrush or nappy rash at some point. The yeasts are carried by a large proportion of healthy people without causing any symptoms. Serious invasive candidiasis is far rarer and occurs almost exclusively in hospitals, particularly in intensive care patients, people with central intravenous catheters, and those with weakened immune systems from chemotherapy, transplantation, or advanced HIV. Risk is higher in people with diabetes, in pregnancy, in the very young and the very old, and with the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, which disturb the normal microbial balance.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\textit{Candida} is a yeast that normally coexists with the body's other microbes, kept in check by the immune system and by competing bacteria. Infection occurs when this delicate balance is disturbed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} kill off protective bacteria, allowing yeasts to overgrow on skin and mucous membranes
    \item \textbf{Hormones:} high oestrogen levels in pregnancy or with the combined contraceptive pill encourage vaginal thrush
    \item \textbf{Diabetes:} high glucose concentrations in tissues provide nutrients for the yeast
    \item \textbf{Immunosuppression:} HIV infection, chemotherapy, transplant medicines, or corticosteroid treatment
    \item \textbf{Damage to skin or mucous membranes:} including ill-fitting dentures, mouth ulcers, or maceration from persistent moisture
    \item \textbf{Moist environments:} warm, damp skin folds and nappies favour yeast growth
\end{itemize}
\textit{Candida} attaches to and invades surface tissues, provoking inflammation, and in severe immunosuppression it can breach the mucosa, enter the bloodstream, and spread to organs. Some species, such as \textit{Candida auris}, are more drug-resistant and have become a concern in hospitals because of their ability to persist in the environment and cause outbreaks.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms depend on the site of infection:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Oral thrush:} white, curd-like patches on the tongue and inside the cheeks that can be wiped off to leave a red, sometimes bleeding base; soreness, a cottony feeling, and altered taste
    \item \textbf{Vaginal thrush:} itching, soreness, and a thick white "cottage cheese" discharge, often with redness and swelling of the vulva and surrounding skin
    \item \textbf{Nappy rash:} a bright red, well-defined rash within the skin creases, sometimes with scattered satellite spots beyond the main area
    \item \textbf{Skin-fold infection:} an itchy red rash in moist areas such as under the breasts, in the armpits, or in the groin, often with maceration
    \item \textbf{Oesophageal infection:} pain or difficulty swallowing, and sometimes retrosternal chest discomfort, mainly in immunocompromised people
    \item \textbf{Invasive candidiasis:} fever, chills, and organ dysfunction in hospitalised patients, often indistinguishable from bacterial sepsis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions can look like candidiasis at each site, and the diagnosis is often clinical:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Oral:} lichen planus, oral leukoplakia, herpes simplex infection, oral hairy leukoplakia, and geographic tongue
    \item \textbf{Vaginal:} bacterial vaginosis, trichomoniasis, allergic or irritant dermatitis, lichen sclerosus, and herpes infection
    \item \textbf{Skin and nappy rash:} bacterial infections such as impetigo, eczema, psoriasis, and seborrhoeic dermatitis
    \item \textbf{Invasive disease:} bacterial sepsis, other fungal infections such as aspergillosis, and non-infectious causes of fever in hospital
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most minor candidiasis can be treated at home with over-the-counter antifungal products. See a doctor if:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Symptoms are severe, recurrent, or not improving after treatment
    \item You are pregnant, breastfeeding, or have diabetes or a weakened immune system
    \item Oral thrush occurs in an adult who is otherwise well, as it may warrant a check for underlying conditions
    \item You have pain or difficulty swallowing
    \item There is bleeding, ulceration, or a worsening rash
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever, chills, or feeling severely unwell in someone with weakened immunity
    \item Difficulty breathing or severe chest pain
    \item Confusion or severe drowsiness
    \item Signs of sepsis, such as a rapid heart rate, rapid breathing, or mottled skin
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} the characteristic appearance of oral, vaginal, or skin candidiasis is often sufficient to make the diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Microscopy and culture:} swabs, skin scrapings, or a gentle scrape of the affected area to confirm \textit{Candida} and exclude other organisms
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} blood glucose if diabetes is suspected, or immune function tests where appropriate
    \item \textbf{Endoscopy:} to visualise the oesophagus in people with swallowing pain and to obtain biopsy samples
    \item \textbf{Blood cultures, imaging, and species identification:} for invasive candidiasis in hospitalised patients
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on the site and severity of the infection:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Oral thrush:} antifungal mouthwashes, lozenges, or oral antifungal tablets, together with cleaning or replacement of dentures
    \item \textbf{Vaginal thrush:} antifungal creams, pessaries, or a single oral tablet such as fluconazole
    \item \textbf{Skin and nappy rash:} antifungal cream, keeping the area clean and dry, and airing the skin regularly
    \item \textbf{Oesophageal disease:} oral or intravenous antifungal medicines, usually with attention to the underlying immune problem
    \item \textbf{Invasive candidiasis:} intravenous antifungal drugs in hospital, removal of catheters, drainage of abscesses, and supportive care
    \item \textbf{Addressing triggers:} reducing unnecessary antibiotic use, improving blood sugar control, or adjusting hormone treatment
    \item \textbf{Prevention of recurrence:} long-term or intermittent antifungal treatment may be offered for frequent vaginal or oral thrush
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Recurrent or persistent thrush that disrupts sleep, comfort, and daily life
    \item Scarring or fissures in severe skin or vaginal infection
    \item Painful swallowing, poor intake, and weight loss with oesophageal disease
    \item Invasive candidiasis: bloodstream infection, abscesses in organs, endocarditis, and infection within the eye
    \item Multi-organ failure and death in severe immunosuppression
    \item Drug resistance with repeated or prolonged antifungal use
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For people with normal immunity, candidiasis is generally easy to treat and resolves within days to weeks, although recurrence is common in some women and in people with dentures, diabetes, or persistent skin moisture. Invasive candidiasis remains a serious infection with significant mortality, but outcomes improve with prompt treatment, removal of infected lines, and control of the source. Managing the underlying trigger is the key to long-term control and to preventing repeated episodes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep the skin clean and dry, especially in folds; change nappies frequently and allow air to reach the skin
    \item Avoid prolonged courses of antibiotics unless they are prescribed and needed
    \item Manage blood sugar carefully if you have diabetes
    \item Clean dentures thoroughly and remove them overnight; keep the mouth clean
    \item Wear loose, breathable clothing and cotton underwear, and change out of wet swimwear promptly
    \item Avoid perfumed soaps, douching, and harsh hygiene products around the genital area
    \item Maintain a healthy diet and immune system, and stop smoking
    \item For recurrent thrush, work with a doctor to find and treat the underlying cause rather than relying on repeated antifungal courses
\end{itemize}
