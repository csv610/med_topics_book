\chapter{Know Your Standard Drinks}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A standard drink is a measure of pure alcohol used to help people understand how much they are drinking. In Australia, one standard drink contains 10 grams of pure alcohol, regardless of the type of beverage. Different drinks contain very different amounts of alcohol: a full-strength beer, a glass of wine, and a shot of spirits are not equal in alcohol content unless they each contain exactly one standard drink. Understanding standard drinks is central to drinking safely, following the Australian alcohol guidelines, and estimating blood alcohol levels. This chapter explains what a standard drink is, how to count them, and how this knowledge supports safer drinking.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Most Australian adults drink alcohol, and a substantial minority drink at levels that increase their risk of harm. Alcohol contributes significantly to injury, illness, and death in Australia, including road crashes, violence, liver disease, several cancers, and alcohol use disorder. Surveys show that many people underestimate how many standard drinks they consume, in part because they misjudge the alcohol content of the drinks they pour. Public health campaigns, such as "know your standard drinks" and the labelling of drinks with standard drink counts, aim to improve awareness and reduce harm.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Definition:} one Australian standard drink contains 10 grams of alcohol
    \item \textbf{Common examples:} roughly, one standard drink equals 285 mL of full-strength beer (about a middy), 100 mL of wine, 30 mL of spirits, or 60 mL of fortified wine, but labels should always be checked
    \item \textbf{Alcohol content varies:} light beers, mid-strength beers, and full-strength beers differ; wine pours at home are often larger than 100 mL; cocktails may contain several standard drinks
    \item \textbf{Blood alcohol concentration (BAC):} determined by the number of standard drinks, body weight, sex, rate of drinking, and food intake
    \item \textbf{Metabolism:} the liver breaks down roughly one standard drink per hour, so the body cannot speed up clearance
    \item \textbf{Effects:} alcohol depresses the central nervous system, impairing judgement, coordination, and reaction time in a dose-dependent manner
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Knowing standard drinks helps people recognise when drinking is becoming hazardous.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Most people cannot accurately estimate the standard drinks in their glass; large wine glasses and free-poured spirits are common sources of underestimation
    \item Drinking more than ten standard drinks per week or more than four on a single occasion increases health risks, according to Australian guidelines
    \item A person's BAC cannot be reliably guessed from how they feel; impairment begins well before obvious drunkenness
    \item Standard drink counts on labels allow accurate tallying across a session
    \item Cocktails, pre-mixed drinks, and fortified wines often contain two or more standard drinks per serve
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Confusion between a serve and a standard drink:} a "serve" or glass is not the same as a standard drink
    \item \textbf{Misreading low-alcohol products:} light beers still contain alcohol and count towards intake
    \item \textbf{Varying glass sizes:} wine served in restaurants or at home is rarely a standard 100 mL measure
    \item \textbf{Underestimating mixed drinks:} spirits poured freely can be double or triple a standard measure
    \item \textbf{Standard drink definitions differ internationally:} 10 grams in Australia differs from definitions elsewhere, so international labels cannot be used directly
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who finds it difficult to control their drinking, who drinks more than they intend, or who experiences withdrawal symptoms such as trembling, sweating, or anxiety when not drinking should speak with a GP. Advice about safer drinking and support services is available without judgement.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of alcohol poisoning: unconsciousness, slow or irregular breathing, cold clammy skin, or repeated vomiting
    \item Severe confusion, seizures, or an inability to be woken
    \item Serious injury after drinking, including a head knock or heavy bleeding
    \item Thoughts of harming yourself or others while under the influence
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Self-tracking:} using standard drink counts on labels and the DrinkWise calculator to tally consumption
    \item \textbf{Breath or blood alcohol testing:} used legally and clinically to measure current BAC
    \item \textbf{Screening questionnaires:} tools such as the AUDIT questionnaire help identify hazardous drinking patterns
    \item \textbf{Health review:} a GP may check blood pressure, liver function, and other markers in people who drink heavily
    \item \textbf{Counselling:} brief interventions in primary care are effective at reducing risky drinking
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Learn the counts:} check the label of every drink for its standard drink content
    \item \textbf{Use the guidelines:} the Australian guidelines recommend no more than ten standard drinks per week and no more than four on any one day
    \item \textbf{Alternate with water:} pacing drinks and having alcohol-free days reduces total intake
    \item \textbf{Use smaller glasses and measured pours:} particularly for wine and spirits
    \item \textbf{Choose lower-alcohol options:} light beers and smaller serves
    \item \textbf{Plan for special occasions:} set a limit in advance and ask someone to help keep you to it
    \item \textbf{Avoid drinking games and binge occasions:} rapid consumption greatly increases risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Impaired judgement leading to road crashes, falls, and other injuries
    \item Alcohol poisoning from rapid, heavy consumption
    \item Long-term health harms: liver disease, cardiovascular disease, several cancers, and brain damage
    \item Dependence and alcohol use disorder
    \item Harm to relationships, work, and mental health
    \item Harm to an unborn baby if alcohol is consumed during pregnancy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
People who learn to count standard drinks consistently drink less and experience fewer alcohol-related harms. The Australian guidelines provide a clear target, and even moderate reductions in intake improve blood pressure, sleep, weight, and overall health risk. For those who develop problems controlling their drinking, effective treatments exist, including counselling, support groups, and medication, and recovery is common.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Memorise the common equivalents: 285 mL full-strength beer, 100 mL wine, 30 mL spirits
    \item Read standard drink labels on every bottle and can
    \item Keep a drink diary for a week to see your true intake
    \item Aim for at least two alcohol-free days each week
    \item Never drink and drive; plan transport before you start drinking
    \item Avoid alcohol in pregnancy, when driving, or when taking medicines that interact
    \item If you struggle to cut down, speak with a GP or call the National Alcohol and Other Drug Hotline on 1800 250 015
\end{itemize}
