\chapter{Allergic Rhinitis}
\index{Allergic Rhinitis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Allergic rhinitis is an inflammatory disorder of the nasal mucosa triggered by an IgE-mediated immune response to inhaled allergens such as pollen, house dust mites, animal dander, or mould spores. It is characterised by sneezing, rhinorrhoea, nasal itching, and nasal congestion, frequently accompanied by itchy, watery eyes when allergic conjunctivitis coexists. The condition is classified as seasonal (intermittent) when symptoms occur during specific pollen seasons, or perennial (persistent) when they occur year-round, as with house dust mite or pet allergies. The term \emph{hay fever} is widely used for seasonal allergic rhinitis, though no hay or fever is involved. Allergic rhinitis commonly coexists with asthma, eczema, and conjunctivitis, and it exerts a substantial impact on sleep, school performance, and quality of life. Although it is not curable, effective treatment is available and most people achieve good symptom control.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Allergic rhinitis is one of the most common chronic conditions worldwide, affecting an estimated 10 to 30\% of adults and up to 40\% of children in developed countries. The prevalence has risen over recent decades, in line with other atopic disorders. Onset is most common in childhood and adolescence, but symptoms may appear for the first time at any age. Seasonal forms are strongly associated with grass, tree, and weed pollen counts, while dust mite and animal dander allergies typically produce perennial symptoms. Risk factors include a family history of atopy, personal history of eczema or asthma, urban living, and certain early-life exposures. The condition frequently improves with age, but it can also persist or even worsen over time, and coexisting asthma tends to be more difficult to control in the presence of untreated nasal allergy.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The condition arises when the immune system mounts an exaggerated response to otherwise harmless airborne proteins:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Seasonal allergens:} grass, tree, and weed pollens, which are released in large quantities during specific flowering seasons.
    \item \textbf{Perennial allergens:} house dust mite faeces and body parts, animal dander (skin flakes, saliva, and urine), cockroach debris, and indoor and outdoor mould spores.
    \item \textbf{Occupational allergens:} flour, laboratory animals, wood dust, latex, and industrial chemicals encountered in certain workplaces.
\end{itemize}

On first exposure, allergen-presenting cells activate T-helper type 2 lymphocytes, driving the production of allergen-specific IgE, which binds to mast cells in the nasal mucosa. On subsequent exposure, the allergen cross-links the bound IgE, causing mast cell degranulation and the release of histamine, leukotrienes, and other mediators. These substances cause vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, mucus secretion, and sensory nerve stimulation, producing the classic symptom complex of itching, sneezing, rhinorrhoea, and congestion. A late-phase response follows hours later, driven by eosinophils and other inflammatory cells, which contributes to nasal blockage and hyperresponsiveness.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Typical symptoms include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sneezing:} often in paroxysmal bouts, particularly on waking or after allergen exposure.
    \item \textbf{Rhinorrhoea:} clear, watery nasal discharge, sometimes profuse.
    \item \textbf{Nasal congestion:} blockage that may alternate sides, often worse at night and causing mouth breathing.
    \item \textbf{Nasal and palatal itching:} an itch at the roof of the mouth, throat, and ears.
    \item \textbf{Ocular symptoms:} itchy, red, watery eyes (allergic conjunctivitis) in a large proportion of people.
    \item \textbf{Secondary features:} post-nasal drip, nocturnal cough, snoring, reduced sense of smell, fatigue, and poor concentration related to disturbed sleep.
\end{itemize}

Children may show characteristic signs such as the \emph{allergic salute} (repeatedly pushing the nose up), the \emph{allergic shiner} (dark circles under the eyes), and a transverse nasal crease.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Conditions that can mimic allergic rhinitis include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Non-allergic (vasomotor) rhinitis:} triggered by temperature change, smoke, strong odours, or stress, without an allergic basis.
    \item \textbf{Infectious rhinitis:} the common cold and recurrent viral infections, distinguished by fever and systemic symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Chronic rhinosinusitis and nasal polyps:} often associated with congestion, facial pain, and loss of smell.
    \item \textbf{Structural problems:} a deviated nasal septum, enlarged adenoids, or tumours causing one-sided blockage.
    \item \textbf{Medication-induced rhinitis:} including rhinitis medicamentosa from prolonged decongestant use.
    \item \textbf{Hormonal rhinitis:} in pregnancy, puberty, or hypothyroidism.
    \item \textbf{Occupational rhinitis:} due to workplace exposures, sometimes confused with perennial allergy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical advice if symptoms are persistent, severe, or interfering with sleep, school, or work, or if they do not respond to pharmacy treatments. Review is also warranted for one-sided nasal blockage, recurrent nosebleeds, loss of smell, suspected workplace triggers, or asthma that is becoming harder to control.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden difficulty breathing or wheezing that does not settle.
    \item Swelling of the face or throat, or difficulty swallowing.
    \item Collapse or loss of consciousness.
    \item Any sign of a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is usually clinical, supported by targeted testing where the trigger is unclear or treatment is failing:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} timing and seasonality of symptoms, domestic exposures, occupational factors, and personal or family history of atopy.
    \item \textbf{Examination:} inspection of the nasal lining for pallor, oedema, and watery discharge, and assessment for polyps, septal deviation, and conjunctival injection.
    \item \textbf{Skin prick testing:} rapid detection of sensitisation to common aeroallergens, with results interpreted alongside the clinical history.
    \item \textbf{Serum specific IgE:} allergen-specific antibody blood tests where skin testing is unavailable or impractical.
    \item \textbf{Nasal endoscopy:} by an ear, nose, and throat specialist to exclude polyps and structural disease.
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to an allergy specialist or ENT surgeon for severe, atypical, or treatment-resistant disease, and for assessment of immunotherapy suitability.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is stepped according to symptom severity:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Allergen avoidance:} reducing exposure to identified triggers, including environmental measures for house dust mites and pets.
    \item \textbf{Nasal saline irrigation:} rinsing the nasal passages to remove allergens and mucus.
    \item \textbf{Oral antihistamines:} effective for sneezing, itching, and rhinorrhoea; newer second-generation agents cause less sedation.
    \item \textbf{Intranasal corticosteroids:} the most effective treatment for moderate to severe and persistent disease, working best when used regularly.
    \item \textbf{Combination sprays:} intranasal antihistamine and steroid combinations for rapid relief in severe cases.
    \item \textbf{Decongestants:} short-term use only, because prolonged use causes rebound congestion.
    \item \textbf{Antihistamine or mast-cell-stabilising eye drops:} for allergic conjunctivitis.
    \item \textbf{Allergen immunotherapy:} subcutaneous or sublingual immunotherapy provides disease-modifying treatment for selected people with pollen or dust mite allergy who do not respond adequately to pharmacotherapy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Poorly controlled allergic rhinitis can lead to:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic rhinosinusitis and nasal polyps.
    \item Otitis media with effusion (glue ear) and Eustachian tube dysfunction in children.
    \item Sleep disturbance, snoring, and daytime fatigue.
    \item Worsening of coexisting asthma and increased asthma-related healthcare use.
    \item Reduced concentration and impaired school or work performance.
    \item Diminished quality of life and, in some people, low mood and anxiety.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Allergic rhinitis is a chronic condition for which there is no cure, but most people achieve good control with a combination of allergen avoidance and regular treatment. The course varies widely: seasonal allergy typically fluctuates with pollen seasons and may improve or worsen over the years, while some children outgrow their sensitivities during adolescence. People who fail to respond to optimal pharmacotherapy may benefit from allergen immunotherapy, which can produce sustained improvement. With appropriate management, complications are uncommon and the condition rarely causes serious harm.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Self-care measures are important adjuncts to medical treatment:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pollen season:} keep windows closed, use air conditioning or a filtered air cleaner, shower and change clothes after being outdoors, and check daily pollen counts.
    \item \textbf{Dust mite allergy:} use allergen-proof covers for mattress, pillow, and duvet, wash bedding in hot water weekly, and reduce soft furnishings.
    \item \textbf{Pets:} keep animals out of the bedroom, wash them regularly, and use a HEPA vacuum.
    \item \textbf{Nasal hygiene:} use saline rinses to keep the nasal passages clear.
    \item \textbf{Adherence:} take treatments regularly as prescribed rather than only when symptoms flare.
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} avoid smoking and smoky environments, which irritate the nasal lining and worsen symptoms.
\end{itemize}
