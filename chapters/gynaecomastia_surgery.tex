\chapter{Gynaecomastia Surgery}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Gynaecomastia surgery is a procedure to remove excess breast tissue from the chest of a male with gynaecomastia, the benign enlargement of breast tissue. It is considered when the enlargement is persistent, painful, or causes significant distress, and when non-surgical measures have not been effective. The surgery typically combines liposuction to remove excess fat and direct excision of the glandular tissue, performed through small incisions, usually around the edge of the nipple. It is a common, safe day procedure with high satisfaction rates, though it leaves some scarring and requires a recovery period. Realistic expectations and a thorough assessment are essential before surgery.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Gynaecomastia affects up to half of adult men at some stage, but only a minority seek surgery. The number of gynaecomastia operations has increased in recent decades, and it is now one of the more common male cosmetic procedures. Candidates are typically men with persistent pubertal or adult gynaecomastia that has not resolved, men with significant glandular tissue that causes discomfort, and men whose condition causes substantial psychological distress. Surgery is more commonly sought by men aged 20--50, and results are usually very satisfying.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Surgery is planned according to the composition of the enlarged breast tissue.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Glandular tissue:} firm breast gland tissue beneath the nipple; this requires excision
    \item \textbf{Fatty tissue:} excess fat in the chest, common in overweight men; this is removed with liposuction
    \item \textbf{Combined pattern:} most men have a mixture of both, requiring liposuction and excision
    \item \textbf{Skin excess:} in severe or long-standing cases, excess skin may need to be removed
    \item \textbf{Underlying causes:} hormonal imbalances, medicines, and other factors should be assessed before surgery
    \item \textbf{Goals of surgery:} a flatter, more masculine chest contour with minimal visible scarring
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Men are considered for surgery when they have specific features.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent gynaecomastia beyond puberty that has not resolved
    \item Firm glandular tissue beneath the nipple
    \item Excess chest fat contributing to the enlargement
    \item Pain or tenderness from the breast tissue
    \item Significant psychological distress or effects on body image and confidence
    \item Gynaecomastia that has been stable and not caused by an untreated medical condition
    \item Realistic expectations about the outcome
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before surgery, the diagnosis and suitability are confirmed.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Pseudogynaecomastia (fat-only enlargement), which is managed with weight loss rather than excision
    \item Male breast cancer, which must be excluded before any cosmetic surgery
    \item Gynaecomastia from medicines or substances, which should be addressed first
    \item Gynaecomastia from hormonal or systemic disease, which needs medical management
    \item Benign breast lumps and cysts
    \item Obesity requiring weight loss before or alongside surgery
    \item Ptosis (sagging) of the chest tissues, which may need skin removal
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Men considering surgery should first see their GP to confirm the diagnosis, exclude causes such as medicines or hormonal conditions, and obtain a referral to a specialist plastic or breast surgeon.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item This is an elective procedure; however, after surgery seek urgent care for signs of severe complications such as heavy bleeding, breathing difficulty, or chest pain
    \item High fever, spreading redness, or severe pain after surgery, which may indicate infection
    \item Sudden swelling or shortness of breath, which may indicate a blood clot
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A full assessment precedes surgery.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} examination of the breast tissue, chest, and testicles
    \item \textbf{Medical history:} medicines, hormones, alcohol and drug use, and general health
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} hormone levels where indicated
    \item \textbf{Breast imaging:} ultrasound or mammography to exclude cancer and characterise the tissue
    \item \textbf{Weight assessment:} body mass index and chest fat distribution
    \item \textbf{Discussion of expectations:} the surgeon explains what surgery can achieve and its limitations
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative fitness:} general health and anaesthetic assessment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Surgery is tailored to the individual's tissue composition.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Liposuction:} small cannulas remove excess fat from the chest
    \item \textbf{Glandular excision:} a small incision, often around the lower edge of the nipple, through which the glandular tissue is removed
    \item \textbf{Combined approach:} liposuction followed by excision, which is the most common technique
    \item \textbf{Skin removal:} for significant skin excess, additional incisions may be needed
    \item \textbf{Anaesthesia:} usually performed under general anaesthetic or local anaesthetic with sedation
    \item \textbf{Recovery:} a compression garment is worn for several weeks, and most men return to work within one to two weeks
    \item \textbf{Results:} final results appear over several months as swelling settles
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review visits monitor healing and outcome
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding and bruising, including haematoma requiring drainage
    \item Infection, which is uncommon with good care
    \item Scarring, including visible scars and thickening
    \item Asymmetry or contour irregularities, including a "saucer" depression where tissue was removed
    \item Changes in nipple sensation, which are usually temporary
    \item Seroma, a collection of fluid requiring aspiration
    \item Rarely, necrosis of the nipple or skin
    \item The possibility of residual tissue or recurrence if the cause persists
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Gynaecomastia surgery has high satisfaction rates, with most men very pleased with the flatter, more masculine chest contour. Recovery is straightforward for most, and complications are uncommon. Results are generally permanent for the removed tissue, though weight gain can add fat to the chest, and stopping the cause (such as medicines or steroids) is important to prevent recurrence. Most men report substantial improvements in confidence, body image, and quality of life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a healthy weight before and after surgery
    \item Do not smoke, as smoking impairs healing and increases complications
    \item Stop any medicines or substances that cause gynaecomastia, under medical advice
    \item Follow the surgeon's post-operative instructions, including wearing the compression garment
    \item Avoid strenuous activity and heavy lifting for the advised period
    \item Attend all follow-up appointments
    \item Report unusual pain, swelling, or fever promptly
    \item Have realistic expectations and discuss them with your surgeon
\end{itemize}
