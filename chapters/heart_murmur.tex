\chapter{Heart Murmur}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A heart murmur is an extra or unusual sound heard through a stethoscope when blood flows turbulently within the heart or its great vessels. Murmurs are extremely common and are usually harmless, particularly in children, in whom most are innocent "flow" murmurs caused by normal blood movement. However, some murmurs indicate underlying structural heart problems, such as faulty valves, holes between heart chambers, or abnormal connections. The challenge is to distinguish benign murmurs from those that reflect significant disease, which requires clinical assessment and, when appropriate, echocardiography.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Heart murmurs are heard in a large proportion of the population at some point in life. Up to 70--80\% of children have an innocent murmur detected during routine examinations, and most disappear as the child grows. In adults, murmurs become more common with age as valve disease, particularly aortic stenosis and mitral regurgitation, becomes more prevalent. Valvular heart disease affects around 2--3\% of the adult population and is more common in older people. Most murmurs detected in clinical practice are innocent, but a doctor's assessment is needed to confirm this.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Innocent murmurs:} turbulent but normal blood flow, common in children, pregnancy, anaemia, fever, and with a thin chest wall
    \item \textbf{Aortic stenosis:} narrowing of the aortic valve, often age-related calcification or a congenital bicuspid valve
    \item \textbf{Mitral regurgitation:} leakage of the mitral valve, from prolapse, ischaemia, or rheumatic disease
    \item \textbf{Aortic regurgitation and mitral stenosis:} other valve lesions that produce characteristic murmurs
    \item \textbf{Congenital heart defects:} holes (ventricular and atrial septal defects), patent ductus arteriosus, and valve malformations
    \item \textbf{Endocarditis:} infection of the heart valves can cause or change murmurs
    \item \textbf{Physiology:} murmurs are caused by high-velocity, turbulent flow across abnormal structures or through normal structures under increased flow
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Most murmurs produce no symptoms and are found incidentally
    \item The murmur's timing (systolic or diastolic), location, loudness, and character guide its significance
    \item Symptoms arise from the underlying condition, not the murmur itself
    \item Breathlessness, chest pain, fainting, or palpitations with exercise suggest significant valve or heart disease
    \item Fatigue, poor feeding, and failure to thrive in infants with significant congenital lesions
    \item Peripheral signs, such as bounding pulses, may accompany particular valve lesions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Innocent vs pathological:} the most important distinction; features of the murmur and the rest of the examination help separate them
    \item \textbf{Systolic murmurs:} aortic stenosis, mitral regurgitation, ventricular septal defect, and innocent flow murmurs
    \item \textbf{Diastolic murmurs:} aortic regurgitation and mitral stenosis, which are always pathological
    \item \textbf{Continuous murmurs:} patent ductus arteriosus and other vascular connections
    \item \textbf{Other sounds:} extra heart sounds and pericardial rubs are not murmurs but may be confused with them
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
A murmur discovered during a routine examination usually needs a careful clinical assessment, and referral for echocardiography if the doctor has any concern. Symptoms such as breathlessness, chest pain, or fainting with a known murmur warrant prompt review.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, severe breathlessness, or fainting during exercise
    \item Fever with a known murmur and signs of infection (possible endocarditis)
    \item Sudden weakness, confusion, or other signs of a stroke in someone with a murmur and a valve infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} characterising the murmur's timing, location, radiation, loudness, and response to manoeuvres
    \item \textbf{Echocardiogram:} the definitive test, visualising valve structure and function, chamber sizes, and heart pumping
    \item \textbf{Electrocardiogram:} detects hypertrophy, strain, and rhythm disturbances
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} shows heart size and lung vessel congestion
    \item \textbf{Cardiac MRI and CT:} used in complex or uncertain cases
    \item \textbf{Blood cultures:} required urgently if endocarditis is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Innocent murmurs:} no treatment is needed; reassurance and explanation are all that is required
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} significant valve lesions are followed with periodic clinical review and echocardiography
    \item \textbf{Medication:} treats symptoms and complications of valve disease, such as heart failure and arrhythmias
    \item \textbf{Endocarditis prevention:} good dental hygiene and prompt treatment of infections; antibiotics before certain procedures for the small group at highest risk
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} valve repair or replacement when stenosis or regurgitation becomes severe
    \item \textbf{Congenital defects:} closure devices or surgery for significant shunts and malformations
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progression of valve disease to severe stenosis or regurgitation
    \item Heart failure from long-standing valve overload
    \item Arrhythmias, including atrial fibrillation
    \item Infective endocarditis in people with structural valve disease
    \item Stroke from emboli in infective endocarditis or atrial fibrillation
    \item Anxiety caused by misunderstanding the significance of a murmur
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for a heart murmur depends entirely on the underlying cause. Innocent murmurs carry no risk and require no restrictions; children outgrow most of them. Valvular and congenital lesions have a wide range of outcomes: many remain stable for decades, while severe lesions progress and benefit from timely surgery. Valve repair or replacement has excellent outcomes in suitable patients, and modern management allows most people with valve disease to live full, active lives with appropriate monitoring.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend routine check-ups so that new murmurs are detected and assessed
    \item Follow recommended follow-up schedules for known valve conditions
    \item Maintain good dental hygiene and treat infections promptly
    \item Report new symptoms such as breathlessness, chest pain, or fainting
    \item Ask your doctor what type of murmur you have and what it means
    \item In pregnancy, attend antenatal care so that murmurs are assessed appropriately
\end{itemize}
