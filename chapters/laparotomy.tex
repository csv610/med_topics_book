\chapter{Laparotomy}
\index{Laparotomy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A laparotomy is a major surgical procedure involving a large incision through the abdominal wall to gain access to the abdominal cavity. Also known as celiotomy, it can be performed as an exploratory procedure to diagnose abdominal pathology or as a therapeutic intervention to treat a wide range of diseases, injuries, or obstetric emergencies. The incision can be vertical (such as a midline laparotomy, which provides rapid and extensive access) or transverse, depending on the indication. While advancements in minimally invasive surgery (laparoscopy) have reduced the frequency of open laparotomies, the procedure remains a cornerstone of emergency abdominal surgery, trauma management, and complex oncological resections where open exposure is vital for patient safety and surgical efficacy.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Laparotomies are performed frequently in acute care hospitals worldwide, representing a major portion of emergency general surgery. In many countries and other developed countries, emergency laparotomies carry significant clinical weight, with thousands performed annually. The epidemiology of the procedure is closely tied to its underlying indications: emergency laparotomies are most frequently performed in older adults, who are more susceptible to bowel obstructions, perforated diverticula, and ischaemic bowel disease. Conversely, trauma-related laparotomies are more common in younger males due to higher rates of motor vehicle accidents and penetrating injuries. The mortality rate for elective laparotomies is generally low, whereas emergency laparotomies carry a much higher risk of mortality (typically 5\% to 15\%) and post-operative complications, particularly in elderly patients with multiple co-morbidities.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Laparotomy is indicated when intra-abdominal pathology requires direct visual inspection, palpation, or extensive manual intervention that cannot be safely accomplished via laparoscopy. The physiological strain of a laparotomy is substantial, involving activation of the neuroendocrine stress response, potential fluid shifts, and risk of hypothermia.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute gastrointestinal perforation:} Perforation of a peptic ulcer, diverticulum, or appendix, leading to contamination of the peritoneal cavity with faecal matter or gastric contents.
    \item \textbf{Intestinal obstruction:} Mechanical blockage of the small or large bowel caused by adhesions, hernias, volvulus, or tumours, which requires relief to prevent ischaemia and necrosis.
    \item \textbf{Abdominal trauma:} Blunt or penetrating injuries (such as stab or gunshot wounds) causing severe intra-abdominal haemorrhage or visceral rupture.
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal and gynaecological malignancies:} Advanced cancers of the bowel, stomach, pancreas, or ovaries requiring radical resection, lymph node dissection, or cytoreduction.
    \item \textbf{Intra-abdominal sepsis:} Severe infections, such as abscesses or peritonitis, that require surgical drainage, toilet, and lavage.
    \item \textbf{Obstetric emergencies:} Complicated Caesarean sections or ruptured ectopic pregnancies where rapid access to the pelvis is life-saving.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features prompting a laparotomy are typically those of an acute abdomen or severe trauma. Patients undergoing emergency laparotomies present with a constellation of signs and symptoms that signify critical intra-abdominal disease.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Severe abdominal pain:} Often sudden in onset, generalised or localised, and aggravated by movement or coughing.
    \item \textbf{Peritoneal signs:} Marked abdominal tenderness, involuntary guarding, rigidity (a board-like abdomen), and rebound tenderness on physical examination.
    \item \textbf{Haemodynamic instability:} Hypotension, tachycardia, cold clammy extremities, and oliguria, indicating internal haemorrhage, severe dehydration, or septic shock.
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal distress:} Persistent vomiting, severe abdominal distension, obstipation (inability to pass flatus or faeces), and absent bowel sounds.
    \item \textbf{Systemic inflammatory response:} High fever, rigors, tachypnoea, and altered mental state associated with worsening sepsis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
In patients presenting with acute abdominal symptoms, clinicians must differentiate surgical emergencies requiring laparotomy from conditions that can be managed non-operatively.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute pancreatitis:} Characterised by severe epigastric pain radiating to the back and elevated lipase levels; generally managed medically unless complications arise.
    \item \textbf{Renal colic:} Intense, spasmodic loin-to-groin pain associated with haematuria, caused by urinary tract stones and usually managed with analgesia and hydration.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic inflammatory disease:} An infection of the female reproductive tract causing lower abdominal pain and vaginal discharge, treated primarily with antibiotics.
    \item \textbf{Myocardial infarction or pneumonia:} Referred pain from cardiac or pulmonary pathology that mimics upper abdominal disease; diagnosed via electrocardiography, troponin levels, and chest X-rays.
    \item \textbf{Diabetic ketoacidosis:} A metabolic emergency presenting with diffuse abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting, which resolves with insulin and fluid resuscitation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients recovering from a laparotomy require careful post-operative monitoring. A general practitioner or surgeon should be consulted for non-urgent issues such as progressive wound redness, mild fever, or persistent constipation.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, worsening abdominal pain that is not relieved by prescribed pain medication.
    \item Sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, or coughing up blood.
    \item Rapidly spreading redness, severe swelling, or purulent drainage from the incision site.
    \item Opening or separation of the wound edges (wound dehiscence).
    \item Persistent vomiting, inability to tolerate fluids, or failure to pass gas or stool.
    \item High fever, chills, confusion, or a feeling of being extremely unwell.
    \item Dizziness, fainting, or collapse.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The decision to perform a laparotomy is based on clinical assessment supported by urgent diagnostic investigations.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} Detailed abdominal assessment including inspection, palpation for rigidity, percussion for tympany, and auscultation for bowel sounds.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} Full blood count showing leucocytosis or anaemia; inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein); biochemistry (renal function, electrolytes, liver function tests, and serum lipase); and coagulation profiles.
    \item \textbf{Arterial blood gas:} Used in critically ill patients to assess metabolic acidosis, lactate levels, and oxygenation status.
    \item \textbf{Urgent imaging:} Erect chest X-ray to detect free gas under the diaphragm (indicating visceral perforation); abdominal CT scan with contrast, which is the gold standard for defining intra-abdominal pathology.
    \item \textbf{Point-of-care ultrasound:} Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST) scan to identify free fluid or blood in the abdominal cavity.
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test:} Mandatory in all females of childbearing potential to exclude ectopic pregnancy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Laparotomy is a major operative intervention requiring careful perioperative management to optimise patient outcomes and minimise risks.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgical procedure:} Making a large incision (usually midline), systematically exploring the abdominal cavity, identifying and repairing the pathology (e.g., bowel resection, haemostasis, or abscess drainage), and performing thorough peritoneal lavage.
    \item \textbf{Anaesthesia:} General anaesthesia with endotracheal intubation and muscle relaxation is required, often supplemented by epidural analgesia for post-operative pain control.
    \item \textbf{Fluid and haemodynamic resuscitation:} Intravenous crystalloids, blood products, and vasoactive medications to maintain organ perfusion and blood pressure.
    \item \textbf{Antimicrobial therapy:} Prophylactic or therapeutic intravenous antibiotics directed against gastrointestinal pathogens.
    \item \textbf{Post-operative wound care:} Monitoring the incision for infection, maintaining sterile dressings, and removing staples or sutures when healing is adequate.
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation and mobilisation:} Early mobilisation, chest physiotherapy, and deep breathing exercises to prevent respiratory complications and thromboembolism.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Laparotomy is associated with a high rate of post-operative complications, particularly in emergency settings.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgical site infection:} Superficial or deep wound infection, or intra-abdominal abscess formation.
    \item \textbf{Wound dehiscence:} Separation of the fascial layers of the wound, which may lead to evisceration and require urgent surgical repair.
    \item \textbf{Post-operative ileus:} Delayed return of normal bowel motility, causing distension, nausea, and inability to tolerate oral intake.
    \item \textbf{Haemorrhage:} Persistent bleeding from the surgical field, which may necessitate re-operation.
    \item \textbf{Thromboembolism:} Deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism due to prolonged immobility and surgical stress.
    \item \textbf{Adhesion formation:} Internal scar tissue that can cause chronic pelvic pain or mechanical bowel obstruction years after the surgery.
    \item \textbf{Incisional hernia:} Weakening of the abdominal wall at the scar site, allowing abdominal contents to bulge through.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis after a laparotomy varies widely depending on the underlying cause, whether the surgery was elective or an emergency, and the patient's general health. Elective procedures for benign conditions generally have excellent outcomes, with patients returning to normal activities within six to eight weeks. Conversely, emergency laparotomies, especially in older adults or those with advanced malignancy or septic shock, carry a significant risk of prolonged hospitalisation, intensive care stay, and long-term functional decline. Adequate post-operative pain relief, early mobilisation, and active nutritional support are critical factors in accelerating recovery and improving the long-term prognosis.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Post-operative care and self-management are vital to ensure proper healing and prevent complications after discharge.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Wound care:} Keep the incision site clean and dry; wash hands thoroughly before and after touching the area or changing dressings.
    \item \textbf{Activity restriction:} Avoid lifting objects heavier than a few kilograms, strenuous exercise, or driving until cleared by the surgeon (usually six weeks).
    \item \textbf{Abdominal support:} Hold a pillow gently but firmly against the abdomen when coughing, sneezing, or deep breathing to protect the incision.
    \item \textbf{Dietary modification:} Drink plenty of fluids and eat a fibre-rich diet to prevent constipation, which can strain the abdominal wall.
    \item \textbf{Pain management:} Take prescribed analgesics regularly to maintain comfort, allowing for deep breathing and gentle walking.
    \item \textbf{Follow-up compliance:} Attend all scheduled appointments with the general practitioner or surgical team for wound checks and staple removal.
\end{itemize}
