\chapter{Cardiac Enzyme Blood Tests}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cardiac enzyme tests are blood tests that measure substances released into the blood when heart muscle is damaged. The most important of these is \textbf{troponin}, a protein involved in muscle contraction that leaks into the bloodstream within hours of a heart attack. The tests help doctors confirm whether someone is having a heart attack, judge how much heart muscle has been damaged, and decide on urgent treatment. They are interpreted together with the person's symptoms and the electrocardiogram (ECG), rather than on their own, and they form a central part of the standard assessment of chest pain in emergency departments.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Blood tests for cardiac enzymes are performed millions of times each year, mainly on people arriving at emergency departments with chest pain. Heart attacks remain one of the leading causes of hospital admission and death in high-income countries, so these tests are among the most commonly ordered in medicine. Because heart attacks are more common in older people, in men, and in people with diabetes, high blood pressure, or a history of smoking, those groups account for most of the testing. With the introduction of high-sensitivity assays, even very small amounts of troponin can now be measured, allowing faster and more accurate decisions for a very large number of patients.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Cardiac enzymes are not a disease themselves but a marker of heart muscle injury. Their levels rise when heart cells are damaged:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Troponin:} the preferred test; it rises 3--6 hours after injury, peaks at about 12--24 hours, and can remain elevated for up to two weeks. It is highly specific to heart muscle
    \item \textbf{CK-MB (creatine kinase, heart fraction):} rises a little later and returns to normal within a few days; used mainly when troponin is not available
    \item \textbf{Myoglobin:} an older, less specific marker that rises early but also returns to normal quickly
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} when heart muscle cells are stressed or die, their contents spill into the blood, so higher levels generally indicate more extensive damage
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The test itself causes only a brief sting from the blood draw. The symptoms that lead to testing are those of a possible heart attack:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, pressure, or tightness that may spread to the arm, jaw, neck, or back
    \item Shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, or light-headedness
    \item In some people, particularly women, older adults, and people with diabetes, unusual tiredness, indigestion-like discomfort, or minimal symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A raised troponin confirms that heart muscle has been injured, but the cause is not always a heart attack:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A heart attack from a blocked coronary artery
    \item Severe heart strain without a blockage, such as in heart failure, very fast arrhythmias, or hypertensive crises
    \item Myocarditis, inflammation of the heart muscle from a virus or another cause
    \item Pulmonary embolism, in which a blood clot in the lungs stresses the right side of the heart
    \item Sepsis, severe infection, or major surgery placing stress on the heart
    \item Kidney failure, which can slow the removal of troponin from the blood and produce a chronically elevated level
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Chest pain should always be taken seriously. Anyone with symptoms that could be a heart attack should seek urgent care rather than waiting.
\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain or pressure lasting more than a few minutes, or occurring at rest
    \item Pain spreading to the arm, jaw, neck, or back
    \item Breathlessness, sweating, nausea, or feeling faint together with chest discomfort
    \item Any sudden, severe symptom that worries you
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Cardiac enzyme tests are interpreted as part of a standard heart attack assessment:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Timed samples:} an initial troponin on arrival and a repeat a few hours later; a rising or falling pattern indicates an acute injury
    \item \textbf{Electrocardiogram (ECG):} records the heart's electrical activity and may show the characteristic changes of a heart attack
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the nature and timing of symptoms, together with risk factors and examination findings
    \item \textbf{High-sensitivity troponin:} modern assays detect very small rises, allowing rapid rule-in or rule-out in some departments
    \item \textbf{Other blood tests:} full blood count, kidney function, and electrolytes, which affect interpretation and treatment
    \item \textbf{Follow-up imaging:} echocardiogram or coronary angiography if a heart attack is confirmed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The test result guides immediate and long-term treatment:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal results with a normal ECG:} the person is often observed and re-tested, and can usually be sent home with advice
    \item \textbf{Raised troponin:} indicates heart muscle injury, and the person is admitted for monitoring and investigation
    \item \textbf{If a heart attack is confirmed:} aspirin and other antiplatelet drugs, blood thinners, and urgent catheterisation to open a blocked artery where appropriate
    \item \textbf{If another cause is found:} treatment is directed at that condition, such as fluids and oxygen for heart strain or antibiotics for sepsis
    \item \textbf{Long-term care:} medicines to protect the heart, cardiac rehabilitation, and risk-factor management after a heart attack
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The blood test itself is very safe, with only rare problems:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bruising, discomfort, or slight bleeding at the puncture site
    \item Fainting or light-headedness during the blood draw, which is uncommon
    \item Infection at the puncture site, which is very rare
    \item Misinterpretation when a slightly raised result reflects a non-heart cause, leading to unnecessary worry or further tests
    \item A falsely reassuring normal result if the blood is taken too early, before the marker has had time to rise
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The size of the troponin rise broadly reflects the amount of heart muscle injured, and larger rises generally carry a greater risk of complications such as heart failure. However, modern high-sensitivity testing detects small increases that do not always mean a heart attack, so the result must always be read alongside the symptoms, the ECG, and the overall clinical picture. With prompt treatment of a confirmed heart attack, many people recover well and return to a full life, and a normal troponin with a normal ECG is reassuring in most presentations with chest pain.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Do not ignore chest pain; seek urgent assessment even if the symptoms are mild or intermittent
    \item Control blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood sugar with medical guidance
    \item Stop smoking, keep alcohol within recommended limits, and maintain a healthy weight
    \item Stay physically active and follow a heart-healthy diet
    \item Attend cardiac rehabilitation and follow-up after a heart attack
    \item Carry a list of your medicines and allergies when attending hospital
    \item Ask for an explanation of your results in plain language before you leave
\end{itemize}
