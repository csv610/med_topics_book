\section*{Definition and Overview}
Childhood cancer is a group of diseases in which abnormal cells in a child's body grow and divide without control. Unlike adult cancers, which are often linked to lifestyle and ageing, childhood cancers usually arise from cells that are developing and are rarely caused by things the child or family did. The most common types are leukaemia (cancer of the blood cells), brain and spinal cord tumours, lymphomas, and tumours of the kidney, nerve, bone, and muscle. Childhood cancers behave differently from adult cancers and usually respond well to treatment, with most children now surviving.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cancer is rare in childhood. Roughly 1 in 600 children will develop cancer before age 15, and around 1 in 4 of those affected will have leukaemia. The most common age group is children under 5, although some cancers, such as bone and muscle tumours, are more common in teenagers. Boys are affected slightly more often than girls. There is no strong link between childhood cancer and anything a family did; only a small minority of cases are caused by inherited gene changes. Survival has improved greatly over recent decades, and the majority of children with cancer now recover.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
In most cases the cause is unknown. Cancer begins when a single developing cell acquires changes (mutations) in its DNA that let it multiply out of control. In children these changes usually happen by chance during normal growth rather than being inherited:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chance mutations:} errors that occur as cells divide rapidly during growth
    \item \textbf{Inherited gene changes:} a small proportion of cancers run in families or occur with conditions such as neurofibromatosis or Down syndrome
    \item \textbf{Radiation:} high-dose radiation, such as previous treatment for another cancer, slightly increases risk
    \item \textbf{Weakened immunity:} children with certain immune conditions or on immune-suppressing treatment
\end{itemize}

Because the abnormal cells grow in tissues that are still developing, childhood cancers often grow quickly, but they also tend to respond more sensitively to treatment than many adult cancers.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on the type of cancer but often include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent fever, infections, or tiredness without an obvious cause (leukaemia)
    \item Bruising, nosebleeds, or bleeding that is hard to stop
    \item A lump or swelling that does not go away
    \item Headaches, especially in the morning, with vomiting or trouble walking (brain tumours)
    \item Bone pain, limping, or pain that wakes the child at night
    \item Weight loss or poor appetite
    \item Swollen glands that last more than a few weeks
    \item A change in the eye, such as a white glow seen in photos or a squint
\end{itemize}

Many of these symptoms are caused by common childhood illnesses, which is why doctors look carefully at patterns -- for example, symptoms that persist, get worse, or do not fit a usual illness.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Viral infections, glandular fever, and other illnesses causing fever and swollen glands
    \item Growing pains, injuries, or joint conditions vs.\ bone tumours or leukaemia
    \item Migraine and other causes of headaches vs.\ brain tumours
    \item Benign lumps, cysts, and enlarged lymph nodes vs.\ cancer
    \item Infection or other illness causing weight loss and tiredness
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a GP if your child has any symptom that is persistent, unusual, or getting worse -- particularly a lump, unexplained fever, night-time bone pain, weight loss, or bruising that is not explained. Most will turn out to be minor, but early review matters.

\textbf{Call 000 or go to an emergency department if your child:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Has a seizure, or is unusually drowsy or hard to wake
    \item Cannot breathe properly or is very pale and unwell
    \item Has a fever during cancer treatment (possible serious infection)
    \item Has sudden severe pain, weakness, or difficulty passing urine (possible cord compression)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} careful review of symptoms, family history, and a full physical check
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count and blood film, which are abnormal in most leukaemias
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} ultrasound, X-ray, CT, or MRI to find and map tumours
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} a sample of the tumour, or a bone marrow test for leukaemia, examined under a microscope to confirm the diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Genetic and molecular testing:} to identify the exact type of cancer and guide treatment
    \item \textbf{Staging tests:} scans and other checks to see whether the cancer has spread
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is planned by a specialist team and usually combines several approaches:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy:} medicines that kill dividing cancer cells, usually given in cycles
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} to remove solid tumours where possible
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} high-energy radiation to treat certain tumours or residual disease
    \item \textbf{Targeted therapy and immunotherapy:} newer medicines that attack specific features of cancer cells
    \item \textbf{Stem cell (bone marrow) transplant:} for some high-risk leukaemias
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} blood transfusions, medicines to prevent infection and nausea, and specialist nursing
    \item \textbf{Psychosocial support:} counselling, play therapy, and schooling support for the child and family
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection, bleeding, and anaemia due to chemotherapy lowering blood counts
    \item Nausea, mouth sores, and fatigue during treatment
    \item Late effects of treatment, including effects on growth, fertility, hearing, heart, or risk of a second cancer
    \item Learning or concentration difficulties, especially after brain treatment
    \item Emotional and financial strain on the family
    \item For children who do not respond to treatment, the illness can be life-limiting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for childhood cancer has improved dramatically. Overall, around 8 in 10 children diagnosed with cancer in high-income countries now survive long term, and for some types, such as acute lymphoblastic leukaemia and many lymphomas, survival is even higher. Outcome depends on the type of cancer, how advanced it is at diagnosis, and how it responds to treatment. Survivors may need long-term follow-up for late effects of treatment. Most children who are cured go on to live full, active adult lives.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Childhood cancers cannot usually be prevented, since most arise by chance during growth
    \item Reduce the risk of certain virus-related cancers by keeping HPV vaccination up to date
    \item Seek prompt medical review of persistent symptoms -- early diagnosis improves outcome
    \item If your child is on treatment, follow the team's advice on infection risk, mouth care, and when to seek urgent help
    \item Ask the treatment team about late-effects clinics and follow-up plans
    \item Access support services, such as hospital social workers and cancer support charities, to help the family cope
\end{itemize}
