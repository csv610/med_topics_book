\chapter{Genetic Disorders}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A genetic disorder is a health condition caused by an abnormality in a person's DNA. These abnormalities can range from a change in a single gene, to extra or missing chromosomes, to alterations affecting large segments of a chromosome. Some genetic disorders are inherited from parents, while others arise spontaneously as new mutations. Genetic disorders include well-known conditions such as cystic fibrosis, sickle cell disease, Down syndrome, and Huntington disease, and they affect virtually every body system. While many genetic disorders are rare individually, together they are common and are a major cause of illness, disability, and early death, particularly in children. Understanding the genetic basis of a condition informs diagnosis, prognosis, treatment, and family planning.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Genetic disorders collectively affect a substantial proportion of the population. Around 3--5\% of all live-born babies have a significant congenital abnormality, many of which have a genetic basis. Chromosomal disorders such as Down syndrome occur in about 1 in 700 births, with risk increasing with maternal age. Single-gene disorders vary widely in frequency: cystic fibrosis affects about 1 in 2,500 to 3,500 births in populations of European descent, sickle cell disease is common in people of African ancestry, and thalassaemia is common in Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, and Asian populations. Many genetic disorders are rare, but more than 6,000 such conditions have been described. Genetic factors also contribute to common diseases including many cancers, diabetes, and heart disease.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Genetic disorders arise from alterations in the genetic material that affect the function of genes and proteins.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Single-gene (Mendelian) disorders:} caused by mutations in one gene, such as cystic fibrosis (autosomal recessive), Huntington disease (autosomal dominant), and Duchenne muscular dystrophy (X-linked)
    \item \textbf{Chromosomal disorders:} caused by changes in chromosome number or structure, such as Down syndrome (an extra copy of chromosome 21), Turner syndrome, and chromosomal deletions or duplications
    \item \textbf{Multifactorial disorders:} caused by the interaction of several genes with environmental factors, such as cleft lip and palate, neural tube defects, and many common diseases
    \item \textbf{Mitochondrial disorders:} caused by mutations in the DNA of mitochondria, which are inherited from the mother
    \item \textbf{Somatic mutations:} genetic changes that occur in body cells during life and are not inherited, such as those driving most cancers
    \item \textbf{New mutations:} some disorders arise from spontaneous mutations in the egg or sperm of unaffected parents
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features of genetic disorders are extremely varied, depending on the underlying cause and the body systems affected. Some general patterns are useful to recognise.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Birth defects and structural abnormalities, including of the heart, limbs, and face
    \item Intellectual disability or developmental delay, with varying severity
    \item Growth problems, including short stature or overgrowth
    \item Neurological features such as seizures, movement disorders, or progressive loss of skills
    \item Metabolic disturbances such as episodes of vomiting, lethargy, or unusual body odours in metabolic disorders
    \item Immune deficiency and recurrent infections
    \item Predisposition to cancers at unusually young ages or in multiple family members
    \item Family history of a condition, stillbirth, or infant death in close relatives
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because genetic disorders can present in many ways, they are often considered alongside non-genetic causes of the same features.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Environmental causes of birth defects, such as fetal alcohol spectrum disorder, infections in pregnancy, and exposure to certain medicines
    \item Nutritional deficiencies, including iron, vitamin B12, or folate deficiency
    \item Endocrine and metabolic disease acquired in childhood or adult life
    \item Infections causing developmental regression or intellectual disability
    \item Autoimmune, inflammatory, and degenerative conditions
    \item Non-accidental injury when considering unexplained fractures or bruising
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Speak with your GP if you have concerns about a child's development, growth, or health that might be genetic, or if there is a family history of a genetic condition and you are planning a family. Anyone considering testing for an inherited condition should seek referral to a clinical genetics service for counselling before and after testing.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A baby or child with sudden lethargy, poor feeding, unusual breathing, or seizures, which may indicate a metabolic crisis or other serious condition
    \item Any collapse or severe illness in a person with a known metabolic or genetic disorder, as they may require urgent specialised care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosing a genetic disorder often requires a combination of clinical assessment and specialised laboratory testing.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} detailed history including family tree (pedigree), examination for dysmorphic features, and developmental assessment
    \item \textbf{Chromosomal analysis (karyotype):} identifies large chromosomal abnormalities such as Down syndrome
    \item \textbf{Chromosomal microarray:} detects small deletions or duplications not visible on a standard karyotype
    \item \textbf{Genetic (DNA) testing:} examines specific genes for known disease-causing mutations
    \item \textbf{Gene panels and whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing:} used when the cause is unclear or many genes are possible
    \item \textbf{Biochemical testing:} blood, urine, or enzyme assays that detect inborn errors of metabolism
    \item \textbf{Prenatal testing:} screening and diagnostic tests during pregnancy, including non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT), amniocentesis, and chorionic villus sampling
    \item \textbf{Newborn screening:} bloodspot screening at birth detects a range of treatable metabolic and genetic conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Most genetic disorders cannot yet be cured, but modern care focuses on treating symptoms, preventing complications, and improving quality of life.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Multidisciplinary care:} specialists in genetics, paediatrics, cardiology, neurology, and allied health work together to meet individual needs
    \item \textbf{Symptom management:} medications, surgery, physiotherapy, and occupational therapy tailored to the condition
    \item \textbf{Metabolic management:} special diets, supplements, or medications that control many inborn errors of metabolism
    \item \textbf{Enzyme replacement and targeted therapies:} available for a growing number of specific disorders, including some lysosomal storage diseases
    \item \textbf{Screening for complications:} regular surveillance for known associations such as heart, kidney, or eye problems
    \item \textbf{Educational and developmental support:} early intervention and special education services maximise a child's potential
    \item \textbf{Genetic counselling:} helps individuals and families understand the condition, its inheritance, and their options
    \item \textbf{Family planning options:} prenatal diagnosis, pre-implantation genetic testing, and carrier screening
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive organ damage or failure in many conditions
    \item Intellectual disability and its educational and social consequences
    \item Recurrent infections and immune deficiency
    \item Increased risk of certain cancers
    \item Chronic pain, mobility problems, and reduced independence
    \item Psychological and financial burden on individuals and families
    \item Grief, guilt, and relationship stress associated with diagnosis and care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of genetic disorders varies enormously, from conditions that are compatible with a full and normal life to those that are life-limiting in early childhood. Advances in diagnosis, treatment, and supportive care have dramatically improved outcomes for many conditions. Newborn screening and early treatment have transformed the outlook for several metabolic disorders, and targeted therapies are changing the course of previously untreatable diseases. For families, genetic counselling provides the information needed to make informed decisions, and many families find support through patient organisations and peer networks.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek genetic counselling before pregnancy if there is a family history of a genetic condition
    \item Consider carrier screening, which can identify couples at risk of having a child with certain recessive disorders
    \item Attend all recommended newborn screening tests
    \item Take folic acid before and during early pregnancy to reduce the risk of neural tube defects
    \item Avoid alcohol, tobacco, and harmful substances during pregnancy
    \item Ensure all childhood immunisations are up to date to prevent infections that can complicate genetic conditions
    \item Connect with patient support organisations for information, advocacy, and peer support
    \item Maintain regular medical follow-up and screening for the complications of any diagnosed condition
\end{itemize}
