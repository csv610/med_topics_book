\chapter{Huntington's (Huntington's Disease)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Huntington's disease, often shortened to Huntington's, is a progressive inherited brain disorder caused by a single faulty gene. It damages nerve cells in parts of the brain that control movement, thinking, and emotion, producing a characteristic combination of involuntary movements, cognitive decline, and psychiatric symptoms. The disease typically begins in midlife and advances gradually, and it affects every aspect of daily life. While there is no cure, coordinated care, medication for symptoms, and family support make a substantial difference to quality of life.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Huntington's affects approximately 1 in 7,000--10,000 people, with around 2,000--3,000 people living with it in Australia. Onset usually occurs between 30 and 50 years of age, and about 5--10\% of cases begin in childhood or adolescence. Because the disease is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, each child of an affected parent has a 50\% chance of carrying the gene, and many thousands of Australians live with that risk. Predictive testing is available through specialist services.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cause:} an expanded CAG repeat in the huntingtin (HTT) gene
    \item \textbf{Inheritance:} autosomal dominant; one copy of the faulty gene is enough to cause the disease
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} the abnormal gene produces a toxic protein that accumulates in neurons and disrupts their function, especially in the basal ganglia (striatum)
    \item \textbf{Progressive cell death:} neuronal loss underlies the worsening movement, cognitive, and psychiatric symptoms
    \item \textbf{Repeat expansion:} larger repeats cause earlier onset and more rapid progression
    \item \textbf{Anticipation:} repeat length can increase across generations
    \item \textbf{No environmental cause:} the disease is purely genetic
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chorea: involuntary, jerky, dance-like movements of the face, limbs, and trunk
    \item Impaired coordination, balance, and fine motor skills
    \item Cognitive changes: difficulty planning, learning, and concentrating
    \item Psychiatric features: depression, anxiety, irritability, and obsessions
    \item Behavioural changes and social withdrawal
    \item Speech and swallowing difficulties in later stages
    \item Juvenile onset presents with rigidity, seizures, and learning difficulties
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Other causes of chorea: medications, metabolic disorders, and other neurodegenerative diseases
    \item Parkinson's disease and related conditions for the rigid forms
    \item Depression, bipolar disorder, and other psychiatric illness, which can precede movement symptoms
    \item Dementia syndromes
    \item Genetic testing is diagnostic
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with a family history of Huntington's should consider genetic counselling, and anyone with progressive movement or psychiatric symptoms should see a neurologist.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Thoughts of suicide or self-harm
    \item Choking or severe swallowing difficulty
    \item Falls with serious injury
    \item Severe confusion or agitation
    \item Any life-threatening emergency
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurological examination:} assessment of movement, coordination, cognition, and mood
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} confirms the CAG repeat length
    \item \textbf{Predictive testing:} for at-risk people, through programs with counselling
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} MRI may show striatal atrophy
    \item \textbf{Psychiatric assessment:} for mood and behavioural symptoms
    \item \textbf{Prenatal and pre-implantation options:} available with genetic counselling
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chorea treatment:} medicines that reduce involuntary movements
    \item \textbf{Psychiatric treatment:} antidepressants and other medicines for mood and behaviour
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} exercise, balance, and mobility programs
    \item \textbf{Speech pathology:} communication and swallowing management
    \item \textbf{Nutrition:} dietary support to maintain weight
    \item \textbf{Multidisciplinary team:} coordinated specialist care
    \item \textbf{Family and caregiver support:} education, counselling, and respite
    \item \textbf{Clinical trials:} research into treatments that target the underlying disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Aspiration pneumonia from swallowing problems
    \item Falls and fractures
    \item Malnutrition and weight loss
    \item Depression and suicide risk
    \item Progressive disability and loss of independence
    \item Caregiver burden and family stress
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Huntington's is a progressive disease without a cure, but its course is manageable in important ways. Medication and therapy improve symptoms, multidisciplinary care maintains function and quality of life for years, and families are supported through counselling and services. Life expectancy after diagnosis is typically 10--25 years, often from complications. Research into gene-silencing and other disease-modifying therapies is advancing and may change the outlook for future generations.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek genetic counselling before predictive testing
    \item Participate in specialist care programs
    \item Maintain physical activity, nutrition, and social connection
    \item Plan ahead for future care needs
    \item Use support groups and community services
    \item Caregivers should arrange respite and personal support
    \item Consider research participation with specialist advice
    \item Discuss reproductive options with a genetics service
\end{itemize}
