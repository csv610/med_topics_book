\chapter{Dog Bites}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dog bites are injuries caused by a dog's teeth, ranging from minor scratches to deep puncture wounds, tissue damage, and fractures. They are common, especially in children, and can become infected because dog mouths contain many bacteria. Prompt cleaning of the wound reduces the risk of infection, and some bites require antibiotics, stitches, or surgery. Rabies is not present in Australia, but tetanus is a risk, and other infections can occur. Most dog bites can be prevented with supervision and responsible pet ownership. This chapter covers first aid, when to seek medical care, and prevention of dog bites.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Dog bites are a significant public health issue: around 100,000 people attend Australian hospitals for dog bite treatment each year, with children most at risk. Children under five are most likely to be bitten on the face and head, while adults are more often bitten on the hands and legs. Most bites come from the family dog or a dog known to the child. Around half of dog bites become infected without proper care, and a small proportion require hospitalisation or surgery.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Injury:} teeth can tear, crush, and puncture tissue
    \item \textbf{Bacteria:} dog mouths contain many bacteria, including Pasteurella, streptococci, and staphylococci
    \item \textbf{Infection:} puncture wounds seal over quickly, trapping bacteria deep in the tissue
    \item \textbf{High-risk sites:} hands, joints, tendons, and the face have higher infection risk
    \item \textbf{Risk factors for infection:} deep wounds, delayed treatment, diabetes, and weakened immunity
    \item \textbf{Tetanus:} any bite can introduce tetanus spores
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Scratches, puncture wounds, lacerations, or crush injuries
    \item Pain, swelling, bruising, and bleeding
    \item Signs of infection: increasing pain, redness, heat, swelling, pus, and fever
    \item Damage to tendons, nerves, or bone in deep bites
    \item Fractures of small bones, especially in hand bites
    \item Rabies is not a concern in Australia, but tetanus protection matters
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infected versus non-infected bites, which need different management
    \item Superficial versus deep bites involving tendons, joints, or bone
    \item Bites requiring antibiotics versus those managed with cleaning alone
    \item Other animal and human bites, which have different infection risks
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for any bite that breaks the skin, especially on the hands, face, or joints, and for bites in children, older adults, or people with diabetes or weakened immunity. Prompt care allows wound cleaning, antibiotics when needed, and tetanus assessment.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heavy bleeding that does not stop with pressure
    \item A bite that has severed or severely damaged tissue
    \item Signs of serious infection: spreading redness, high fever, or feeling very unwell
    \item Difficulty using the affected hand or limb
    \item A large or dangerous dog attack, especially involving a child
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Wound assessment:} depth, size, site, and tissue damage
    \item \textbf{Examination for deeper injury:} checking tendons, nerves, and bone, especially in hand bites
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} where fracture or foreign material is suspected
    \item \textbf{Tetanus status:} review of vaccination history
    \item \textbf{Cultures:} swabs if the wound is already infected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First aid:} wash the wound immediately with soap and water, apply pressure to stop bleeding, and cover with a clean dressing
    \item \textbf{Professional cleaning:} thorough cleaning of the wound, sometimes under local anaesthetic
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} given for deep, high-risk, or infected bites, and for bites in people with weakened immunity
    \item \textbf{Tetanus:} a booster is given if vaccination is not up to date
    \item \textbf{Stitches:} some wounds are closed, while others are left open to drain and reduce infection risk
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} for severe tissue damage, fractures, or deep infections
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review to check healing and detect infection early
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wound infection, including cellulitis and abscess
    \item Deep infection of joints, tendons, or bone
    \item Scarring, including facial scars in children
    \item Psychological effects, including fear of dogs
    \item Rarely, severe infection such as sepsis
    \item Rabies risk is negligible in Australia but relevant to travellers bitten abroad
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most dog bites heal well with prompt care, and the risk of infection is low when wounds are cleaned early and antibiotics are used appropriately. Deep or infected bites may take longer to heal and leave scars, but serious complications are uncommon. Children who are bitten usually recover fully, though emotional support may be needed. Prevention through supervision and safe handling of dogs is the most effective approach.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Supervise children around dogs at all times
    \item Teach children to be gentle and to avoid disturbing eating, sleeping, or playing dogs
    \item Do not approach unfamiliar dogs or run from a dog
    \item Choose and train dogs appropriately for the family
    \item Keep dogs under control and on lead in public
    \item Report dangerous dogs to the local council
    \item Wash any bite promptly with soap and water
    \item Keep tetanus vaccination up to date
\end{itemize}
