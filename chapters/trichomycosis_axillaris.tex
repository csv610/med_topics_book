\chapter{Trichomycosis axillaris}
\index{Trichomycosis axillaris}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Trichomycosis axillaris (also referred to in medical literature as trichobacteriosis axillaris, trichobacteriosis, or trichomycosis nodosa) is a common, superficial, and entirely benign bacterial pseudomycosis affecting the hair shafts of the axillary vault, and less frequently, the pubic and perianal regions (where it is termed trichomycosis pubis or trichobacteriosis pubis). Despite the historical nomenclature incorporating the word ``mycosis''---which implies a fungal etiology---it is crucial to establish that this condition is exclusively bacterial. The term ``trichomycosis'' was coined in the mid-nineteenth century, a period when microscopic discrimination between bacteria and fungi was rudimentary, and many superficial cutaneous eruptions characterized by nodular encrustations were presumed to be fungal diseases. While modern dermatological taxonomy favors ``trichobacteriosis'' to reflect its bacterial origin, ``trichomycosis axillaris'' remains the most widely recognized term in clinical practice.

The hallmark clinical feature of this condition is the formation of adherent, granular, waxy nodules or continuous sheaths that coat and encircle the hair shafts. These concretions are composed of massive colonies of coryneform bacteria embedded in an insoluble, cement-like matrix. The infection is primarily cosmetic and symptomatic, characterized by a persistent and unpleasant body odor (bromhidrosis) and discolored sweat that stains undergarments. Although the disease does not cause hair loss, structural damage to the hair cortex, or inflammatory skin lesions, it can lead to significant aesthetic concerns, social embarrassment, and psychological distress for the patient. Understanding this condition is essential for healthcare providers to ensure correct diagnosis, prevent unnecessary antifungal treatments, and implement simple, effective therapeutic interventions that improve the patient's quality of life.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of trichomycosis axillaris is influenced by geographic, environmental, demographic, and behavioral factors. The condition occurs worldwide, but its prevalence is substantially higher in tropical, subtropical, and warm, humid temperate regions. High environmental temperatures and humidity levels stimulate sweating and increase skin hydration, creating an ideal microenvironment for the proliferation of coryneform bacteria.

Demographically, trichomycosis axillaris can affect individuals of any age, gender, or ethnic background. However, it is most frequently diagnosed in post-pubertal adolescents and young adults. This age distribution corresponds to the developmental activation of the apocrine sweat glands, which produce a lipid-rich secretion that serves as a primary nutrient source for the causative bacteria. The condition is significantly more common in males than in females. This gender discrepancy is attributed to several factors, including the greater density and thickness of axillary hair in males, higher rates of apocrine sweat production, and cultural grooming practices, as females in many societies regularly shave their axillary hair, thereby removing the substrate required for bacterial colonization.

The exact global incidence and prevalence rates remain poorly defined because the condition is often asymptomatic, and many affected individuals do not seek medical advice. However, in specific cohorts exposed to predisposing conditions, such as military recruits, athletes, manual laborers, and individuals living in crowded or low-resource settings with limited hygiene facilities, studies have reported prevalence rates ranging from 15\% to over 30\%. It is not considered highly contagious, but transmission can occur through direct physical contact or the sharing of contaminated personal items like towels, clothing, or razors in close-contact environments such as locker rooms, barracks, or households.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The aetiology of trichomycosis axillaris is centered on the overgrowth of aerobic, Gram-positive, non-spore-forming, pleomorphic rod-shaped bacteria belonging to the genus \textit{Corynebacterium}. The primary causative pathogen is \textit{Corynebacterium flavescens} (historically classified as \textit{Corynebacterium tenuis}). Other species, including \textit{Corynebacterium propinquum} and occasionally other cutaneous coryneforms, have also been isolated from the concretions. These bacteria are normal commensals of human skin, particularly in areas rich in sebaceous and apocrine glands.

The pathophysiology of the condition involves a series of sequential steps:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bacterial Proliferation}: Under conditions of warmth, moisture, and abundant apocrine secretions, the resident \textit{Corynebacterium} population undergoes rapid multiplication.
    \item \textbf{Cuticular Adhesion}: The bacteria attach to the cuticle of the hair shaft. They do not penetrate the hair shaft cuticle or invade the hair cortex or the hair follicle. The infection remains strictly extracellular and superficial.
    \item \textbf{Biofilm and Cement Production}: Once attached, the bacteria secrete an insoluble, sticky, extracellular mucopolysaccharide matrix. This substance acts as a cement, gluing the bacterial cells to each other and to the hair shaft. This process leads to the formation of the characteristic nodules or sheaths that encase the hair.
    \item \textbf{Chromogenesis}: The concretions display three distinct color variants: yellow (flava), red (rubra), and black (nigra). Yellow concretions are the most common, accounting for over 90\% of cases globally. Red and black variants are rare and are typically seen in warm, tropical climates. The color variation is determined by the specific bacterial strain, the production of carotenoid pigments by the bacteria, or chemical interactions between bacterial metabolites and sweat components.
    \item \textbf{Apocrine Sweat Metabolism}: The bacteria metabolize the lipids, proteins, and steroids present in apocrine sweat. This metabolic process releases volatile organic compounds, such as short-chain fatty acids (including isovaleric acid) and ammonia. These compounds are responsible for the characteristic pungent and offensive axillary odor (bromhidrosis).
    \item \textbf{Clothing Discoloration}: The pigmented bacterial masses and their water-soluble metabolic products dissolve in sweat and are transferred to clothing, causing persistent yellow, red, or dark staining that is resistant to standard laundering.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of trichomycosis axillaris is distinctive and often allows for a straightforward diagnosis. The primary clinical features include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hair Shaft Concretions}: The key physical finding is the presence of adherent, waxy, or granular nodules or a continuous sheath encircling the hair shafts of the axilla. These nodules are typically 1 to 2 millimeters in diameter and can be spaced along the hair shaft, giving it a beaded appearance, or they may form a uniform sleeve that covers the entire length of the hair.
    \item \textbf{Nodule Consistency and Adhesion}: The concretions vary in texture from soft and waxy to hard and gritty. They are firmly cemented to the hair shaft and cannot be wiped, washed, or easily scraped off, which distinguishes them from accumulation of deodorants or hygiene products.
    \item \textbf{Color Presentation}: The yellow variant (\textit{trichomycosis flava}) is by far the most prevalent. The red (\textit{trichomycosis rubra}) and black (\textit{trichomycosis nigra}) variants are much less common and are characterized by vibrant red or dark brown-to-black nodules, respectively, which can be alarming to the patient.
    \item \textbf{Axillary Bromhidrosis}: Patients frequently report a strong, unpleasant, and persistent body odor emanating from the underarms. This malodor is resistant to regular washing and the application of standard deodorants, often serving as the primary reason the patient seeks medical attention.
    \item \textbf{Sweat and Clothing Staining}: The sweat in the affected axillae may appear discolored, matching the color of the hair nodules. This discolored sweat transfers to clothing, particularly white or light-colored shirts, leaving stubborn yellow, orange, red, or dark underarm stains.
    \item \textbf{Lack of Cutaneous Inflammation}: The underlying skin of the axillary vault, the hair follicles, and the hair roots are completely unaffected. There is no associated erythema, pruritus, pain, scaling, or hair loss. The patient does not experience physical discomfort other than the secondary effects of sweat accumulation and odor.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A careful differential diagnosis is necessary to distinguish trichomycosis axillaris from other superficial infections and structural hair disorders. The key conditions to consider include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Erythrasma}: This is a superficial skin infection also caused by a \textit{Corynebacterium} species, specifically \textit{Corynebacterium minutissimum}. While it can co-exist with trichomycosis axillaris, erythrasma affects the skin of intertriginous areas (such as the axillae, groin, and toe webs) rather than the hair shafts. It presents as well-demarcated, reddish-brown, macular patches with a fine scale. Under Wood's lamp, erythrasma exhibits a characteristic coral-red fluorescence due to the production of coproporphyrin III by the bacteria, whereas trichomycosis axillaris affects the hair shaft and typically fluoresces pale-yellow.
    \item \textbf{White Piedra}: A superficial fungal infection of the hair shaft caused by yeasts of the genus \textit{Trichosporon} (such as \textit{Trichosporon beigelii}). It is characterized by soft, white, green, or light brown nodules along the hair shaft. Unlike trichomycosis axillaris, white piedra can affect scalp, facial, and body hair. Microscopic examination of a KOH mount reveals fungal hyphae, arthroconidia, and blastoconidia, distinguishing it from the bacterial coccobacilli of trichomycosis.
    \item \textbf{Black Piedra}: A superficial fungal infection of the hair shaft caused by the dematiaceous fungus \textit{Piedraia hortae}. It presents as hard, black, firmly adherent nodules that primarily affect scalp hair. Microscopic examination reveals fungal asci containing ascospores, confirming its fungal origin.
    \item \textbf{Pediculosis Pubis}: Also known as ``crabs,'' this is an infestation by the pubic louse \textit{Pthirus pubis}. The nits (eggs) are glued to the hair shafts and can be mistaken for the nodules of trichomycosis pubis. However, nits are teardrop-shaped, uniform in size, and located close to the skin surface. Microscopic examination easily identifies the characteristic structure of the nit and the presence of adult lice or nymphs, which are absent in trichomycosis.
    \item \textbf{Trichorrhexis Nodosa}: A structural defect of the hair shaft characterized by microscopic nodes that resemble two brushes pushed together. It is caused by physical or chemical trauma to the hair (such as excessive heat, chemical treatments, or vigorous brushing). Under microscopy, there are no microorganisms; instead, the hair shaft is fractured and split.
    \item \textbf{Monilethrix}: A rare, inherited hair shaft disorder characterized by a beaded appearance of the hair. It leads to hair fragility and patchy alopecia. Microscopic examination shows alternating nodes and constrictions of the hair shaft itself, without any external concretions.
    \item \textbf{Hair Casts (Pseudonits)}: These are tubular, white-to-gray sheaths that encircle the hair shaft. They represent persistent inner and outer root sheaths that fail to desquamate. Unlike the concretions of trichomycosis axillaris, hair casts slide easily along the hair shaft when pulled, and microscopy shows only keratinous debris without bacterial colonies.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Trichomycosis axillaris is a benign, non-systemic, and non-contagious condition that does not present an immediate threat to physical health. However, medical advice from a primary care physician or dermatologist should be sought in the following circumstances:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The nodules and associated body odor do not improve after several weeks of improved hygiene practices, daily washing with antibacterial soaps, or shaving the affected hair.
    \item The persistent body odor or staining of clothing causes significant psychological distress, social anxiety, or embarrassment, interfering with daily activities, work, or relationships.
    \item There is co-existing skin irritation, itching, pain, or redness in the axillae, which may indicate a secondary fungal or bacterial skin infection, or an allergic reaction to self-treatment products.
\end{itemize}
It is important to note that while trichomycosis axillaris is a localized skin condition, patients should seek immediate emergency medical care if they experience severe, rapidly spreading skin redness, swelling, warmth, severe pain, or systemic symptoms such as high fever, chills, confusion, rapid heart rate, or difficulty breathing. In such situations, patients must contact emergency services immediately by dialing local emergency services in many countries, 911 in the United States, or 999 in the United Kingdom, or proceed to the nearest emergency department, as these symptoms may indicate a serious, invasive bacterial infection like cellulitis or sepsis.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of trichomycosis axillaris is primarily clinical, based on a detailed physical examination and history. However, several simple and non-invasive diagnostic investigations can be performed to confirm the diagnosis and rule out look-alike conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical Examination}: A thorough visual inspection of the axillary vault and pubic area under adequate lighting. The physician looks for the characteristic yellow, red, or black nodules or sheaths coating the hair shafts. Palpation of the hair reveals a waxy or gritty texture, and the firmness of the attachment is assessed.
    \item \textbf{Wood's Lamp Examination}: Examination of the affected hair under a long-wave ultraviolet light (wavelength approximately 365 nanometers). In many cases, the concretions of trichomycosis axillaris will fluoresce a pale-yellow or greenish-white color. This helps differentiate it from erythrasma (which fluoresces bright coral-red) and fungal infections like microsporum tinea capitis (which fluoresces bright green). It is important to note that the red and black variants, and some yellow variants, may not exhibit fluorescence.
    \item \textbf{Direct Microscopic Examination (KOH Preparation)}: A simple, rapid, and confirmatory test. Several affected hairs are plucked and placed on a microscope slide. A few drops of 10\% to 20\% potassium hydroxide (KOH) solution are added, and a coverslip is applied. The slide is gently heated to dissolve keratin and examined under a light microscope. Microscopy reveals a thick, amorphous, or granular sheath of bacterial coccobacilli wrapping around the intact hair shaft. The absence of fungal hyphae, spores, or arthroconidia rules out superficial fungal infections like white or black piedra.
    \item \textbf{Gram Staining}: A smear can be prepared by crushing a concretion on a glass slide, fixing it, and performing a Gram stain. Under high-power magnification, the stain will reveal abundant Gram-positive coccobacilli, confirming the bacterial nature of the infection.
    \item \textbf{Dermoscopy (Trichoscopy)}: The use of a handheld dermatoscope allows for magnified visualization of the hair shafts in the clinic. Under dermoscopy, the nodules appear as waxy, yellow-brown, or reddish concretions that partially or completely encircle the hair shaft. They may display a ``brush-like,'' ``feather-like,'' or ``beaded'' appearance, which is highly characteristic.
    \item \textbf{Bacterial Culture}: Culturing the bacteria is rarely necessary for routine diagnosis. However, in atypical or treatment-resistant cases, a sample of the hair can be sent to a microbiology laboratory. The bacteria can be cultured on specialized media, such as sheep blood agar or brain-heart infusion agar, to isolate and identify the specific \textit{Corynebacterium} species and determine antibiotic sensitivity if necessary.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of trichomycosis axillaris is straightforward and highly effective. The treatment plan is tailored to the patient's preferences, cultural practices, and the severity of the symptoms. The primary management strategies include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Shaving the Affected Hair}: The most rapid, effective, and simple treatment. Shaving the axillary or pubic hair completely removes the bacterial biofilm, the concretions, and the localized bacterial reservoir. In many cases, a single shave combined with subsequent daily washing is curative. If the patient is willing to shave, no further medical treatment is usually required.
    \item \textbf{Topical Antibiotic Therapy}: For patients who prefer not to shave their hair due to personal, aesthetic, or cultural reasons, or as an adjunct to shaving to prevent recurrence. The most commonly prescribed topical antibiotics include clindamycin 1\% gel or solution, erythromycin 2\% gel or solution, or fusidic acid cream. These are applied to the affected area twice daily for two to three weeks. Topical antibiotics are highly effective at eliminating the causative \textit{Corynebacterium} species.
    \item \textbf{Antiseptic Cleansers}: Daily washing of the underarms with antiseptic washes is recommended to reduce the bacterial load on the skin surface. Highly effective agents include benzoyl peroxide (5\% to 10\% wash) or chlorhexidine gluconate solution. Benzoyl peroxide has potent antibacterial and keratolytic properties that help dissolve the concretions. However, patients must be warned that benzoyl peroxide can bleach clothing, bedsheets, and towels, and may cause skin irritation.
    \item \textbf{Management of Hyperhidrosis}: Since excessive sweating is the main predisposing factor for the condition, controlling sweat is crucial to prevent recurrence. The first-line treatment is the application of a clinical-strength antiperspirant containing 15\% to 20\% aluminum chloride hexahydrate. This should be applied to completely dry axillary skin at bedtime once or twice a week. In severe, refractory cases of hyperhidrosis, dermatological interventions such as botulinum toxin type A injections into the axillary skin or oral anticholinergic medications (e.g., glycopyrrolate) may be considered.
    \item \textbf{Topical Antifungal Agents}: Topical azole creams, such as miconazole, clotrimazole, or ketoconazole, can be used. Although designed as antifungals, these agents possess intrinsic Gram-positive antibacterial activity and can help clear the infection while addressing any co-existing fungal components.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Trichomycosis axillaris is a superficial infection that does not invade deep tissues, enter the bloodstream, or cause systemic illness. Consequently, physical complications are minimal, but the condition can lead to several secondary issues:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Social and Psychological Anxiety}: The most significant complication for many patients is the psychosocial impact. The persistent, pungent body odor (bromhidrosis) and visible, unsightly nodules on the hair can cause severe embarrassment, self-consciousness, social withdrawal, and anxiety, particularly in professional or intimate settings.
    \item \textbf{Co-existing Dermatological Infections}: The warm, moist environment that allows \textit{Corynebacterium} to thrive also predisposes the patient to other superficial infections. It is common for patients with trichomycosis axillaris to have co-existing erythrasma (manifesting as skin discoloration and scaling) or intertrigo (inflammation of the skin folds).
    \item \textbf{Treatment-Induced Contact Dermatitis}: The use of strong topical treatments, such as benzoyl peroxide washes or aluminum chloride antiperspirants, can cause skin irritation, redness, peeling, itching, and burning (irritant contact dermatitis). Rarely, patients may develop an allergic contact dermatitis to topical antibiotic preparations.
    \item \textbf{Secondary Folliculitis}: If the bacterial overgrowth is severe or if the skin barrier is compromised by shaving or harsh treatments, the bacteria can invade the hair follicles, leading to localized folliculitis characterized by painful, red papules and pustules.
    \item \textbf{Permanent Clothing Damage}: The pigmented sweat and bacterial proteins can permanently stain white and light-colored clothing, resulting in financial loss and frustration for the patient.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals diagnosed with trichomycosis axillaris is excellent. The condition is completely benign and is easily cured with appropriate treatment.
If the patient chooses to shave the affected axillary or pubic hair, the condition resolves immediately. For patients who undergo topical antibiotic or antiseptic therapy without shaving, complete clearance of the bacterial concretions and resolution of symptoms are typically achieved within two to three weeks of consistent treatment.

Recurrence is the main clinical challenge. Because the causative \textit{Corynebacterium} species are normal components of the human skin flora, they will readily repopulate the hair shafts if the predisposing factors---namely excessive sweating (hyperhidrosis), poor hygiene, and high local humidity---are not addressed. Therefore, long-term success depends on lifestyle modifications and preventive self-care measures. The infection does not lead to permanent hair loss, structural damage to the hair follicles, scarring of the skin, or systemic health complications. With proper compliance, a 100\% cure rate is expected.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventing the recurrence of trichomycosis axillaris and maintaining healthy underarm hygiene involves keeping the axillary and pubic areas clean, dry, and well-ventilated. The following self-care measures are recommended:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Daily Hygiene Practices}: Wash the underarms daily with mild soap and water, especially after physical exertion, exercise, or exposure to hot and humid weather, to remove sweat, sebum, and transient bacteria.
    \item \textbf{Regular Hair Maintenance}: Keep axillary and pubic hair short or shaved. Regular shaving or trimming reduces the surface area available for bacterial attachment and biofilm formation, and allows for better air circulation and easier cleansing.
    \item \textbf{Use of Antiperspirants}: Apply a daily antiperspirant containing aluminum compounds to reduce sweat production. For individuals with heavy sweating, clinical-strength antiperspirants containing aluminum chloride hexahydrate should be applied at night to dry skin.
    \item \textbf{Appropriate Clothing Choices}: Wear loose-fitting clothing made of natural, breathable fibers such as cotton, linen, or wool, which allow sweat to evaporate. Avoid tight-fitting synthetic fabrics (like polyester or nylon) that trap moisture and heat against the skin.
    \item \textbf{Thorough Drying}: After bathing or showering, ensure the axillary and groin areas are completely dry before applying deodorants, antiperspirants, or dressing. If necessary, a hair dryer on a cool setting can be used.
    \item \textbf{Avoid Sharing Personal Items}: Do not share towels, washcloths, clothing, or razors with others to minimize the risk of transferring bacteria, even though the condition is not highly contagious.
    \item \textbf{Weight Management}: In individuals where obesity contributes to deep skin folds, increased localized sweating, and friction, achieving and maintaining a healthy weight can help reduce moisture accumulation in intertriginous areas.
    \item \textbf{Self-Monitoring}: Inspect the underarm hair periodically for any signs of recurring waxy nodules or changes in body odor, allowing for early intervention before the infection becomes extensive.
\end{itemize}
