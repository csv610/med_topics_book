\chapter{Contraception}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Contraception refers to the deliberate use of temporary or permanent methods, devices, medications, or surgical procedures to prevent pregnancy. It is a critical component of reproductive autonomy, family planning, and preventative healthcare, allowing individuals to decide if, when, and how many children they wish to have. Contraceptive options are categorised based on their duration of action and user dependency: short-acting reversible contraception (SARC, such as oral pills, patches, and vaginal rings), long-acting reversible contraception (LARC, such as intrauterine devices and subdermal implants), barrier methods (such as condoms and diaphragms), and permanent sterilisation (such as vasectomy and tubal ligation). Effective contraceptive counseling utilizes a shared decision-making framework, balancing clinical efficacy, safety profiles, non-contraceptive benefits, and individual patient preferences.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Globally, contraceptive use is widespread, with over 60\% of reproductive-aged women (15 to 49 years) employing some form of birth control. In Australia, contraception is highly accessed, with approximately 70\% of women of reproductive age utilizing a contraceptive method. Historically, the combined oral contraceptive pill and male condoms have been the most widely used methods. However, in recent years, clinical guidelines have strongly promoted the uptake of long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs) due to their superior "real-world" efficacy and cost-effectiveness. The widespread availability of contraception has been epidemiologically linked to a significant decline in unintended pregnancies, lower rates of abortion, and reduced maternal and infant mortality rates.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Contraceptive methods function by interrupting key stages of the human reproductive cycle, specifically ovulation, fertilisation, or blastocyst implantation.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inhibition of ovulation:} Combined hormonal contraceptives (containing both oestrogen and progestogen) suppress the release of gonadotropins (FSH and LH) from the anterior pituitary, preventing follicular development and the mid-cycle luteinising hormone surge.
    \item \textbf{Thickening of cervical mucus:} Progestogen-only methods (such as the mini-pill, subdermal implant, and hormonal IUD) increase the viscosity and density of cervical mucus, creating a physical barrier that prevents sperm penetration.
    \item \textbf{Endometrial suppression:} Chronic exposure to exogenous progestogens leads to endometrial decidualisation and subsequent atrophy, making the uterine lining thin and unreceptive to blastocyst implantation.
    \item \textbf{Spermicidal uterine microenvironment:} Intrauterine devices (IUDs) trigger a localized sterile inflammatory response within the endometrium; copper-releasing IUDs release copper ions that are directly cytotoxic to sperm and ova, preventing fertilisation.
    \item \textbf{Mechanical barrier transmission block:} Condoms and diaphragms physically prevent semen from entering the female genital tract, with male and female latex condoms serving as the only methods that also reduce the transmission of sexually transmitted infections (STIs).
    \item \textbf{Surgical gamete occlusion:} Permanent sterilisation procedures physically sever, ligate, or clip the fallopian tubes (tubal ligation) or vas deferens (vasectomy), preventing the meeting of sperm and egg.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical assessment of contraception focuses on selecting a method that matches the patient's lifestyle, reproductive goals, and physical tolerance.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Efficacy differences (Perfect vs. Typical use):} Understanding the discrepancy in efficacy; user-dependent methods (e.g., pills, condoms) have higher typical-use failure rates compared to user-independent LARCs which maintain >99\% efficacy.
    \item \textbf{Bleeding pattern alterations:} Anticipating cycle changes, ranging from amenorrhoea or light spotting (common with hormonal IUDs and implants) to increased menstrual volume and dysmenorrhoea (associated with copper IUDs).
    \item \textbf{Non-contraceptive health benefits:} Utilizing secondary benefits of specific contraceptives, such as the reduction of menorrhagia, improvement of dysmenorrhoea and pelvic pain (endometriosis), and management of acne or hirsutism.
    \item \textbf{Hormonal side effects:} Potential transient symptoms during the initial months of use, including breast tenderness, mild nausea, headaches, bloating, and mood fluctuations.
    \item \textbf{Procedural requirements:} Considering the necessity of clinical procedures for device insertion, monitoring, and removal (applicable to implants and intrauterine devices).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
In contraception, the "differential diagnosis" involves evaluating safety profiles and contraindications using the Medical Eligibility Criteria (MEC) to select the safest and most appropriate options for patients with pre-existing medical conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{MEC Category 4 (Unacceptable health risk):} Absolute contraindications, such as combined hormonal contraceptives in women with a history of venous thromboembolism, ischemic heart disease, severe hypertension, liver tumors, breast cancer, or migraine with aura.
    \item \textbf{MEC Category 3 (Risks outweigh benefits):} Relative contraindications requiring careful clinical judgement, such as copper IUD use in patients with active pelvic inflammatory disease or unexplained vaginal bleeding prior to diagnosis.
    \item \textbf{MEC Category 2 (Benefits outweigh risks):} Conditions where the method is generally acceptable, such as the use of progestogen-only implants in patients with well-controlled hypertension.
    \item \textbf{MEC Category 1 (No restrictions):} Situations where any contraceptive method can be safely used without restrictions, typical for healthy, non-smoking individuals of reproductive age.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should consult their healthcare provider if they experience persistent side effects, suspect device displacement (such as not feeling IUD strings), miss multiple contraceptive doses, or suspect they may be pregnant.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe, or localized lower abdominal or pelvic pain, particularly when associated with irregular vaginal bleeding and a positive pregnancy test (suspected ectopic pregnancy).
    \item Severe, unilateral calf pain, swelling, and redness (suggestive of deep vein thrombosis).
    \item Sudden shortness of breath, sharp chest pain, or coughing up blood (suggestive of pulmonary embolism).
    \item A sudden, severe headache, speech difficulties, visual disturbances, or unilateral weakness (suggestive of stroke).
    \item High fever, chills, and severe pelvic pain developing shortly after an intrauterine device insertion.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Prior to prescribing or inserting a contraceptive, a clinical assessment must be performed to screen for contraindications and confirm pregnancy status.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure screening:} Mandatory assessment prior to initiating any combined hormonal method (COCP, patch, or ring) due to the risk of hypertension-induced cardiovascular events.
    \item \textbf{Urine beta-hCG test:} Performed to exclude pregnancy if the patient's menstrual history is irregular or if unprotected intercourse has occurred within the current cycle.
    \item \textbf{Body mass index (BMI) measurement:} Calculated to assess eligibility, as obesity can reduce the efficacy of emergency contraception (e.g., levonorgestrel) and increase cardiovascular risks for combined hormonal methods.
    \item \textbf{Sexually transmitted infection (STI) screening:} PCR swabs for chlamydia and gonorrhoea, highly recommended prior to IUD insertion to minimise the risk of ascending pelvic infection.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic assessment and ultrasound:} Physical examination to determine uterine position and size prior to IUD insertion; pelvic ultrasound is indicated if there is suspicion of uterine abnormalities or IUD malposition.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Contraceptive management involves the prescribing of pharmacological agents, the clinical insertion of devices, and providing education on correct use and emergency procedures.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prescribing oral contraceptives:} Selecting either a combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP) or a progestogen-only pill (POP), including detailed patient education on pill-taking schedules and missed-pill protocols.
    \item \textbf{Subdermal implant insertion:} Clinical insertion of a progestogen-releasing rod (e.g., etonogestrel implant) under local anaesthetic into the subdermal tissue of the non-dominant upper arm.
    \item \textbf{Intrauterine device (IUD) insertion:} Sterile placement of either a levonorgestrel-releasing IUD or a copper-bearing IUD into the uterine cavity during an in-clinic procedure.
    \item \textbf{Emergency contraception provision:} Recommending and dispensing oral emergency contraceptive pills (ulipristal acetate or levonorgestrel) or arranging for emergency copper IUD insertion within 120 hours of unprotected intercourse.
    \item \textbf{Sterilisation referral:} Facilitating referral to a gynaecologist for laparoscopic tubal occlusion or to a urologist for vasectomy, accompanied by comprehensive counseling on the permanent nature of the procedure.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While modern contraceptives are highly safe, several rare but significant complications can arise from their use.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Venous thromboembolism (VTE):} A rare risk of deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism, primary associated with the oestrogen component in combined hormonal contraceptives.
    \item \textbf{IUD expulsion or perforation:} Partial or complete expulsion of an IUD from the uterus (occurring in 2\% to 8\% of cases), or the rare risk of uterine wall perforation during insertion (approximately 1 in 1000 insertions).
    \item \textbf{Ectopic pregnancy:} Although contraception reduces the absolute risk of all pregnancies, if a pregnancy does occur while using a progesterone-only method or an IUD, there is a higher relative probability of it being ectopic.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID):} A small risk of ascending pelvic infection within the first 20 days following the insertion of an IUD, usually related to pre-existing undiagnosed cervical infection.
    \item \textbf{Reduction in bone mineral density:} Associated with the long-term use of depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA) injections, which generally reverses after discontinuation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for successfully preventing pregnancy is extremely high when long-acting reversible methods (LARCs) are utilized, with failure rates below 0.2\% under typical-use conditions. User-dependent methods have a wider variation in outcomes, with typical-use failure rates rising to 9\% for oral pills and 18\% for male condoms due to compliance issues. Upon the discontinuation of temporary contraceptives, fertility returns rapidly, usually within a few weeks. The exception is depot injections, where a return to normal ovulatory cycles can take up to 12 months. Long-term satisfaction remains high when patients are actively involved in contraceptive selection and receive prompt management for minor side effects.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Self-care strategies are vital to maintaining contraceptive efficacy, managing minor side effects, and protecting overall reproductive health.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adhering to daily schedules:} Taking oral contraceptive pills at the exact same time every day, particularly for the progestogen-only pill, which has a narrow administration window.
    \item \textbf{Regular IUD string checks:} Checking for the presence and length of the IUD threads after each menstrual period to verify that the device has not slipped.
    \item \textbf{Dual protection practices:} Combining hormonal methods with male or female condoms to ensure simultaneous protection against both pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections.
    \item \textbf{Managing drug interactions:} Seeking pharmacist advice before starting new medications, as hepatic enzyme-inducing drugs (e.g., carbamazepine, rifampicin, and St. John's Wort) can significantly reduce hormonal contraceptive efficacy.
    \item \textbf{Maintaining an emergency plan:} Knowing how to access emergency contraception and advice in the event of a broken condom, missed pills, or late contraceptive injections.
\end{itemize}
