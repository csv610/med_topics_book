\chapter{The Immune System}
\index{The Immune System}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The immune system is the body's complex network of cells, tissues, and organs that defends against infection and disease. It recognises and attacks invading organisms such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites, and also detects and removes abnormal cells, including cancer cells. The system is often described as having two linked arms: the innate immune system, which provides rapid, non-specific first-line defence, and the adaptive immune system, which develops targeted, long-lasting responses with immunological memory. Understanding how the immune system works underpins the practice of vaccination, allergy care, transplantation, and the treatment of immune-related and autoimmune diseases.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The immune system is not a disease but a fundamental system of the body, and its importance is reflected in the health consequences when it fails. Worldwide, infectious diseases remain among the leading causes of death, especially in children and in settings with limited access to vaccines and healthcare. In many countries, immunisation has dramatically reduced the burden of diseases such as measles, polio, and whooping cough, while immune-related conditions are common: allergies affect around one in five people, autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis and type 1 diabetes affect hundreds of thousands, and primary immunodeficiencies, though rare, affect roughly 1 in 1,000 people. Immunodeficiency from HIV infection and from immunosuppressive treatment also shapes patterns of illness.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The innate immune system is the first line of defence, comprising physical barriers such as the skin and mucous membranes, chemical defences such as stomach acid and antimicrobial proteins, and cells such as neutrophils, macrophages, and natural killer cells that respond quickly and non-specifically. The adaptive immune system involves lymphocytes: B cells produce antibodies that neutralise pathogens, while T cells coordinate responses and kill infected cells. A small number of lymphocytes that recognise a particular pathogen are selected and expand massively on first exposure, and a proportion persist as memory cells, providing rapid protection on re-exposure. This is the principle of vaccination. When regulation fails, the immune system can attack the body's own tissues (autoimmunity), overreact to harmless substances (allergy), or underperform (immunodeficiency).

\section*{Clinical Features}
The immune system works silently in health, but signs of its activity and problems with it are recognisable.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever, swollen lymph nodes, and inflammation are normal immune responses to infection
    \item Frequent, severe, or unusual infections suggest possible immunodeficiency
    \item Autoimmune disease produces organ-specific symptoms, such as joint swelling in rheumatoid arthritis or fatigue and rashes in lupus
    \item Allergic reactions range from hay fever and eczema to life-threatening anaphylaxis
    \item Vaccination produces immunity without causing the disease, sometimes with mild transient symptoms
    \item Immunosuppressive treatment increases susceptibility to infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Problems attributed to "low immunity" have a wide range of possible causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary immunodeficiency:} inherited disorders of immune cells or antibodies, presenting in childhood or adulthood
    \item \textbf{Secondary immunodeficiency:} from HIV infection, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or immunosuppressive medicines
    \item \textbf{Recurrent infections from anatomical or functional causes:} sinusitis, urinary reflux, or foreign bodies
    \item \textbf{Chronic conditions:} diabetes, malnutrition, and chronic kidney disease impair immune function
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune disease:} overactivity of the immune system rather than deficiency
    \item \textbf{Allergy:} inappropriate immune responses to harmless triggers
    \item \textbf{Cancer:} including leukaemia and lymphoma, which can present with recurrent infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with a family history of primary immunodeficiency, or who experience unusually frequent, severe, or persistent infections, recurrent pneumonia, unusual organisms, or failure to thrive, should be assessed by a doctor. Unexplained swollen glands, fevers, weight loss, or rashes with joint symptoms also warrant review. Anyone concerned about their immune health, including after treatment that suppresses immunity, should discuss this with their GP.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever with confusion, difficulty breathing, or a rapidly spreading rash, which may indicate sepsis
    \item Difficulty breathing, wheezing, or facial swelling, which may indicate anaphylaxis
    \item Seizures, stiff neck, or severe headache with fever (possible meningitis)
    \item A person who is very unwell or deteriorating quickly
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} a detailed history of infections, allergies, autoimmune symptoms, and family history
    \item \textbf{Full blood count:} to assess white cell numbers and subtypes
    \item \textbf{Immunoglobulin levels:} measurement of antibody classes to detect antibody deficiency
    \item \textbf{Lymphocyte and T-cell studies:} flow cytometry to quantify immune cell populations
    \item \textbf{Functional antibody tests:} response to vaccines or infections
    \item \textbf{Complement and neutrophil function tests:} for specific suspected disorders
    \item \textbf{Allergy testing:} skin prick tests or specific IgE blood tests where allergy is suspected
    \item \textbf{Autoantibody tests:} where autoimmune disease is a possibility
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} for confirmed primary immunodeficiencies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vaccination:} the most important way to strengthen and train the immune system
    \item \textbf{Healthy lifestyle:} adequate sleep, nutrition, physical activity, and stress management support immune function
    \item \textbf{Immunoglobulin replacement:} regular infusions for people with antibody deficiency
    \item \textbf{Antimicrobial prophylaxis:} preventive antibiotics or antivirals for susceptible individuals
    \item \textbf{Treatment of underlying causes:} antiretroviral therapy for HIV and treatment of other contributing conditions
    \item \textbf{Stem cell transplantation:} curative treatment for some severe primary immunodeficiencies
    \item \textbf{Immunosuppressive therapy:} for autoimmune disease, balanced against infection risk
    \item \textbf{Allergy management:} avoidance, antihistamines, and immunotherapy where appropriate
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe and recurrent infections, including life-threatening sepsis and pneumonia
    \item Opportunistic infections with organisms that rarely cause disease in healthy people
    \item Organ damage from chronic or repeated infections
    \item Autoimmune complications in people with immune dysregulation
    \item Failure to thrive in children with severe immunodeficiency
    \item Increased risk of certain cancers in immunodeficient states
    \item Complications of treatments, including infection during immunosuppression
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The immune system generally maintains effective protection throughout life, and immune competence is preserved with healthy habits and vaccination. When the immune system fails, outcomes depend on the cause and how early it is recognised: children with severe primary immunodeficiency benefit greatly from early diagnosis and treatment, and antibody deficiency is well controlled with replacement therapy. Autoimmune and allergic conditions are managed long term. The prognosis for infections themselves has been transformed by vaccines, antibiotics, and modern care, and understanding of the immune system continues to produce new treatments for cancer and immune disease.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Stay up to date with vaccinations, including influenza, COVID-19, and childhood immunisations
    \item Eat a varied diet, exercise regularly, sleep well, and manage stress
    \item Practise good hand hygiene and food safety
    \item Avoid smoking, limit alcohol, and avoid unnecessary use of antibiotics
    \item Protect against sexually transmissible infections, including HIV
    \item Seek early medical review for infections that are slow to settle or keep returning
    \item Discuss any planned immunosuppressive treatment with a doctor, including its effects on vaccination
\end{itemize}
