\chapter{Head and Neck Cancer}
\index{Head and Neck Cancer}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Head and neck cancer is a group of cancers that arise in the mouth, throat, voice box (larynx), sinuses, salivary glands, and other structures of the head and neck. The most common type is squamous cell carcinoma, and the main risk factors are tobacco and alcohol use, together with infection with the human papillomavirus (HPV), particularly for cancers of the tonsils and back of the tongue. Symptoms include a persistent sore throat, a lump in the neck, difficulty swallowing, hoarseness, and mouth sores that do not heal. Treatment often combines surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, and outcomes are good when the cancer is detected early. Because the disease affects functions such as speaking, eating, and breathing, care involves a multidisciplinary team.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Head and neck cancers account for around 3--4% of all cancers in many countries, with more than 5,000 new cases diagnosed each year. They are about twice as common in men as in women and are most often diagnosed after age 50, though HPV-related cancers are increasing in younger people. Around two-thirds of cases are related to tobacco and alcohol, and around a third are related to HPV infection. Oral cancer is among the most common head and neck cancers in many countries, and survival ranges from over 90% for early HPV-related throat cancer to much lower for advanced disease.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Head and neck cancers arise from the cells lining the upper aerodigestive tract.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tobacco:} smoking and chewing tobacco are the leading risk factors
    \item \textbf{Alcohol:} alcohol, especially combined with tobacco, greatly increases risk
    \item \textbf{HPV infection:} high-risk HPV types, particularly HPV 16, cause an increasing proportion of oropharyngeal cancers, which have a better prognosis
    \item \textbf{Other risk factors:} betel nut chewing, Epstein-Barr virus (nasopharyngeal cancer), poor oral hygiene, and occupational exposures
    \item \textbf{Pathology:} most are squamous cell carcinomas arising from the mucosal lining
    \item \textbf{Spread:} cancers spread locally and to lymph nodes in the neck, and later to distant organs
    \item \textbf{Field change:} tobacco and alcohol cause changes across the whole lining, increasing the risk of multiple cancers
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on the location of the cancer and are often persistent.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A sore throat or mouth ulcer that does not heal within three weeks
    \item A lump in the neck
    \item Difficulty or pain on swallowing
    \item Hoarseness or voice change lasting more than three weeks
    \item Ear pain on one side
    \item A feeling of something stuck in the throat
    \item Nosebleeds, nasal blockage, or blood in the sputum
    \item Unexplained weight loss
    \item Loose teeth or ill-fitting dentures
    \item White or red patches in the mouth
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Many benign conditions cause similar symptoms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Tonsillitis, pharyngitis, and other infections
    \item Enlarged lymph nodes from infection
    \item Vocal cord nodules and polyps
    \item Benign tumours of the mouth, throat, and salivary glands
    \item Thyroid enlargement and nodules
    \item Gastro-oesophageal reflux causing throat symptoms
    \item Dental and gum disease
    \item Temporomandibular joint problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for any symptom lasting more than three weeks, including a sore throat, hoarseness, mouth ulcer, difficulty swallowing, or a lump in the neck. Early detection dramatically improves outcomes.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing or noisy breathing
    \item Sudden difficulty swallowing or choking
    \item Coughing up blood in significant amounts
    \item A rapidly enlarging neck mass causing airway problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is confirmed by examination and biopsy, with imaging for staging.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} including a full examination of the mouth, throat, and neck
    \item \textbf{Nasendoscopy:} a flexible camera examination of the nose, throat, and voice box
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} a tissue sample from the lesion confirms the diagnosis and HPV status
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT, MRI, and PET scans assess the extent and spread of the cancer
    \item \textbf{Panendoscopy:} examination under anaesthetic to assess the whole aerodigestive tract
    \item \textbf{Staging:} the cancer is staged to guide treatment
    \item \textbf{HPV testing:} determines prognosis and treatment
    \item \textbf{Multidisciplinary review:} the case is discussed by surgeons, oncologists, and allied health
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is individualised and often combines modalities.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} removal of the tumour, with reconstruction of the affected area where needed
    \item \textbf{Neck dissection:} removal of lymph nodes containing cancer
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} targeted radiation, often with chemotherapy (chemoradiation), for many head and neck cancers
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy:} used with radiation or for advanced disease
    \item \textbf{Targeted and immunotherapy:} newer medicines for some head and neck cancers
    \item \textbf{Speech and swallowing therapy:} rehabilitation of speech and eating
    \item \textbf{Nutritional support:} feeding tubes are sometimes needed during treatment
    \item \textbf{Dental care:} dental assessment and treatment before radiation
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular surveillance for recurrence and second cancers
    \item \textbf{Palliative care:} for advanced disease, focused on quality of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty speaking, swallowing, and breathing after treatment
    \item Side effects of radiotherapy: dry mouth, dental problems, and tissue damage
    \item Side effects of chemotherapy
    \item Changes in appearance after surgery
    \item Malnutrition and weight loss
    \item Recurrence and second cancers, especially with continued smoking and drinking
    \item Psychological distress and effects on social life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis depends on the site, stage, and HPV status. Early-stage cancers are cured in the majority of cases, and HPV-related oropharyngeal cancers have excellent outcomes, with survival above 90% in early disease. Advanced cancers have lower survival but are often still treated with curative intent. Rehabilitation helps people adapt to changes in speech, swallowing, and appearance. Stopping smoking and alcohol improves outcomes and reduces the risk of second cancers. Many people with head and neck cancer are cured and return to good quality of life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Do not smoke, and quit if you do
    \item Limit alcohol, and avoid combining tobacco and alcohol
    \item Have the HPV vaccine, which protects against HPV-related cancers
    \item Practise good oral hygiene and see a dentist regularly
    \item Protect your lips from the sun
    \item Know the warning signs and seek review of any symptom lasting more than three weeks
    \item Attend regular follow-up after treatment
    \item Seek support for smoking cessation and for the psychological effects of the disease
\end{itemize}
