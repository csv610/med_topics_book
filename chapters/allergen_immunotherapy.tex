\chapter{Allergen Immunotherapy}
\index{Allergen Immunotherapy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Allergen immunotherapy (AIT), often called allergy shots or desensitisation, is a treatment that gradually reduces a person's sensitivity to specific allergens such as grass pollen, dust mites, or insect venom. Unlike medicines that merely suppress symptoms, AIT aims to retrain the immune system so that exposure to the allergen triggers less reaction over time.

It is given as a course of injections (subcutaneous immunotherapy, or SCIT) or, for some allergens, as drops or tablets placed under the tongue (sublingual immunotherapy, or SLIT). Treatment typically runs for several years and is supervised by specialists in allergy and clinical immunology.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Allergic conditions such as hay fever, allergic asthma, and venom allergy affect a large share of the population in many countries, and allergic symptoms can appear at any age. Allergen immunotherapy is most often considered for people whose allergies are severe, persist through the season or throughout the year, or respond poorly to usual medicines.

It is most commonly offered for pollen, dust mite, and insect venom allergies. Food allergen immunotherapy is still largely restricted to specialist settings and clinical research, and the treatment is generally reserved for people in whom avoidance and medication have been insufficient.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
In an allergic person, the immune system mistakenly treats a harmless substance as a threat and produces antibodies of the IgE class against it. On re-exposure, these antibodies trigger the release of inflammatory chemicals such as histamine from mast cells, causing symptoms.

AIT works by repeatedly exposing the immune system to slowly increasing amounts of the allergen.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immunological shift:} the body produces fewer allergy-promoting responses and more protective ones
    \item \textbf{Blocking antibodies:} levels of IgG antibodies rise and compete with IgE for the allergen
    \item \textbf{Tolerance:} immune cells become progressively less reactive to the allergen
    \item \textbf{Duration:} treatment typically runs for 3 to 5 years to produce lasting benefit
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Before treatment, the person has the typical symptoms of their allergy.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pollen or dust mite allergy:} sneezing, runny or blocked nose, itchy or watery eyes
    \item \textbf{Allergy-related asthma:} wheeze, cough, and breathlessness triggered by the allergen
    \item \textbf{Venom allergy:} local swelling, hives, or severe reactions to insect stings
    \item \textbf{During AIT:} some people notice minor local reactions such as redness, itching, or swelling where the treatment is given
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Allergen immunotherapy is a treatment rather than a diagnosis, so the relevant comparisons are alternative management approaches and conditions that mimic allergy.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Allergic rhinitis caused by an allergen other than the one being treated
    \item Non-allergic (vasomotor) rhinitis, which does not respond to AIT
    \item Drug allergy, food allergy, or other allergic triggers requiring separate treatment
    \item Chronic sinusitis, nasal polyps, or anatomical blockage causing symptoms
    \item Symptom control with antihistamines, nasal sprays, or reliever inhalers alone
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Discuss allergen immunotherapy with a doctor -- usually an allergist or clinical immunologist -- in the following circumstances.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Symptoms are severe and interfere with sleep, work, school, or daily life
    \item Regular medicines do not control the symptoms
    \item A venom sting has caused a severe reaction
    \item Allergic asthma is present and not well controlled
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, wheezing, or throat tightness after a sting, a treatment dose, or allergen exposure
    \item Swelling of the tongue or face, or difficulty swallowing
    \item Collapse, dizziness, or loss of consciousness after allergen exposure
    \item Any signs of anaphylaxis, which is a medical emergency
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment for AIT confirms both the diagnosis and the precise trigger, because treatment is specific to a particular allergen.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the timing of symptoms, exposure patterns, and severity
    \item \textbf{Skin prick testing:} small drops of allergen placed on the skin to check for a wheal response
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} measurement of allergen-specific IgE antibodies
    \item \textbf{Lung function tests:} spirometry if asthma is present
    \item \textbf{Review of suitability:} discussion of risks, benefits, and the commitment required
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
AIT is a specialist treatment given alongside good general allergy care.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Injections (SCIT):} regular doses given in a clinic under observation, starting with weekly visits and moving to less frequent maintenance doses
    \item \textbf{Under-the-tongue therapy (SLIT):} daily or intermittent tablets or drops taken at home
    \item \textbf{Continuing usual medicines:} antihistamines and nasal sprays may still be needed, especially early in treatment
    \item \textbf{Allergen avoidance:} reducing exposure to the allergen where practical
    \item \textbf{Emergency plan:} knowing what to do in a reaction, and carrying an adrenaline autoinjector if prescribed
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular specialist review to monitor response and side effects
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Complications of AIT are uncommon but can occur.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Local reactions such as swelling, redness, or itching at the injection site
    \item Worsening of hay fever or asthma symptoms around the time of a dose
    \item Rarely, a serious allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) during or shortly after treatment
    \item Risk of reactions if the schedule is interrupted or a dose is missed
    \item The cost and time commitment required over several years
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
When completed successfully, AIT reduces allergy symptoms and the need for other medicines for many years, and some people remain improved for a long time after stopping. The treatment is not a cure, and results vary between people and between allergens.

Benefits usually begin within months and build gradually. Severe reactions are rare when treatment is given by trained staff, and most people tolerate the course well. Outcomes are better when the diagnosis and trigger are clear and when treatment is continued as planned.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Before and during immunotherapy, sensible self-care supports the treatment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Avoid allergens:} reduce exposure where practical, such as keeping windows closed during pollen season
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} use prescribed medicines reliably so symptoms stay under control
    \item \textbf{Attendance:} attend all scheduled doses and report any missed doses
    \item \textbf{Emergency kit:} carry an adrenaline autoinjector if prescribed and know how to use it
    \item \textbf{Identification:} wear a medical alert bracelet if at risk of severe reactions
    \item \textbf{Action plan:} keep an up-to-date action plan agreed with your specialist
\end{itemize}
