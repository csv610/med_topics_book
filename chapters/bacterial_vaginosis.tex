\chapter{Bacterial Vaginosis}
\index{Bacterial Vaginosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bacterial vaginosis (BV) is a very common condition in which the normal balance of bacteria in the vagina is disturbed. In a healthy vagina, \textit{Lactobacillus} bacteria dominate and keep the environment mildly acidic. In bacterial vaginosis these helpful bacteria become less numerous and other, mostly anaerobic bacteria multiply, causing a change in the vaginal discharge.

BV is not a sexually transmitted infection, though sexual activity influences it. It often causes no symptoms at all, and when symptoms do occur they usually involve a thin, grey-white discharge with a fishy odour. BV matters because it can increase the risk of other infections and, in pregnancy, is linked with a higher chance of premature birth. It is treatable, but it commonly comes back.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bacterial vaginosis is the most common cause of abnormal vaginal discharge in women of reproductive age, affecting roughly one in three women at some point. It is most common between puberty and the menopause. It is more frequent in sexually active women, in women with a new or multiple sexual partners, in women who use an intrauterine device (IUD), and in women who smoke or who douche (clean the vagina with water or products). Many women with BV have no symptoms, so the true number of cases is higher than the number who seek care.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
BV develops when something upsets the vaginal environment and allows the normal \textit{Lactobacillus}-dominant bacteria to be outnumbered by a mixture of other bacteria such as \textit{Gardnerella vaginalis} and various anaerobes. Contributing factors include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Douching or washing inside the vagina, which removes protective bacteria and raises the pH
    \item Perfumed soaps, bubble baths, and intimate products that irritate the vagina
    \item A new sexual partner or multiple partners, which can change the mix of bacteria
    \item Hormonal changes, such as pregnancy or the menopause
    \item An intrauterine device (IUD)
    \item Smoking and the use of some antibiotics, which can alter the vaginal flora
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Up to half of women with bacterial vaginosis have no symptoms. When symptoms occur, they typically include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A thin, white or grey vaginal discharge, often more than usual
    \item A strong fishy odour, especially after sex or during a period
    \item Mild irritation or itching around the vagina, though often no pain or soreness
    \item Occasionally a mild burning with urination
    \item No fever, redness, or swelling, which helps distinguish it from other conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Thrush (candidiasis):} thick, white, curd-like discharge with intense itching; no fishy odour
    \item \textbf{Trichomoniasis:} a sexually transmitted infection with a frothy, yellow-green discharge and fishy odour
    \item \textbf{Chlamydia or gonorrhoea:} cervicitis with discharge, pelvic pain, or bleeding between periods
    \item \textbf{Physiological discharge:} normal clear or milky discharge that varies with the cycle
    \item \textbf{Contact dermatitis or allergy:} reaction to soaps, perfumes, or condoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if you have a new or changed vaginal discharge, a persistent fishy odour, burning, itching, or pelvic pain. Because symptoms of BV, thrush, and sexually transmitted infections overlap, a proper assessment is needed rather than treating on guesswork.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever, chills, or severe pelvic pain
    \item The discharge is accompanied by bleeding between periods or after sex
    \item You are pregnant and notice a new discharge or odour
    \item Symptoms are severe, or do not settle after treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} nature of the discharge, odour, sexual history, contraception, and pregnancy status
    \item \textbf{Examination:} speculum examination of the vagina and cervix to inspect the discharge
    \item \textbf{Vaginal pH:} BV raises the pH above 4.5
    \item \textbf{Microscopy:} a wet-mount swab looked at under a microscope; clue cells (vaginal cells studded with bacteria) support the diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Whiff test:} adding a drop of potassium hydroxide to the discharge releases a fishy odour in BV
    \item \textbf{Testing for other infections:} swabs for thrush and sexually transmitted infections may be taken at the same time
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} a short course of oral or vaginal antibiotic treatment (usually metronidazole or clindamycin) cures most cases
    \item \textbf{Treating symptoms only:} women without symptoms do not always need treatment, but it is recommended in pregnancy and before certain procedures
    \item \textbf{No need to treat partners:} BV is not transmitted to sexual partners, so partner treatment is not required
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} BV is usually treated in pregnancy because it is associated with preterm birth
    \item \textbf{Recurrence:} if BV returns, longer or repeated courses may be offered; some women benefit from a maintenance course
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} avoid douching and scented intimate products while treatment is underway
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Increased risk of acquiring sexually transmitted infections, including HIV
    \item Increased risk of pelvic inflammatory disease after procedures such as IUD insertion or surgery
    \item In pregnancy: an association with preterm birth and low birth weight
    \item Infection after gynaecological surgery, such as hysterectomy
    \item Recurrent BV, which can be distressing and affect quality of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most cases of bacterial vaginosis clear up within days of starting antibiotics, and symptoms such as discharge and odour resolve quickly. However, recurrence is common: about half of women have another episode within a year, and some women get repeated infections that need more than one course of treatment. BV itself is not dangerous in healthy non-pregnant women, but because of the links with other infections, follow-up and safe-sex practices are sensible. In pregnancy, treatment reduces the risk of complications.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Do not douche or wash inside the vagina
    \item Use plain water and mild, unscented soap on the outside of the genitals only
    \item Avoid perfumed soaps, bubble baths, and intimate sprays
    \item Use condoms, especially with a new partner, and limit the number of partners
    \item Do not smoke
    \item Wear cotton underwear and avoid tight, non-breathable clothing
    \item If you get repeated BV, discuss prevention strategies such as maintenance treatment with your doctor
    \item Attend promptly if you develop a discharge, especially in pregnancy
\end{itemize}
