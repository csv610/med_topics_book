\chapter{Infective Endocarditis (Infectious Endocarditis)}
\index{Infective Endocarditis (Infectious Endocarditis)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Infective endocarditis, also called infectious endocarditis, is an infection of the endocardium, the inner lining of the heart, usually involving the heart valves. Bacteria, and occasionally fungi, enter the bloodstream and colonise a valve, forming vegetations that destroy tissue and can embolise to other organs. The illness can present acutely with high fever and rapid deterioration, or subacutely with weeks of fatigue, low-grade fever, and weight loss. Because of its high mortality, endocarditis demands urgent recognition, prolonged intravenous antibiotics, and surgery in a significant proportion of cases. Prevention centres on dental hygiene, prompt treatment of infections, and protecting high-risk patients.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The incidence of infective endocarditis is around three to ten cases per 100,000 people per year and has been rising, driven by ageing populations, prosthetic valves, implantable cardiac devices, and healthcare-associated bloodstream infections. Men are affected about twice as often as women, and incidence rises steeply after age 60. Underlying risk factors are present in most cases: prosthetic valves, previous endocarditis, congenital heart disease, rheumatic heart disease, and degenerative valve disease, with intravenous drug use and long-term intravenous lines important in younger and hospitalised patients. Staphylococcus aureus has overtaken oral streptococci as the most common cause in many regions.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Infective endocarditis begins when organisms in the bloodstream settle on an abnormal valve surface. The mouth, skin, and gut are the main portals: dental procedures and poor oral hygiene release oral streptococci; skin organisms enter through wounds, catheters, and injection sites; gut organisms follow abdominal infection or procedures. On a damaged or prosthetic valve, bacteria adhere to deposits of platelets and fibrin, multiply, and form vegetations. These vegetations destroy valve tissue, causing regurgitation and abscesses, and fragments embolise to the brain, kidneys, spleen, and limbs. Some organisms, such as Staphylococcus aureus, are particularly virulent and produce rapid destruction and abscess formation.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever and chills, sometimes low-grade and intermittent in subacute disease
    \item Night sweats, fatigue, malaise, and weight loss over weeks
    \item A new or changed heart murmur, present in most but not all cases
    \item Skin and nail signs: petechiae, splinter haemorrhages, and painful nodules
    \item Embolic events: stroke, sudden limb pain, flank pain from kidney infarction, or abdominal pain
    \item Back pain and joint pain, especially with some organisms
    \item Heart failure with breathlessness, swelling, and reduced exercise tolerance
    \item Acute presentation with high fever and sepsis in staphylococcal disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the early features are non-specific, many conditions mimic endocarditis.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Common viral infections:} influenza and other febrile illnesses
    \item \textbf{Pneumonia and urinary infection:} bacterial infections with fever
    \item \textbf{Malaria, dengue, and other tropical infections:} in returned travellers
    \item \textbf{Connective tissue disease:} systemic lupus and vasculitis can mimic the signs
    \item \textbf{Atrial myxoma:} a tumour with embolic and systemic features
    \item \textbf{Bacteraemia and sepsis:} bloodstream infection without valve involvement
    \item \textbf{Rheumatic fever:} in younger people and endemic regions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Persistent or unexplained fever, especially with a heart murmur, prosthetic valve, previous endocarditis, congenital heart disease, or injecting drug use, requires urgent assessment. Any neurological symptom, sudden pain in a limb, or breathlessness in a febrile person warrants immediate care. People with heart conditions should maintain excellent dental health and seek prompt treatment of infections.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden weakness, numbness, facial droop, or difficulty speaking, which may indicate a stroke
    \item Severe breathlessness, chest pain, or collapse
    \item A sudden pale, cold, or painful limb, which may indicate arterial embolism
    \item High fever with confusion, rapid breathing, or deterioration
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood cultures:} at least two to three sets before antibiotics, to identify the organism and sensitivities
    \item \textbf{Echocardiography:} transthoracic echocardiogram first, then transoesophageal echocardiogram for better visualisation of vegetations and abscesses
    \item \textbf{Full blood count and inflammatory markers:} anaemia and raised markers are typical
    \item \textbf{Urinalysis:} blood and protein indicating renal involvement
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT or MRI of the brain and other organs to detect emboli
    \item \textbf{Duke criteria:} the validated framework combining clinical, microbiological, and imaging findings
    \item \textbf{Cardiac surgical assessment:} early involvement where complications are likely
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intravenous antibiotics:} high-dose, prolonged therapy, typically four to six weeks, guided by cultures and sensitivity
    \item \textbf{Early surgery:} valve repair or replacement for heart failure, uncontrolled infection, abscess, large mobile vegetations, or recurrent embolism
    \item \textbf{Embolism prevention:} early surgery may reduce stroke risk in high-risk patients
    \item \textbf{Complication management:} treatment of stroke, heart failure, renal failure, and metastatic infection
    \item \textbf{Drug dependence treatment:} essential where injecting drug use is the cause
    \item \textbf{Long-term follow-up:} monitoring for valve dysfunction and recurrence
    \item \textbf{Preventive dental care:} treatment of dental disease after recovery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Valvular destruction causing acute or chronic heart failure
    \item Embolic stroke and infarction of the spleen, kidney, or limbs
    \item Perivalvular abscesses and heart block
    \item Septic shock and multiorgan failure
    \item Glomerulonephritis and renal failure
    \item Metastatic abscesses in bone, joints, and muscle
    \item Recurrence and reinfection
    \item Death in around 15 to 25 per cent of cases, higher with staphylococcal disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Despite modern care, infective endocarditis carries a mortality of around one in five to one in four, with higher rates in staphylococcal infection, prosthetic valve disease, and older patients. Early diagnosis, organism identification, and timely surgery improve outcomes substantially. Many people who survive require valve surgery and lifelong follow-up. Recurrence is uncommon when the source of bacteraemia is controlled and drug use ceases. Prevention through dental care and appropriate antibiotic prophylaxis for high-risk patients remains the most effective strategy.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain rigorous oral hygiene and regular dental visits
    \item Treat dental and gum disease promptly
    \item Seek early medical care for persistent or unexplained fever
    \item Follow prescribed antibiotic prophylaxis before dental and some surgical procedures if you have a high-risk condition
    \item Avoid injecting drugs and seek help for dependence
    \item Ensure clean technique for intravenous lines and skin wounds
    \item Carry information about your heart condition and take it to medical and dental visits
    \item Attend follow-up appointments after treatment
\end{itemize}
