\chapter{Vitamin A Deficiency}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Vitamin A deficiency occurs when the body does not have enough vitamin A, a fat-soluble vitamin essential for vision, immune function, growth, and healthy skin. Vitamin A is obtained from animal sources such as liver, eggs, and dairy, and from plant sources containing beta-carotene, which the body converts to vitamin A. Severe deficiency causes night blindness, dry eyes that can progress to blindness, and increased susceptibility to infections. It is rare in Australia but remains a major cause of preventable blindness in developing countries.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Vitamin A deficiency is the leading cause of preventable childhood blindness worldwide, affecting an estimated 250 million preschool children, particularly in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa. In these regions, it is associated with poverty, limited dietary variety, and repeated infections such as measles and diarrhoea. In Australia and other high-income countries, deficiency is uncommon because diets are generally adequate, but it can occur in people with malabsorption disorders, restrictive diets, liver disease, or chronic alcohol use.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inadequate intake:} diets lacking liver, dairy, eggs, and colourful vegetables and fruits containing beta-carotene
    \item \textbf{Malabsorption:} coeliac disease, Crohn's disease, cystic fibrosis, and surgery affecting the gut impair absorption of fat-soluble vitamins
    \item \textbf{Liver disease:} the liver stores vitamin A and produces transport proteins; chronic liver disease reduces availability
    \item \textbf{Alcohol:} chronic heavy drinking depletes vitamin A stores and interferes with its metabolism
    \item \textbf{Infections:} measles, diarrhoea, and parasitic infections worsen deficiency by increasing losses and reducing intake
    \item \textbf{Physiological demand:} rapid growth in children and pregnancy increase requirements
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Night blindness, difficulty seeing in dim light, is the earliest symptom
    \item Dry eyes (xerophthalmia), including Bitot's spots and corneal dryness
    \item Progressive corneal damage and scarring leading to blindness if untreated
    \item Dry, scaly skin and follicular hyperkeratosis
    \item Increased susceptibility to infections, particularly measles, respiratory infections, and diarrhoea
    \item Impaired growth and poor immune response in children
    \item Effects on fertility and pregnancy outcomes in adults
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Other causes of night blindness, including retinitis pigmentosa and inherited retinal disorders
    \item Other causes of dry eyes, such as Sjögren's syndrome and medication effects
    \item Other causes of dry skin, including eczema and ichthyosis
    \item Other causes of growth failure and immunodeficiency in children
    \item Zinc deficiency, which can coexist and causes similar skin and immune changes
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with symptoms such as night blindness, persistently dry eyes, or unexplained dry skin should see a doctor. Those at risk, including people with malabsorption, liver disease, or restrictive diets, should discuss vitamin A status and supplementation with their doctor. Pregnant women should not take high-dose vitamin A supplements without medical advice, because excessive amounts can harm the developing baby.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden loss of vision or severe eye pain with redness
    \item Confusion, severe headache, or vomiting, which can be signs of vitamin A toxicity from excessive supplementation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} serum retinol concentration reflects vitamin A status; levels are low in established deficiency
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} characteristic eye and skin changes support the diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Dark adaptation testing:} measures the ability of the eyes to adjust to darkness
    \item \textbf{Investigation of underlying causes:} coeliac serology, liver function tests, or other tests depending on suspected malabsorption
    \item \textbf{Dietary assessment:} review of food intake to identify inadequate intake
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High-dose vitamin A:} oral supplements are used in severe deficiency and in children with measles in high-risk settings
    \item \textbf{Correction of diet:} foods rich in vitamin A and beta-carotene, including liver, eggs, dairy, carrots, sweet potato, pumpkin, and dark leafy greens
    \item \textbf{Treatment of underlying conditions:} management of coeliac disease, Crohn's disease, or liver disease
    \item \textbf{Fat-soluble vitamin support:} combined supplementation when multiple fat-soluble vitamins are deficient
    \item \textbf{Eye care:} specialist review and treatment to prevent corneal damage
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} repeat blood tests to ensure levels normalise without toxicity
    \item \textbf{Food fortification programmes:} in regions where deficiency is widespread
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Corneal dryness, ulceration, and permanent blindness
    \item Increased severity and mortality from infections, especially measles and diarrhoea in children
    \item Anaemia and impaired iron metabolism
    \item Growth failure and delayed development
    \item Adverse pregnancy outcomes, including increased maternal mortality
    \item Vitamin A toxicity from excessive supplementation, causing liver damage, bone pain, and birth defects
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With early recognition and treatment, vitamin A deficiency is fully reversible. Night blindness and skin changes resolve within weeks of adequate intake, and progression to corneal blindness is prevented. In developing countries, vitamin A supplementation programmes have substantially reduced child mortality and blindness. Once corneal scarring has occurred, blindness is permanent, which is why early detection matters. In Australia, the prognosis is excellent when the underlying cause is identified and treated.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Eat a varied diet with regular sources of vitamin A and beta-carotene
    \item Include liver, eggs, dairy, and orange or dark green vegetables and fruits in weekly meals
    \item Manage conditions that impair absorption with medical and dietitian support
    \item Limit alcohol intake, which depletes vitamin A
    \item Avoid high-dose vitamin A supplements during pregnancy unless prescribed
    \item Seek medical review early for night blindness or persistent dry eyes
\end{itemize}
