\chapter{Fever in Children}
\index{Fever in Children}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Fever in children is a body temperature of 38 degrees Celsius or higher, and it is one of the most common childhood problems parents encounter. In the vast majority of cases it is caused by a viral infection that runs its course in a few days, and the fever itself is a normal immune response that helps the body fight the infection. The most important skill for parents is not measuring the temperature precisely but judging how unwell the child looks. This chapter provides a full guide to fever in children: when it is safe to manage at home, how to treat it, and the warning signs that require urgent medical care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Fever is very common in childhood, and most children have several febrile episodes before they start school. Viral infections account for the large majority, especially in winter and among children attending daycare or school. Serious bacterial infections causing fever, such as pneumonia, urinary tract infection, and meningitis, are uncommon, and the risk is highest in babies under 3 months. Around one-third of children with fever see a doctor, and a smaller number attend emergency departments, most of whom are safely sent home. Febrile seizures affect about 3--5\% of children and are almost always benign.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral infections:} the most common cause, including colds, influenza, gastroenteritis, roseola, and hand, foot and mouth disease
    \item \textbf{Bacterial infections:} ear infections, tonsillitis, chest infections, urinary tract infections, and skin infections
    \item \textbf{Serious bacterial infections:} sepsis, meningitis, and kidney infection, which are rare but must be considered in unwell children
    \item \textbf{Immunisation:} a brief fever after vaccination is a normal immune response
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} immune cells respond to infection by releasing signalling molecules that raise the brain's temperature set point; shivering generates heat until the new level is reached
    \item \textbf{The fever's role:} higher temperature helps immune cells work and inhibits some microbes
    \item \textbf{Febrile seizure mechanism:} the immature brain of a child aged 6 months to 5 years can convulse in response to a rapid temperature rise
    \item \textbf{Infant vulnerability:} babies under 3 months cannot localise infections well and are at higher risk of serious disease, so any fever is taken seriously
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Temperature of 38 degrees or more, confirmed with a thermometer
    \item The child feels hot, looks flushed, and may shiver or have cold hands and feet while the fever rises
    \item Reduced appetite, increased sleepiness, irritability, or clinginess
    \item A well-looking child between fever peaks: alert, interested in play, drinking, and producing wet nappies
    \item Symptoms indicating the source: cough, runny nose, ear pain, sore throat, vomiting, diarrhoea, or pain on urinating
    \item Duration: most viral fevers settle within 3 days
    \item Danger features: floppiness, difficulty waking, fast or laboured breathing, non-blanching rash, neck stiffness, or seizures
    \item In babies under 3 months: fever may be the only sign of serious illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The cause of fever is usually found on assessment.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Viral upper respiratory infections and influenza
    \item Gastroenteritis
    \item Otitis media (middle ear infection)
    \item Pharyngitis and tonsillitis, which may be viral or bacterial
    \item Urinary tract infection, especially in young children
    \item Pneumonia
    \item Meningitis and sepsis, which must be considered in unwell children
    \item Post-immunisation fever
    \item Non-infectious causes, including inflammatory conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents should seek medical care if the child is under 3 months with a temperature of 38 degrees or more; if the fever lasts more than 3 days; if the child is not drinking, is in pain, or is behaving differently; or if they are simply worried.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A baby under 3 months with a fever
    \item A child who is floppy, unresponsive, or very difficult to wake
    \item Difficulty breathing, very fast breathing, or blue lips
    \item A rash that does not fade when pressed with a glass
    \item A seizure lasting more than 5 minutes, or any seizure in a baby under 6 months
    \item No wet nappies for 6--8 hours, sunken eyes, or a dry mouth
    \item Neck stiffness, severe headache, or a high-pitched, unusual cry
    \item A child getting worse despite treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Temperature measurement:} digital thermometer, under the arm or rectally in babies, and in the ear or mouth in older children
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the doctor looks for a source of infection and assesses how unwell the child appears
    \item \textbf{Urine testing:} for young children with fever and no other cause, especially girls
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} in unwell children, those with prolonged fever, and babies under 3 months
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} for suspected pneumonia
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture:} when meningitis is suspected
    \item \textbf{Observation period:} seeing how the child responds to fluids and treatment informs the plan
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Comfort and fluids:} dress lightly, keep the room comfortable, offer fluids regularly, and continue breast or formula feeds in babies
    \item \textbf{Paracetamol or ibuprofen:} for comfort at the weight-based dose, at correct intervals; use one medicine at a time and do not exceed doses
    \item \textbf{No aspirin} for children under 16
    \item \textbf{Treat the cause:} antibiotics for bacterial infections; viral infections require only supportive care
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} watch the child's behaviour, fluid intake, urine output, and any new symptoms
    \item \textbf{Febrile seizures:} first aid is to place the child on their side, protect the head, and time the seizure; contact local emergency services if it lasts more than 5 minutes
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review if fever persists beyond 3 days or the child does not improve
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dehydration from reduced intake and increased losses
    \item Febrile seizures in susceptible children
    \item Progression of unrecognised bacterial infection
    \item Medication errors from incorrect dosing
    \item Parental anxiety and unnecessary emergency visits when guidance is unclear
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for children with fever is excellent. Most febrile children recover fully within a few days, and the fever itself causes no harm or brain damage. Serious bacterial infections are uncommon and respond well to prompt treatment. Febrile seizures are frightening but do not cause lasting harm. With clear advice about comfort measures, correct medicine use, and the warning signs that require urgent care, parents can manage most fevers confidently at home, and outcomes for children are overwhelmingly good.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep a digital thermometer and age-appropriate paracetamol and ibuprofen at home
    \item Give fluids frequently and keep the child cool and comfortable
    \item Record temperatures, fluid intake, and nappy output
    \item Do not over-wrap a febrile child or use cold sponging, which is not recommended
    \item Follow immunisation schedules, which prevent many causes of fever
    \item Encourage hand washing and limit exposure to unwell people
    \item Seek medical advice whenever in doubt; a fever assessment is always reasonable
\end{itemize}
