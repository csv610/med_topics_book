\chapter{Dental Fissure Sealants}
\index{Dental Fissure Sealants}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Fissure sealants are thin, protective coatings applied to the chewing surfaces of back teeth to prevent tooth decay. The back teeth (molars and premolars) have deep grooves and pits, called fissures, where food and bacteria collect and where brushing cannot always reach. A sealant fills these grooves with a plastic resin, creating a smooth surface that is easy to clean and protects the tooth from decay. Sealants are most effective when applied soon after the permanent teeth erupt, usually in childhood. This chapter explains how sealants work, who should have them, and what the procedure involves.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Tooth decay is the most common chronic disease of childhood, and the chewing surfaces of the back teeth are the most common site of decay in children and teenagers. Fissure sealants are a proven preventive measure, recommended by dental authorities worldwide. In many countries, sealant programmes in school dental services have applied sealants to many children, and sealants are subsidised or provided through public dental services. Decay in fissures is particularly common in the first few years after the permanent molars erupt, around ages 6 and 12.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fissures:} the deep grooves and pits in the chewing surfaces of back teeth are difficult to clean and trap food and plaque
    \item \textbf{Decay development:} plaque bacteria in the fissures ferment sugars and produce acid that dissolves enamel, causing decay
    \item \textbf{Protective barrier:} sealant resin flows into the grooves and bonds to the enamel, sealing out bacteria and food
    \item \textbf{Timing:} sealants are most effective when applied to newly erupted permanent teeth, before decay develops
    \item \textbf{Mechanism of protection:} by smoothing the surface, sealants prevent plaque accumulation and make the tooth easy to clean
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sealants are clear or tooth-coloured coatings on the chewing surfaces of back teeth
    \item The procedure is quick, painless, and requires no drilling
    \item There is no change in how the tooth feels or functions
    \item Sealants need checking at dental visits and may need repair or re-application over time
    \item Children are the main candidates, but adults with deep grooves may also benefit
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sealants versus fluoride treatment, which protects all tooth surfaces rather than sealing fissures
    \item Sealants versus fillings: sealants are applied to healthy teeth to prevent decay, while fillings repair existing decay
    \item Sealants versus monitoring: shallow grooves that are easy to clean may not need sealing
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Children should have their teeth assessed for sealants as part of routine dental care, usually around ages 6--7 when the first permanent molars erupt, and again around age 11--13 for the second molars. Adults with deep grooves or a history of decay may also be offered sealants. See a dentist regularly so sealants are placed at the right time and checked thereafter.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe toothache or facial swelling, which indicates advanced decay requiring urgent care
    \item Serious dental trauma
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dental examination:} assessment of the chewing surfaces and depth of the grooves
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} the child's decay risk, diet, and history determine the need for sealants
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} may be taken to check for decay between teeth or beneath the surface
    \item \textbf{Timing:} sealants are planned to coincide with the eruption of the permanent molars
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Procedure:} the tooth is cleaned, dried, and lightly etched; the sealant is applied and hardened with a curing light
    \item \textbf{No anaesthesia:} the procedure is painless and requires no injections or drilling
    \item \textbf{School dental services:} in many local states, sealants are provided through school dental programs
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} sealants are checked at routine visits and repaired or reapplied if partially lost
    \item \textbf{Combined prevention:} sealants complement fluoride toothpaste, diet, and regular care
    \item \textbf{Adults:} sealants may be offered to adults with deep fissures and high decay risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Partial or complete loss of the sealant, requiring re-application
    \item Decay developing under a sealant if it was placed over existing decay
    \item A slight change in the bite if a sealant is too thick, which is easily adjusted
    \item Allergic reaction to the resin, which is very rare
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Fissure sealants are highly effective, reducing decay in sealed teeth by around 70--80\% in children. Sealants remain protective for many years when maintained, and they last on average 5--10 years with good care. Because they prevent decay before it starts, sealants reduce the need for fillings and other restorative treatment. Combined with fluoride and good habits, sealants help children and adults keep their natural teeth healthy for life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take children for dental visits from the age of the first teeth and ask about sealants
    \item Brush twice daily with fluoride toothpaste and limit sugary snacks and drinks
    \item Attend regular check-ups so sealants are monitored
    \item Encourage a healthy diet with plenty of vegetables, fruit, and water
    \item Ask about sealants for adult family members with deep grooves
\end{itemize}
