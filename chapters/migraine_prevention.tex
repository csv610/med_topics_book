\chapter{Migraine Prevention}
\index{Migraine Prevention}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Migraine prevention (prophylaxis) refers to the ongoing therapeutic strategies (including pharmacotherapy, lifestyle modifications, and non-pharmacological interventions) aimed at reducing the frequency, severity, and duration of migraine attacks. Unlike acute abortive treatments, which are taken during an attack, preventive therapies are administered daily or at scheduled intervals. Prophylactic therapy is indicated for patients who experience frequent attacks (typically 4 or more migraine days per month), those with severe disability despite optimal acute therapy, individuals who cannot tolerate abortive treatments, or those at risk of medication overuse headache. The primary goal is to improve the patient's quality of life and prevent the progression of episodic migraine to chronic migraine.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Migraine is a highly prevalent and disabling neurological disorder, affecting approximately 4.9 million people in many countries (around 20\% of the population). The condition is three times more common in females than in males, a discrepancy largely driven by hormonal fluctuations. Chronic migraine, defined as experiencing headaches on 15 or more days per month for at least 3 months (with at least 8 days meeting migraine criteria), affects approximately 1\% to 2\% of the global population. Despite the significant personal and economic burden of frequent migraines, clinical registries indicate that preventive therapies are vastly underutilised, with less than 20\% of eligible patients receiving appropriate prophylaxis.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The pathophysiology of migraine involves abnormal neuronal excitability and neurovascular inflammation, and preventive treatments are designed to target these mechanisms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Trigeminal system hyperexcitability:} An underlying genetic predisposition leads to cortical hyperexcitability and abnormal brainstem nociceptive processing, lowering the threshold for migraine triggers.
    \item \textbf{Calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) pathway:} CGRP is a potent neuropeptide released from trigeminal nerve endings that drives dural vasodilation and neurogenic inflammation; blocking this pathway is a key target of modern preventive agents.
    \item \textbf{Cortical spreading depression (CSD):} A wave of neuronal depolarisation followed by suppression that propagates across the cortex, triggering migraine aura and activating trigeminal nociceptors.
    \item \textbf{Hormonal and environmental triggers:} Variations in oestrogen levels, sleep deprivation, chronic stress, and dietary changes can disrupt brain homeostasis and precipitate attacks.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Candidates for migraine prevention present with clinical indicators of a high disease burden and functional impairment:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High headache frequency:} Experiencing frequent migraine days (e.g., episodic migraine with high frequency or chronic migraine).
    \item \textbf{Significant functional impairment:} Severe disruption to work, study, and social activities, often quantified using the Migraine Disability Assessment (MIDAS) score.
    \item \textbf{Prolonged or debilitating aura:} Auras that are long-lasting, hemiplegic (causing unilateral weakness), or atypical, requiring prevention to minimise neurological risks.
    \item \textbf{Acute medication failure or overuse:} Inability to gain relief from triptans or NSAIDs, or escalating use of these agents leading to medication overuse headache.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before commencing long-term preventive therapy, clinicians must rule out other headache disorders and secondary causes of headache:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tension-type headache:} Typically presenting as a bilateral, non-throbbing, band-like pressure, without associated symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, photophobia, or phonophobia.
    \item \textbf{Medication overuse headache (MOH):} A secondary headache disorder caused by the regular overuse of acute headache medications (e.g., triptans or codeine on 10 or more days per month) that must be resolved before preventives can work effectively.
    \item \textbf{Cluster headache:} Characterised by severe, strictly unilateral orbital or temporal pain lasting 15 to 180 minutes, associated with autonomic symptoms (e.g., lacrimation, nasal congestion) and restlessness.
    \item \textbf{Secondary headaches (structural/vascular):} Headaches caused by intracranial neoplasms, giant cell arteritis, idiopathic intracranial hypertension, or vascular malformations.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should seek medical evaluation if their migraines become more frequent, change in pattern, or if acute medications are no longer effective.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{"Thunderclap" headache:} A sudden, severe headache that peaks in intensity within 60 seconds (suggesting a subarachnoid haemorrhage).
    \item \textbf{Headache with focal neurological deficits:} Sudden weakness, numbness, difficulty speaking, vision loss, or loss of balance (indicating a potential stroke or hemiplegic migraine).
    \item \textbf{Headache with systemic features:} High fever, neck stiffness, confusion, or a purpuric rash (indicating potential meningitis).
    \item \textbf{New headache with visual loss:} Particularly in individuals over 50 years of age, which may indicate giant cell arteritis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The decision to start preventive therapy is based on a clinical diagnosis of migraine and a formal assessment of disease burden:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prospective headache diary:} A patient-kept log tracking headache frequency, duration, severity, trigger factors, and acute medication consumption for at least 4 weeks.
    \item \textbf{MIDAS or HIT-6 questionnaires:} Standardised patient-reported outcome measures used to quantify migraine-related disability and justify the need for prevention.
    \item \textbf{Neuroimaging (MRI or CT brain):} Indicated if the patient has atypical headache features, a change in headache pattern, or focal neurological signs (ruling out secondary causes).
    \item \textbf{Screening for comorbidities:} Assessing for pre-existing depression, anxiety, hypertension, or sleep disorders, which can guide the choice of preventive medication (e.g., choosing amitriptyline for insomnia/depression, or beta-blockers for hypertension).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is multi-dimensional and individualised, combining lifestyle adjustments, daily oral medications, targeted injectables, and nutraceuticals:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First-line oral preventives:} Prescribing daily oral medications such as beta-blockers (e.g., propranolol), anticonvulsants (e.g., topiramate), or tricyclic antidepressants (e.g., amitriptyline). These must be titrated slowly and trialled for at least 8 to 12 weeks.
    \item \textbf{CGRP-targeted therapies:} Monoclonal antibodies (e.g., erenumab, galcanezumab, fremanezumab, eptinezumab) administered subcutaneously or intravenously, which offer high specificity and rapid onset.
    \item \textbf{OnabotulinumtoxinA (Botox):} A series of 31 injections across 7 head and neck muscle groups administered every 12 weeks, indicated for chronic migraine.
    \item \textbf{Evidence-based nutraceuticals:} Recommending daily supplementation with riboflavin (vitamin B2, 400 mg), magnesium (400 to 600 mg), or coenzyme Q10 (150 to 300 mg).
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle modification and trigger avoidance:} Maintaining a consistent sleep schedule, staying hydrated, eating regular meals, exercising moderately, and practicing stress reduction techniques (such as mindfulness or biofeedback).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated frequent migraines and poorly managed prevention can lead to several complications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Progression to chronic migraine:} Central sensitisation leading to daily or near-daily head pain, which is more resistant to treatment.
    \item \textbf{Medication overuse headache:} Developing a daily rebound headache due to frequent, escalating use of acute analgesics or triptans.
    \item \textbf{Psychosocial burden:} High rates of comorbid major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, and chronic insomnia.
    \item \textbf{Migrainous infarction:} A rare complication where an ischaemic stroke occurs during the course of a typical migraine aura.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for patients undergoing migraine prevention has significantly improved with the advent of CGRP-targeted therapies. A successful response to preventive therapy is defined as a 50\% or greater reduction in monthly migraine days, which is achieved by approximately 40\% to 60\% of patients using oral preventives, and up to 70\% of patients using CGRP monoclonal antibodies or botulinum toxin. While complete elimination of migraines is rare, effective prophylaxis reduces the intensity and duration of breakthrough attacks, enhances the efficacy of acute medications, and dramatically reduces overall disability.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Self-care measures and patient active participation are vital to the success of any migraine prevention plan:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Maintain a headache diary:} Continue to log headaches to track treatment response, identify patterns, and detect early signs of medication overuse.
    \item \textbf{Adhere to the medication schedule:} Take preventive medications daily as prescribed, understanding that benefits may not be felt for several weeks or months.
    \item \textbf{Establish consistent routines:} Go to sleep and wake up at the same time every day, eat meals at regular intervals, and avoid skipping meals.
    \item \textbf{Manage stress:} Incorporate relaxation techniques, gentle exercise (such as walking or swimming), and cognitive behavioural strategies into daily life.
\end{itemize}
