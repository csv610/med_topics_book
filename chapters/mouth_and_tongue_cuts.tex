\chapter{Mouth and tongue cuts}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Mouth and tongue cuts (intraoral lacerations) refer to traumatic tissue tears or injuries affecting the mucosal lining of the mouth, including the lips, buccal mucosa, gingiva, palate, and tongue. The oral cavity is highly vascularised, meaning even minor cuts can result in profuse, alarming bleeding. Common causes include accidental biting during mastication or seizures, dental trauma, sharp food particles, foreign objects, and facial impact during sports or falls. Most minor intraoral cuts heal rapidly without surgical intervention due to the excellent blood supply and the antimicrobial properties of saliva. However, deep or complex lacerations, particularly those involving the muscular tongue or the border of the lips, require thorough medical evaluation and primary closure to prevent deformity, function loss, and infection.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Intraoral lacerations are common injuries across all age groups, but they are particularly prevalent in toddlers and young children who are learning to walk and are prone to falls with objects in their mouths. In Australia, sports-related facial injuries, including mouth and tongue cuts, are highly frequent in contact sports (such as rugby, Australian rules football, and basketball) where mouthguards are not worn. Accidental tongue biting is also a common clinical feature in patients with epilepsy, occurring during generalised tonic-clonic seizures. The majority of minor lacerations are managed at home and do not present to emergency departments; however, severe intraoral injuries represent a significant portion of paediatric emergency visits and maxillofacial trauma consultations.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The aetiology of mouth and tongue cuts is primarily mechanical trauma. The pathophysiology of healing in the oral cavity is unique due to the moist, vascular environment.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Accidental self-injury:} Biting of the tongue, inner cheek, or lip, often triggered by distraction during chewing, dental anaesthesia, or generalised seizure activity.
    \item \textbf{Direct impact trauma:} Falls, motor vehicle collisions, or sports injuries where the teeth are forced into the surrounding soft tissues (such as a lip laceration caused by incisors).
    \item \textbf{Foreign body penetration:} Injuries from sharp objects like toothpicks, forks, toys, or broken glass, which can penetrate the oral mucosa.
    \item \textbf{Rapid wound healing:} The oral mucosa heals faster than skin. This is driven by high vascularity, salivary growth factors, and the expression of specific mucosal proteins that promote rapid re-epithelialisation and minimise scar formation.
    \item \textbf{Bacterial exposure:} The mouth contains a diverse microbiome. Saliva contains IgA antibodies and lysozymes that help control bacterial proliferation, but deep wounds are still vulnerable to infection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features of mouth and tongue cuts are dominated by local pain and bleeding, alongside visible tissue disruption.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Profuse, immediate bleeding from the wound, which can mix with saliva and appear more voluminous than it actually is, causing patient anxiety.
    \item Localised pain, tenderness, and swelling, which can interfere with speaking, swallowing, and chewing.
    \item A visible tear, gap, or flap in the mucosal tissue, particularly along the lateral borders of the tongue or the inner lining of the lip.
    \item Lip lacerations involving the vermilion border (the red line of the lip), which require precise anatomical alignment.
    \item Tissue oedema, which can develop rapidly, especially in the tongue, potentially causing mild speech distortion.
    \item Mild halitosis and a whitish-grey coating over the healing wound (fibrinous exudate, which is a normal part of healing and not pus).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
While the history of trauma is usually clear, clinicians must differentiate simple lacerations from other oral mucosal lesions or underlying complications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Aphthous ulcers:} Painful, non-traumatic inflammatory ulcers (canker sores); present as small, round, white-yellow lesions with a red halo, lacking a history of acute mechanical cut.
    \item \textbf{Herpes simplex virus:} Causes clustered vesicles that rupture to form ulcers (cold sores), typically on the keratinised outer lips, accompanied by prodromal tingling.
    \item \textbf{Oral candidiasis:} Causes white, scrapeable plaques on the mucosa (thrush), distinguished by its diffuse distribution and lack of acute tissue tear.
    \item \textbf{Foreign body retention:} A complication where dirt, tooth fragments, or dental hardware remain embedded within the cut, causing persistent swelling and delayed healing.
    \item \textbf{Oral malignancy:} Chronic, non-healing ulcers (lasting more than 3 weeks) that may mimic a healing cut, but are firm, irregular, and require biopsy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most minor mouth cuts can be managed at home. However, large, deep, or persistently bleeding cuts require professional medical or dental assessment.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe swelling of the tongue or neck that causes difficulty breathing, swallowing, or talking (airway compromise).
    \item Uncontrolled bleeding from the mouth that does not stop after 15--20 minutes of direct pressure.
    \item Severe facial trauma involving head injury, loss of consciousness, or suspected jaw fracture.
\end{itemize}

Other non-emergency reasons to seek medical or dental care include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The cut is deep, gaping, or longer than 1--2 cm, which may require sutures.
    \item The cut crosses the vermilion border of the lip, requiring precise surgical alignment to prevent cosmetic deformity.
    \item The cut was caused by a dirty, rusty, or contaminated object, or your tetanus vaccination is not up to date.
    \item You suspect a tooth is broken, loose, or that a fragment of a tooth is embedded in the cut.
    \item The wound shows signs of infection after a few days, such as worsening pain, spreading redness, fever, or thick pus draining from the area.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnostic evaluation focuses on physical examination and checking for secondary injuries.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical oral examination:} Careful inspection of the oral cavity under good lighting, assessing the depth of the laceration, active bleeding, and involvement of muscles, ducts, or nerves.
    \item \textbf{Dental assessment:} Checking for loose, fractured, or missing teeth that may have caused or complicate the injury.
    \item \textbf{Soft tissue X-ray or ultrasound:} Indicated if there is suspicion of a foreign body (such as a tooth fragment or dental filling) embedded within the lips or cheeks.
    \item \textbf{Tetanus status review:} Confirming the patient's vaccination history to determine if a tetanus booster or immunoglobulin is required.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Minor cuts require only home care, while more severe lacerations need clinical repair and infection prophylaxis.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Direct pressure:} The first-line management for active bleeding; apply firm pressure using a clean gauze or cloth for 10--15 minutes.
    \item \textbf{Surgical repair:} Deep, gaping, or muscular tongue cuts are repaired under local anaesthesia using absorbable sutures. Lip lacerations involving the vermilion border are meticulously aligned using sutures.
    \item \textbf{Pain management:} Use of paracetamol or ibuprofen to control discomfort; avoid aspirin, as it can promote bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Oral hygiene:} Rinsing the mouth with warm salt water (half a teaspoon of salt in a glass of warm water) 3--4 times a day, especially after meals, to keep the wound clean.
    \item \textbf{Dietary adjustments:} Eating soft, bland, and cool foods (such as yoghurt, custard, or ice cream) and avoiding hot, spicy, acidic, or hard foods that can irritate the cut.
    \item \textbf{Tetanus prophylaxis:} Administering a booster vaccine if the wound is dirty and the last dose was more than 5 years ago.
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} Rarely indicated for simple cuts, but may be prescribed for high-risk wounds, contaminated injuries, or immunocompromised patients.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Secondary bacterial infection:} Rare due to the protective effects of saliva, but can present as localised abscesses or cellulitis.
    \item \textbf{Cosmetic deformity:} Poor alignment of the vermilion border of the lip, resulting in a visible step-off deformity or scarring.
    \item \textbf{Speech or swallowing dysfunction:} Can follow severe, poorly repaired tongue muscle lacerations.
    \item \textbf{Fistula or mucocele formation:} Caused by inadvertent damage to salivary gland ducts during the injury or repair.
    \item \textbf{Foreign body granuloma:} Chronic inflammation resulting from unrecognised debris or tooth fragments left in the wound.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for mouth and tongue cuts is outstanding. Because of the excellent blood supply of the oral cavity, mucosal wounds heal much faster than cutaneous wounds, with minor cuts resolving completely within 3--7 days. Properly repaired deep lacerations also heal well, with minimal long-term functional impairment of the tongue or lips. Scarring within the mouth is rarely noticeable or problematic.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wear a custom-fitted mouthguard when participating in contact sports or activities with a high risk of facial impact.
    \item Avoid chewing on hard objects like ice, pens, or fingernails, and take care when eating foods with bones or sharp edges.
    \item Supervise young children to prevent them from running or playing while holding objects (such as toothbrushes, pencils, or cutlery) in their mouths.
    \item Use dental wax over braces or orthodontic brackets if they are sharp and irritating the inner cheeks or lips.
    \item Chew slowly and mindfully, especially if you have had local dental anaesthesia, to prevent accidental biting of the tongue or lips.
    \item Apply an ice cube or ice pack wrapped in a clean cloth to the lip or tongue immediately after a cut to reduce swelling and bleeding.
    \item Keep your tetanus vaccinations up to date, with boosters as recommended by health guidelines.
\end{itemize}
