\section*{Definition and Overview}
Choking occurs when a foreign object becomes lodged in the throat or windpipe (trachea), partially or completely blocking the airway. It is a medical emergency: a complete blockage stops air from reaching the lungs, and brain damage can occur within minutes. Choking is most common in young children, who put objects in their mouths, and in older adults, often while eating. Simple first-aid manoeuvres such as back blows and abdominal thrusts can dislodge the object and are often life-saving.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Choking on food is one of the leading causes of accidental death from injury, particularly in young children and older adults. Children under the age of 5 are at highest risk because they explore the world by mouth and have small airways. In adults, the risk rises with age and with conditions that affect swallowing, such as stroke, Parkinson's disease, or dementia. Certain foods, including hot dogs, grapes, nuts, and hard sweets, are common causes in children. Alcohol use while eating also increases the risk of choking.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Choking happens when an object or piece of food blocks the airway. When the airway is partially blocked, some air can still pass. With a complete blockage, no air reaches the lungs, and oxygen levels in the blood fall rapidly.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Common objects in children:} small toys, balloons, coins, buttons, and small food items
    \item \textbf{Common foods in adults:} meat, bones, and large pieces of food swallowed whole
    \item \textbf{Swallowing difficulties:} from stroke, neurological disease, or ageing
    \item \textbf{Poorly chewed food:} eating too quickly or talking while eating
    \item \textbf{Dental problems:} missing teeth or poorly fitting dentures make chewing less effective
    \item \textbf{Alcohol or sedatives:} dulling the reflexes that protect the airway
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features depend on whether the airway is partially or completely blocked.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mild (partial) blockage:} the person can cough, speak, or make sounds; there may be wheezing or noisy breathing
    \item \textbf{Severe (complete) blockage:} the person cannot cough, speak, or breathe, and may clutch their throat (the universal sign of choking)
    \item \textbf{Bluish colour} of the lips, face, or skin (cyanosis)
    \item \textbf{Panic and distress,} often with gagging or stridor
    \item \textbf{Loss of consciousness} if the blockage is not relieved
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A person who appears to be choking may actually have another cause of breathing difficulty.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Anaphylaxis (severe allergic reaction with swelling of the airway)
    \item Epiglottitis -- a serious infection causing swelling of the throat
    \item Croup -- a viral infection causing a barking cough in young children
    \item Severe asthma or bronchiolitis
    \item Seizure or stroke causing difficulty swallowing
    \item Heart attack or cardiac arrest
    \item Fainting (syncope) or a sudden medical collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
If the person can cough, speak, or breathe, encourage them to keep coughing and stay with them -- do not slap their back forcefully. Seek urgent medical care if coughing continues, if there is persistent wheezing, or if you suspect something is still lodged.

\textbf{Call 000 immediately for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Inability to cough, speak, or breathe
    \item Bluish lips or face
    \item Loss of consciousness
    \item Choking in an infant who cannot cry, cough, or breathe
    \item A person who becomes limp or unresponsive
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
In a choking emergency, diagnosis is made immediately from the appearance and behaviour of the person, and first aid should start without delay.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Assessment:} can the person cough, speak, or breathe? Is there cyanosis or loss of consciousness?
    \item \textbf{History:} what was being eaten or played with, and how the episode began
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} may show a radio-opaque object if a fragment is still lodged
    \item \textbf{Bronchoscopy:} direct examination of the airway if a foreign body is suspected but not seen on X-ray
    \item \textbf{Observation:} a child who has choked may be observed in hospital for signs of aspiration
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management depends on whether the blockage is mild or severe, and on the age of the person.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mild blockage:} encourage coughing; do not interfere while the person is effectively coughing
    \item \textbf{Adults and children (severe):} up to 5 sharp back blows between the shoulder blades, then up to 5 abdominal thrusts, alternating until the object is expelled
    \item \textbf{Infants:} 5 back slaps followed by 5 chest thrusts (never abdominal thrusts)
    \item \textbf{Unconscious person:} call 000, place the person on the floor, and begin CPR while waiting for emergency services
    \item \textbf{Removal of the object:} only attempt to sweep a visible object from the mouth if you can see it clearly
    \item \textbf{Medical review:} anyone who has choked should be checked by a doctor to ensure nothing is left behind
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Most people who are treated promptly recover fully, but serious outcomes can occur.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Brain damage} from prolonged lack of oxygen
    \item \textbf{Aspiration pneumonia:} food or fluid entering the lungs
    \item \textbf{Lung collapse (atelectasis)} if a fragment remains in the airway
    \item \textbf{Injuries from first aid:} bruising or rib fractures, rarely damage to the stomach or other organs from abdominal thrusts
    \item \textbf{Cardiac arrest} from complete airway obstruction
    \item \textbf{Ongoing swallowing problems} in older adults after a choking episode
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt first aid, most choking episodes are resolved successfully and the person makes a full recovery. Outcome depends on how quickly the airway is cleared and how long the brain was without oxygen. If the object is dislodged within a few minutes, the outlook is usually excellent. Delays in treatment increase the risk of brain damage or death. Children and adults with underlying swallowing or breathing problems may need further assessment and ongoing management to reduce the risk of future episodes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cut food into small pieces and chew thoroughly before swallowing
    \item Supervise young children while eating and keep small objects and balloons out of reach
    \item Avoid giving hard sweets, whole nuts, grapes, and raw carrot pieces to young children
    \item Eat slowly and avoid talking or laughing with food in the mouth
    \item Limit alcohol while eating
    \item Ensure dentures fit well and dental problems are treated
    \item Consider a swallowing assessment if an older person has difficulty swallowing
    \item Learn first aid and CPR -- including back blows, abdominal thrusts, and infant techniques
\end{itemize}
