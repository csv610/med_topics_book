\chapter{Bacterial Versus Viral Infections}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bacterial and viral infections are the two most common types of infection, and telling them apart matters because they are treated very differently. Bacteria are single-celled organisms that can live and multiply on their own, and they can be killed by antibiotics. Viruses are much smaller, cannot reproduce outside living cells, and are not affected by antibiotics; most viral infections resolve with the body's own immune system and supportive care.

The two often cause similar symptoms. A sore throat, cough, fever, or stomach upset can be caused by either, which is why it is not always possible to tell them apart without tests. Correctly distinguishing them avoids unnecessary antibiotic use, which helps slow the growth of antibiotic-resistant bacteria.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Viral infections are by far the most common cause of everyday infections, particularly of the upper airways. The common cold, influenza, and COVID-19 are viral, as are most childhood infections such as chickenpox, measles, and viral gastroenteritis. Bacterial infections are also very common and include strep throat, urinary tract infections, many cases of pneumonia, and skin infections. Most sore throats and most acute coughs and diarrhoeal illnesses in healthy people are viral. Some infections are mixed or follow one another, for example a bacterial pneumonia developing after a viral flu.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bacteria:} complete single-celled organisms that multiply independently; they cause disease by damaging tissue directly or by producing toxins. Examples include \textit{Streptococcus}, \textit{Staphylococcus}, \textit{E. coli}, and \textit{Salmonella}
    \item \textbf{Viruses:} tiny particles of genetic material wrapped in protein; they enter host cells and hijack the cell's machinery to make copies of themselves. Examples include cold viruses, influenza, COVID-19, rotavirus, and herpes viruses
    \item \textbf{Invasion:} both enter through the airways, gut, skin, or genital tract; the site of entry and the type of organism determine which organ is affected
    \item \textbf{Immune response:} fever, inflammation, and antibody production occur in both, and much of the feeling of being unwell comes from the immune response rather than the organism itself
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
There are some general clues that suggest one or the other, but they are not reliable on their own:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Often viral:} gradual onset, runny nose, sneezing, cough, hoarse voice, muscle aches, and a mild fever that settles in a week or so
    \item \textbf{May be bacterial:} high fever that persists, thick or coloured phlegm, pus on the tonsils, tender swollen glands, a productive cough lasting more than a week, pain on urination, or a spreading skin infection
    \item \textbf{Overlap:} bacterial meningitis and pneumonia can begin suddenly with high fever, while some viral infections (such as influenza) can also be severe
    \item \textbf{Severe features:} difficulty breathing, confusion, a rash that does not fade, and signs of dehydration or sepsis can occur in either type
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The key distinction is viral versus bacterial, but other possibilities must be considered:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Viral sore throat versus strep throat (bacterial tonsillitis)
    \item Viral bronchitis versus bacterial pneumonia
    \item Viral gastroenteritis versus bacterial food poisoning
    \item Viral versus bacterial meningitis
    \item Fungal infection, which can mimic both, especially in immunocompromised people
    \item Non-infectious causes of similar symptoms, such as allergy, asthma, or reflux
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most viral infections are managed at home with rest, fluids, and simple pain relief, and most bacterial infections are managed by a GP. Antibiotics are not helpful for viral infections.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, fast breathing, or chest pain
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or a stiff neck
    \item A spreading rash that does not fade when pressed
    \item Signs of sepsis: mottled or cold skin, very fast heart rate, low urine output, or fainting
    \item High fever in a baby under 3 months old, or in anyone with a weakened immune system
\end{itemize}

See a GP if symptoms are severe, persist beyond about a week, or are getting worse, and for anyone with a cough, sore throat, or urinary symptoms that are not settling.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} onset, duration, fever pattern, and site of symptoms; the tonsils, ears, chest, skin, and urine may be checked
    \item \textbf{Rapid tests:} rapid antigen tests for influenza, COVID-19, and strep throat can give a quick answer
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} a raised white cell count and high inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein favour bacterial infection, but are not conclusive
    \item \textbf{Cultures:} throat, urine, stool, or blood cultures can grow bacteria and test which antibiotics kill them
    \item \textbf{PCR (molecular) tests:} very sensitive tests that detect the genetic material of specific viruses or bacteria
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} a chest X-ray may be needed if pneumonia is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral infections:} supportive care -- rest, fluids, and paracetamol or ibuprofen for fever and aches; antivirals are available for a few infections such as influenza, COVID-19, and herpes
    \item \textbf{Bacterial infections:} antibiotics, chosen against the likely or confirmed organism, taken for the full prescribed course
    \item \textbf{Do not demand antibiotics for viral illness:} they will not help and increase antibiotic resistance
    \item \textbf{Symptom relief:} saline sprays or lozenges for sore throat, honey for cough in adults, and rehydration fluids for vomiting or diarrhoea
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} seek review if symptoms worsen, do not improve as expected, or new red-flag features appear
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} for severe infections of either type, with fluids, oxygen, and specific treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Secondary bacterial infection after a viral illness, such as pneumonia after influenza
    \item Dehydration from fever, vomiting, or poor intake
    \item Sepsis, which can complicate severe bacterial infection
    \item Post-infectious complications, such as rheumatic fever after strep throat
    \item Antibiotic resistance, driven by antibiotics being used for viral infections
    \item Prolonged illness, cough, or fatigue that may follow either type of infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most viral infections, including colds and flu, resolve on their own within a week to ten days, although a cough can linger for weeks. Most bacterial infections such as strep throat and urinary tract infections settle within a couple of days of starting the right antibiotic. Serious infections of either type can be life-threatening, but outcomes are usually excellent with prompt treatment. In general, if symptoms are not improving as expected, or a previously mild illness suddenly worsens, this is a reason to seek medical assessment rather than to continue waiting.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep vaccinations up to date, including influenza, COVID-19, and childhood vaccines
    \item Wash hands regularly and use tissues or your elbow when coughing or sneezing
    \item Stay home when unwell to avoid spreading infection
    \item Prepare and store food safely to avoid bacterial food poisoning
    \item Clean and cover cuts to prevent skin infections
    \item Take antibiotics only when prescribed, and finish the full course
    \item Drink plenty of fluids, rest, and use simple pain and fever relief while unwell
\end{itemize}
