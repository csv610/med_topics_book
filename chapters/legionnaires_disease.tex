\chapter{Legionnaires' disease}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Legionnaires' disease is a severe, potentially life-threatening form of atypical pneumonia caused by Gram-negative bacteria belonging to the genus \textit{Legionella}, most commonly \textit{Legionella pneumophila}. The disease acquired its name in 1976 following an outbreak among people attending an American Legion convention in Philadelphia. The bacteria thrive in warm, stagnant water environments, both natural and artificial. Transmission occurs through the inhalation of microscopic water droplets (aerosols) containing the bacteria, rather than via person-to-person contact. Clinically, it is distinguished from other forms of pneumonia by its propensity to cause prominent extrapulmonary symptoms, such as severe gastrointestinal distress, neurological impairment, and biochemical abnormalities.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Legionnaires' disease occurs worldwide, both sporadically and as outbreaks. In Australia, it is a notifiable disease, with several hundred cases reported annually. The disease exhibits a seasonal pattern, with higher incidence during the warmer months of summer and autumn when cooling towers and air-conditioning systems operate at capacity. It predominantly affects individuals over the age of 50, with males representing approximately two-thirds of all cases. High-risk groups include current and former smokers, individuals with chronic lung diseases (such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), and those who are immunocompromised. In Australia, a distinct epidemiological feature is the relatively high proportion of cases caused by \textit{Legionella longbeachae}, which is found in soil and potting mixes, in addition to the water-borne \textit{Legionella pneumophila}.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The infection is caused by exposure to \textit{Legionella} bacteria, which are ubiquitous in aquatic environments. Once inhaled into the lungs, the bacteria bypass initial host defences and multiply within the respiratory system.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Causative pathogens:} \textit{Legionella pneumophila} (particularly serogroup 1) is responsible for most water-borne infections, while \textit{Legionella longbeachae} is associated with soil, compost, and potting mixes.
    \item \textbf{Environmental reservoirs:} Artificial water systems including cooling towers, evaporative condensers, hot and cold water systems (especially in hospitals and hotels), spa pools, and misting machines where water temperatures range between 20°C and 45°C.
    \item \textbf{Transmission mechanism:} Inhalation of contaminated aerosols generated by showers, taps, air-conditioning units, or spas, or the microaspiration of contaminated water. For soil-associated strains, inhalation of dust from compost or potting mix is the primary route.
    \item \textbf{Intracellular replication:} Legionellae are phagocytosed by alveolar macrophages but evade intracellular destruction. Instead, they replicate within specialised vacuoles until the host cell lyses, releasing bacteria to infect adjacent cells.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory response:} The release of bacterial toxins and the host's immune response trigger an intense alveolar inflammatory reaction, resulting in consolidated lobar pneumonia, impaired gas exchange, and systemic inflammation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The incubation period ranges from 2 to 10 days, though it can extend up to two weeks. The clinical presentation is characterised by a rapid onset of severe systemic and respiratory symptoms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prodromal systemic symptoms:} High fever (frequently exceeding 40°C), rigors, severe myalgia, headache, and profound malaise.
    \item \textbf{Respiratory symptoms:} Initially a dry, non-productive cough that later yields mucoid or blood-stained sputum; progressive dyspnoea; and pleuritic chest pain.
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal features:} Watery diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain, which occur in up to half of all cases and are more prominent than in typical pneumonias.
    \item \textbf{Neurological manifestations:} Confusion, disorientation, delirium, lethargy, and occasionally cerebellar signs such as ataxia, which are disproportionate to the degree of fever or hypoxaemia.
    \item \textbf{Physical signs:} Tachypnoea, inspiratory crackles on auscultation, relative bradycardia (a pulse rate slower than expected for the height of the fever), and hypotension.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the clinical features overlap with other acute respiratory infections, Legionnaires' disease must be differentiated from various infectious and non-infectious conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Typical bacterial pneumonia:} Caused by pathogens such as \textit{Streptococcus pneumoniae} or \textit{Haemophilus influenzae}, which typically present with sudden productive cough, high fever, and localised chest signs.
    \textbf{Other atypical pneumonias:} Infections due to \textit{Mycoplasma pneumoniae} (more common in younger cohorts) or \textit{Chlamydia pneumoniae}.
    \item \textbf{Viral respiratory tract infections:} Influenza, COVID-19, and adenovirus, which present with prominent upper respiratory symptoms and can be confirmed via molecular swabs.
    \item \textbf{Psittacosis:} A rare atypical pneumonia caused by \textit{Chlamydia psittaci}, linked to exposure to infected birds.
    \item \textbf{Non-infectious conditions:} Pulmonary embolism, aspiration pneumonitis, or acute interstitial lung disease.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone experiencing persistent fever, cough, and shortness of breath should seek medical evaluation. Prompt medical review is especially critical for older adults, smokers, or those with underlying health conditions who have recently handled potting mix or travelled.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe difficulty breathing, gasping for air, or rapid shallow breathing.
    \item Sharp, severe chest pain when breathing or coughing.
    \item New onset of confusion, severe drowsiness, or difficulty waking up.
    \item Bluish discolouration of the lips, tongue, or skin (cyanosis).
    \item Collapse, fainting, or signs of shock.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Early diagnosis is essential for guiding appropriate antibiotic therapy, as delay increases the risk of mortality.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urinary antigen test:} A rapid and widely used test that detects \textit{Legionella pneumophila} serogroup 1 antigen in the urine, offering results within hours, though it does not detect other serogroups or species.
    \item \textbf{Sputum PCR:} Polymerase chain reaction assay of sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid, which is highly sensitive and detects multiple \textit{Legionella} species, including \textit{L. longbeachae}.
    \item \textbf{Sputum culture:} Culturing the bacteria on buffered charcoal yeast extract (BCYE) agar; this is the diagnostic gold standard and allows for epidemiological typing, though it takes several days.
    \item \textbf{Chest radiography:} Typically reveals patchy, unilateral, or bilateral alveolar infiltrates that may progress to consolidation or pleural effusion.
    \item \textbf{Laboratory biochemistry:} Characteristically demonstrates hyponatraemia (low serum sodium), elevated inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein and ESR), raised liver transaminases, and elevated creatine kinase.
    \item \textbf{Haematology:} Full blood count showing neutrophilic leucocytosis and lymphopenia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment involves prompt antibiotic administration and supportive care, often in a hospital setting.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological treatment:} Intravenous or oral antibiotics that achieve high intracellular concentrations. First-line therapies include macrolides (e.g., azithromycin) or fluoroquinolones (e.g., levofloxacin or ciprofloxacin) for 7 to 14 days.
    \item \textbf{Oxygen therapy:} Supplemental oxygen is provided to maintain oxygen saturation levels, with high-flow oxygen or non-invasive ventilation utilized for severe respiratory compromise.
    \item \textbf{Fluid and electrolyte management:} Intravenous fluids to treat dehydration and shock, with careful correction of hyponatraemia to avoid neurological complications.
    \item \textbf{Intensive care support:} Mechanical ventilation and vasopressor support may be required for patients who develop respiratory failure or septic shock.
    \item \textbf{Public health intervention:} Notification of the diagnosis to local health authorities to facilitate contact tracing and environmental testing of suspected cooling towers or water sources.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Legionnaires' disease is associated with significant morbidity, particularly if treatment is delayed.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS):} Severe, widespread inflammation of the lungs leading to acute respiratory failure.
    \item \textbf{Septic shock:} Systemic infection causing profound hypotension and organ hypoperfusion.
    \item \textbf{Acute kidney injury:} Renal failure resulting from direct bacterial toxins, dehydration, or systemic hypotension.
    \item \textbf{Multiorgan dysfunction syndrome:} Failure of multiple vital organs, including the liver, kidneys, and heart.
    \item \textbf{Neurological sequelae:} Persistent cerebellar ataxia, memory impairment, or cognitive deficits.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis depends heavily on the age and immune status of the patient, the presence of co-morbidities, and the speed of antibiotic initiation. In previously healthy individuals who receive prompt, appropriate antibiotic therapy, the prognosis is generally favourable, though recovery can be slow, with fatigue and shortness of breath persisting for several months. For hospitalised patients, the mortality rate is approximately 10\% but can exceed 30\% in healthcare-associated cases, the elderly, or those who are immunocompromised.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Prevention focuses on controlling the growth and dispersal of \textit{Legionella} bacteria in water systems and soil.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Water system maintenance:} Regular cleaning, disinfection, and thermal or chemical treatment of cooling towers, hot water systems, and spa pools.
    \item \textbf{Water temperature control:} Maintain hot water storage systems at 60°C or higher (and deliver at 50°C to prevent scalding) and cold water systems below 20°C to inhibit bacterial replication.
    \item \textbf{Potting mix safety:} Wear a well-fitting P2 or P1 mask and gardening gloves when handling compost, soil, or potting mix; dampen the product slightly before use to minimise dust.
    \item \textbf{Hygiene practices:} Wash hands thoroughly with soap and water after gardening or handling soil products.
    \item \textbf{Smoking cessation:} Avoid smoking and vaping, which damage the respiratory tract's natural defences and increase the risk of infection.
\end{itemize}
