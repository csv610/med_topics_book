\chapter{Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorder (FASD)}
\index{Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorder (FASD)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Fetal alcohol spectrum disorder (FASD) is the umbrella term for the range of physical, behavioural, and learning difficulties that can occur in a person whose mother drank alcohol during pregnancy. Alcohol crosses the placenta and can damage the developing brain, which is the most vulnerable organ; there is no known safe level or safe time for drinking in pregnancy. FASD is a lifelong disability: it does not go away, but early diagnosis and the right supports greatly improve outcomes. The most severe end of the spectrum, fetal alcohol syndrome, includes characteristic facial features and growth problems, but most people with FASD have no facial features at all and present with brain-based difficulties. This chapter explains the causes, features, diagnosis, and support for FASD.

\section*{Epidemiology}
FASD is the leading preventable cause of developmental disability in many countries. Conservative estimates suggest that at least 2\% of children are affected, which means thousands of babies are born with FASD every year. Rates are higher in communities with greater alcohol use during pregnancy, including some Indigenous communities and groups where drinking is common. Many cases go unrecognised because the condition has no outward signs in most affected people, and diagnosis requires specialist assessment. Internationally, prevalence estimates range from 1 to 5\% of the general population, making FASD at least as common as autism in many settings.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The cause of FASD is alcohol exposure before birth, but exactly how individual children are affected depends on many factors.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Direct alcohol toxicity:} alcohol and its breakdown product, acetaldehyde, cross the placenta and interfere with cell division, migration, and survival in the developing brain
    \item \textbf{Timing of exposure:} the first trimester carries the highest risk of facial and structural malformations, but brain damage can occur at any stage, including late pregnancy when the brain grows fastest
    \item \textbf{Amount and pattern:} binge drinking is particularly harmful, but regular low-level drinking also carries risk
    \item \textbf{Maternal and fetal factors:} genetics, nutrition, maternal age, other substance use, and the mother's health modify susceptibility, which is why two pregnancies with identical exposure can have different outcomes
    \item \textbf{Effects on the brain:} damage to the corpus callosum, hippocampus, and frontal regions produces problems with memory, learning, attention, impulse control, and executive function
    \item \textbf{Effects on the body:} disrupted development can cause growth restriction, facial dysmorphism, and malformations of the heart, kidneys, and bones
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features of FASD are divided into physical signs and brain-based difficulties, and vary widely between individuals.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Brain-based impairments: difficulties with learning and memory, attention and concentration, problem solving, language, and motor skills
    \item Behavioural problems: hyperactivity, impulsivity, poor judgement, difficulty understanding consequences, and trouble making and keeping friends
    \item Difficulty with daily living: trouble managing money, time, and routines, and vulnerability to exploitation
    \item In fetal alcohol syndrome only: the characteristic facial features of a smooth philtrum, thin upper lip, and small palpebral fissures, plus growth deficiency
    \item Physical problems: heart defects, kidney problems, hearing and vision issues, and poor coordination
    \item Mental health difficulties: anxiety, depression, and behavioural disorders are common, especially without a diagnosis and supports
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The behavioural and learning features of FASD overlap with many other conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and other neurodevelopmental disorders
    \item Autism spectrum disorder, with shared social and communication difficulties
    \item Intellectual disability of other causes
    \item Specific learning disorders such as dyslexia
    \item Reactive attachment disorder and the effects of trauma or neglect
    \item Other genetic and prenatal conditions, including fetal alcohol syndrome being distinguished from syndromes such as Williams and Noonan syndromes
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents and carers should seek assessment if a child has a known history of alcohol exposure in pregnancy, or shows learning, behaviour, growth, or facial-feature concerns. Early diagnosis unlocks funding, therapy, and school support, so prompt referral to a specialised FASD diagnostic service is important.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Any medical emergency in a child, including severe injury, fitting, or breathing difficulty
    \item Suicidal behaviour or statements, which are more common in older children and adults with FASD
    \item Any situation in which a child or young person is unsafe
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
FASD is diagnosed by a multidisciplinary team using local diagnostic guidelines.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Confirmed prenatal alcohol exposure:} a reliable history of alcohol use in pregnancy, which may come from the mother or child protection records
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive neurodevelopmental assessment:} testing of intelligence, memory, attention, executive function, language, and motor skills by psychologists
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} measurement of growth and facial features, and examination for other birth defects
    \item \textbf{Medical review:} assessment by a paediatrician or physician, including hearing, vision, and cardiac checks
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic criteria:} a diagnosis of FASD requires severe impairment in at least three brain domains, with or without the facial features of fetal alcohol syndrome
    \item \textbf{Family and school information:} reports of daily functioning from parents, teachers, and carers are essential to the assessment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
There is no cure for FASD; management focuses on support, structure, and strengths.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Early intervention:} speech therapy, occupational therapy, physiotherapy, and early childhood education programs in the preschool years
    \item \textbf{Educational support:} individualised learning plans, reduced expectations for rote memory, and teaching through routines and repetition
    \item \textbf{Behavioural strategies:} consistent routines, clear and simple instructions, supervision around money and safety, and positive reward systems
    \item \textbf{Medication:} treatment of co-occurring ADHD, anxiety, or depression where diagnosed
    \item \textbf{Family support and training:} helping carers understand that difficult behaviour reflects brain damage, not defiance
    \item \textbf{Transition support:} assistance with moving from school to work, vocational training, and independent living with appropriate supervision
    \item \textbf{Advocacy and services:} national disability-support scheme funding, disability services, and legal protections for young people who may be vulnerable
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Secondary disabilities that develop when FASD is unrecognised: school failure, trouble with the law, substance misuse, and unsafe sexual behaviour
    \item Mental health problems, including anxiety, depression, and elevated suicide risk
    \item Exploitation and victimisation because of impaired judgement and suggestibility
    \item Difficulty maintaining employment, relationships, and independent living in adulthood
    \item Chronic physical health problems from birth defects affecting the heart, kidneys, and senses
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
FASD is a lifelong condition, but the outcome is strongly shaped by diagnosis and support. Children diagnosed early and given structured, consistent care frequently finish school, work, and live satisfying lives. Without recognition, the same children are far more likely to experience school exclusion, mental illness, and contact with the justice system. Protective factors include a stable home, strong caregiver understanding, and services that build on the person's strengths. Research continues into prevention and into supports that improve brain-based skills, but the single most powerful intervention remains not drinking alcohol in pregnancy.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Abstain from alcohol if pregnant, trying to conceive, or not using effective contraception
    \item Stop drinking as soon as a pregnancy is discovered; stopping at any stage reduces harm to the baby
    \item Seek support from a doctor, midwife, or alcohol counselling service to reduce or stop drinking
    \item Ensure partners, family, and communities support alcohol-free pregnancies
    \item For parents of a child with FASD: build routines, simplify instructions, supervise high-risk activities, and connect with support groups and respite care
    \item Advocate for the child at school and with services, and seek national disability-support scheme assessment
    \item Look after your own wellbeing as a carer, including counselling and support networks
\end{itemize}
