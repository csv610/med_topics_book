\chapter{Dietary Fats}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dietary fats are an essential part of a healthy diet, providing energy, helping the body absorb fat-soluble vitamins, and contributing to cell structure and hormone production. Fats are not all the same: unsaturated fats are generally healthy and are found in fish, nuts, seeds, avocado, and plant oils, while saturated and trans fats are associated with higher cholesterol and increased heart disease risk. The type and amount of fat matter more than avoiding fat altogether. This chapter explains the different types of dietary fats and how to make heart-healthy fat choices.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Dietary fat intake and its composition influence population rates of heart disease, obesity, and diabetes. Many Australians consume more saturated fat than recommended, mainly from processed foods, fatty meats, and full-fat dairy. At the same time, many people do not eat enough of the healthy fats found in fish, nuts, and seeds. Guidelines recommend that total fat provide 20--35\% of daily energy, with most fats coming from unsaturated sources.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Unsaturated fats:} monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fats, including omega-3 fatty acids, lower LDL cholesterol and support heart health
    \item \textbf{Monounsaturated fats:} found in olive oil, avocado, and nuts
    \item \textbf{Polyunsaturated fats:} found in sunflower and safflower oils, and omega-3s in oily fish such as salmon and sardines
    \item \textbf{Saturated fats:} found in fatty meats, butter, cream, full-fat dairy, and palm oil; raise LDL cholesterol
    \item \textbf{Trans fats:} found in some processed and fried foods; raise LDL and lower HDL cholesterol
    \item \textbf{Cholesterol:} dietary and blood cholesterol are related, but saturated and trans fats are the main dietary drivers of blood cholesterol
    \item \textbf{Energy:} fat is energy-dense, so large amounts contribute to weight gain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Healthy fat intake supports absorption of vitamins A, D, E, and K
    \item Unsaturated fats, especially omega-3s, support heart, brain, and joint health
    \item Diets high in saturated and trans fats raise LDL ("bad") cholesterol
    \item High cholesterol is a major risk factor for heart attack and stroke
    \item Fat is needed in the diet, but choosing the right types matters
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Saturated versus unsaturated fats, which affect cholesterol differently
    \item Naturally occurring versus industrial trans fats
    \item Fat in whole foods versus added fats in processed products
    \item Dietary fats versus other causes of high blood cholesterol, such as genetics
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with high cholesterol, heart disease, diabetes, or a family history of these conditions should discuss their diet and fat intake with a doctor, and may benefit from a referral to a dietitian.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, sudden breathlessness, or signs of a heart attack or stroke
    \item Any medical emergency
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} lipid profile measuring total, LDL, and HDL cholesterol and triglycerides
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} cardiovascular risk is calculated from lipids, blood pressure, age, and other factors
    \item \textbf{Dietary assessment:} review of fat intake and food choices
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} repeat lipid tests to monitor the effect of diet and treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Choose unsaturated fats:} olive oil, canola oil, nuts, seeds, avocado, and oily fish
    \item \textbf{Limit saturated fats:} reduce fatty meats, full-fat dairy, butter, and processed meats
    \item \textbf{Avoid trans fats:} limit deep-fried and commercially baked foods
    \item \textbf{Eat oily fish:} two to three serves a week for omega-3s
    \item \textbf{Cook smart:} bake, grill, or steam rather than fry
    \item \textbf{Read labels:} check saturated fat content
    \item \textbf{Medication:} statins or other medicines when diet alone does not control cholesterol
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item High LDL cholesterol from excess saturated and trans fats
    \item Atherosclerosis, heart attack, and stroke
    \item Weight gain and obesity from excess energy intake
    \item Gallbladder disease in some people with very high-fat diets
    \item Nutrient deficiencies from very low-fat diets
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Switching from saturated to unsaturated fats lowers LDL cholesterol and reduces cardiovascular risk, and these benefits occur within weeks to months of dietary change. A heart-healthy fat pattern combined with overall healthy eating, activity, and weight management significantly reduces the risk of heart disease. People with genetic high cholesterol may still need medication, but diet remains important alongside it.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use olive or canola oil for cooking and dressings
    \item Eat a handful of nuts most days
    \item Include oily fish such as salmon, sardines, and mackerel weekly
    \item Choose reduced-fat dairy and lean meats
    \item Limit processed, fried, and fast foods
    \item Check nutrition labels for saturated fat
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and stay active
    \item Have your cholesterol checked as recommended
\end{itemize}
