\chapter{Amnesia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Amnesia is the loss of memory. It may be temporary or permanent and can affect the ability to form new memories, to recall past events, or both. Anterograde amnesia is the inability to form new memories after an injury or illness, whereas retrograde amnesia is the loss of memories formed before the event. Amnesia is a symptom of an underlying problem rather than a disease in itself, and its causes range from head injury and stroke to infection, alcohol, seizures, dementia, and psychological stress. The pattern of memory loss, its timing, and the presence of other neurological features provide crucial clues to the cause. Some forms of amnesia resolve fully, while others leave permanent gaps in memory that require structured support.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Memory disturbance is common, but true amnesia is less so. Transient global amnesia affects roughly 3 to 5 people per 100,000 per year, typically in people over the age of 50. Traumatic brain injury, a major cause of amnesia, is most common in young men and in older adults, often following falls, road traffic crashes, and sport injuries. Amnesia related to alcohol, stroke, brain infection, and dementia becomes more common with age and with the risk factors for those conditions. As populations age, progressive causes of memory loss, particularly dementia, are increasing in prevalence worldwide.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Amnesia results from damage to the brain's memory circuits, most importantly the hippocampus and the medial temporal lobes, which are essential for laying down new memories and retrieving old ones:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Head injury:} concussion and more severe traumatic brain injury disrupt memory pathways, commonly causing loss of the period around the injury.
    \item \textbf{Stroke:} interruption of the blood supply to memory areas, for example through occlusion of the posterior cerebral arteries.
    \item \textbf{Infection:} encephalitis, particularly herpes simplex virus infection, can selectively damage the temporal lobes.
    \item \textbf{Lack of oxygen:} cardiac arrest, severe asthma, or carbon monoxide poisoning can injure the hippocampus.
    \item \textbf{Alcohol and drugs:} acute alcoholic blackouts and chronic Korsakoff syndrome, which follows thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency, both impair memory.
    \item \textbf{Seizures:} some epileptic seizures, especially temporal lobe seizures, transiently affect memory.
    \item \textbf{Dementia:} Alzheimer's disease and related disorders cause progressive, irreversible memory loss.
    \item \textbf{Transient global amnesia:} a temporary, dense episode of amnesia, often precipitated by emotional stress, exertion, or a Valsalva manoeuvre.
    \item \textbf{Psychological causes:} dissociative (functional) amnesia after severe trauma or stress.
    \item \textbf{Medication:} sedatives, general anaesthesia, and some other drugs can impair memory.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The pattern of memory loss gives important clues to the cause:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Repeatedly forgetting recent events, names, or conversations.
    \item Difficulty learning new information (anterograde amnesia).
    \item Loss of memories from before the event (retrograde amnesia).
    \item Asking the same question over and over.
    \item Disorientation to time or place.
    \item In transient global amnesia: sudden confusion and repetitive questioning that resolves within hours.
    \item Preservation of more distant memories, language, and the ability to walk and talk, which is characteristic of pure amnestic syndromes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Memory loss must be distinguished from generalised confusion and from treatable conditions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Dementia, including Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia, and other neurodegenerative disorders.
    \item Delirium, a sudden confused state with a physical cause such as infection or metabolic upset.
    \item Depression or anxiety, which impair concentration and memory.
    \item Medication side effects, especially from sedatives and anticholinergics.
    \item Head injury and post-concussion syndrome.
    \item Stroke or transient ischaemic attack.
    \item Epilepsy, including non-convulsive seizures.
    \item Encephalitis and other brain infections.
    \item Korsakoff syndrome and alcohol-related brain damage.
    \item Dissociative (functional) amnesia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if memory loss is sudden, persistent, worsening, or interfering with daily life, and especially if it follows a head injury or is accompanied by confusion, headache, or fever.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden confusion, difficulty speaking, or weakness on one side, which may indicate a stroke.
    \item Sudden memory loss after a head injury.
    \item A seizure or collapse.
    \item Fever with confusion or a stiff neck, which may indicate meningitis or encephalitis.
    \item Any sudden change in consciousness or behaviour.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The history, especially when taken from a witness or family member, is the most important part of the assessment:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} from the person and from family or witnesses, covering the onset, timing, triggers, and pattern of memory loss.
    \item \textbf{Cognitive testing:} structured assessment of memory, attention, and other thinking skills.
    \item \textbf{Neurological examination:} assessment of reflexes, coordination, eye movements, and other neurological signs.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} thyroid function, vitamin B12, glucose, liver function, and inflammatory markers.
    \item \textbf{Brain imaging:} CT or MRI to look for stroke, head injury, infection, or brain shrinkage.
    \item \textbf{Electroencephalogram (EEG):} if seizures are suspected.
    \item \textbf{Spinal fluid analysis:} if encephalitis or another inflammatory cause is suspected.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management targets the underlying cause and provides practical support for the person and their family:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Treat the cause:} antiviral therapy for encephalitis, acute stroke care, thiamine for alcohol-related amnesia, and anticonvulsant medication for seizures.
    \item \textbf{Review medication:} stop or adjust drugs that impair memory where possible.
    \item \textbf{Memory aids:} notebooks, calendars, alarms, and phone reminders.
    \item \textbf{Routine and structure:} familiar surroundings and clear daily routines reduce confusion and support orientation.
    \item \textbf{Cognitive rehabilitation:} occupational therapy and neuropsychology to retrain memory skills and adapt daily tasks.
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} counselling for dissociative amnesia and for adjustment to memory loss.
    \item \textbf{Safety measures:} supervision where the person wanders or cannot manage independently.
    \item \textbf{Carer support:} education, respite care, and carer support groups.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Amnesia can be disabling and dangerous without supervision:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Wandering and getting lost.
    \item Falls and accidents due to disorientation.
    \item Inability to manage medication, money, or daily tasks safely.
    \item Social withdrawal and strain on relationships.
    \item Depression and anxiety.
    \item Loss of independence and the need for long-term care.
    \item Injury to self or others if safety is compromised.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends entirely on the cause. Transient global amnesia resolves within 24 hours in nearly all cases, with a low but real risk of recurrence. Memory after concussion usually improves over weeks to months. Amnesia from stroke or encephalitis may improve as the brain recovers, although some loss can be permanent. Progressive causes such as Alzheimer's disease worsen over time. Even where memory does not return fully, aids, routine, and structured support can greatly improve daily life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Many causes of amnesia are preventable:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Wear seatbelts and helmets to prevent head injury.
    \item Limit alcohol and eat a balanced diet with adequate thiamine (vitamin B1).
    \item Control blood pressure, diabetes, and cholesterol to reduce stroke risk.
    \item Stay mentally and socially active.
    \item Review medicines with a doctor or pharmacist for effects on memory.
    \item Protect against brain infections with recommended vaccinations.
    \item Seek help early for memory problems rather than waiting for them to worsen.
\end{itemize}
