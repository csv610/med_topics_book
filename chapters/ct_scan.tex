\chapter{CT Scan}
\index{CT Scan}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A computed tomography (CT) scan is a medical imaging test that uses X-rays to create detailed cross-sectional images of the body. A CT scanner rotates around the patient, taking many X-ray pictures that a computer combines into slices, allowing doctors to see bones, organs, blood vessels, and soft tissues in fine detail. CT scans are fast, widely available, and excellent at identifying injuries, tumours, bleeding, and infection. They involve a small amount of radiation, so they are used when the benefit clearly outweighs the risk. A contrast dye is often given to improve the visibility of blood vessels and organs.

\section*{Epidemiology}
CT scanning is one of the most commonly performed advanced imaging tests, with millions of scans performed each year in many countries and worldwide. It is used across emergency medicine, oncology, cardiology, and many other specialties. CT is the first-line test for many emergencies, including head injury, stroke, and abdominal pain. Because each scan involves radiation, use is carefully justified, and alternative imaging without radiation, such as ultrasound or MRI, is preferred where it will answer the question equally well, particularly in children and pregnancy.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
A CT scan works by measuring how X-rays pass through different tissues.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{X-ray absorption:} dense tissues such as bone absorb more X-rays and appear white; air appears black
    \item \textbf{Cross-sectional imaging:} the scanner takes images from many angles and reconstructs slices
    \item \textbf{3D reconstruction:} slices can be combined into three-dimensional views
    \item \textbf{Contrast dye:} iodine-based dye makes blood vessels and well-perfused organs brighter
    \item \textbf{Radiation:} a CT scan delivers a radiation dose higher than a plain X-ray
    \item \textbf{Image quality:} modern scanners produce high-resolution images quickly
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
CT scans are performed to answer specific clinical questions, and the experience is quick and usually painless.

\begin{itemize}
    \item The scan takes only minutes, though preparation can take longer
    \item The patient lies still on a table that moves through a ring-shaped scanner
    \item A contrast drink or injection may be given beforehand
    \item The scan itself is painless and silent from inside
    \item Patients with contrast may feel a warm flush or a metallic taste
    \item Claustrophobia is uncommon with modern open scanners but can occur
    \item Children may need sedation or preparation to keep still
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The choice of imaging depends on the clinical question.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Plain X-ray, for simple bone and chest questions with less radiation
    \item Ultrasound, which uses no radiation and is excellent for soft tissue, pregnancy, and children
    \item MRI, which gives superior soft tissue detail without radiation but takes longer
    \item PET-CT, which adds functional information in cancer imaging
    \item No imaging at all, where it will not change management
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
CT scans are ordered by doctors when clinically indicated, and patients do not self-refer. Before the scan, tell staff if you are pregnant, have kidney problems, or have had allergic reactions to contrast dye.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe symptoms during or after a scan, such as difficulty breathing, facial swelling, or collapse (possible contrast reaction)
    \item Chest pain or a severe allergic reaction
    \item Symptoms of a serious condition for which the scan was ordered, such as stroke or injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
CT is used to diagnose or exclude many conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Head CT:} for head injury, stroke, bleeding, and tumours
    \item \textbf{Chest CT:} for pulmonary embolism, lung cancer, and chest trauma
    \item \textbf{Abdominal and pelvic CT:} for appendicitis, kidney stones, bowel conditions, and abdominal injury
    \item \textbf{CT angiography:} imaging of blood vessels, including coronary arteries
    \item \textbf{CT-guided biopsy:} using the scan to guide a needle precisely
    \item \textbf{Trauma CT:} a whole-body scan in major trauma
    \item \textbf{Cancer staging:} to determine the extent of disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Preparation and aftercare are straightforward.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Preparation:} fasting may be required for some scans; a contrast drink may be given
    \item \textbf{Contrast precautions:} people with kidney disease or contrast allergies receive special consideration
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} CT is avoided in pregnancy unless essential
    \item \textbf{Children:} protocols are adjusted to minimise radiation
    \item \textbf{After the scan:} no restrictions for most people; drink extra fluids after contrast
    \item \textbf{Results:} a radiologist reports the scan and the referring doctor explains the findings
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Radiation exposure, with a small theoretical long-term cancer risk
    \item Allergic reaction to contrast dye, ranging from mild to severe
    \item Kidney injury from contrast in people with kidney disease
    \item Metformin interactions with contrast in people with diabetes
    \item Anxiety or claustrophobia during the scan
    \item Incidental findings that require further investigation
    \item Sedation risks in children
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
CT scanning is safe and extremely valuable when used appropriately. It provides rapid, accurate diagnosis of many serious conditions, often saving lives in emergencies, and guides treatment decisions across medicine. The radiation risk from a single scan is very small, and the benefit of a correct diagnosis almost always outweighs it. Doctors aim to avoid unnecessary scans, particularly repeated scanning and scans in children, by using alternative imaging where appropriate.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep a record of your imaging history and mention it to your doctor
    \item Tell the imaging team about pregnancy, kidney disease, diabetes, and allergies
    \item Ask why the scan is needed and whether an alternative without radiation is possible
    \item Follow preparation instructions, including fasting and contrast requirements
    \item Drink fluids after a contrast scan as advised
    \item Do not delay urgent scans; the clinical benefit is the priority
    \item Discuss results and next steps with your doctor
\end{itemize}
