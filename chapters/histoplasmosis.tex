\chapter{Histoplasmosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Histoplasmosis is an infection caused by the fungus Histoplasma capsulatum, which is found in soil enriched with bird or bat droppings. Most people inhale the fungal spores without becoming ill or develop only a mild, flu-like illness. The infection can be more serious in people with weakened immune systems, in whom it can spread throughout the body, and in people with pre-existing lung disease, in whom it can cause chronic lung disease. Histoplasmosis is not spread from person to person and is not common in Australia, but it affects travellers to endemic regions such as parts of North and South America.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Histoplasmosis is most common in the central United States, particularly the Ohio and Mississippi River valleys, and in parts of Central and South America, Africa, and Asia. In Australia the infection is rare, though cases occur in travellers and in people exposed to bat caves or bird roosts. Most exposed people develop no symptoms or a mild illness that resolves without treatment. Severe and disseminated disease occurs mainly in people with HIV, organ transplants, or other immunosuppression, and in infants.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The fungus:} Histoplasma capsulatum grows in soil, especially where bird and bat droppings accumulate
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} inhalation of airborne spores from disturbed soil, caves, old buildings, and chicken coops
    \item \textbf{Acute pulmonary histoplasmosis:} spores germinate in the lungs and trigger inflammation
    \item \textbf{Chronic pulmonary histoplasmosis:} develops in people with underlying lung disease, such as emphysema, causing cavitating lung infection
    \item \textbf{Disseminated histoplasmosis:} the fungus spreads through the bloodstream to many organs, mainly in immunocompromised people
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} the fungus multiplies within immune cells; the outcome depends on the dose of exposure and the strength of the immune response
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Most infections are asymptomatic or cause mild fever, cough, and fatigue
    \item Acute illness: fever, chills, headache, cough, chest pain, and breathlessness, 1--3 weeks after exposure
    \item Some people develop joint pain and skin lesions with acute infection
    \item Chronic pulmonary histoplasmosis: progressive cough, weight loss, night sweats, and breathlessness over months
    \item Disseminated disease: fever, weight loss, enlarged liver and spleen, mouth ulcers, and skin lesions
    \item Eye disease (ocular histoplasmosis syndrome) can cause vision changes
    \item Symptoms can mimic tuberculosis and other infections
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Tuberculosis, which has a similar chronic lung and systemic picture
    \item Pneumonia from bacteria and viruses
    \item Other fungal infections, including blastomycosis and coccidioidomycosis
    \item Sarcoidosis and other inflammatory lung diseases
    \item Lymphoma and other malignancies causing fever and weight loss
    \item Travel and exposure history guide testing
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for a persistent fever, cough, or flu-like illness after travel to endemic areas or exposure to caves or bird roosts, especially if you have a weakened immune system or lung disease.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing or rapid breathing
    \item Coughing up blood
    \item High fever with confusion or severe drowsiness
    \item Chest pain or collapse
    \item Signs of severe illness in an immunocompromised person
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} travel and exposure to bird or bat environments
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray and CT:} show lung infiltrates, nodules, and cavities
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} antigen testing in blood and urine, and antibody testing
    \item \textbf{Fungal culture:} from sputum, blood, or tissue, though it grows slowly
    \item \textbf{Microscopy and histology:} identification of the fungus in tissue samples
    \item \textbf{Immunity assessment:} HIV testing is recommended in disseminated disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mild acute infection:} most people recover without treatment
    \item \textbf{Moderate to severe acute infection:} antifungal therapy with itraconazole or amphotericin
    \item \textbf{Chronic pulmonary histoplasmosis:} long courses of oral itraconazole
    \item \textbf{Disseminated disease:} intensive antifungal therapy, initially with amphotericin in severe cases
    \item \textbf{Immunocompromised care:} antifungal treatment plus management of the underlying immunosuppression
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} oxygen, fluids, and treatment of complications
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} monitoring response and preventing relapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic cavitary lung disease with progressive breathlessness
    \item Disseminated infection involving the liver, spleen, bone marrow, and nervous system
    \item Pericarditis and mediastinal complications
    \item Eye disease with vision loss
    \item Relapse after treatment, especially in immunocompromised people
    \item Death in severe disseminated disease if untreated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on immune status and the form of the disease. Acute histoplasmosis in healthy people resolves completely in nearly all cases, with only fatigue lingering for weeks. Chronic pulmonary disease responds to prolonged treatment in most people. Disseminated disease is serious but treatable with antifungal therapy, particularly when immunosuppression can be improved. With appropriate treatment, the majority of patients recover, though immunocompromised people may need lifelong antifungal suppression.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Avoid disturbing soil and sites with bird or bat droppings in endemic areas
    \item Wear masks and wet down soil when working in high-risk sites
    \item Avoid caving in endemic regions
    \item Immunocompromised people should avoid high-risk exposures
    \item See a doctor promptly for symptoms after relevant exposure
    \item Complete prescribed antifungal courses fully
    \item Attend follow-up to monitor for relapse
\end{itemize}
