\chapter{Cri du Chat Syndrome}
\index{Cri du Chat Syndrome}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cri du chat syndrome, which means "cry of the cat" in French, is a rare genetic condition caused by the loss (deletion) of a piece of the short arm of chromosome 5. It is named for the distinctive high-pitched, cat-like cry that affected babies make. Children with cri du chat syndrome have characteristic facial features, intellectual disability, slow growth, and delayed development. The severity varies widely depending on how much genetic material is missing. There is no cure, but early intervention, therapies, and family support greatly improve outcomes and quality of life.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cri du chat syndrome affects about 1 in 20,000--50,000 babies, making it a rare condition. It occurs slightly more often in girls. Most cases are new (de novo) deletions that happen during the formation of egg or sperm, rather than being inherited, and the risk does not usually increase with parental age. In about 10--15\% of cases, one parent carries a balanced rearrangement of chromosome 5 that does not affect them but can cause the syndrome in their children, which is why genetic testing of parents is offered.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The condition results from loss of genetic material on chromosome 5.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chromosome deletion:} a piece of the short arm of chromosome 5 (5p) is missing
    \item \textbf{Size of deletion:} larger deletions generally cause more severe features
    \item \textbf{Key region:} loss of a specific area (5p15) is associated with the cat-like cry
    \item \textbf{De novo deletion:} the deletion arises new in most cases, with no family history
    \item \textbf{Inherited rearrangement:} a parent may carry a balanced translocation that passes on the deletion
    \item \textbf{Genetic effect:} the missing genes disturb early brain and body development
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Features are usually recognisable in the newborn period.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A high-pitched, cat-like cry in infancy, which becomes less noticeable with age
    \item Low birth weight and slow growth
    \item Characteristic facial features: a small head (microcephaly), round face, widely spaced eyes, a broad nasal bridge, and a small jaw
    \item Intellectual disability, ranging from mild to severe
    \item Delayed motor and speech development
    \item Feeding difficulties and poor weight gain in infancy
    \item Behavioural features: hyperactivity, repetitive movements, and sensitivity to sound
    \item Medical problems: heart defects, hernias, and recurrent infections in some children
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The syndrome is diagnosed by genetic testing, but its features overlap with other conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Other chromosomal deletion or duplication syndromes
    \item Down syndrome and other trisomies
    \item Microdeletion syndromes such as Prader-Willi or Angelman syndrome
    \item Cerebral palsy or developmental delay from other causes
    \item Hearing or vision impairment causing delayed development
    \item Congenital infections causing similar features
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
A diagnosis is usually made shortly after birth or in early infancy when the features are noticed, and is confirmed by genetic testing. Children need regular paediatric follow-up and access to therapies.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing or a bluish colour (possible congenital heart disease)
    \item A seizure
    \item A high fever with an unwell child
    \item Choking or feeding emergencies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is confirmed through genetic testing.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chromosome analysis (karyotype):} examination of the chromosomes, which shows the deletion
    \item \textbf{FISH testing:} a targeted test that confirms the deletion of the 5p region
    \item \textbf{Chromosomal microarray:} a more detailed test that defines the size of the deletion
    \item \textbf{Parental testing:} chromosomes of the parents are checked for balanced rearrangements
    \item \textbf{Cardiac assessment:} echocardiography to check for heart defects
    \item \textbf{Hearing and vision tests:} to detect sensory impairment
    \item \textbf{Developmental assessment:} monitoring of growth and milestones
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is multidisciplinary and focuses on development, health, and support.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Paediatric care:} regular review of growth, feeding, and general health
    \item \textbf{Early intervention:} physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and speech therapy from infancy
    \item \textbf{Special education:} tailored educational support at school
    \item \textbf{Feeding support:} dietetic advice and management of feeding difficulties
    \item \textbf{Treatment of medical problems:} surgery for heart defects and hernias; treatment of infections
    \item \textbf{Behavioural support:} strategies for hyperactivity and challenging behaviour
    \item \textbf{Genetic counselling:} information for families about recurrence risks
    \item \textbf{Family support:} connection with parent support groups and disability services
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Congenital heart defects, which may need surgery
    \item Feeding problems and poor weight gain in infancy
    \item Recurrent ear and respiratory infections
    \item Constipation and hernias
    \item Scoliosis and other musculoskeletal problems
    \item Severe intellectual disability in some children
    \item Behavioural difficulties at school
    \item The emotional and practical impact on families
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook varies widely and depends on the size of the deletion and the severity of associated problems. Most children with cri du chat syndrome survive into adulthood, and life expectancy is often near normal when there is no severe heart disease. With early intervention, many individuals achieve independent walking, develop communication skills, and lead fulfilling lives with appropriate support. The cat-like cry fades with age, and intellectual disability, while lifelong, is compatible with a good quality of life in a supportive environment.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend genetic counselling if you have a child with the syndrome or a family history
    \item Engage with early intervention services as early as possible
    \item Keep up to date with immunisations and paediatric reviews
    \item Ensure regular hearing, vision, and dental checks
    \item Connect with parent support organisations for information and community
    \item Plan for transitions: preschool, school, and adulthood
    \item Look after the wellbeing of siblings and parents, including respite care
\end{itemize}
