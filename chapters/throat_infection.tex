\chapter{Throat infection}
\index{Throat infection}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Educational reference; always seek professional advice for individual care.}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A throat infection is inflammation of the pharynx and/or tonsils caused by microorganisms, producing sore throat, painful swallowing, and often fever. Most cases are viral and self-limiting. A minority are caused by bacteria, chiefly Streptococcus pyogenes (group A streptococcus), which may warrant antibiotics---especially in people at risk of acute rheumatic fever or when complications are present or likely. Related presentations include scarlet fever, glandular fever, and secondary bacterial complications such as peritonsillar abscess. In many countries, primary care guidelines emphasise symptomatic care, judicious antibiotics, and heightened strep management in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples and other groups with elevated rheumatic fever risk. This chapter explains causes, home care, when tests or antibiotics help, and emergency warning signs.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Acute pharyngitis peaks in childhood and winter--spring viral seasons. Group A strep is most often seen in school-age children and adolescents; it is uncommon under three years. Adults still experience strep throat but less frequently. Outbreaks occur in households, schools, and closed communities. Antibiotic prescribing for sore throat has historically exceeded true bacterial rates, contributing to resistance and side effects; stewardship aims to close that gap without missing high-risk patients. Viral co-circulation (influenza, RSV, COVID-19, adenovirus) shapes yearly patterns of presentations to GPs and emergency departments.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral invasion:} respiratory viruses infect pharyngeal epithelium, causing oedema, pain, and immune cell influx; antibiotics do not shorten uncomplicated viral illness
    \item \textbf{Group A streptococcus:} adheres to mucosa, provokes intense inflammation, and can release toxins (scarlet fever rash) or seed deeper tissues
    \item \textbf{Immune complications:} molecular mimicry links some strep strains to acute rheumatic fever; post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis follows certain strains
    \item \textbf{EBV pharyngotonsillitis:} marked lymphoid swelling, exudate, and fatigue; splenic enlargement risk
    \item \textbf{Mixed oral flora:} contribute to Vincent's angina and some ulcerative presentations in vulnerable hosts
    \item \textbf{Secondary abscess:} loculated pus in peritonsillar space after tonsillitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Painful throat and swallowing:} hallmark symptom
    \item \textbf{Fever, headache, and malaise:} systemic features
    \item \textbf{Tonsillar erythema, swelling, exudate:} seen in viral and bacterial disease
    \item \textbf{Tender anterior cervical nodes:} common in strep
    \item \textbf{Cough, runny nose, hoarseness, conjunctivitis:} favour viral cause when prominent
    \item \textbf{Scarlet fever rash:} fine sandpaper erythema with strep
    \item \textbf{Halitosis and coated tongue:} non-specific
    \item \textbf{Severe unilateral pain, trismus, uvular shift:} think quinsy
    \item \textbf{Prolonged fatigue and generalised nodes:} consider glandular fever
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral upper respiratory infection:} most common
    \item \textbf{Streptococcal pharyngitis:} fewer viral catarrhal features, tender nodes, fever, exudate---still imperfect clinically
    \item \textbf{Infectious mononucleosis:} adolescent/young adult, marked fatigue, hepatosplenomegaly risk
    \item \textbf{COVID-19, influenza, adenovirus:} testing during circulation
    \item \textbf{Primary HIV acute infection:} occasionally pharyngitis with rash and nodes
    \item \textbf{Epiglottitis and deep neck infection:} airway threat
    \item \textbf{Kawasaki disease and other paediatric systemic illnesses:} incomplete sore throat alone
    \item \textbf{Non-infective causes:} allergy, reflux, smoking, irritants, haematologic disease, chemotherapy mucositis
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a GP if sore throat is severe, lasts more than three to four days, prevents adequate fluid intake, or occurs with high fever, rash, or marked neck swelling. Children in high-risk groups for rheumatic fever need early assessment as per local protocols.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Breathing difficulty, stridor, drooling, or inability to swallow saliva
    \item Severe neck swelling, confusion, cold clammy skin, or non-blanching rash with illness
    \item Suspected anaphylaxis rather than infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical decision tools:} FeverPAIN or Centor scores support---not replace---judgement
    \item \textbf{Throat swab for culture or rapid molecular/antigen tests:} when antibiotics hang on the result or in high-risk rheumatic fever settings
    \item \textbf{EBV tests:} if mononucleosis suspected
    \item \textbf{Respiratory virus PCR:} when it changes isolation or antiviral decisions
    \item \textbf{Blood counts:} atypical lymphocytosis in EBV; marked neutrophilia in some bacterial cases
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} only if abscess or deep infection suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Analgesia and hydration:} first-line for almost everyone; soft cool foods if preferred
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics for confirmed or high-probability strep:} phenoxymethylpenicillin remains first-line for many; alternatives for penicillin allergy per guidelines; longer courses used in rheumatic fever prevention contexts as directed
    \item \textbf{Avoid amoxicillin if EBV likely:} maculopapular rash risk
    \item \textbf{Corticosteroids:} occasional adjunct for very severe swelling under clinician advice
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} IV fluids, airway observation, abscess drainage, and IV antibiotics for complications
    \item \textbf{Work and school:} stay home while febrile and unwell; follow strep clearance advice if given
    \item \textbf{Symptom relief products:} lozenges and sprays may soothe; they do not replace medical review for red flags
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Otitis media, sinusitis:} contiguous spread
    \item \textbf{Peritonsillar abscess:} needs drainage
    \item \textbf{Invasive group A strep disease:} uncommon but severe
    \item \textbf{Acute rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease:} preventable priority in high-risk people
    \item \textbf{Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis:} tea-coloured urine, oedema, hypertension days to weeks later
    \item \textbf{Dehydration and febrile convulsions:} paediatric concerns
    \item \textbf{Antibiotic adverse effects and resistance:} when antibiotics are unnecessary
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Uncomplicated viral throat infection improves within about a week. Strep throat symptoms often ease within a few days of appropriate antibiotics, though the main antibiotic benefit in low-risk populations is modest symptom shortening plus complication reduction in selected groups. Full recovery is expected in the vast majority. Recurrent infections deserve ENT discussion if criteria for tonsillectomy are approached.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hand hygiene and cough etiquette reduce spread
    \item Do not share utensils and drink bottles during illness
    \item Complete prescribed antibiotics when they are indicated
    \item Rest voice and throat; avoid smoke exposure
    \item Keep vaccinations current, including influenza where recommended
    \item Seek early care for sore throat if you live in or come from a community with high rheumatic fever rates, following local health pathways
    \item Replace toothbrushes after strep treatment if advised by your clinician
\end{itemize}
