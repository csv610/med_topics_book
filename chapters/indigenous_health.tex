\chapter{Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Health}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health is defined by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples themselves as not merely the absence of disease, but the social, emotional, and cultural wellbeing of the whole community. It encompasses physical, emotional, social, and cultural dimensions, and connection to land, family, and community. The health of Indigenous Australians is shaped by history, including colonisation and the Stolen Generations, and by contemporary social determinants such as housing, education, employment, and racism. This chapter provides an overview of the health status of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, the major conditions affecting them, and the approaches, services, and reforms aimed at closing the gap in health outcomes.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The life expectancy of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples is around eight to nine years shorter than that of non-Indigenous Australians. Rates of chronic disease, including heart disease, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and respiratory disease, are two to three times higher. Infant mortality, while improving, remains higher, and rates of ear disease, rheumatic heart disease, and hospitalisation for injury are markedly elevated. Suicide, substance use, and psychological distress disproportionately affect Indigenous communities. There have been genuine improvements: infant mortality has fallen, many cancers are detected and treated earlier, and smoking rates have declined substantially, particularly in remote areas. The gap persists, however, and closing it remains a national priority.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The drivers of poor health outcomes are overwhelmingly social and historical rather than biological. Colonisation led to dispossession, disruption of traditional food and lifestyle, and intergenerational trauma. Contemporary social determinants include poverty, unemployment, lower educational attainment, overcrowded and poor-quality housing, food insecurity, and experiences of racism and discrimination. These conditions increase exposure to risk factors such as smoking, alcohol, poor nutrition, and physical inactivity, and reduce access to timely and culturally appropriate care. Protective factors are powerful: strong cultural identity, connection to country, family and community support, and self-determination are associated with better health. Addressing health therefore requires action across health services, social policy, and community-led initiatives.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Higher prevalence of chronic conditions: heart disease, type 2 diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and chronic lung disease
    \item Greater severity and earlier onset of disease than in non-Indigenous populations
    \item High rates of infectious diseases, including skin infections, ear disease, and rheumatic fever
    \item High rates of mental health distress, self-harm, and suicide
    \item Risk factors such as smoking, high body weight, and physical inactivity
    \item Lower rates of participation in preventive care and cancer screening
    \item In children: ear disease, tooth decay, and respiratory infections
    \item Community strengths: family connection, cultural knowledge, and resilience
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Health assessment must consider the full range of physical, mental, and social contributors to illness.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chronic disease:} cardiac, renal, respiratory, and metabolic conditions that often coexist
    \item \textbf{Infectious disease:} skin, ear, respiratory, and rheumatic infections
    \item \textbf{Mental health and substance use:} often interrelated with physical illness
    \item \textbf{Injury and violence:} accidents, family violence, and self-harm
    \item \textbf{Social factors:} housing, food security, and access to care as contributors to presentation
    \item \textbf{Environmental exposures:} poor housing, overcrowding, and regional or remote service gaps
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
All Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people are encouraged to have regular health checks, including the annual Medicare-funded Indigenous health check, which allows early detection and prevention of chronic disease. Symptoms such as chest pain, breathlessness, coughing blood, unusual lumps, wounds that do not heal, and persistent distress should be assessed promptly. Pregnant women and young children need particular attention and early, regular care.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, sudden breathlessness, or collapse
    \item Signs of stroke: facial drooping, arm weakness, or difficulty speaking
    \item Severe bleeding, serious injury, or burns
    \item A child who is very unwell, difficult to wake, or struggling to breathe
    \item Immediate risk of suicide or harm to self or others
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Regular health checks:} the annual Indigenous health assessment covering physical, mental, and social health
    \item \textbf{Chronic disease screening:} blood pressure, blood glucose, cholesterol, and kidney function tests
    \item \textbf{Cancer screening:} bowel, breast, and cervical screening programs
    \item \textbf{Infectious disease surveillance:} testing and treatment for skin, ear, and respiratory infections, and for sexually transmissible infections
    \item \textbf{Child health assessments:} growth, development, hearing, and vision checks
    \item \textbf{Antenatal care:} early and regular pregnancy care with culturally safe services
    \item \textbf{Mental health assessment:} culturally safe exploration of distress, trauma, and wellbeing
    \item \textbf{Involvement of Aboriginal health workers:} to support engagement, communication, and follow-up
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Services (ACCHS):} community-governed services providing comprehensive, culturally safe primary care
    \item \textbf{Chronic disease management:} coordinated care plans, medicines, and self-management support through Medicare programs
    \item \textbf{Preventive care:} immunisation, cancer screening, and health promotion programs
    \item \textbf{Maternal and child health:} early antenatal care, child health checks, and support programs
    \item \textbf{Mental health and social and emotional wellbeing:} culturally safe counselling, community programs, and crisis support
    \item \textbf{Drug and alcohol services:} culturally appropriate treatment and support
    \item \textbf{Closing the Gap initiatives:} partnerships between governments and Indigenous organisations targeting health targets
    \item \textbf{Workforce development:} training and employment of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health professionals
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Premature death and disability from chronic disease
    \item Complications of diabetes, including kidney failure, amputations, and blindness
    \item Rheumatic heart disease following untreated streptococcal infections
    \item Chronic ear disease causing hearing loss and educational disadvantage
    \item End-stage kidney disease requiring dialysis, often far from home
    \item Suicide and self-harm
    \item Avoidable hospitalisations for conditions that good primary care can prevent
    \item Intergenerational impacts of poor health on children and families
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook is improving. Smoking rates among Indigenous Australians have fallen markedly, cancer survival is improving, and many chronic disease deaths have declined. Where culturally safe services are accessible, engagement and outcomes improve substantially, and community-controlled health organisations achieve strong results in immunisation, screening, and chronic disease care. Closing the life expectancy gap requires sustained action on social determinants and continued investment in community-led health services. With self-determination and partnership, there is genuine optimism for continued progress.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Have the annual Medicare Indigenous health check
    \item Quit smoking and limit alcohol
    \item Eat well and stay physically active, drawing on family and community support
    \item Attend all recommended screenings and immunisations
    \item Take medicines as prescribed for chronic conditions
    \item Access antenatal care early and attend child health checks
    \item Seek help early for new symptoms, injuries, or distress
    \item Strengthen cultural connection, which supports wellbeing
    \item Use Aboriginal Community Controlled Health Services where available
\end{itemize}
