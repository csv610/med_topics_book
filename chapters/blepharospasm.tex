\chapter{Blepharospasm}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Blepharospasm is a neurological condition in which the muscles around the eyes squeeze the eyelids shut involuntarily. It is a form of focal dystonia -- a disorder of muscle control affecting one body region -- in which the eyes may blink forcefully and repetitively, or remain squeezed closed for prolonged periods. In severe cases, people are functionally blind because they cannot open their eyes, even though the eyes themselves are perfectly healthy. Blepharospasm should not be confused with eyelid twitching (myokymia), which is usually brief, harmless, and confined to one lid.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Blepharospasm most often begins in middle age or later, typically between the ages of 40 and 70, and is roughly twice as common in women as in men. It is estimated to affect between 1 in 20,000 and 1 in 50,000 people, although mild cases are probably under-reported. A family history is present in a minority of cases, and some inherited forms are recognised. Symptoms usually begin gradually and worsen over the first few years before stabilising, occasionally progressing to involve the jaw, mouth, or neck muscles.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The precise cause of blepharospasm is not fully understood, but the condition is thought to arise from dysfunction of the basal ganglia, the deep brain structures that coordinate and suppress voluntary movement. In affected people, the orbicularis oculi muscles that close the eyelids contract excessively, while the levator muscles that open them are overwhelmed, producing sustained spasms. Most cases occur without an identifiable cause, but several contributing or triggering factors are recognised:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} a family history in a minority of cases, suggesting an inherited predisposition
    \item \textbf{Ocular irritation:} dry eyes, bright light, wind, or eye strain, which can precipitate or worsen spasms
    \item \textbf{Fatigue, stress, and anxiety:} which lower the threshold for spasms
    \item \textbf{Medication side effects:} certain dopamine-blocking drugs can rarely cause it
    \item \textbf{Associated dystonias:} it frequently coexists with oromandibular dystonia and cervical dystonia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The presentation evolves over months to years, and the features are distinctive:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Increased blinking that begins as an occasional annoyance
    \item Forceful, involuntary closing of both eyelids
    \item Spasms that come and go but may become prolonged or near-constant
    \item Difficulty keeping the eyes open in bright light or while driving
    \item Temporary relief from sensory tricks, such as touching the face, humming, or looking downwards
    \item Functional blindness -- an inability to see while the lids are closed, despite normal eyes
    \item Symptoms that improve with relaxation and worsen under stress
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Several conditions produce similar symptoms and must be distinguished from blepharospasm:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Eyelid myokymia: common, brief, harmless twitching of one lid
    \item Hemifacial spasm: one-sided facial spasms, often caused by a blood vessel pressing on the facial nerve
    \item Ptosis: drooping of the lid from levator weakness, without spasm
    \item Apraxia of eyelid opening: difficulty opening the eyes without forceful spasm
    \item Reflexive blinking from dry eye, conjunctivitis, or corneal irritation
    \item Tics or habit spasms, which are suppressible and more variable
    \item Rarely, Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, or other neurological disorders
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if eyelid spasms are frequent, affect both sides, or interfere with everyday activities such as driving, reading, and working. Seek prompt attention if the picture changes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Spasms spread to other parts of the face or body
    \item The eye becomes red, painful, or swollen
    \item Weakness or drooping of the face develops
    \item Loss of vision occurs that is not simply because the lids are closed
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden loss of vision
    \item Sudden facial weakness or drooping, which may indicate a stroke
    \item Difficulty breathing or swallowing occurring with spasms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Blepharospasm is diagnosed clinically, and there is no definitive test. The clinician takes a careful history, examines the eyes, lids, and face, and often observes the spasms directly. Investigations are used mainly to exclude other causes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Eye examination:} slit-lamp assessment for dry eye, conjunctivitis, or corneal surface disease
    \item \textbf{Neurological examination:} assessment of the face, cranial nerves, and limbs for features of other movement disorders
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} in selected cases, to look for other neurological or metabolic disease
    \item \textbf{MRI of the brain:} mainly when symptoms are atypical, one-sided, or rapidly progressive
    \item \textbf{Electromyography (EMG):} used in some specialist centres to confirm the pattern of muscle activation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment aims to reduce the frequency and severity of spasms and to preserve function. The most effective option is:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Botulinum toxin injections:} small doses injected into the eyelid muscles every three to four months weaken the overactive muscles; most people experience significant improvement
    \item \textbf{Lubricating eye drops:} treating coexisting dry eye, which aggravates spasms
    \item \textbf{Spectacles:} dark, tinted, or prismatic lenses help some people, particularly outdoors
    \item \textbf{Oral medication:} drugs such as clonazepam or baclofen help a minority but are often limited by drowsiness
    \item \textbf{Therapy:} occupational therapy, stress management, and relaxation techniques to maintain independence
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} myectomy (partial removal of eyelid muscle) for severe cases that do not respond to injections
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Functional blindness during prolonged spasms, making driving, walking, and work unsafe
    \item Falls and injuries resulting from impaired vision
    \item Social withdrawal, embarrassment, and difficulty maintaining employment
    \item Depression and reduced quality of life
    \item Side effects of treatment, including temporary lid drooping, double vision, or tearing after injections
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Blepharospasm is a chronic condition that rarely disappears completely on its own. Most people, however, respond well to botulinum toxin injections, with each treatment lasting several weeks to a few months, and the treatment can be repeated indefinitely. With regular treatment, most affected people maintain normal sight and daily function. A small proportion develop more widespread dystonia over time, and research into the underlying mechanisms and new treatments continues.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wear dark or tinted glasses outdoors to reduce bright-light triggers
    \item Keep the eyes lubricated with artificial tear drops
    \item Get adequate sleep and manage stress, both of which influence spasm severity
    \item Take regular breaks from screens and close work
    \item Follow the schedule of botulinum toxin injections, since the effect wears off between sessions
    \item Seek psychological support or peer groups if the condition is distressing
    \item Tell your doctor about any new medicines, as some drugs can trigger or worsen spasms
\end{itemize}
