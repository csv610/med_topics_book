\chapter{Ibuprofen}
\index{Ibuprofen}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Ibuprofen is a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) used widely to relieve pain, reduce inflammation, and lower fever. It belongs to the same family of medicines as aspirin and naproxen and works by reducing the production of prostaglandins, chemical messengers that promote pain, swelling, and heat. Ibuprofen is available over the counter in pharmacies and supermarkets for short-term use, and in higher strengths by prescription. It is used to treat a wide range of conditions, including headaches, dental pain, menstrual cramps, muscle and joint injuries, arthritis, and cold and flu symptoms. While it is an effective and generally safe medicine when used correctly, it carries important risks, particularly for the stomach, kidneys, and cardiovascular system, that should be considered before use.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Ibuprofen is one of the most commonly used medicines in many countries and worldwide, with millions of doses purchased each year without a prescription. Surveys suggest that NSAIDs as a class are used by around one-third of adults in any given year, most commonly for headache, back pain, arthritis, and acute injuries. Older adults are more likely to use them long term for chronic pain, which increases the risk of adverse effects. Despite its widespread availability, many users are unaware of the interactions and risks of the medicine, and hospital admissions for gastrointestinal bleeding and kidney injury related to NSAID use remain a significant and largely preventable problem, particularly in older people and those taking other medicines.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Ibuprofen exerts its effects by inhibiting cyclo-oxygenase (COX) enzymes, which convert arachidonic acid into prostaglandins. Prostaglandins serve many functions: in injured tissue they sensitise pain receptors and promote inflammation, and in the stomach lining they help maintain the protective mucus barrier and regulate blood flow. The COX-1 isoform is particularly important for protecting the stomach and supporting platelet function and kidney blood flow, while COX-2 is induced mainly at sites of inflammation. Ibuprofen inhibits both isoforms to varying degrees, which explains both its therapeutic benefits and its main adverse effects. At typical doses it is a relatively balanced inhibitor; higher doses and prolonged use increase the likelihood of gastrointestinal, renal, and cardiovascular harm.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain relief in headache, migraine, dental pain, menstrual pain, and musculoskeletal injury
    \item Reduction of fever in viral and other infections
    \item Decrease of swelling and stiffness in arthritis, sprains, and strains
    \item Onset of action within 30--60 minutes, with effects lasting 4--6 hours
    \item Adverse effects may include indigestion, nausea, heartburn, and abdominal discomfort
    \item More serious effects include gastrointestinal bleeding, worsening of asthma, kidney impairment, and fluid retention
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The decision to use ibuprofen is straightforward for most people, but alternatives should be considered in specific situations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Paracetamol:} better first-line choice for simple pain or fever in people at risk of stomach or kidney problems, and in pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Other NSAIDs:} diclofenac, naproxen, and meloxicam have different potency and risk profiles
    \item \textbf{Aspirin:} used at low dose for cardiovascular protection, but higher doses cause similar stomach risks
    \item \textbf{Codeine or tramadol:} reserved for pain not controlled by simple analgesics, with their own side effects and dependence risks
    \item \textbf{Specialist treatments:} disease-modifying medicines or corticosteroids for inflammatory arthritis
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor before using ibuprofen if you have a history of stomach ulcers or bleeding, kidney or liver disease, heart disease, high blood pressure, or asthma, or if you are pregnant, planning pregnancy, or breastfeeding. Seek medical advice if you need ibuprofen regularly or for more than a few days, or if you take other medicines that could interact with it, including blood thinners, other NSAIDs, and some blood pressure and heart medicines.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vomiting blood or material resembling coffee grounds, or black, tarry stools (possible gastrointestinal bleeding)
    \item Severe abdominal pain, especially after taking large doses
    \item Difficulty breathing, wheezing, or swelling of the face and lips after a dose
    \item Sudden chest pain, weakness on one side, or difficulty speaking
    \item Signs of a severe allergic reaction with rash, itching, and dizziness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Investigations are needed mainly when ibuprofen has caused or may worsen an underlying problem.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} review of the reason for use, dose, duration, and risk factors
    \item \textbf{Kidney function tests:} urea, creatinine, and electrolytes if there is concern about renal impairment or dehydration
    \item \textbf{Liver function tests:} if there is liver disease or prolonged high-dose use
    \item \textbf{Blood count:} to look for anaemia in the setting of suspected gastrointestinal bleeding
    \item \textbf{Faecal occult blood testing or endoscopy:} if chronic bleeding is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest time:} this is the single most important principle of safe NSAID use
    \item \textbf{Take with food or milk:} reduces stomach irritation, though it does not eliminate ulcer risk
    \item \textbf{Standard dosing:} 200--400 mg every 6--8 hours for adults, with a maximum of 1,200 mg per day without medical supervision
    \item \textbf{Stop if warning symptoms develop:} indigestion, heartburn, or abdominal pain should prompt review
    \item \textbf{Alternative pain management:} heat or cold therapy, rest, physiotherapy, and paracetamol where appropriate
    \item \textbf{Gastroprotection:} a proton pump inhibitor may be prescribed alongside long-term NSAIDs in high-risk patients
    \item \textbf{Avoid combining with other NSAIDs:} including those in cold and flu products
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Gastrointestinal irritation, ulceration, and bleeding, especially in older adults and with long-term use
    \item Kidney impairment, particularly in dehydration, heart failure, and pre-existing renal disease
    \item Fluid retention and worsening of high blood pressure and heart failure
    \item Increased cardiovascular risk with prolonged high-dose use
    \item Worsening of asthma in sensitive individuals
    \item Risk of bleeding when combined with warfarin, other anticoagulants, or antiplatelet medicines
    \item Risk of harm to the unborn baby if used in late pregnancy, and of complications in early pregnancy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For most people, ibuprofen provides safe and effective short-term relief of pain and fever, and symptoms settle as the underlying condition resolves. The main risks relate to prolonged use, high doses, older age, and coexisting medical conditions. When used carefully under medical supervision, the benefits usually outweigh the risks, but anyone needing regular pain relief should be assessed for the underlying cause rather than relying on ongoing self-medication.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Read the label carefully and never exceed the recommended dose or duration
    \item Use the lowest dose that controls symptoms, and stop as soon as possible
    \item Check the ingredients of combination cold and flu products to avoid accidental double dosing
    \item Stay well hydrated when taking ibuprofen, particularly in hot weather or with exercise
    \item Avoid alcohol, which increases the risk of stomach irritation
    \item Keep a record of all medicines and review them with a pharmacist or doctor annually
    \item Seek medical advice before use in pregnancy, in older age, or with chronic disease
\end{itemize}
