\chapter{Food Poisoning}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Food poisoning, also called foodborne illness, is illness caused by eating food or drinking water contaminated with bacteria, viruses, parasites, or their toxins. It is extremely common: most people have experienced it at some point, usually as vomiting and diarrhoea that resolve within a few days. The most common causes in Australia include campylobacter, salmonella, and listeria from contaminated food, and norovirus from infected food handlers. Most cases are managed at home with fluids and rest, but some require medical care, particularly in young children, older adults, pregnant women, and people with weakened immunity, and a small number of bacteria, such as listeria, are dangerous in pregnancy. This chapter explains the causes, symptoms, and management of food poisoning.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Foodborne illness affects an estimated 4 million Australians each year, around one in five people, causing thousands of hospitalisations and over a hundred deaths. Campylobacter and salmonella are the leading bacterial causes, largely from undercooked poultry and eggs, while norovirus is the most common viral cause, spreading rapidly in communities and institutions. Outbreaks are frequently traced to shared meals, restaurants, and events. Rates rise in summer when food is more likely to be mishandled. The majority of cases are mild and unreported, so the true burden is far larger than notified figures suggest.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bacteria:} campylobacter and salmonella (poultry, eggs, meat), listeria (deli meats, soft cheeses, unpasteurised dairy), E.\ coli (undercooked beef, contaminated produce), and staphylococcus and bacillus (prepared foods left warm)
    \item \textbf{Viruses:} norovirus, spread by infected food handlers and contaminated food and water; hepatitis A from contaminated produce and shellfish
    \item \textbf{Parasites:} giardia, cryptosporidium, and others from contaminated water and food
    \item \textbf{Toxins:} some bacteria release toxins in food before it is eaten (staphylococcal, bacillus), causing rapid onset vomiting
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} ingested organisms multiply in the gut, damage the bowel lining, and produce inflammation, causing vomiting and diarrhoea; some produce toxins that act quickly
    \item \textbf{Incubation:} symptoms can appear from 1 hour (toxins) to days (campylobacter, listeria) after eating
    \item \textbf{Dehydration:} the main danger is fluid and electrolyte loss from vomiting and diarrhoea
    \item \textbf{Who is at risk:} young children, older adults, pregnant women, and immunocompromised people are most vulnerable to severe illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Nausea and vomiting, often sudden in onset
    \item Diarrhoea, which may be watery or contain blood and mucus
    \item Abdominal cramps and pain
    \item Fever, headache, and muscle aches
    \item Feeling generally unwell and lacking energy
    \item Dehydration: thirst, dry mouth, reduced urine, dizziness, and dark urine
    \item The time of onset after a shared meal helps identify the cause
    \item Most cases resolve within 24--72 hours, though some bacteria cause longer or more severe illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Not all vomiting and diarrhoea is food poisoning.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Viral gastroenteritis, which is similar and often spread person to person
    \item Influenza and other infections with gut symptoms
    \item Appendicitis and other acute abdominal conditions, which can begin with vomiting and pain
    \item Irritable bowel syndrome and inflammatory bowel disease, with chronic or recurring symptoms
    \item Medication side effects
    \item Coeliac disease and food intolerance
    \item The severe or persistent cases need assessment to exclude these
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most food poisoning resolves at home, but medical care is needed for persistent vomiting, signs of dehydration, blood in the stool, high fever, severe pain, or illness in a vulnerable person.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Blood in vomit or stool, or black stools
    \item Severe abdominal pain, especially with fever and rigidity
    \item Signs of severe dehydration: no urine for 8 hours, confusion, dizziness on standing, or collapse
    \item Difficulty breathing, which may indicate a severe reaction
    \item Illness in a baby, pregnant woman, or immunocompromised person who is severely unwell
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the food eaten, shared meals, timing, and symptoms
    \item \textbf{Stool tests:} culture and PCR identify bacteria, viruses, and parasites; used in severe, persistent, or outbreak cases
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} for dehydration, kidney function, and infection markers in severe cases
    \item \textbf{Notification:} certain organisms, such as salmonella and listeria, are notifiable, and laboratories report them to health authorities
    \item \textbf{Outbreak investigation:} public health teams trace shared meals to identify and control sources
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fluids:} the mainstay; oral rehydration solution is best, with water, and for children, rehydration fluids designed for them
    \item \textbf{Rest:} the gut needs time to recover; eat when appetite returns, starting with bland foods
    \item \textbf{Avoid anti-diarrhoeal medicines:} generally not recommended, as they can prolong some infections
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} only for specific bacterial infections, such as severe salmonella or campylobacter, and not for viral illness
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} intravenous fluids for severe dehydration
    \item \textbf{Special cases:} listeria in pregnancy requires urgent antibiotics; babies and older adults may need hospital review
    \item \textbf{Prevent spread:} good hand hygiene, and staying home from work, school, and food handling until symptoms settle
    \item \textbf{Report:} tell a doctor if the illness may have come from a restaurant or shared event
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dehydration, the most common serious complication, especially in children and older adults
    \item Kidney injury from severe dehydration
    \item Specific bacterial complications: reactive arthritis after campylobacter and salmonella, and haemolytic uraemic syndrome after some E.\ coli strains
    \item Listeria infection in pregnancy, which can harm the baby
    \item Spread to others, including in households and institutions
    \item Rarely, long-term gut symptoms after the infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The vast majority of people with food poisoning recover fully within a few days with fluids and rest, and serious complications are uncommon in healthy people. Outcomes are good even in severe cases with prompt medical care, especially for the vulnerable groups. The best approach is prevention: proper cooking, refrigeration, and hygiene prevent nearly all food poisoning. When illness does occur, early rehydration is the treatment that matters most.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cook meat, poultry, and eggs thoroughly, and use a thermometer for large cuts
    \item Refrigerate perishable food promptly and keep the fridge below 5 degrees
    \item Wash hands before preparing food and after handling raw meat
    \item Separate raw and cooked foods, and use separate boards and utensils
    \item Wash fruit and vegetables before eating
    \item Avoid unpasteurised dairy, undercooked eggs, and high-risk foods in pregnancy
    \item Discard food left out for more than 2 hours, or 1 hour in hot weather
    \item During illness, rest, rehydrate, and stay home until 48 hours after symptoms settle
\end{itemize}
