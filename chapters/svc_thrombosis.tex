\section*{Definition and Overview}
Superior vena cava (SVC) thrombosis is the formation of a blood clot, or thrombus, within the superior vena cava, the large vein that returns deoxygenated blood from the head, neck, arms, and upper chest to the right side of the heart. Because this vein drains such a large area of the body, a blockage within it can interfere seriously with the return of blood to the heart. The word "thrombosis" refers to the clot forming where it is found, distinguishing this condition from an embolus, which travels to the vessel from elsewhere. SVC thrombosis most often develops in people who already have a central venous catheter or pacemaker lead in place, or who have cancer involving the chest, and it is much less common in otherwise healthy people. In mild cases the body may form collateral veins that reroute blood around the blockage and produce few or no symptoms; in severe cases the pressure that builds up can cause facial swelling, arm swelling, headache, and shortness of breath, a picture traditionally called superior vena cava syndrome. This chapter explains what SVC thrombosis is, who is at risk, how it is recognised, how it is diagnosed and treated, and when it requires urgent care, written in plain language for patients, families, and students. Early recognition matters because SVC thrombosis is treatable and because some of its complications, such as a clot breaking away to the lungs, can be life-threatening.

\section*{Epidemiology}
SVC thrombosis is uncommon in the general population but is increasingly seen in hospital settings because of the growing use of central venous devices. People with cancer are most at risk, and among patients with malignancy and an indwelling catheter the reported rates of catheter-related thrombosis vary widely, depending on the type of device and how carefully thrombosis is sought. Lung cancer and lymphoma are the tumours most often associated with SVC obstruction, because of their position in or near the chest. Most cases now occur as a complication of medical care rather than as a spontaneous event: long-term central lines used for chemotherapy, haemodialysis, parenteral nutrition, and intensive care are the leading risk factor, and the risk rises the longer the line stays in place. Pacemaker and defibrillator leads are another important cause. SVC thrombosis can affect adults of any age and occasionally occurs in children, particularly those with long-term lines for chronic illness. It is seen equally in men and women when device-related, while the malignancy-related form follows the pattern of the underlying tumour. Because many people with an SVC clot have few symptoms and the clot is found incidentally on scanning, the true frequency is almost certainly higher than the number of diagnosed cases suggests.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Blood is kept fluid by a careful balance between factors that promote clotting and factors that prevent it. Three elements, sometimes called Virchow's triad, favour the formation of a clot in a vein: damage to the inner lining of the vessel, sluggish or stagnant blood flow, and blood that is abnormally prone to clotting. In SVC thrombosis all three are commonly at work together.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Central venous catheters:} a catheter irritates and injures the inner lining of the vein, disturbs the normal flow of blood around it, and may be coated in clot; this is the most common cause
    \item \textbf{Cancer:} tumours in or near the chest can compress the SVC and slow blood flow, while cancer itself and some chemotherapy drugs make the blood more prone to clotting
    \item \textbf{Cardiac devices:} pacemaker and defibrillator leads lie within the vein and act much like a catheter in causing injury and turbulence
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} radiation to the chest can injure the vein wall and surrounding tissues, leading to narrowing and scarring over years
    \item \textbf{Inherited and acquired clotting disorders:} conditions such as antiphospholipid syndrome or inherited thrombophilias raise the risk of unusual-site thrombosis
    \item \textbf{General thrombophilia:} dehydration, immobility, infection, and surgery all increase clot risk in unwell patients
\end{itemize}

The consequence of the clot is a rise in pressure in the veins that drain into the SVC, so blood pools in the veins of the face, neck, and arms and they swell. The body tries to compensate by opening collateral channels, extra veins that carry blood around the blockage; when these are sufficient the patient may have no symptoms at all, but when the clot forms quickly or the collaterals are overwhelmed, symptoms develop. The speed of onset is therefore important: an acute clot causes sudden symptoms, while a slowly narrowing vein gives the body time to adapt.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms of SVC thrombosis range from none to severe and depend on how fast the blockage develops and how complete it is.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Swelling of the face and neck:} the most typical sign, often worse in the morning or after lying down
    \item \textbf{Swelling of the arms and hands:} especially on the side of a catheter or pacemaker, sometimes with the rings feeling tight
    \item \textbf{Feeling of fullness in the head:} a sensation of pressure or a dull headache, worse when bending forward or lying flat
    \item \textbf{Visible veins on the chest:} prominent dilated veins across the upper chest and shoulders as collateral channels enlarge
    \item \textbf{Shortness of breath:} often mild at first and worse with exertion or when lying down
    \item \textbf{Catheter problems:} a line that stops drawing blood or infuses poorly is a common first clue in hospital
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of SVC thrombosis can be produced by several other conditions, and telling them apart usually requires imaging.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Superior vena cava syndrome from compression alone:} a tumour or enlarged lymph node can compress the vein without a clot; the distinction is made on ultrasound or CT and matters because the treatment differs
    \item \textbf{Lymphoedema of the arm:} swelling after breast cancer surgery or axillary radiotherapy is chronic and does not involve the face
    \item \textbf{Heart failure:} fluid retention causes leg and ankle swelling and breathlessness, but typically with fluid in the lungs rather than isolated upper-body swelling
    \item \textbf{Allergic reactions:} facial swelling in allergy is often itchy, comes on suddenly, and may involve hives, unlike the gradual venous swelling of SVC thrombosis
    \item \textbf{Cellulitis:} a skin infection causes localised redness, warmth, and pain rather than generalised upper-body swelling
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with a central line, pacemaker, or chest cancer who develops facial or arm swelling, headache, or new breathlessness should be reviewed promptly. People already being treated for a clot should seek review if symptoms worsen despite treatment, if fever develops, or if the arm becomes pale, cold, or very painful. Any new swelling in a person with known cancer should not be assumed to be benign.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe shortness of breath or difficulty breathing
    \item Sudden chest pain, coughing up blood, or a rapid heart rate, which may signal a clot that has travelled to the lungs
    \item Collapse, fainting, or loss of consciousness
    \item Sudden confusion or altered level of awareness
    \item Rapidly worsening facial swelling with a sensation of the throat closing or difficulty swallowing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is based on the history, the examination, and imaging that shows the vein and any clot within it.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical history and examination:} the doctor asks about devices, cancer, radiotherapy, and symptoms, and looks for facial and arm swelling and dilated chest veins
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound with Doppler:} quick and radiation-free, it can show the jugular and subclavian veins, although it sees the chest portion of the SVC poorly because of the ribs and lungs
    \item \textbf{CT venography:} a contrast CT of the chest is the best single test, showing the clot, its extent, any compression by a tumour, and the state of the collateral veins
    \item \textbf{Magnetic resonance venography:} an alternative that avoids radiation, useful when CT contrast is not safe
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} a full blood count, kidney and liver function, and clotting tests help identify contributing illness and guide anticoagulant dosing
    \item \textbf{D-dimer:} a clot marker that is sensitive but not specific, so it is used cautiously when the diagnosis is unclear
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} oncology, haematology, or vascular medicine input is usually needed to plan treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment aims to stop the clot growing, reduce symptoms, restore flow where possible, and treat any underlying cause.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anticoagulation:} blood-thinning medication, most often a low-molecular-weight heparin or a newer oral anticoagulant, is the mainstay of treatment for catheter-related SVC thrombosis and is generally continued for several months
    \item \textbf{Anticoagulation for cancer patients:} cancer-associated clots are usually treated with low-molecular-weight heparin or a direct oral anticoagulant chosen with the oncology team
    \item \textbf{Catheter management:} if the clot relates to a central line, the team decides whether to remove it, keep it with anticoagulation, or exchange it
    \item \textbf{Thrombolysis:} clot-dissolving drugs may be used for severe, sudden, or life-threatening SVC syndrome, balanced against the risk of bleeding
    \item \textbf{Endovascular procedures:} a specialist may use catheter-directed techniques or a stent to open a narrowed or blocked SVC, particularly when a tumour is compressing the vein
    \item \textbf{Treatment of the underlying cancer:} for malignancy-related cases, treating the tumour with chemotherapy or radiotherapy is often the most effective way to relieve SVC obstruction
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} elevating the head of the bed and sitting upright ease facial swelling while definitive treatment works
    \item \textbf{Graded compression and arm care:} limb elevation and gentle exercise help arm swelling settle
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pulmonary embolism:} a piece of clot can break off and travel to the lungs, which may cause breathlessness, chest pain, or collapse and is the most feared complication
    \item \textbf{Post-thrombotic syndrome:} chronic swelling, heaviness, and skin changes in the affected arm can persist after the clot resolves
    \item \textbf{Recurrent thrombosis:} without adequate treatment or control of the cause, the clot can reform, especially if a catheter stays in place
    \item \textbf{Catheter-related infection:} thrombus provides a surface for bacteria, and line infections are more likely when clot is present
    \item \textbf{Bleeding from treatment:} anticoagulation and thrombolysis carry their own bleeding risks, which must be weighed against the benefit
    \item \textbf{Severe SVC syndrome:} increased pressure in the head can rarely cause headache, blurred vision, or swelling of the brain, and airway swelling can threaten breathing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for SVC thrombosis depends largely on the underlying cause. When the clot relates to a catheter or device in a person who is otherwise well, the prognosis is very good: with anticoagulation the clot usually shrinks or stabilises, symptoms settle over days to weeks, and most people return to their previous activities without lasting disability. The main remaining concerns are recurrence if the device stays in and chronic swelling in a minority. When the cause is cancer, the outcome is governed chiefly by the tumour and its response to treatment, and SVC thrombosis is treated as part of overall cancer care rather than as an isolated event. In acute, severe SVC syndrome, modern treatment with thrombolysis or stenting usually relieves symptoms quickly, and death directly from the clot is now uncommon. Residual arm swelling can be managed with elevation, compression, and physiotherapy. Overall, with early recognition and appropriate specialist treatment, most people make a good functional recovery, and the emphasis of care shifts to preventing recurrence and managing the underlying illness.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discuss whether a central line is still needed; the longer a line stays, the higher the risk, so lines should be removed as soon as they are no longer required
    \item Keep mobile after surgery or illness and stay well hydrated to reduce general clotting risk
    \item If you have a central line or pacemaker, watch for arm or facial swelling and report it early rather than waiting for it to resolve
    \item Follow anticoagulant treatment exactly as prescribed, and carry a medication list in case of emergency
    \item Report any signs of a bleeding complication, such as unusual bruising, blood in the urine or stool, or prolonged bleeding from cuts
    \item Protect the affected arm from injury, sunburn, insect bites, and tight jewellery, and keep it elevated when possible
    \item Attend scheduled scans and specialist reviews so that recurrence is caught early
\end{itemize}
