\section*{Definition and Overview}
Tendinosis is the medical term for a specific type of chronic tendon disease in which the tendon degenerates over time without the classic signs of inflammation. Where acute tendonitis is thought of as an inflamed, recently injured tendon, tendinosis is the long-term, wear-and-tear problem that develops in a tendon subjected to years of repetitive load or overuse, or that fails to heal properly after repeated episodes of injury. The word "osis" means a state of degeneration, and under the microscope a tendinosis tendon shows disorganised and frayed collagen fibres, increased numbers of tendon cells that have lost their normal orderly alignment, areas of scar tissue, and sometimes tiny tears or even small calcium deposits, but no significant inflammatory cells. This distinction matters because it changes treatment: anti-inflammatory medicines, which are helpful for acute inflammation, do little for tendinosis, and the treatments that do help, such as graded strengthening exercises, work by stimulating the tendon to rebuild itself. Tendinosis commonly affects the same tendons that trouble active people, including the Achilles tendon, the patellar tendon, the rotator cuff tendons of the shoulder, and the tendons of the outer elbow and wrist, and it is especially common in people in their forties and older. This chapter explains how tendinosis develops, how it is recognised and imaged, why it can be so persistent, and how modern rehabilitation approaches can restore comfort and strength.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Tendinosis is a common finding, and its frequency rises steadily with age as tendons accumulate years of load and repair. Imaging studies of pain-free shoulders and Achilles tendons in older adults frequently show changes of tendinosis, which is an important reminder that the tissue change and the symptom of pain do not always line up exactly. Among people who do seek treatment for chronic tendon pain, tendinosis is the usual underlying pathology, particularly in those over thirty-five to forty. It affects men and women in roughly similar numbers, although particular sites are more common in particular groups: patellar tendinosis in jumping athletes, Achilles tendinosis in runners and in people whose work or sport involves a lot of walking, rotator cuff tendinosis in those doing overhead work, and wrist and elbow tendinosis in people with repetitive hand use. Occupations and sports that involve years of repetitive loading of a particular tendon are the clearest risk pattern, and the burden is significant because tendinosis tends to be slow to recover and prone to recurrence.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The development of tendinosis is best understood as a failure of the normal balance between tendon damage and tendon repair. Healthy tendon is constantly remodelled: minor damage from everyday loading is repaired by tendon cells that lay down new, organised collagen. In tendinosis, this balance shifts so that damage outpaces repair over months or years.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chronic overload:} years of repetitive or heavy loading of a tendon, as in running, jumping, throwing, or manual work, outpaces the tendon's capacity to repair
    \item \textbf{Repeated micro-injury:} small, subclinical tears accumulate and heal imperfectly, leaving disorganised scar tissue that is weaker than normal tendon
    \item \textbf{Age-related change:} tendon cells become less active and collagen becomes stiffer and more prone to damage with age
    \item \textbf{Failed healing after acute injury:} a tendon that is not rehabilitated properly after tendonitis or a strain can lapse into chronic degeneration
    \item \textbf{Reduced blood supply:} tendons are relatively poorly supplied with blood, and the areas of greatest wear often coincide with the zones of poorest blood flow, limiting repair
    \item \textbf{Biomechanical and muscle factors:} weakness, poor technique, and altered movement patterns place abnormal loads on the tendon
    \item \textbf{Metabolic conditions:} diabetes, high cholesterol, and gout are associated with more severe tendon degeneration, and some medicines, including quinolone antibiotics and repeated corticosteroid injections, can damage tendons
\end{itemize}

The result is a tendon that is thicker, weaker, and less elastic than normal, with areas of degeneration and sometimes micro-tears, but without the white blood cells and fluid of true inflammation. This is why the tendon looks "degenerated" rather than "inflamed", and why treatments aimed purely at inflammation often disappoint.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chronic pain:} pain that has been present for weeks, months, or years, and that comes and goes with activity
    \item \textbf{Pain with loading:} discomfort when the tendon works hard, such as pushing off the ground with the Achilles, landing on the patellar tendon, or lifting overhead
    \item \textbf{Morning and start-up pain:} soreness and stiffness early in the day and after rest, which improves with a few minutes of movement
    \item \textbf{Tenderness and thickening:} the tendon feels tender and often firmer or thicker than the other side, sometimes with a localised lump or nodule
    \item \textbf{Delayed pain:} pain that is mild during activity but worse the next morning or later in the day
    \item \textbf{Stiffness:} reduced flexibility of the tendon and the muscle attached to it
    \item \textbf{Variable disability:} some people are barely troubled, while others have pain that limits sport, work, stairs, or reaching
    \item \textbf{Absence of acute features:} there is generally no redness, heat, or sudden swelling, which distinguishes it from acute tendonitis
\end{itemize}

Because the tissue change can be silent, it is possible to have significant tendinosis on imaging with little or no pain, and conversely to have severe pain with only modest imaging changes.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute tendonitis:} recent-onset pain with swelling and tenderness over the tendon, more inflammatory in nature; the two can coexist, with an acute flare on top of chronic tendinosis
    \item \textbf{Tendon tear or partial tear:} a sudden pop, bruising, and weakness suggest a tear, and imaging distinguishes it from degeneration alone
    \item \textbf{Tendinopathy (general term):} the umbrella term for all tendon pain; tendinosis is one subtype, so the terms overlap, and the clinical label often depends on duration and imaging
    \item \textbf{Bursitis:} inflammation of nearby bursae causes similar pain with swelling over the point of the joint
    \item \textbf{Arthritis:} joint pain and stiffness from osteoarthritis or inflammatory arthritis can be confused with tendon pain, especially around the shoulder and hip
    \item \textbf{Nerve entrapment:} conditions such as carpal tunnel syndrome or ulnar nerve problems cause tingling and referred pain that can mimic tendon complaints
    \item \textbf{Calcium deposits in the tendon:} calcific tendinopathy can be intensely painful and is seen on imaging as distinct deposits rather than diffuse degeneration
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Because tendinosis develops gradually, people often live with it for a long time before seeking help, and an early consultation can shorten that road.

Seek medical advice if you have:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Tendon pain that has lasted more than a few weeks despite rest and sensible self-care
    \item Pain that is getting worse, or that now limits sport, work, or everyday activities
    \item A tender lump or thickening in a tendon that is new or growing
    \item Sudden weakness or a popping sensation in a tendon
    \item Pain in a person with diabetes, gout, or another condition that affects tendons
    \item Pain that is not confined to the tendon, or that comes with swelling, redness, or fever
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services or seek urgent care for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden inability to stand on tiptoe after a pop in the calf or heel, which suggests an Achilles rupture
    \item Severe pain with rapidly spreading redness, swelling, and fever, which can indicate infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Tendinosis is diagnosed from the history of chronic, load-related pain, and imaging confirms the tissue change and excludes other problems.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the duration and pattern of pain, the aggravating activities, the training or work history, and any past tendon injury or steroid injections
    \item \textbf{Examination:} feeling the tendon for tenderness and thickening, and testing the strength and loading capacity of the muscle with resisted and weight-bearing movements
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} the standard imaging test, showing a thickened tendon with disorganised, hypoechoic (darker) areas, and sometimes micro-tears or calcium
    \item \textbf{MRI:} provides detailed structural images and is used when ultrasound is unclear, when a partial tear is suspected, or when surgery is being considered
    \item \textbf{Doppler ultrasound:} shows blood flow within the tendon, which is often increased in abnormal, painful areas
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} only occasionally helpful, to check for gout, inflammatory disease, or metabolic contributors
    \item \textbf{Referral:} persistent cases benefit from a sports medicine physician or a physiotherapist experienced in tendon rehabilitation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The core of tendinosis management is loading the tendon in a way that stimulates repair, along with addressing the factors that caused the overload. It is a slow process and requires patience.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Education:} explaining that tendinosis is a degeneration-and-repair problem, not acute inflammation, and that recovery takes months, not weeks
    \item \textbf{Relative rest:} reduce the aggravating activity to a level that allows pain to settle, without complete rest, which further weakens the tendon
    \item \textbf{Isometric exercises:} pain-free static holds of the affected muscle can reduce pain quickly and are a useful starting point
    \item \textbf{Progressive strengthening:} a graduated program of eccentric and concentric exercises, ideally guided by a physiotherapist, that gradually increases load and rebuilds the tendon
    \item \textbf{Heavy slow resistance training:} higher-weight, slow-speed training under guidance, which is well supported by evidence for many tendinoses
    \item \textbf{Manual therapy and massage:} can help surrounding muscles and stiffness, though they do not replace the strengthening program
    \item \textbf{Shockwave therapy:} extracorporeal shockwave treatment has evidence for some chronic tendinoses, particularly the Achilles and elbow, when exercise alone has not worked
    \item \textbf{Avoid routine corticosteroid injections:} they give short-term pain relief but do not repair the tendon and can weaken it; their role is limited
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} reserved for people who fail a full, supervised rehabilitation program, and performed only after imaging confirms the site and nature of the problem
    \item \textbf{Address contributing factors:} correct muscle weakness, technique, footwear, posture, and any metabolic condition
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prolonged pain and disability:} the slow course can mean months of modified sport and work
    \item \textbf{Tendon rupture:} a degenerate tendon is structurally weaker and more prone to partial or complete tearing
    \item \textbf{Recurrence:} the condition readily returns if the strengthening program is abandoned or the overload resumes
    \item \textbf{Secondary muscle and joint problems:} guarding and altered movement can strain other tendons, muscles, and joints
    \item \textbf{Treatment-related harm:} repeated corticosteroid injections can further weaken the tendon and increase rupture risk
    \item \textbf{Psychological impact:} chronic pain and interrupted training and work can cause frustration, low mood, and loss of confidence in physical activity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Tendinosis is a chronic condition, and its natural course is variable: some people improve substantially with a good rehabilitation program, others improve but have recurrences, and a minority continue to have symptoms despite the best care. The single strongest predictor of a good outcome is adherence to a progressive loading program over several months, together with correction of the underlying cause of overload. Imaging changes can persist even when symptoms settle, so it is more useful to judge recovery by function and pain than by repeating scans. With committed rehabilitation, most people are able to return to their previous sport or work, though they may need to maintain a maintenance strengthening program long term and build up any increase in activity gradually. The message is realistic but hopeful: tendinosis responds to the right kind of effort, and that effort is well within reach of most people with the support of a good physiotherapist or sports medicine team.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progress training and work loads gradually, following the principle of increasing volume and intensity by a small percentage each week
    \item Maintain strength in the muscles around commonly affected tendons, especially the calf, thigh, shoulder, and forearm muscles
    \item Warm up before activity and cool down after, and include rest and recovery days
    \item Choose appropriate footwear and correct poor technique with professional guidance
    \item Keep diabetes, cholesterol, and gout well managed
    \item If tendon pain begins, reduce the aggravating activity early and start gentle strengthening rather than pushing through
    \item Maintain a maintenance strengthening program after recovery to prevent recurrence
\end{itemize}
