\chapter{Agnosia}
\index{Agnosia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Agnosia is a rare neurological disorder in which a person cannot recognise objects, people, sounds, or other sensory information, even though the senses themselves work normally. The problem lies not in the eyes or ears but in the parts of the brain that interpret what is seen, heard, or felt. A person with agnosia may, for example, be unable to name a key when shown one but can identify it immediately when it is placed in their hand.

Agnosia is usually caused by damage to specific areas of the brain, most often from stroke, head injury, brain infection, or dementia. The condition is classified according to the affected sensory channel, with visual agnosia, prosopagnosia (face blindness), auditory agnosia, and tactile agnosia being the main forms. Recognition is typically preserved through other senses, which is a key clue that the problem is one of interpretation rather than perception.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Agnosia is uncommon, and its exact prevalence is not well established, largely because it varies with the underlying cause. It occurs most often in older adults, reflecting the higher rates of stroke and dementia in this age group, but it can affect people of any age after a head injury or brain infection.

Visual agnosia is probably the most common form, and prosopagnosia is the form most frequently noticed by families. Because agnosia is often missed or mistaken for confusion, disorientation, or dementia, the true number of affected people is likely higher than formal records suggest.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Agnosia results from damage to the association areas of the brain -- the regions that integrate and interpret sensory information.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stroke:} the most common cause, when a blocked or bleeding vessel damages the temporal or parietal lobes
    \item \textbf{Head injury:} trauma to the relevant brain areas
    \item \textbf{Brain tumours:} growths that compress or destroy sensory association areas
    \item \textbf{Brain infections:} encephalitis, especially herpes simplex encephalitis
    \item \textbf{Dementia:} Alzheimer's disease and frontotemporal dementia progressively damage these regions
    \item \textbf{Carbon monoxide poisoning:} can damage the temporal and parietal lobes
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} rare damage to these regions during neurosurgery
\end{itemize}

In each case, the primary senses remain intact, but the brain can no longer match what is perceived with stored memories of what the object, sound, or face means. The specific form reflects the site of damage: visual agnosia follows damage to visual association areas, face recognition is lost with damage to the fusiform gyrus, and tactile recognition is lost with damage to the parietal lobe.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The core feature is an inability to recognise information in one sensory channel despite normal sensation in that channel.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visual agnosia:} the person cannot recognise objects, drawings, or everyday tools by sight, although they can be named by touch
    \item \textbf{Prosopagnosia:} the person cannot recognise familiar faces, including family members or their own face in a mirror
    \item \textbf{Auditory agnosia:} the person cannot recognise sounds or music, although hearing itself is normal
    \item \textbf{Tactile agnosia:} the person cannot identify objects by feel, such as a coin or a key placed in the hand
    \item Difficulty navigating familiar surroundings or using familiar objects correctly
    \item Recognition through other senses is usually preserved, which helps identify the problem
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Agnosia must be distinguished from conditions that produce similar appearances for very different reasons.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Sensory loss, such as poor vision or hearing, which makes perception impossible
    \item Aphasia, a language disorder in which the person cannot name objects because of a language problem rather than a recognition failure
    \item Delirium, which causes fluctuating confusion and reduced attention
    \item Dementia with generalised cognitive decline rather than a specific recognition problem
    \item Depression or psychosis, which can present with apparent withdrawal or odd behaviour
    \item Hemineglect, where a person ignores one side of their world after a brain injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek urgent medical care if agnosia appears suddenly, because this can signal a stroke or other acute brain injury.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden difficulty recognising faces, objects, or surroundings
    \item Any sudden weakness, numbness, difficulty speaking, or confusion
    \item A head injury followed by confusion or odd behaviour
    \item A seizure or loss of consciousness
\end{itemize}

For a gradual onset, such as occurs in dementia, see a GP for assessment and referral to a neurologist or neuropsychologist.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detailed history:} taken from the person and family, covering when symptoms started and what changed
    \item \textbf{Neurological examination:} testing vision, hearing, touch, and reflexes
    \item \textbf{Neuropsychological assessment:} structured testing of each sensory channel to confirm that recognition is impaired in one but intact in others
    \item \textbf{Brain imaging:} CT or MRI scans to look for stroke, tumour, or brain damage
    \item \textbf{Eye and ear tests:} to confirm that the senses themselves are normal
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} sometimes used to look for infection or other medical causes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
There is no direct cure for agnosia; treatment focuses on the underlying cause and on strategies to compensate.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Treating the cause:} urgent stroke care, surgery or radiotherapy for tumours, and antibiotics for infections
    \item \textbf{Occupational therapy:} retraining and developing strategies for daily tasks
    \item \textbf{Speech and language therapy:} help with naming and communication strategies
    \item \textbf{Compensatory techniques:} using other senses, written labels, verbal descriptions, or asking someone to name objects
    \item \textbf{Environmental adjustments:} organising the home so that objects are easier to recognise and hazards are removed
    \item \textbf{Support:} family education, and advice on safety, mobility, and driving
    \item \textbf{Regular follow-up:} monitoring for change, especially if the underlying cause may progress
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Falls and injuries from misjudging objects or surroundings
    \item Misuse of objects, such as picking up a knife instead of a pen, or leaving a stove on
    \item Difficulty cooking, driving, working, and managing money independently
    \item Social isolation and relationship strain, especially with face-recognition problems
    \item Depression and anxiety as a reaction to the loss
    \item Progression of the underlying disease, such as dementia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the cause. When agnosia follows a stroke or head injury, some improvement may occur over weeks to months as the brain recovers, although the recognition problem can persist. When it is caused by a progressive condition such as Alzheimer's disease, the difficulty tends to worsen over time. Many people learn to compensate with strategies and support, and the aim of care is to maintain safety, independence, and quality of life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stroke prevention:} control blood pressure, cholesterol, and diabetes, and do not smoke
    \item \textbf{Head injury:} wear a helmet for cycling, riding, and high-risk activities
    \item \textbf{Carbon monoxide:} have detectors fitted and serviced in the home
    \item \textbf{Prompt care:} seek medical attention immediately for any new or sudden neurological symptom
    \item \textbf{Compensation:} once diagnosed, use written labels, verbal cues, and routines
    \item \textbf{Environment:} keep the home tidy and consistent to reduce confusion and falls
\end{itemize}
