\chapter{Cancer Types}
\index{Cancer Types}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cancer is the name given to a large family of diseases in which abnormal cells divide without control and can invade nearby tissues or spread to other parts of the body. It is not a single disease but more than a hundred distinct conditions, each usually named after the organ where it begins or the type of cell from which it arises. Cancers are commonly grouped by their tissue of origin: \textbf{carcinomas} arise in the skin or the lining cells of organs, \textbf{sarcomas} in bone, muscle, and connective tissue, \textbf{lymphomas} in the lymphatic system, \textbf{leukaemias} in the blood-forming tissues of the bone marrow, and \textbf{myelomas} in antibody-producing plasma cells. Carcinomas account for most adult cancers and are subdivided into adenocarcinomas, which arise from glandular cells, and squamous cell carcinomas, which arise from the flattened cells that line surfaces such as the skin, mouth, and airways. Understanding the type of cancer matters because behaviour, treatment, and outlook differ greatly between them.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cancer is one of the leading causes of illness and death worldwide, and the types seen most often vary by region. In many high-income countries the most common are breast cancer in women, prostate cancer in men, colorectal (bowel) cancer, and lung cancer, followed by melanoma. Globally, lung, colorectal, stomach, and liver cancers cause the greatest number of deaths. Roughly one in two men and one in three women will be diagnosed with a cancer at some point in their lives. Incidence rises steeply with age, although some cancers, such as acute leukaemias and certain brain tumours, occur mainly in children. Rates of many common cancers are increasing as populations age, while others, such as cervical cancer, have fallen sharply where screening and HPV vaccination are widespread. Survival has improved substantially over recent decades because of earlier diagnosis and better treatments, but outcomes still differ markedly between countries and between socioeconomic groups.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Cancer develops when genetic changes in a cell's DNA disrupt the normal controls on cell division, repair, and death. These changes are usually acquired over a lifetime rather than inherited, and most result from the accumulation of several mutations over many years. Common contributing factors include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tobacco smoke:} the leading cause of lung cancer and a major cause of many others, including bladder, mouth, throat, and pancreatic cancer
    \item \textbf{Ultraviolet and ionising radiation:} linked to melanoma and other skin cancers, and to cancers after therapeutic radiation
    \item \textbf{Infections:} for example, human papillomavirus (HPV) in cervical and head-and-neck cancers, hepatitis B and C in liver cancer, and \textit{Helicobacter pylori} in stomach cancer
    \item \textbf{Hormones:} oestrogen drives many breast and endometrial cancers, and the growth of many prostate cancers is stimulated by testosterone
    \item \textbf{Diet, alcohol, and obesity:} excess alcohol and body fat modestly increase the risk of several cancers, while a diet high in processed and red meat is linked to colorectal cancer
    \item \textbf{Inherited gene changes:} such as \textit{BRCA1} and \textit{BRCA2} in breast and ovarian cancer and \textit{APC} in familial bowel cancer, which account for around 5--10\% of cases
\end{itemize}
As abnormal cells multiply they may form a lump, invade surrounding tissue, and send cells through the blood or lymphatic system to establish secondary tumours (metastases) elsewhere. The hallmarks of cancer include sustained growth signalling, resistance to cell death, and the ability to evade the immune system.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend heavily on the type and site of the cancer, but common warning features include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A new lump, thickening, or swelling anywhere in the body
    \item Unexplained weight loss, fatigue, or loss of appetite
    \item Persistent pain, cough, hoarseness, or difficulty swallowing
    \item A change in bowel or bladder habits that lasts several weeks
    \item Unusual bleeding or discharge, such as blood in the stools, urine, or sputum
    \item A sore, ulcer, or skin lesion that does not heal, or a mole that changes in size, shape, or colour
    \item Persistent fever, drenching night sweats, or unexplained anaemia
\end{itemize}
Many cancers cause no symptoms at all in their early stages and are found through screening or during tests performed for other reasons.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of cancer can be caused by many non-cancerous conditions, which is why investigations are needed before a diagnosis is confirmed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Benign growths and cysts, such as fibroids, polyps, lipomas, and sebaceous cysts
    \item Infections, including tuberculosis, abscesses, and infectious mononucleosis
    \item Chronic inflammatory conditions, such as inflammatory bowel disease, sarcoidosis, and rheumatoid arthritis
    \item Autoimmune and connective tissue disorders
    \item Reactive enlargement of lymph nodes from infection or inflammation
    \item Endocrine or metabolic disorders, such as hyperthyroidism or diabetes, causing weight loss and fatigue
    \item Benign blood disorders, such as iron-deficiency anaemia or reactive changes in the blood count
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor promptly if you notice any persistent, unusual, or progressive symptom, particularly:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A new or growing lump anywhere in the body
    \item Blood in the stools, urine, or coughed-up phlegm
    \item Unexplained weight loss or persistent fatigue
    \item A change in bowel habit lasting more than a few weeks
    \item A persistent cough, hoarseness, or difficulty swallowing
    \item A mole or skin lesion that changes in size, shape, or colour
    \item Unusual or persistent pain, especially if it wakes you at night
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe chest pain or sudden difficulty breathing
    \item Coughing or vomiting large amounts of blood
    \item Sudden weakness, confusion, or difficulty speaking
    \item Symptoms of spinal cord compression, such as new back pain with loss of leg power or control of the bladder or bowels
    \item Fever with feeling very unwell in someone who is having chemotherapy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} careful review of symptoms, risk factors, and family history, followed by examination of the affected site and lymph nodes
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, liver and kidney function, and tumour markers where relevant, such as PSA for prostate cancer and CA-125 for ovarian cancer
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} X-ray, ultrasound, CT, MRI, or PET scans to find, characterise, and stage the tumour
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} removal of a tissue sample for examination under a microscope; this is the only way to confirm a cancer diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Endoscopy:} internal viewing of the bowel, stomach, or airways with a camera, allowing direct inspection and biopsy
    \item \textbf{Bone marrow biopsy:} required for the diagnosis and classification of leukaemias, lymphomas, and myelomas
    \item \textbf{Molecular and genetic testing:} performed on the biopsy to identify mutations that guide targeted therapy, predict response, and reveal inherited risk
    \item \textbf{Staging:} determining the size of the tumour and its spread to lymph nodes or distant organs, which guides treatment and estimates outlook
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Cancer care is coordinated by a multidisciplinary team and tailored to the type, stage, and biology of the disease as well as the person's overall fitness and wishes. Treatment options include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} removal of the tumour, sometimes with nearby lymph nodes, and the treatment most likely to cure localised disease
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} high-energy radiation to kill cancer cells, used alone or before or after surgery
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy:} drugs that damage rapidly dividing cells, given by mouth, injection, or infusion
    \item \textbf{Hormone therapy:} for cancers driven by hormones, such as many breast and prostate cancers
    \item \textbf{Targeted therapy:} medicines aimed at specific molecules within cancer cells, such as tyrosine kinase inhibitors
    \item \textbf{Immunotherapy:} treatments that help the immune system recognise and attack the cancer
    \item \textbf{Supportive and palliative care:} control of symptoms, nutrition, and psychological support offered at every stage of the illness, including at the end of life
\end{itemize}
Treatment may aim for cure, long-term control, or relief of symptoms, depending on the situation.

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Spread of disease to bone, liver, brain, or lungs, causing organ failure, pathological fractures, or spinal cord compression
    \item Obstruction of the bowel, airway, or bile ducts by tumour growth, sometimes requiring emergency treatment
    \item Anaemia and fatigue from chronic bleeding or bone marrow involvement
    \item Paraneoplastic effects, where the tumour causes distant symptoms such as high blood calcium, abnormal blood clotting, or nerve damage
    \item Treatment side effects, including infection, bleeding, nausea, fatigue, neuropathy, heart and kidney toxicity, and the rare development of secondary cancers
    \item Blood clots in the deep veins of the legs or the lungs
    \item Long-term psychological distress, anxiety, and depression in both patients and their families
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Outlook varies enormously by cancer type, stage at diagnosis, grade, and response to treatment. Many early-stage cancers are cured with surgery alone, and survival for cancers such as breast, prostate, testicular, and thyroid cancer is generally very good when they are detected early. Some cancers, particularly of the pancreas, liver, and lung, are often found late and carry a poorer outlook. Overall five-year survival across all cancers in high-income countries is around 70\%, having risen steadily over the past four decades. Survivors may need long-term follow-up for recurrence, monitoring for late effects of treatment, and support for general health and wellbeing, including screening for secondary cancers.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Do not smoke and avoid second-hand smoke
    \item Limit alcohol intake to within national guidelines
    \item Maintain a healthy body weight and stay physically active
    \item Eat a varied diet with plenty of vegetables, fruit, and whole grains, and limit processed and red meat
    \item Protect skin from the sun, avoid solariums, and use sunscreen and shade
    \item Take part in national screening programmes for bowel, breast, and cervical cancer
    \item Be vaccinated against HPV and hepatitis B
    \item See a doctor early for any persistent or worrying symptom rather than waiting for it to resolve
\end{itemize}
