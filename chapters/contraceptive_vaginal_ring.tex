\chapter{Contraceptive vaginal ring}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The contraceptive vaginal ring is a flexible, transparent, combined hormonal contraceptive device measuring approximately 54\,mm in diameter and 4\,mm in thickness. Inserted into the vagina by the user, it slowly releases a daily low dose of an oestrogen (ethinylestradiol) and a progestogen (etonogestrel) over a three-week period (e.g., NuvaRing). The ring is kept in place for 21 days, followed by a 7-day ring-free interval during which the user experiences a withdrawal bleed. It represents a highly effective, reversible method of contraception that offers the cycle control and efficacy of the combined oral contraceptive pill, with the added benefit of once-monthly administration, thereby bypassing the need for daily adherence.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The contraceptive vaginal ring is widely used across developed nations, particularly by women who desire combined hormonal contraception but prefer not to take a daily tablet. In Australia, the ring is popular among young, urban, and professional women, although its overall uptake is somewhat lower than that of oral pills or intrauterine devices. Because the ring is not currently subsidised by the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) and is only available on private prescription, cost remains a primary factor influencing its epidemiological distribution and user demographics. Typical-use failure rates are around 9\%, whereas perfect-use efficacy is exceptionally high at over 99\%.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The contraceptive vaginal ring functions via systemic absorption of hormones through the vaginal epithelium, achieving stable blood concentrations without the daily peaks and troughs associated with oral pills.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ovulation suppression:} The primary mechanism is the inhibition of gonadotropin secretion (LH and FSH) from the anterior pituitary, which prevents ovarian follicular development and ovulation.
    \item \textbf{Cervical mucus thickening:} The progestogenic component increases the viscosity of cervical mucus, rendering it highly resistant to sperm penetration.
    \item \textbf{Endometrial modification:} It alters the endometrium, making it thin, atrophic, and unsuitable for blastocyst implantation.
    \item \textbf{First-pass hepatic bypass:} Because the hormones are absorbed directly through the vaginal mucosa into the systemic circulation, the liver is bypassed initially, allowing for lower therapeutic hormone doses and potentially reducing gastrointestinal side effects.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of a vaginal ring user involves predictable cycle control alongside localized and systemic hormonal effects.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Withdrawal bleeding:} Regular, predictable, and generally lighter withdrawal bleeding occurs during the 7-day ring-free interval.
    \item \textbf{Vaginal symptoms:} An increase in physiological vaginal discharge (leukorrhoea) is common due to local tissue reaction, and users occasionally report mild vaginal wetness or irritation.
    \item \textbf{Systemic hormonal symptoms:} Minor side effects include breast tenderness, mild nausea, headaches, mood changes, or a slight decrease in libido.
    \item \textbf{Expulsion or displacement:} The ring may occasionally slip down or be accidentally expelled during bowel movements, tampon removal, or sexual intercourse.
    \item \textbf{Partner awareness:} In a small percentage of cases, either the user or their sexual partner may feel the ring during intercourse, though this is rarely reported as bothersome.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When evaluating a vaginal ring user presenting with localized or systemic symptoms, other clinical conditions must be excluded.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vaginal infections:} Increased discharge or irritation must be differentiated from bacterial vaginosis, vulvovaginal candidiasis, trichomoniasis, or sexually transmitted infections (STIs).
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} Breakthrough bleeding or amenorrhoea requires a pregnancy test to rule out intrauterine or ectopic pregnancy.
    \item \textbf{Venous thromboembolism (VTE):} Leg pain, swelling, or sudden chest pain must be carefully evaluated to rule out deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism.
    \item \textbf{Cervical or uterine pathology:} Persistent breakthrough bleeding or spotting during the 21 days of ring use warrants investigation for cervical ectropion, polyps, or dysplasia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Users should consult a doctor if they experience persistent vaginal irritation, suspect the ring has been misplaced, experience severe breakthrough bleeding, or develop signs of systemic illness.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, unexplained shortness of breath, sharp chest pain, or coughing up blood (possible pulmonary embolism)
    \item Severe, persistent pain, swelling, redness, or warmth in one calf or leg (suggestive of deep vein thrombosis)
    \item Sudden, severe headache, weakness or numbness in the face, arm, or leg (especially on one side), or difficulty speaking (signs of stroke)
    \item Severe abdominal pain (ruling out hepatic or gallbladder complications)
    \item Sudden loss of vision, blurred vision, or double vision (possible retinal vein thrombosis)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Initiation of the contraceptive vaginal ring requires a thorough clinical assessment to identify contraindications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Medical history and risk screening:} Assessing for contraindications identical to combined oral contraceptives, including age over 35 and smoking, uncontrolled hypertension, history of venous thromboembolism, migraine with aura, active breast cancer, or severe liver disease.
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure check:} Essential before prescribing and at regular follow-ups, as combined hormones can elevate blood pressure.
    \item \textbf{Body Mass Index (BMI) assessment:} High BMI may slightly increase the risk of thromboembolism and reduce contraceptive efficacy.
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test:} Performed if the ring is initiated outside the first five days of the menstrual cycle or if pregnancy is suspected.
    \item \textbf{Microbiological swabs:} Done if the patient presents with abnormal vaginal discharge to exclude candidiasis, bacterial vaginosis, or STIs.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management involves patient education on proper ring handling, scheduling, and emergency management of displacement.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Insertion and placement:} The user compresses the ring and inserts it into the vagina. Precise positioning is not critical for efficacy, as long as the ring is comfortable and completely inside.
    \item \textbf{Standard schedule:} The ring is left in place for exactly 3 weeks (21 days), removed for 1 week (7 days) to allow a withdrawal bleed, and a new ring is inserted at the end of the 7-day break.
    \item \textbf{Management of expulsion:} If the ring is out of the vagina for less than 3 hours, it should be rinsed with cool water and reinserted immediately, with no loss of efficacy. If it is out for more than 3 hours (especially in the second or third week), barrier contraception must be used for 7 days post-reinsertion.
    \item \textbf{Extended use:} The ring can be used continuously (replacing it every 3 to 4 weeks without a break) to delay or skip periods, which is safe and clinically acceptable.
    \item \textbf{Storage conditions:} Unused rings must be stored below 30\textdegree C (preferably in a refrigerator between 2\textdegree C and 8\textdegree C) and must be used within 4 months of dispensing.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While the vaginal ring is highly tolerable, users must be aware of potential adverse events.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Thromboembolic events:} As a combined hormonal contraceptive, the ring carries a small but clinically significant risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE) and arterial thromboembolism, slightly higher than non-hormonal methods.
    \item \textbf{Vaginal infections and vaginitis:} The physical presence of the ring can alter the vaginal microenvironment, leading to recurrent thrush or bacterial vaginosis in susceptible women.
    \item \textbf{Accidental expulsion:} If unnoticed, expulsion can result in contraceptive failure and unplanned pregnancy.
    \item \textbf{Toxic shock syndrome (TSS):} An extremely rare but life-threatening complication associated with vaginal foreign bodies; proper hygiene during insertion and removal is critical.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for contraceptive success with the vaginal ring is excellent. The failure rate is under 0.3\% with perfect use and about 9\% with typical use. High rates of patient satisfaction are reported due to the ease of use and low rate of breakthrough bleeding. Following removal, ovulation typically resumes within 14 to 30 days, and baseline fertility is restored rapidly.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Patients can take specific steps to ensure safe use and prevent complications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Use digital calendar reminders:} Set alarms for the exact day and time of insertion and removal to maintain the 21/7-day cycle.
    \item \textbf{Check ring position regularly:} Check for the presence of the ring after bowel movements or sexual intercourse.
    \item \textbf{Practice hand hygiene:} Always wash hands thoroughly before inserting, adjusting, or removing the ring.
    \item \textbf{Store safely:} Keep unused rings in their original packaging in the refrigerator or in a cool place below 30\textdegree C.
    \item \textbf{Avoid hot water:} Never rinse the ring in hot water if it is expelled, as heat can accelerate hormone release.
\end{itemize}
