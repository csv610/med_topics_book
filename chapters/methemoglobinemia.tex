\chapter{Methemoglobinemia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Methemoglobinemia (also spelled methaemoglobinaemia) is a blood disorder characterised by an abnormally high level of methemoglobin (methaemoglobin) in the blood. Methemoglobin is a form of haemoglobin in which the iron in the heme group is oxidised from the ferrous state (Fe2+) to the ferric state (Fe3+). In this ferric state, methemoglobin is unable to bind and transport oxygen, and it also increases the oxygen affinity of the remaining normal ferrous haemoglobin, shifting the oxygen-haemoglobin dissociation curve to the left. This prevents the release of oxygen to peripheral tissues, leading to tissue hypoxia and cellular dysfunction. The condition can be congenital (inherited genetic defects) or acquired (triggered by exposure to certain oxidising drugs, chemicals, or toxins).

\section*{Epidemiology}
Acquired methemoglobinemia is far more common than the congenital form and can occur in any age group, although infants under six months are particularly susceptible due to immature levels of protective enzymes. The global incidence is difficult to quantify as mild cases often go undiagnosed or resolve spontaneously. In Australia, cases are typically sporadic and associated with exposure to local anaesthetics (such as benzocaine or prilocaine), antibiotics (such as dapsone), or nitrates in well water. Congenital methemoglobinemia is rare, showing no specific sex predilection, with certain types (like Type II cytochrome b5 reductase deficiency) presenting with severe systemic neurological deficits early in life.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Methemoglobinemia results from either an increased rate of haemoglobin oxidation or a decreased rate of methemoglobin reduction back to normal haemoglobin:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Congenital enzyme deficiencies:} Most commonly autosomal recessive mutations in the CYB5R3 gene, causing deficiency of the NADH-cytochrome b5 reductase enzyme (Type I affects red cells; Type II affects all tissues).
    \item \textbf{Haemoglobin M disease:} Autosomal dominant mutations in the globin genes (alpha, beta, or gamma) that stabilise iron in the ferric (Fe3+) state, making it resistant to enzymatic reduction.
    \item \textbf{Acquired oxidising agents:} Exposure to medications such as dapsone, prilocaine, benzocaine, lidocaine, nitrates or nitrites, antimalarials (chloroquine, primaquine), or sulfonamides.
    \item \textbf{Infantile susceptibility:} High pH in the infant gut facilitates nitrate-reducing bacteria, converting nitrates in formula made with well water into nitrites, combined with lower levels of cytochrome b5 reductase in infants.
    \item \textbf{Impairment of alternative pathways:} Congenital deficiency of glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD), which limits the NADPH-dependent pathway (needed for methylene blue activity).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms correlate with the percentage of methemoglobin in the blood, reflecting the degree of tissue hypoxia:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cyanosis:} Characteristically presenting as slate-grey or brownish skin and mucous membrane discoloration that does not respond to oxygen administration.
    \item \textbf{Chocolate-brown blood:} Dark, brownish discoloration of arterial and venous blood that does not turn bright red when exposed to air.
    \item \textbf{Hypoxic symptoms (15--30\%):} Anxiety, mild headache, fatigue, tachycardia, and lightheadedness.
    \item \textbf{Severe symptoms (30--50\%):} Confusion, severe headache, dyspnoea, tachypnoea, weakness, and cerebellar symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Life-threatening signs (>50\%):} Cardiac arrhythmias, metabolic acidosis, seizures, coma, and circulatory collapse.
    \item \textbf{Normal arterial oxygen partial pressure (PaO2):} A classic diagnostic feature where arterial blood gas shows a normal PaO2 despite low oxygen saturation measured by pulse oximetry (saturation gap).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Methemoglobinemia must be distinguished from other causes of cyanosis and hypoxia:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cyanotic congenital heart disease:} Right-to-left shunting causing cyanosis, which responds partially to high-flow oxygen and is confirmed by echocardiography.
    \item \textbf{Severe cardiopulmonary failure:} Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) or cardiogenic shock causing hypoxia, showing abnormal PaO2 on arterial blood gas.
    \item \textbf{Sulfhemoglobinemia:} A rare condition where sulfur is incorporated into haemoglobin, causing cyanosis; does not respond to methylene blue and is diagnosed by spectrophotometry.
    \item \textbf{Carbon monoxide poisoning:} Presents with cherry-red skin, normal pulse oximetry readings, but severe tissue hypoxia, confirmed by carboxyhemoglobin levels.
    \item \textbf{Pseudocyanosis:} Skin discoloration from heavy metals (silver, gold) without true arterial hypoxia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone presenting with sudden cyanosis, unexplained shortness of breath, or confusion after exposure to oxidising medications or chemicals requires immediate emergency medical evaluation.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Severe respiratory distress:} Gasping for air, rapid breathing, or cessation of breathing.
    \item \textbf{Altered consciousness:} Extreme lethargy, confusion, seizures, or coma.
    \item \textbf{Sudden grey or blue skin changes:} Especially if accompanied by rapid heart rate or chest pain.
\end{itemize}
Other reasons to seek medical care include persistent fatigue, headaches, or dizziness in patients taking long-term medications known to cause methemoglobinemia (like dapsone).

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Accurate diagnosis involves identifying the "saturation gap" and performing co-oximetry:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Multiple-wavelength co-oximetry:} The gold standard test. Unlike standard two-wavelength pulse oximeters (which read falsely high saturation around 85\%), co-oximetry directly measures methemoglobin fraction in arterial or venous blood.
    \item \textbf{Arterial blood gas (ABG):} Shows a normal partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) despite clinically obvious cyanosis and low calculated oxygen saturation (saturation gap).
    \item \textbf{Visual inspection of blood:} Placing a drop of blood on white filter paper; methemoglobin remains chocolate-brown, whereas normal deoxyhaemoglobin turns red upon exposure to oxygen.
    \item \textbf{Enzyme assays:} Measurement of NADH-cytochrome b5 reductase activity to confirm congenital deficiency.
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} DNA sequencing of the CYB5R3 gene or globin genes to confirm hereditary forms.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on symptom severity and the percentage of methemoglobin in the blood:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Removal of offending agent:} Immediately discontinue any suspected oxidising drugs or chemical exposures.
    \item \textbf{Methylene blue (first-line antidote):} Administered intravenously (1 to 2 mg/kg over 5 minutes) for symptomatic patients or methemoglobin levels greater than 20\% to 30\%. Methylene blue acts as an electron acceptor, facilitating the NADPH-dependent reduction of methemoglobin.
    \item \textbf{Contraindications to methylene blue:} Patients with G6PD deficiency (can cause severe haemolysis and is ineffective) or those taking serotonergic medications (risk of serotonin syndrome).
    \item \textbf{Alternative therapies:} Hyperbaric oxygen therapy, ascorbic acid (high-dose vitamin C, although slow-acting), or exchange transfusion (indicated for G6PD-deficient patients or severe refractory cases).
    \item \textbf{Congenital management:} Avoidance of all potential oxidising agents. Ascorbic acid or riboflavin may be used daily to reduce methemoglobin levels in Type I disease.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Potential complications of severe or untreated methemoglobinemia include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Myocardial infarction:} Secondary to severe myocardial tissue hypoxia, particularly in patients with pre-existing coronary artery disease.
    \item \textbf{Seizures and encephalopathy:} Brain injury from prolonged cerebral hypoxia.
    \item \textbf{Severe metabolic acidosis:} Lactic acidosis due to anaerobic cellular metabolism.
    \item \textbf{Severe haemolysis:} Destruction of red blood cells, which can be triggered by methylene blue administration in G6PD-deficient individuals.
    \item \textbf{Neurological delay:} Progressive neurodevelopmental impairment in patients with Type II congenital cytochrome b5 reductase deficiency.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for acquired methemoglobinemia is excellent when diagnosed promptly and treated with methylene blue, with most patients showing rapid resolution of cyanosis and symptoms within 30 to 60 minutes of administration. Congenital Type I methemoglobinemia has a benign prognosis, with individuals having a normal life expectancy, although they remain cosmetically cyanotic. In contrast, Congenital Type II disease has a poor prognosis, characterised by progressive neurological deterioration and shortened life expectancy, usually ending in early childhood.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Prevention strategies focus on avoiding chemical exposures and managing inherited risks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Avoid known oxidising drugs:} Inform all healthcare providers of any congenital diagnosis or G6PD deficiency before receiving local anaesthetics or antibiotics.
    \item \textbf{Test well water:} Ensure well water in rural areas is tested for nitrate levels, and avoid using well water to prepare infant formula.
    \item \textbf{Wear protective equipment:} Use respirators and protective clothing when working with industrial chemicals, aniline dyes, or nitrites.
    \item \textbf{Carry medical alert identification:} Wear a medical alert bracelet indicating G6PD deficiency or congenital methemoglobinemia.
    \item \textbf{Monitor medication use:} Regularly check for early signs of cyanosis (e.g. blue lips or nails) if prescribed long-term dapsone therapy.
\end{itemize}
