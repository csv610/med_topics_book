\chapter{Vascular system}
\index{Vascular system}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The vascular system, also referred to as the circulatory system, is a vast and intricate network of vessels responsible for the transport of blood and lymph throughout the human body. This system is crucial for maintaining homeostasis, serving as the primary conduit for the delivery of oxygen, essential nutrients, hormones, and chemical messengers to tissues, while simultaneously facilitating the removal of carbon dioxide, metabolic waste products, and toxins for excretion. Additionally, the vascular system plays an instrumental role in thermoregulation, systemic pH maintenance, fluid balance, and the systemic circulation of immune cells required for immunological defense.

Anatomically and physiologically, the vascular system is divided into three primary components: the arterial system, the venous system, and the microcirculation, complemented by the lymphatic system.
\begin{itemize}
    \item The \textbf{arterial system} is a high-pressure circuit that carries oxygenated blood away from the heart to the systemic tissues (with the exception of the pulmonary arteries, which transport deoxygenated blood to the lungs). Arteries possess thick, muscular, and elastic walls capable of withstanding the high pulsatile pressures generated by ventricular contraction.
    \item The \textbf{venous system} is a low-pressure, high-capacitance reservoir that returns deoxygenated blood from the tissues back to the heart (excepting the pulmonary veins, which carry oxygenated blood from the lungs). Because venous pressure is low, veins rely on one-way bicuspid valves and the compressive action of surrounding skeletal muscles (the muscle pump) to facilitate unidirectional flow against gravity.
    \item The \textbf{microcirculation} consists of capillaries, which are microscopic, single-cell-walled vessels linking arterioles and venules. The ultra-thin endothelial layer of capillaries provides the optimal surface area and permeability required for the passive and active exchange of gases, nutrients, and waste products between the blood and the interstitial fluid.
\end{itemize}

Vascular health is fundamental to the survival and optimal functioning of every organ system. Chronic or acute disruptions in vascular integrity, structure, or function impair tissue perfusion, leading to cellular hypoxia, organ dysfunction, and tissue necrosis. Consequently, vascular diseases represent a major category of systemic illness, contributing substantially to global morbidity, disability, and mortality.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Vascular diseases represent a massive global public health burden, affecting hundreds of millions of individuals worldwide. The epidemiology of vascular disorders varies significantly depending on the specific condition, such as Peripheral Arterial Disease (PAD), Chronic Venous Insufficiency (CVI), and Abdominal Aortic Aneurysms (AAA), but they share common demographic trends linked to aging populations and the global rise in cardiometabolic risk factors.

Peripheral Arterial Disease is estimated to affect over 200 million people globally. The prevalence of PAD is highly age-dependent, rising from less than 5\% in individuals under 50 years of age to more than 20\% in populations aged 80 years and older. While historically documented as more common in men, contemporary large-scale epidemiological studies indicate that PAD affects men and women almost equally, though women are more likely to present with atypical or asymptomatic disease. In terms of racial and ethnic demographics, non-Hispanic Black populations experience a disproportionately higher prevalence of PAD compared to white, Hispanic, and Asian populations, even after adjusting for traditional cardiovascular risk factors.

Chronic Venous Insufficiency is one of the most common vascular conditions managed in primary care, affecting up to 30\% of the adult population in developed nations. Varicose veins, the visible precursor or component of CVI, are present in up to 60\% of adults. The prevalence of CVI increases with age and is significantly higher in females, driven in part by hormonal influences and pregnancy. Other predisposing factors include occupational prolonged standing or sitting, obesity, and a history of deep vein thrombosis.

Abdominal Aortic Aneurysms are notably more prevalent in males and are strongly associated with a history of tobacco smoking. Epidemiological data show that AAA affects approximately 4--8\% of men aged over 65 years who have a history of smoking, compared to less than 2\% of women in the same age bracket. The incidence of AAA rupture, a catastrophic and often fatal event, peaks in men in their late 70s and early 80s, highlighting the critical importance of targeted screening programs.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The developmental pathways and structural alterations underlying vascular diseases are multifactorial, involving a complex interplay of genetic susceptibility, hemodynamic forces, metabolic abnormalities, and chronic inflammatory processes.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Atherosclerosis}: This is the primary pathological mechanism underlying arterial diseases like PAD and coronary artery disease. It is a chronic inflammatory disease of the large and medium-sized muscular arteries. The process begins with endothelial injury or dysfunction, often caused by mechanical shear stress from hypertension, chemical irritants from tobacco smoke, or circulating LDL cholesterol. Endothelial dysfunction increases permeability to lipids, allowing low-density lipoprotein (LDL) to accumulate and become oxidized in the intima. In response, endothelial cells express adhesion molecules, recruiting monocytes and T-lymphocytes. Monocytes differentiate into macrophages, engulf oxidized LDL, and transform into foam cells. The accumulation of these cells forms a fatty streak. Over time, vascular smooth muscle cells migrate from the media to the intima, secreting extracellular matrix to form a fibrous cap over the lipid core. As the plaque grows, it restricts blood flow, causing stable ischemia (e.g., claudication). If the fibrous cap becomes thin and ruptures, it exposes the thrombogenic lipid core to the circulation, triggering acute platelet aggregation and thrombus formation, which can lead to acute limb ischemia, myocardial infarction, or stroke.
    \item \textbf{Venous Hypertension and Valvular Incompetence}: The pathophysiology of chronic venous insufficiency centers on elevated venous pressure within the lower extremities. This occurs due to primary valvular incompetence (weakness of the valve leaflets), secondary valvular damage (following a deep vein thrombosis that destroys valves), or venous outflow obstruction. When venous valves fail to close completely, gravity causes retrograde flow of blood, leading to venous pooling and venous hypertension. The chronic elevation of hydrostatic pressure is transmitted to the microcirculation, causing capillary dilatation, increased permeability, and transudation of fluid, plasma proteins, and red blood cells into the interstitium. The extravasation of red blood cells leads to their breakdown and the deposition of haemosiderin, causing characteristic skin hyperpigmentation. The chronic inflammatory response in the skin and subcutaneous tissue leads to lipodermatosclerosis and eventually to ischemic skin breakdown and venous ulceration.
    \item \textbf{Medial Degeneration and Aneurysm Formation}: The development of aneurysms, particularly AAA, involves the progressive degradation of the structural components of the arterial wall. The aortic wall is composed of elastic fibers and collagen, which provide tensile strength and elasticity. In AAA, chronic inflammation mediated by macrophages and T-cells leads to the excessive production of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), specifically MMP-2 and MMP-9. These proteolytic enzymes degrade elastin and collagen in the tunica media. Simultaneously, there is a depletion of vascular smooth muscle cells via apoptosis, which prevents the repair of the extracellular matrix. The loss of elastic recoil and structural integrity, combined with constant systemic blood pressure, leads to progressive dilatation, ectasia, and eventual rupture of the vessel.
\end{itemize}

Key risk factors that accelerate these pathophysiological pathways include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tobacco Smoking}: Induces endothelial dysfunction through oxidative stress, accelerates plaque formation, decreases nitric oxide bioavailability, and directly increases the rate of aneurysm expansion.
    \item \textbf{Diabetes Mellitus}: Promotes endothelial dysfunction, accelerates advanced glycation end-product (AGE) accumulation, impairs collateral vessel formation, and leads to diffuse, distal arterial calcification.
    \item \textbf{Hypertension}: Elevates mechanical shear stress on arterial walls, accelerating atherogenesis and promoting arterial wall remodeling, stiffness, and aneurysmal expansion.
    \item \textbf{Hyperlipidaemia}: Elevated levels of LDL-C provide the primary lipid substrate for atherosclerotic plaque formation and propagation.
    \item \textbf{Advanced Age}: Characterized by progressive arterial stiffening, loss of elastic fibers, and cumulative exposure to cardiovascular risk factors.
    \item \textbf{Genetic Factors}: Genetic variations influence susceptibility to hyperlipidaemia, hypertension, and connective tissue disorders such as Marfan syndrome or Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, which carry high risks of aneurysmal disease.
    \item \textbf{Sedentary Lifestyle and Obesity}: Leads to impaired muscle pump activity, increased venous pressure, and systemic low-grade inflammation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of vascular disorders is highly variable, depending on whether the pathology affects the arterial or venous system, its chronic or acute nature, and the anatomical region involved.

\subsection*{Peripheral Arterial Disease (PAD)}
The clinical presentation of PAD is categorized into stages ranging from asymptomatic to severe limb-threatening ischemia:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asymptomatic PAD}: Many patients have significant arterial stenosis but do not report classical symptoms, often due to sedentary lifestyles or the development of collateral circulation.
    \item \textbf{Intermittent Claudication}: The classic symptom of PAD, characterized by cramp-like pain, ache, fatigue, or numbness in the calf, thigh, or buttock muscles. This discomfort is reproducibly brought on by walking a consistent distance and is promptly relieved within minutes of standing still. The location of the pain correlates with the level of arterial occlusion (e.g., calf pain points to femoropopliteal disease; buttock pain points to aortoiliac disease).
    \item \textbf{Critical Limb-Threatening Ischemia (CLTI)}: Represents the end-stage of chronic arterial insufficiency. It is characterized by ischemic rest pain, which is typically a burning or throbbing pain in the distal foot or toes, occurring at night when the patient is recumbent. The pain is often partially relieved by hanging the affected foot over the side of the bed or by gravity-assisted perfusion (dangling). CLTI also presents with non-healing arterial ulcers (typically found on the tips of the toes, heel, or malleoli, appearing deep, dry, and punched-out) and gangrene (dry or wet necrosis).
    \item \textbf{Physical Signs}: Clinical examination signs include absent or diminished peripheral pulses (femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial, and dorsalis pedis), localized bruits over stenotic arteries, muscle atrophy of the lower leg, cool skin temperature, loss of hair on the lower leg and dorsum of the foot, thickened or brittle toenails, and thin, shiny, or pale skin. A characteristic finding is dependent rubor (the foot turns dark red when hanging down) and elevation pallor (the foot turns pale when elevated).
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Chronic Venous Insufficiency (CVI)}
Chronic venous insufficiency presents with signs and symptoms related to chronic venous congestion:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Symptoms}: Patients frequently complain of a dull ache, heaviness, tension, throbbing, or fatigue in the lower extremities. These symptoms are typically worse at the end of the day, after prolonged standing or sitting, and are relieved by leg elevation or walking. Restless legs and nocturnal muscle cramps are also common.
    \item \textbf{Varicose Veins}: Dilated, tortuous superficial veins, measuring 3 mm or more in diameter, usually visible on the thigh or calf.
    \item \textbf{Oedema}: Pitting edema of the lower leg, ankle, and foot, which is typically bilateral but can be asymmetric.
    \item \textbf{Skin Changes}: Chronic venous congestion leads to stasis dermatitis (erythema, scaling, and pruritus), hyperpigmentation (yellow-brown discoloration from haemosiderin deposition), and lipodermatosclerosis (subcutaneous fibrosis leading to a hard, wooden texture of the skin, giving the lower leg an inverted champagne bottle appearance).
    \item \textbf{Venous Ulceration}: Typically located over the medial malleolus. Unlike arterial ulcers, venous ulcers are usually shallow, have irregular borders, are highly exudative, and are surrounded by hyperpigmented, indurated skin.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asymptomatic AAA}: The vast majority of AAAs are completely asymptomatic and are discovered incidentally during abdominal imaging for unrelated complaints or via screening.
    \item \textbf{Symptomatic Non-Ruptured AAA}: Patients may complain of a persistent, dull, throbbing pain in the abdomen, lower back, or flank. On physical exam, a prominent, pulsatile abdominal mass may be palpable periumbilically.
    \item \textbf{Ruptured AAA}: A catastrophic emergency characterized by the classic triad of sudden-onset, excruciating abdominal, back, or flank pain; profound hypotension or shock (dizziness, syncope, tachycardia, diaphoresis); and a palpable pulsatile abdominal mass.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Distinguishing vascular diseases from other clinical entities with similar presentations is essential for directing correct therapy and avoiding inappropriate interventions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spinal Stenosis (Neurogenic Pseudoclaudication)}: Often misdiagnosed as vascular claudication. Neurogenic claudication is caused by compression of the lumbar nerve roots. Like vascular claudication, it causes lower limb pain and weakness with walking. However, neurogenic pain is exacerbated by prolonged standing (even without walking) and spinal extension, and it is relieved by sitting or bending forward (the ``shopping cart sign'') rather than simply standing still. Peripheral pulses are typically normal, and there are no ischemic skin changes.
    \item \textbf{Peripheral Neuropathy}: Frequently coexists with vascular disease in diabetic patients. Neuropathy presents with burning pain, numbness, tingling, or paresthesias in a ``stocking-glove'' distribution. Unlike claudication, the symptoms are constant, worse at night, and unrelated to physical exertion or walking.
    \item \textbf{Osteoarthritis of the Hip or Knee}: Joint-related pain is exacerbated by weight-bearing and movement of the joint. It is typically localized to the joint line or groin, is associated with morning stiffness that improves with activity, and is not accompanied by changes in skin temperature, hair loss, or abnormal peripheral pulses.
    \item \textbf{Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)}: Acute unilateral lower extremity pain, swelling, and warmth can mimic acute venous insufficiency or cellulitis. DVT is distinguished by its sudden onset and is diagnosed via venous duplex ultrasound. Chronic CVI is typically bilateral and develops over years, featuring hyperpigmentation and lipodermatosclerosis.
    \item \textbf{Cellulitis}: A bacterial skin infection that presents with acute, unilateral erythema, warmth, pain, swelling, and systemic symptoms like fever and chills. It is frequently confused with stasis dermatitis associated with CVI. However, stasis dermatitis is chronic, bilateral, and lacks systemic signs of infection like leukocytosis or fever.
    \item \textbf{Acute Musculoskeletal Strain}: Localized muscle pain and tenderness that follows a history of acute trauma or strain. The pain is reproducible with muscle contraction or palpation, and vascular signs (pulses, skin changes) are entirely normal.
    \item \textbf{Renal Colic}: Acute, severe, unilateral flank pain radiating to the groin can mimic a symptomatic or expanding AAA. The absence of a pulsatile abdominal mass and hemodynamic stability points toward renal colic, which is confirmed by urinalysis (haematuria) and a non-contrast CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients with underlying vascular risks or chronic symptoms should engage with primary care physicians or specialists for routine evaluation. Medical consultation should be scheduled if an individual experiences progressive walking limitations (claudication), non-healing sores or ulcers on the feet or legs, persistent leg swelling, skin discoloration, or new, prominent varicose veins.

Certain vascular presentations represent acute, life- or limb-threatening emergencies that require immediate activation of emergency medical services (e.g., calling \textbf{local emergency services} in many countries, \textbf{911} in the United States, or \textbf{999} in the United Kingdom). Immediate emergency care must be sought for the following symptoms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden onset of severe, tearing, ripping, or sharp pain in the abdomen, chest, back, or flank (highly suggestive of aortic dissection or ruptured aortic aneurysm).
    \item Sudden numbness, weakness, paralysis, coldness, or extreme pallor in a leg or arm (indicative of acute limb ischemia, where a delay in revascularisation beyond 4--6 hours can lead to permanent muscle damage, amputation, or systemic toxicity).
    \item Unilateral leg pain and significant swelling that develops rapidly, accompanied by shortness of breath, chest pain, or coughing up blood (suggestive of deep vein thrombosis with secondary pulmonary embolism).
    \item Uncontrolled, heavy bleeding from a ruptured varicose vein or vascular access site.
    \item Sudden loss of vision, sudden weakness or numbness on one side of the body, difficulty speaking, or facial drooping (signs of a stroke or transient ischemic attack, often due to carotid artery disease).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of vascular diseases relies on a detailed clinical history, physical examination, and a structured hierarchy of non-invasive and invasive diagnostic investigations.

\subsection*{Physical Examination}
The vascular physical examination includes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pulse Palpation}: Assessment of bilateral pulses at the carotid, radial, femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial, and dorsalis pedis sites, graded from 0 (absent) to 2+ (normal).
    \item \textbf{Auscultation}: Evaluation for arterial bruits (turbulent blood flow) over the carotid, subclavian, renal, abdominal aortic, and femoral arteries.
    \item \textbf{Buerger's Test}: Used to assess arterial insufficiency in the lower limbs. The patient's legs are elevated to 45 degrees while supine; pallor of the foot within 30--60 seconds indicates severe arterial disease. The legs are then lowered, and a delay in return of pink color followed by a dark red hue (dependent rubor) confirms arterial insufficiency.
    \item \textbf{Trendelenburg Test}: Historically used to evaluate the competency of venous valves in the superficial and perforator veins of the lower extremity.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Diagnostic Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI)}: The initial screening and diagnostic test for PAD. It is calculated by dividing the higher systolic blood pressure measured at the ankle (dorsalis pedis or posterior tibial artery) by the higher systolic blood pressure measured in either arm.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Normal ABI: 0.90 to 1.40.
        \item Mild-to-Moderate PAD: 0.41 to 0.90.
        \item Severe PAD / Critical Ischemia: less than or equal to 0.40.
        \item Non-compressible vessels: greater than 1.40 (commonly seen in patients with long-standing diabetes or end-stage renal disease due to medial arterial calcification). In these cases, a \textbf{Toe-Brachial Index (TBI)} is performed, as digital arteries are rarely calcified; a TBI of less than 0.70 is diagnostic of PAD.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Duplex Ultrasound}: Combines traditional B-mode imaging with Doppler flow assessment. It is the gold standard first-line test for diagnosing deep vein thrombosis, evaluating chronic venous insufficiency (quantifying venous reflux time, where reflux greater than 0.5 seconds in superficial veins or greater than 1.0 second in deep veins indicates valvular incompetence), and mapping varicose veins. In arterial disease, it localizes stenoses and measures flow velocity changes. It is also the primary modality for screening and monitoring the size of Abdominal Aortic Aneurysms.
    \item \textbf{Segmental Pressures and Pulse Volume Recordings (PVR)}: Uses blood pressure cuffs placed at intervals along the leg to measure pressure drops and arterial waveform amplitudes, helping localize the anatomical site of arterial stenosis.
    \item \textbf{Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA)}: Utilizes intravenous contrast to produce high-resolution, three-dimensional reconstructions of the vascular anatomy. It is highly sensitive and specific, serving as the gold standard for defining the anatomy of abdominal aortic aneurysms prior to repair and mapping complex arterial lesions in PAD for intervention. The use of contrast requires monitoring of renal function.
    \item \textbf{Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA)}: Provides detailed vascular imaging without ionizing radiation. It can be performed with or without gadolinium contrast. It is an alternative to CTA, particularly in patients with renal impairment.
    \item \textbf{Digital Subtraction Angiography (DSA)}: An invasive diagnostic modality where contrast is injected directly into the arterial system via a catheter under fluoroscopy. Bone and soft tissue images are subtracted to leave clear images of the blood vessels. DSA remains the gold standard for defining anatomy but is now reserved for therapeutic interventions (angioplasty/stenting) rather than purely diagnostic purposes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of vascular disease is comprehensive and multimodal, aiming to reduce cardiovascular mortality, improve quality of life, relieve symptoms, and prevent severe limb loss or vessel rupture.

\subsection*{Lifestyle Modifications}
Lifestyle interventions form the cornerstone of management for all vascular patients:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Smoking Cessation}: The single most effective lifestyle change to halt the progression of atherosclerosis and reduce the rate of aneurysm growth. Use of pharmacotherapy (nicotine replacement, varenicline, or bupropion) combined with behavioral support is highly recommended.
    \item \textbf{Exercise Therapy}: For PAD patients, a supervised exercise program consisting of treadmill walking for 30--45 minutes, at least 3 times per week for a minimum of 12 weeks, is first-line therapy. The training involves walking to the point of moderate-to-severe claudication pain, resting, and resuming. For CVI patients, regular low-impact exercise (walking, swimming) promotes the calf muscle pump, reducing venous congestion.
    \item \textbf{Diet and Weight Management}: A Mediterranean diet low in saturated fats, trans fats, and sodium helps manage hyperlipidaemia, hypertension, and obesity.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Pharmacological Management}
Medical therapy aims to modify systemic cardiovascular risk:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antiplatelet Therapy}: Indicated for symptomatic PAD to reduce the risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, and vascular death. Aspirin (75--100 mg daily) or Clopidogrel (75 mg daily) are standard. Dual antiplatelet therapy or low-dose rivaroxaban (2.5 mg twice daily) plus aspirin may be used in high-risk patients.
    \item \textbf{Lipid-Lowering Therapy}: High-intensity statin therapy (e.g., Atorvastatin 40--80 mg or Rosuvastatin 20--40 mg daily) is indicated for all PAD patients, aiming for an LDL-C reduction of 50\% or more, or a target level of less than 1.4 mmol/L (55 mg/dL). Statins also reduce the rate of AAA expansion.
    \item \textbf{Antihypertensive Therapy}: Target blood pressure is typically less than 130/80 mmHg. Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) are preferred for cardiovascular protection.
    \item \textbf{Glycaemic Control}: Strict blood glucose management in patients with diabetes (target HbA1c of less than 7.0\%) reduces microvascular risk and limits PAD progression.
    \item \textbf{Symptom-Specific Medications}: Cilostazol (a phosphodiesterase inhibitor) is used to improve walking distance in claudication patients without heart failure. Venoactive drugs (such as micronized purified flavonoid fraction) can help reduce edema in CVI.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Endovascular and Surgical Interventions}
Interventional options are reserved for patients who do not respond to medical and exercise therapies, or those with life- or limb-threatening disease.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Peripheral Arterial Disease}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Endovascular therapy}: Percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) with balloon dilatation, with or without stent placement, to restore patency to occluded arteries. Atherectomy or drug-eluting balloons/stents are also utilized.
        \item \textbf{Surgical bypass}: Involves using an autologous vein (e.g., great saphenous vein) or synthetic graft (PTFE) to bypass a long or complex arterial occlusion (e.g., femoropopliteal bypass).
        \item \textbf{Amputation}: A last resort in cases of irreversible limb ischemia, severe gangrene, or life-threatening sepsis.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chronic Venous Insufficiency}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Endovenous thermal ablation}: Using radiofrequency or laser energy to close incompetent superficial veins (e.g., great saphenous vein), redirecting flow to competent deep veins.
        \item \textbf{Sclerotherapy}: Injecting a chemical sclerosant (foam or liquid) into small-to-medium varicose veins.
        \item \textbf{Surgical ligation and stripping}: Historically common, now reserved for complex anatomy.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Endovascular Aneurysm Repair (EVAR)}: Minimally invasive insertion of a covered stent-graft via the femoral arteries to line the aorta and exclude the aneurysm sac. Requires favorable anatomy.
        \item \textbf{Open Aortic Repair (OAR)}: Direct surgical exposure of the aorta, clamping of the vessel, and replacement of the aneurysmal segment with a synthetic fabric graft. It is highly invasive but offers a permanent solution.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Neglected or untreated vascular disease, as well as its invasive treatments, carries significant risks of morbidity and mortality.

\subsection*{Disease-Related Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Critical Limb Loss}: Untreated PAD can progress to critical limb-threatening ischemia, resulting in ischemic ulcers, necrosis, gangrene, and eventual major lower-extremity amputation. Amputation is associated with high mortality, depression, and severe loss of independence.
    \item \textbf{Aneurysm Rupture}: The most catastrophic complication of AAA. Rupture causes massive intraperitoneal or retroperitoneal hemorrhage. The overall mortality rate is 80--90\%, with many patients dying prior to hospital arrival.
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular Events}: Systemic atherosclerosis means patients with PAD are at extremely high risk for myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, and cardiovascular death.
    \item \textbf{Chronic Venous Ulcers}: Non-healing venous ulcers are painful, prone to bacterial superinfection (cellulitis, osteomyelitis), and cause chronic disability.
    \item \textbf{Thromboembolism}: Deep vein thrombosis can lead to pulmonary embolism (PE), which can be fatal. DVT also causes post-thrombotic syndrome, a severe form of CVI.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Treatment-Related Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Revascularisation Risks}: Interventions for PAD carry risks of access site hematoma, arterial dissection, distal embolization, contrast-induced nephropathy, and graft or stent thrombosis.
    \item \textbf{EVAR-Specific Complications}: Endoleaks (persistent blood flow into the aneurysm sac outside the graft), graft migration, limb occlusion, or graft infection. These require lifelong imaging surveillance.
    \item \textbf{Surgical Complications}: Open AAA repair is associated with perioperative myocardial infarction, renal failure, bowel ischemia, bleeding, and incisional hernia.
    \item \textbf{Bleeding}: High-intensity statins and antiplatelet or anticoagulant regimens increase the risk of gastrointestinal or intracranial hemorrhage.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals with vascular disease depends on the specific pathology, the severity at presentation, and adherence to risk factor modification.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Peripheral Arterial Disease}: Asymptomatic or mild claudication has a relatively stable limb prognosis; only 5--10\% of these patients progress to critical ischemia over 5 years. However, their systemic prognosis is poor: PAD is a major marker of generalized atherosclerosis. Patients with PAD have a 3-fold higher risk of death from cardiovascular causes compared to age-matched controls. For those presenting with Critical Limb-Threatening Ischemia, the prognosis is grave: within 1 year, up to 25\% will die, and 25--30\% will undergo a major amputation.
    \item \textbf{Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm}: The prognosis of an untreated AAA is directly related to its diameter. Aneurysms less than 4.0 cm have a rupture risk of near 0\% per year, whereas those greater than 5.5 cm have an annual rupture risk exceeding 5--10\%, which rises exponentially with size. Elective repair has excellent outcomes: 30-day perioperative mortality is less than 1.5\% for EVAR and 3--5\% for open repair. In contrast, ruptured AAA has a dismal prognosis, with a survival rate of less than 10--20\% overall.
    \item \textbf{Chronic Venous Insufficiency}: CVI is a chronic, progressive condition that is not life-threatening but severely impacts quality of life. Modern endovenous therapies are highly successful, with occlusion rates of target veins exceeding 90\% at 5 years. However, venous disease is recurrent; new varicose veins develop in approximately 20--30\% of patients within 5 years. Venous ulcers can take months to heal, and recurrence rates are up to 30--40\% without continuous compression therapy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventative strategies and daily self-care are essential to halt the development of vascular disorders, manage symptoms, and prevent complications.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular Health Habits}: Maintaining a healthy diet, engaging in at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise per week, and avoiding tobacco products are fundamental preventative measures.
    \item \textbf{Foot Care (Crucial for PAD and Diabetes)}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Inspect feet daily for cuts, scrapes, blisters, redness, swelling, or calluses. Use a mirror to inspect the soles.
        \item Wash feet daily in lukewarm water and dry thoroughly, especially between the toes.
        \item Moisturize feet daily to prevent dry skin cracking, but avoid applying cream between the toes.
        \item Wear well-fitting, supportive shoes and clean, dry socks. Never walk barefoot.
        \item Avoid self-treating corns or calluses; seek care from a podiatrist.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Compression Therapy}: For patients with CVI, wearing graduated compression stockings (Class II, 20--30 mmHg) daily is the most critical self-care measure. Compression assists the calf muscle pump and prevents venous pooling. Stockings must be worn from morning until evening and replaced every 3--6 months. Note: Compression is strictly contraindicated in patients with severe arterial disease (ABI less than 0.50).
    \item \textbf{Leg Elevation}: Elevating the legs above the level of the heart for 15--30 minutes, 3--4 times per day, utilizes gravity to drain venous blood, dramatically reducing swelling and heaviness.
    \item \textbf{Avoid Prolonged Immobility}: When traveling long distances or working at a desk, stand up, stretch, and walk for a few minutes every hour. Perform calf-muscle exercises (toe raises, ankle circles) while seated to stimulate blood flow.
    \item \textbf{Regular Monitoring}: Patients diagnosed with vascular disease should undergo routine clinical assessments, including ankle-brachial index checks, ultrasound surveillance for AAA, or duplex checks for graft patency.
\end{itemize}
