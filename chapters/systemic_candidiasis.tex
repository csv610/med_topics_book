\section*{Definition and Overview}
Systemic candidiasis, also called invasive candidiasis, is a serious infection in which Candida fungi enter the bloodstream and spread through the body to internal organs. Candida is a yeast that lives naturally in small numbers on the skin, in the mouth, and in the gut of most healthy people, where it usually causes no harm at all. The problem arises when the body's defences are weakened or when the fungus gains a direct route into the bloodstream, for example through a central intravenous line or through damaged bowel lining. Once in the blood, Candida can cause candidaemia, and from there it can seed the eyes, kidneys, liver, heart valves, brain, and bones.

This is quite different from the common and mild forms of candidiasis that most people know about, such as oral thrush, vaginal thrush, or nappy rash. Systemic candidiasis occurs almost exclusively in people who are already seriously unwell, particularly patients in hospital intensive care units, people receiving chemotherapy, transplant recipients, premature babies, and those with severe immune suppression. It is a hospital-acquired infection in the great majority of cases, and it is associated with significant illness and a real risk of death, so prevention, early recognition, and prompt antifungal treatment are vital.

This chapter explains who is at risk, how the infection develops, the signs and symptoms, and the tests and treatments used. Because systemic candidiasis is dangerous and fast-moving, most of its management happens in hospital, but understanding it helps patients, families, and carers to ask the right questions and to recognise what is happening if complications arise.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Systemic candidiasis is the most common serious fungal infection in hospitalised patients, and its incidence has risen steadily with the growth in intensive care, cancer treatment, transplantation, and the use of invasive devices. Candida is now one of the leading causes of hospital-acquired bloodstream infection worldwide, and in many countries it is among the most common causes of bloodstream infection in intensive care units. Most cases occur in adults, but there is a distinct and important pattern in newborns, where invasive candidiasis predominantly affects premature babies with very low birth weight.

Certain groups are at greatly increased risk: people with neutropenia after chemotherapy, solid-organ and bone-marrow transplant recipients, patients on long courses of broad-spectrum antibiotics, people receiving parenteral nutrition, and those with central venous catheters. The species involved have also changed over time. While Candida albicans remains the commonest species, non-albicans species such as Candida glabrata, Candida parapsilosis, and Candida tropicalis now account for a substantial proportion of cases, which matters because some of them are resistant to first-line antifungal drugs. This changing pattern drives the careful microbiology testing that accompanies treatment.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Systemic candidiasis begins when Candida, normally a harmless coloniser of the skin and gut, crosses a barrier that is normally intact. The two main routes are translocation across damaged bowel or skin, and direct entry into the blood through an intravenous catheter. Once in the bloodstream, the yeast forms filaments that invade tissues and travels to organs, where it can cause abscesses, inflammation, and small blood-vessel blockages.

The body's ability to control Candida depends heavily on white blood cells, especially neutrophils, and on an intact immune system. When these defences are depleted or overwhelmed, the fungus multiplies unchecked. Broad-spectrum antibiotics contribute by killing the competing bacteria that normally hold Candida numbers in check, allowing it to overgrow. The infection then behaves much like bacterial sepsis, with widespread inflammation that can lead to shock and organ failure, and the longer the fungus stays in the blood, the greater the chance that it establishes deep organ infections.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Central venous catheters:} Candida can enter the blood directly through the line, and the catheter may become colonised with a fungal biofilm
    \item \textbf{Neutropenia:} chemotherapy and some blood conditions deplete the neutrophils that are the main defence against Candida
    \item \textbf{Immunosuppression:} transplant medicines, steroids, and advanced HIV reduce immune control
    \item \textbf{Broad-spectrum antibiotics:} kill protective bacteria and allow Candida to overgrow in the gut
    \item \textbf{Abdominal surgery and parenteral nutrition:} damaged gut lining and feeding lines provide entry routes
    \item \textbf{Prematurity:} very low birth weight babies have immature immunity and thin, fragile skin
    \item \textbf{Diabetes and renal failure:} underlying chronic illness impairs immune function
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Systemic candidiasis produces few features that are unique to the infection, which is exactly why it is so challenging. Its hallmarks are unexplained fever and deterioration in a person who is already unwell and who is not improving on antibiotics.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Persistent fever:} fever that does not settle with broad-spectrum antibiotics is the classic presentation
    \item \textbf{Sepsis and shock:} falling blood pressure, rapid heart rate, and organ dysfunction, indistinguishable from bacterial sepsis
    \item \textbf{Skin lesions:} small, firm red nodules or pustules that can appear anywhere, sometimes with a pale centre
    \item \textbf{Eye involvement:} Candida endophthalmitis causes blurred vision, eye pain, or floaters, and is a common target organ
    \item \textbf{Kidney and liver abscesses:} deep collections of fungus that may be detected on imaging
    \item \textbf{Endocarditis:} infection of the heart valves, more common when a prosthetic valve is present
    \item \textbf{Meningitis:} Candida infection of the brain and spinal fluid, particularly in premature babies
    \item \textbf{Bone and joint infection:} osteomyelitis and arthritis, often appearing weeks later with pain and swelling
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the symptoms are non-specific, the treating team must systematically exclude other causes of deterioration in an unwell patient.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bacterial sepsis:} the most likely alternative, distinguished by blood cultures that grow bacteria rather than yeast
    \item \textbf{Other fungal infections:} aspergillosis, cryptococcosis, and mucormycosis can mimic invasive candidiasis and need different antifungals
    \item \textbf{Infective endocarditis:} bacterial infection of the heart valves, confirmed by blood cultures and echocardiography
    \item \textbf{Deep abscess:} collections from abdominal or surgical infection require imaging and drainage
    \item \textbf{Catheter-related infection:} other organisms colonising the line produce the same picture
    \item \textbf{Non-infectious causes of fever:} drug reactions, thrombophlebitis, and malignancy-related fever are all in the differential
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Systemic candidiasis develops in people who are already under hospital care, so the "seeking care" responsibility usually rests with the treating team rather than the patient. However, a person being treated at home for a complex condition, or a family member visiting someone in hospital, should alert staff immediately if any of the following develop.

\textbf{Seek urgent medical care for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever, shaking chills, or a general feeling of being severely unwell
    \item Rapid breathing, fast heart rate, confusion, or dizziness suggesting sepsis
    \item New skin nodules, pustules, or a rash that is spreading
    \item Blurred vision, eye pain, or new floaters
    \item Pain or swelling in a joint or bone that persists
\end{itemize}
Any of these in a person with risk factors warrants urgent assessment, and in hospital this should be escalated to the treating team without delay.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is made by growing the fungus from the blood or from a sterile body site, and treatment often begins before the results return because the infection moves fast.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood cultures:} the cornerstone of diagnosis; several bottles are drawn from different sites, but they can be negative in a substantial proportion of cases
    \item \textbf{Tissue and fluid cultures:} samples from skin lesions, abscesses, joint fluid, or spinal fluid are cultured and examined under the microscope
    \item \textbf{Candida biomarkers:} blood tests such as beta-D-glucan detect fungal cell-wall material and are useful when cultures are negative
    \item \textbf{Molecular testing:} PCR tests can detect Candida DNA in the blood quickly and identify the species
    \item \textbf{Fundoscopy:} a specialist eye examination looks for the characteristic flame-shaped retinal lesions of endophthalmitis
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT or ultrasound of the abdomen, and echocardiography, look for abscesses and valve infection
    \item \textbf{Susceptibility testing:} cultured yeast is tested against antifungal drugs to guide choice, especially for non-albicans species
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Systemic candidiasis requires rapid, aggressive treatment, almost always in hospital, and often in intensive care. The key principles are antifungal drugs, removing the source, and supporting failing organs.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Echinocandin antifungals:} drugs such as caspofungin, micafungin, and anidulafungin are the usual first-line treatment given intravenously
    \item \textbf{Fluconazole:} an oral or intravenous azole used for susceptible species, often as step-down therapy once the patient is stable
    \item \textbf{Lipid formulations of amphotericin B:} reserved for severe infection or resistant species because of kidney side effects
    \item \textbf{Removal of central lines:} infected intravenous catheters are removed wherever possible, because they are the most common source
    \item \textbf{Source control:} abscesses are drained surgically or radiologically, and the abdomen is washed out if infected
    \item \textbf{Duration:} treatment continues for two weeks after the blood cultures clear, and for weeks or months with deep-organ infection
    \item \textbf{Eye and valve involvement:} endophthalmitis and endocarditis need specialist treatment and longer courses
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} fluids, blood pressure support, and organ support in intensive care
    \item \textbf{Antifungal stewardship:} dosing is adjusted for kidney and liver function and checked against susceptibility results
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The complications of systemic candidiasis reflect both the infection itself and its tendency to seed organs during the bloodstream phase.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Septic shock:} profound low blood pressure and organ failure, the main cause of death
    \item \textbf{Endophthalmitis:} permanent scarring of the retina and vision loss if not treated promptly
    \item \textbf{Infective endocarditis:} valve damage that may eventually require valve replacement
    \item \textbf{Deep abscesses:} collections in the liver, spleen, kidney, and brain that may need drainage
    \item \textbf{Meningitis:} neurological damage, most concerning in premature babies
    \item \textbf{Osteomyelitis and septic arthritis:} bone and joint infection causing pain, deformity, and disability
    \item \textbf{Chronic disseminated candidiasis:} persistent fever and organ lesions after chemotherapy, sometimes called hepatosplenic candidiasis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Systemic candidiasis is a serious infection, and even with modern treatment the risk of death is substantial, with intensive care case fatality rates commonly quoted in the range of one in four to one in three. However, the outlook has improved steadily with earlier diagnosis, better antifungals, and prompt removal of infected lines. The factors that most strongly predict a good outcome are rapid initiation of treatment, removal of the source, and infection with a species that is susceptible to the drugs used. For patients who survive the acute infection, most make a full recovery, although those with deep-organ involvement may need prolonged treatment and rehabilitation.

Prevention is arguably more important than cure, because the infection is so difficult to treat once established. In the groups at highest risk, antifungal medicines are sometimes given preventively, lines are managed with meticulous care, and teams act quickly when fever persists despite antibiotics. For families, the message is that early escalation of concern in hospital, and honest communication with the treating team, genuinely improve the chances of a good outcome.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Strict hand hygiene by staff, patients, and visitors is the first line of defence in hospital
    \item Catheters and lines are removed as soon as they are no longer needed
    \item Patients at high risk may receive preventive (prophylactic) antifungal treatment in hospital
    \item Careful skin care, including keeping skin folds clean and dry, reduces Candida colonisation
    \item People with diabetes should aim for good glucose control, which strengthens immunity
    \item Broad-spectrum antibiotics are used only when needed, to avoid disturbing the natural balance of microbes
    \item After hospital, report any persistent fever, new skin lesions, or eye symptoms to a doctor promptly
\end{itemize}
