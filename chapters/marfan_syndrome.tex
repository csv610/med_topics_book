\chapter{Marfan syndrome}
\index{Marfan syndrome}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Marfan syndrome is an autosomal dominant, multisystemic connective tissue disorder characterised by abnormalities of the skeletal, cardiovascular, ocular, skin, and pulmonary systems. It is caused by mutations in the \textit{FBN1} gene, which encodes fibrillin-1, a crucial glycoprotein component of the extracellular matrix. Fibrillin-1 microfibrils provide structural support to various tissues and regulate the bioavailability of transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-$\beta$), a key cytokine involved in cell growth and tissue remodelling. Connective tissue weakness in Marfan syndrome leads to classic clinical features, including skeletal overgrowth, ectopia lentis (lens dislocation), and progressive aortic root dilatation. Cardiovascular complications, particularly thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection, represent the primary sources of morbidity and mortality.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Marfan syndrome is one of the most common inherited connective tissue disorders, with an estimated global prevalence of approximately 1 in 5,000 individuals. It occurs across all ethnic groups and geographical regions, with no gender predisposition. Approximately 75\% of patients have an affected parent from whom they inherited the mutated gene. The remaining 25\% of cases are due to \textit{de novo} mutations, where the mutation occurs spontaneously in the germline of a parent with no family history of the disease. The clinical expression of the syndrome is highly variable, even among family members sharing the same genetic mutation, demonstrating variable expressivity and complete penetrance.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The underlying pathophysiology of Marfan syndrome involves structural defects and dysregulated cytokine signalling in the extracellular matrix.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{FBN1 gene mutations:} Located on chromosome 15q21.1, the \textit{FBN1} gene spans 65 exons. Over 3,000 unique mutations (including missense, nonsense, frameshift, and splice-site mutations) have been identified, leading to either reduced synthesis of fibrillin-1 (haploinsufficiency) or synthesis of abnormal fibrillin-1 that disrupts normal microfibrils (dominant-negative effect).
    \item \textbf{Microfibrillar dysfunction:} Fibrillin-1 microfibrils serve as a scaffold for elastic fibre deposition. In their absence or dysfunction, elastic fibres in the aortic media, suspensory ligaments of the eye, and skin undergo fragmentation, reducing tissue elasticity and tensile strength.
    \item \textbf{TGF-beta dysregulation:} Normal microfibrils sequester TGF-$\beta$ in an inactive state. Deficiency of fibrillin-1 leads to excessive active TGF-$\beta$ in the extracellular space. This stimulates intracellular signalling pathways that cause excessive cellular proliferation, matrix metalloproteinase activation, and degradation of the extracellular matrix, particularly within the aortic wall.
    \item \textbf{Autosomal dominant inheritance:} A patient with Marfan syndrome has a 50\% chance of passing the genetic mutation to each of their children.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Marfan syndrome has a highly variable clinical presentation, with features manifesting and progressing throughout childhood and adulthood.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Skeletal abnormalities:} Tall, slender stature with disproportionately long limbs (dolichostenomelia) and long, thin fingers and toes (arachnodactyly). Patients often display joint hypermobility, a high-arched palate with dental crowding, scoliosis or kyphosis, and chest wall deformities such as pectus excavatum (sunken chest) or pectus carinatum (pigeon chest).
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular manifestations:} Mitral valve prolapse (MVP) with associated mitral regurgitation, progressive dilatation of the aortic root (sinuses of Valsalva), aortic valve regurgitation, and a high risk of acute thoracic aortic dissection or rupture.
    \item \textbf{Ocular features:} Ectopia lentis (subluxation of the lens, typically in an upward and temporal direction), severe myopia, flat cornea, early-onset cataracts, glaucoma, and an increased risk of retinal detachment.
    \item \textbf{Systemic skeletal signs:} Positive Steinberg (thumb) sign, where the thumb projects beyond the ulnar border of the clenched fist, and positive Walker-Murdoch (wrist) sign, where the thumb and fifth finger overlap when clasping the contralateral wrist.
    \item \textbf{Other systemic features:} Spontaneous pneumothorax due to apical subpleural blebs, dural ectasia (widening of the dural sac in the lumbosacral region, which can cause back pain and headaches), and striae atrophicae (stretch marks in unusual locations like the shoulders or back without significant weight changes).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because connective tissue disorders have overlapping skeletal and cardiovascular features, precise differentiation is crucial.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Loeys-Dietz syndrome:} An autosomal dominant disorder caused by mutations in TGF-$\beta$ receptor genes (\textit{TGFBR1} or \textit{TGFBR2}) or downstream signaling molecules. It shares skeletal and aortic aneurysm features but is distinguished by hypertelorism (wide-spaced eyes), bifid uvula, cleft palate, and widespread arterial tortuosity and aneurysms throughout the arterial tree.
    \item \textbf{Vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (vEDS):} Caused by \textit{COL3A1} mutations. It presents with hypermobility and joint laxity, but is characterised by thin, translucent skin, easy bruising, and a high risk of spontaneous arterial, bowel, or uterine rupture, without the tall stature or ectopia lentis of Marfan syndrome.
    \item \textbf{Homocystinuria:} An autosomal recessive metabolic disorder. It mimics the skeletal features of Marfan syndrome but presents with developmental delay, intellectual disability, high thromboembolic risk, and downward (rather than upward) lens dislocation.
    \item \textbf{MASS phenotype:} A mild connective tissue disorder that includes mitral valve prolapse, non-progressive aortic dilatation, skin striae, and skeletal features. It does not progress to severe aortic aneurysm or dissection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients with Marfan syndrome require lifetime monitoring. They and their families must be educated to recognise emergency symptoms.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe, sharp, or ripping chest, back, or abdominal pain (indicative of acute aortic dissection).
    \item Sudden onset of severe shortness of breath or difficulty breathing.
    \item Unexplained fainting, collapse, or loss of consciousness.
    \item Sudden, painless loss of vision or a sensation of a shadow falling over the field of vision (suggesting retinal detachment).
    \item Sudden weakness or numbness in the limbs or difficulty speaking.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of Marfan syndrome is clinical, based on the Revised Ghent Nosology, which integrates clinical findings, family history, and genetic testing.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Revised Ghent Nosology:} In the absence of a family history, the diagnosis is confirmed by finding an aortic root Z-score of 2 or more combined with ectopia lentis, a pathogenic \textit{FBN1} mutation, or a systemic score of 7 or more. In the presence of a positive family history, the diagnosis is confirmed by ectopia lentis, a systemic score of 7 or more, or an aortic root Z-score of 2 or more (or 3 or more in individuals under 20 years).
    \item \textbf{Echocardiography:} The primary imaging modality used to measure the aortic root diameter (Z-score adjusted for age and body surface area), assess mitral valve function, and monitor for progress of dilatation.
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} Sequencing of the \textit{FBN1} gene to identify pathogenic mutations, and testing of family members to enable pre-symptomatic diagnosis.
    \item \textbf{Slit-lamp ophthalmic examination:} Essential to detect subtle degrees of ectopia lentis, which may be missed on standard pupillary examination.
    \item \textbf{Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) or CT angiography:} Recommended to evaluate the entire aorta (thoracic and abdominal) for aneurysms or dissection, and to detect lumbosacral dural ectasia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is multidisciplinary, aimed at preventing life-threatening aortic dissection, correcting skeletal and ocular issues, and optimising quality of life.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological therapy:} Initiated to reduce hemodynamic stress on the aortic wall. First-line therapy includes beta-blockers (e.g., atenolol or metoprolol) to reduce heart rate and blood pressure. Angiotensin II Receptor Blockers (ARBs, e.g., losartan or irbesartan) are also highly effective, as they lower blood pressure and directly inhibit pathological TGF-$\beta$ signalling.
    \item \textbf{Surgical aortic intervention:} Prophylactic replacement of the aortic root is indicated when the aortic root diameter reaches 50 millimetres (or 45 millimetres in patients with risk factors such as rapid expansion, family history of dissection, or plans for pregnancy). Surgery options include the Bentall procedure (composite graft and mechanical valve replacement, requiring lifelong anticoagulation) or a valve-sparing aortic root replacement (David procedure).
    \item \textbf{Ocular management:} Corrective lenses for severe myopia, and surgical removal of the lens with intraocular lens implantation if ectopia lentis causes severe visual impairment or secondary glaucoma.
    \item \textbf{Skeletal management:} Monitoring scoliosis progression. Bracing is used for mild curves, while spinal fusion surgery is indicated for severe curves (greater than 40--50 degrees). Pectus deformities are managed conservatively, but can be corrected surgically for cardiorespiratory compromise or severe psychological distress.
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle modification:} Avoid competitive contact sports, heavy isometric exercise (weightlifting), and activities that cause sudden changes in atmospheric pressure (e.g., scuba diving) to reduce the risk of aortic dissection and pneumothorax.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The complications of Marfan syndrome can be severe, requiring urgent medical or surgical intervention.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Aortic dissection and rupture:} The most critical complication, where the inner layer of the aortic wall tears, allowing blood to flow between the layers. This can cause acute pericardial tamponade, stroke, visceral ischaemia, or death.
    \item \textbf{Heart failure:} Progressive mitral or aortic regurgitation leads to volume overload, left ventricular dilation, and congestive heart failure.
    \item \textbf{Retinal detachment:} Separation of the retina from the choroid, leading to permanent vision loss if not repaired surgically.
    \item \textbf{Spontaneous pneumothorax:} Air accumulation in the pleural space, leading to lung collapse and respiratory distress.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Historically, the prognosis for patients with Marfan syndrome was poor, with a median life expectancy of approximately 30 to 40 years, primarily due to premature death from aortic dissection or heart failure. With modern medical care, including early diagnosis, beta-blocker/ARB therapy, and timely prophylactic aortic root surgery, the life expectancy of individuals with Marfan syndrome now approaches that of the general population. Long-term outcomes are heavily dependent on regular cardiovascular surveillance and strict adherence to lifestyle and medical therapies.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
While Marfan syndrome cannot be cured, self-care practices can significantly reduce the risk of complications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adhere to cardiac surveillance:} Attend scheduled echocardiograms (typically annually, or more frequently if the aortic root is dilating rapidly).
    \item \textbf{Strict medication compliance:} Take prescribed beta-blockers or ARBs daily, and monitor resting blood pressure and heart rate.
    \item \textbf{Engage in safe exercise:} Focus on low-impact, moderate-intensity aerobic activities such as walking, swimming, and gentle cycling, while avoiding activities with high strain or impact.
    \item \textbf{Pre-pregnancy counselling:} Women with Marfan syndrome must consult a high-risk obstetrician and cardiologist before conceiving, as pregnancy significantly increases cardiac output and the risk of aortic dissection, especially if the aortic root exceeds 40 millimetres.
    \item \textbf{Avoid smoking:} To protect lung health and reduce the risk of bleb rupture and pneumothorax.
\end{itemize}
