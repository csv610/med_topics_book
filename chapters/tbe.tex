\section*{Definition and Overview}
Tick-borne encephalitis, usually shortened to TBE, is a viral infection of the central nervous system that is spread by the bite of infected ticks. It is caused by the tick-borne encephalitis virus, a member of the flavivirus family, which also includes the viruses that cause Japanese encephalitis, West Nile disease, and dengue. The infection begins like a common flu-like illness, but in some people it progresses to cause inflammation of the brain and the membranes around it, leading to meningitis, encephalitis, or both. TBE occurs in a large belt of countries across central, northern, and eastern Europe, Scandinavia, and northern Asia, and it is an important travel-health issue because there is no specific treatment for the virus itself. In many countries, TBE is not found in local ticks, so the condition matters mainly for travellers going to regions where the disease is endemic, and, very rarely, for travellers who return with symptoms after a tick bite overseas. There is a safe and effective vaccine, and it is the single most important measure for people heading into at-risk areas. This chapter explains what TBE is, where and how it is caught, the symptoms that should raise concern, how it is diagnosed and treated, and the prevention measures, including vaccination, that travellers should discuss with their doctor before departure.

\section*{Epidemiology}
TBE is one of the most important tick-borne viral diseases in the world, with an estimated ten thousand or more cases reported each year across Europe and Asia, and the number is rising as ticks spread to new areas with climate change and changing land use. The disease is seasonal, occurring mainly from spring to autumn, when ticks are active and people spend time outdoors in forests, meadows, and parks. The highest-risk countries include Austria, the Czech Republic, Germany, Switzerland, Poland, the Baltic states, Scandinavia, and Russia. Within a risk area, not every tick carries the virus, and not every infected bite leads to illness, but the risk is real enough that many European countries now run national vaccination programmes. TBE is not endemic in many countries: the ticks found here, including the local paralysis tick, carry other diseases but not the TBE virus, so the infection is essentially confined to travellers returning from affected regions. Most people who are bitten never develop symptoms, and of those who do, the majority have only the mild first phase, but a substantial minority develop neurological disease, which is why vaccination and tick precautions matter for anyone travelling to at-risk regions.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
TBE is caused by a flavivirus that is transmitted between animals and humans by ticks, and the human disease follows a characteristic sequence.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The virus:} tick-borne encephalitis virus is a small RNA virus with three main subtypes, named European, Siberian, and Far Eastern, which differ in severity and in how often they cause long-term damage
    \item \textbf{The vector:} the virus is carried mainly by hard-bodied ticks of the Ixodes family, especially Ixodes ricinus in Europe and Ixodes persulcatus further east, which feed on small mammals, birds, and people
    \item \textbf{Tick-bite transmission:} the virus is transmitted in the saliva of an infected tick when it bites, usually while the tick has been attached for several hours; nymph-stage ticks, which are tiny and easily missed, transmit most infections
    \item \textbf{Alimentary transmission:} rarely, the virus is caught by drinking unpasteurised milk or dairy products from infected goats, sheep, or cows, a route seen in some outbreaks in rural Europe
    \item \textbf{Incubation and spread:} after the bite the virus multiplies locally and in the lymph system, enters the blood, and then, in some people, crosses the blood-brain barrier to infect nerve tissue
    \item \textbf{The biphasic pattern:} the early phase reflects the virus in the blood, while the second, neurological phase reflects invasion of the central nervous system
\end{itemize}

The tick's feeding habit explains why removing ticks promptly and checking for them after outdoor activity is protective, and why protective clothing and repellents are the first line of defence.

\section*{Clinical Features}
TBE is famous for its two-phase course, and the symptoms differ between the phases.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First phase, flu-like:} about one to two weeks after the bite, fever, headache, muscle aches, tiredness, and a general feeling of being unwell develop, lasting about two to seven days
    \item \textbf{Apparent recovery:} many people then improve and feel normal for days to a week, which can lull the traveller into thinking the illness has passed
    \item \textbf{Second phase, neurological:} in about a third of people with symptoms, the fever returns with headache and the nervous system becomes involved, producing meningitis, encephalitis, or both
    \item \textbf{Meningitis symptoms:} severe headache, neck stiffness, sensitivity to light, and nausea
    \item \textbf{Encephalitis symptoms:} confusion, drowsiness, difficulty concentrating, memory problems, and altered behaviour
    \item \textbf{Cerebellar signs:} unsteady walking, tremor, clumsiness, and difficulty coordinating movements
    \item \textbf{Seizures:} convulsions occur in a minority and indicate severe brain involvement
    \item \textbf{Long-lasting symptoms:} fatigue, headaches, and difficulty with memory or mood can persist for months or years after the acute illness settles
\end{itemize}

The severity varies with the viral subtype: the Far Eastern subtype is more often severe and can progress rapidly, while the European subtype more typically follows the mild two-phase pattern.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the early phase of TBE looks like many common fevers, and the neurological phase resembles several serious infections, careful testing is needed.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Influenza and other viral fevers:} the first phase of TBE is indistinguishable from influenza and other flu-like viruses without a history of tick exposure and later testing
    \item \textbf{Lyme disease:} also spread by European ticks, it causes fever, headache, and often a characteristic expanding rash; it is distinguished from TBE by blood tests and responds to antibiotics, unlike TBE
    \item \textbf{Other tick-borne infections:} infections such as Q fever, anaplasmosis, and rickettsial spotted fevers can follow tick bites and are separated by specific blood tests
    \item \textbf{Bacterial meningitis:} a medical emergency with fever, headache, and neck stiffness that is diagnosed by lumbar puncture and treated with antibiotics
    \item \textbf{Herpes simplex encephalitis:} a treatable viral encephalitis that must be considered in anyone with fever and confusion, and which needs urgent antiviral treatment while tests are pending
    \item \textbf{Other flaviviruses:} Japanese encephalitis, West Nile virus, and dengue can produce similar pictures in relevant travellers
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune encephalitis:} some non-infectious brain inflammations look similar but are diagnosed by specific antibodies and imaging
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who has visited a TBE-endemic area and develops a fever should mention the travel history to a doctor, because the diagnosis is easily missed without it. Seek medical review promptly if fever is followed by a severe headache, neck stiffness, sensitivity to light, confusion, or unusual tiredness. Returning travellers should tell the doctor the countries visited, time spent outdoors, and any tick bites. There is no specific medicine for the virus, so the value of early medical care lies in the diagnosis, in ruling out treatable infections such as bacterial meningitis, and in arranging hospital care if the nervous system is affected.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A seizure, or any episode of unconsciousness
    \item Severe confusion, inability to wake, or a sudden change in behaviour
    \item Difficulty breathing or swallowing
    \item Severe weakness or paralysis of an arm, leg, or face
    \item Stiff neck with a high fever and severe headache, which can also signal bacterial meningitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of TBE rests on the right test at the right time, combined with the travel and tick history.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood serology:} antibody tests for the virus in the blood are the mainstay; the first phase is confirmed by antibodies appearing as the fever resolves, and a second sample may be needed to show a rising level
    \item \textbf{Cerebrospinal fluid testing:} a lumbar puncture is performed when the nervous system is involved, to look for inflammation, to test for antibodies, and, importantly, to exclude bacterial meningitis
    \item \textbf{PCR testing:} early in the illness, before antibodies have formed, the virus's genetic material can sometimes be detected by PCR in the blood; this window is brief
    \item \textbf{Vaccination status review:} the interpretation of antibody tests depends on whether the person has been vaccinated, because antibodies also follow vaccination
    \item \textbf{Brain imaging:} an MRI scan may show characteristic changes in the brain and helps exclude other causes of encephalitis
    \item \textbf{Exclusion testing:} tests for bacterial meningitis, herpes simplex virus, and other flaviviruses are run in parallel because the treatments differ
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
There is no antiviral medicine that cures TBE, so treatment is supportive and focuses on keeping the patient safe while the immune system clears the virus.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hospital admission:} anyone with neurological symptoms is admitted to hospital, where breathing, consciousness, and vital signs can be monitored
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} fluids, pain relief, fever control, and rest form the backbone of treatment in both phases
    \item \textbf{Management of brain swelling:} if raised pressure inside the skull develops, positioning, medicines, and careful monitoring are used to reduce it
    \item \textbf{Seizure control:} anti-seizure medicines are used if convulsions occur
    \item \textbf{Respiratory support:} people with severe disease may need oxygen or a ventilator in an intensive-care unit
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics while waiting:} because bacterial meningitis is treatable, antibiotics are often started immediately and stopped once tests show they are not needed
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and speech therapy help people recover strength, balance, memory, and swallowing
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} people with persistent fatigue or memory problems are followed up to support their return to work and daily life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Post-encephalitic syndrome:} prolonged fatigue, headaches, and concentration, memory, and mood problems that can last for months to years, affecting a substantial minority of those who have encephalitis
    \item \textbf{Permanent paralysis:} weakness, most often of the shoulders and arms, can persist, and in severe cases a poliomyelitis-like paralysis occurs
    \item \textbf{Death:} the fatality rate is around one percent for the European subtype but higher for the Far Eastern subtype, and rises with age
    \item \textbf{Seizure disorders:} epilepsy can follow severe encephalitis
    \item \textbf{Cognitive and behavioural change:} long-term memory loss or personality change is possible after severe brain inflammation
    \item \textbf{Older travellers:} TBE is more severe in older adults, in whom hospitalisation and long-term disability are more common
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for TBE depends heavily on age and on whether the nervous system becomes involved. Most people who are bitten and infected never develop symptoms, and of those who develop the mild first phase only, nearly all recover fully. When the second, neurological phase occurs, most people eventually recover, but recovery can be slow and incomplete. Around a third to half of people with encephalitis are left with long-term symptoms, most commonly fatigue, difficulty concentrating, and memory or mood problems, and a minority have lasting weakness or paralysis. Death is uncommon in the European subtype but more frequent in the Siberian and Far Eastern subtypes and in older people. There is no treatment that can change the course once the virus reaches the brain, which is why the emphasis falls so heavily on prevention. Vaccination is highly effective and gives travellers to risk areas the best protection, while tick precautions reduce the chance of ever being bitten.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discuss TBE vaccination with your GP or a travel-health clinic well before travel to central, eastern, or northern Europe, Scandinavia, Russia, or northern Asia, and complete the course before you leave
    \item Wear long sleeves, long trousers, and closed shoes when walking in forested or grassy areas, and tuck trousers into socks
    \item Use an effective insect repellent containing DEET or picaridin on exposed skin, and reapply as directed
    \item Check your whole body for ticks after being outdoors, including the scalp, behind the ears, and in skin folds, and check children and pets too
    \item Remove any attached tick promptly with fine-tipped tweezers, grasping it close to the skin and pulling straight out without twisting
    \item Avoid unpasteurised milk and dairy products in endemic areas, because the virus can be transmitted through them
    \item Remember that tick risk is seasonal and rises with time spent outdoors, so plan activities and protective measures accordingly
    \item Tell any doctor you see about your travel history if you become unwell after returning from a TBE-risk area
\end{itemize}
