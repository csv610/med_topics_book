\section*{Definition and Overview}
Coma is a state of deep unconsciousness in which a person cannot be woken, does not respond to pain, sound, or touch, and does not open their eyes. It is caused by damage to the parts of the brain that control wakefulness and awareness. A persistent vegetative state (now often called the vegetative state or unresponsive wakefulness syndrome) is a disorder of consciousness that can follow a coma: the person wakes in the sense that their eyes open and they have sleep--wake cycles, but they show no meaningful awareness of themselves or their surroundings. A person may remain in a vegetative state for weeks, months, or longer; when it lasts beyond a month it is described as persistent, and when it lasts for more than a year (or six months after a head injury) it may be called permanent. Coma and the vegetative state are emergencies that require urgent medical care and long-term specialist support.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Coma is a common reason for admission to intensive care units. Head injury is the leading cause of coma in younger people, while stroke, cardiac arrest, and drug overdose become more important causes in older age groups. The vegetative state is uncommon but devastating; it follows severe brain injury in a small proportion of survivors of cardiac arrest, major head injury, or stroke. There are no simple figures for how often it occurs, but it affects people of all ages, including children. Recovery is most likely in younger people and in the first few months after the injury, and the longer the state persists, the lower the chance of meaningful recovery.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Coma and the vegetative state result from widespread damage to the brain's arousal system (the reticular activating system) or to both sides of the cerebral hemispheres:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Traumatic brain injury:} diffuse damage to the brain from a major blow or shaking injury, often with bleeding or bruising
    \item \textbf{Stroke:} haemorrhage into the brain or blockage of the blood supply to large areas, often affecting both sides or the brainstem
    \item \textbf{Lack of oxygen:} cardiac arrest, drowning, or asphyxia causing global brain damage within minutes
    \item \textbf{Metabolic and toxic causes:} severe hypoglycaemia, liver or kidney failure, very high or low sodium, or overdose of drugs such as sedatives, opioids, and alcohol
    \item \textbf{Infections:} meningitis, encephalitis, or severe sepsis
    \item \textbf{Brain tumours and swelling:} pressure on the brainstem or both hemispheres
\end{itemize}

The brain needs a constant supply of glucose and oxygen. When this is interrupted, or the brain is compressed, the circuits that keep a person awake and aware fail. In the vegetative state the lower brainstem survives enough to keep the person breathing, sleeping, and opening their eyes, but the higher brain (cortex) that sustains awareness is severely damaged.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coma:} eyes remain closed, the person does not wake to voice or pain, and there is no purposeful response, speech, or movement
    \item \textbf{Vegetative state:} eyes open spontaneously and there are sleep--wake cycles, but no awareness of self or surroundings, no communication, and no purposeful responses
    \item Reflex movements may persist: blinking, swallowing, yawning, and withdrawing from pain are reflex, not signs of awareness
    \item The person may breathe unaided, and heart rate and blood pressure may be stable
    \item Crying, groaning, or moving the limbs can occur as reflex activity without meaning
    \item Complications of immobility: muscle wasting, joint stiffness, bedsores, and infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A person who appears unresponsive or unaware may have conditions that must be distinguished:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Locked-in syndrome:} a rare state after brainstem damage in which the person is fully aware but cannot move or speak, often communicating only by eye movements
    \item \textbf{Minimally conscious state:} severe brain damage with intermittent, inconsistent signs of awareness that are not present in the vegetative state
    \item \textbf{Sedation or drug effects:} overdose or anaesthetic drugs causing deep unresponsiveness that resolves as the drug wears off
    \item \textbf{Psychogenic unresponsiveness:} an uncommon psychiatric state that mimics coma but with normal brain scans and some preserved responses
    \item \textbf{Severe paralysis with sedation:} where weakness and medication combine to mimic unconsciousness
    \item \textbf{Epilepsy:} prolonged seizure activity (non-convulsive status) can mimic coma
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any unexplained loss of consciousness or sudden unresponsiveness is a medical emergency. Call for help immediately.

\textbf{Call 000 for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A person who cannot be woken, does not respond to you, or is not breathing normally
    \item Collapse with head injury, seizure, or known heart disease
    \item Sudden weakness, facial droop, or difficulty speaking before loss of consciousness (stroke)
    \item Breathing stops or becomes very slow or irregular
    \item Confusion or drowsiness developing in someone with diabetes, after drug use, or after a head injury
\end{itemize}

While waiting for help, place the person on their side if they are breathing, keep them warm, and do not give them anything to eat or drink.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The cause and prognosis of coma are established through urgent, coordinated assessment:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} details of how the person was found, any injury, drugs, medicines, or illnesses, and time of onset
    \item \textbf{Neurological examination:} the Glasgow Coma Scale (eye, verbal, and motor responses) is used repeatedly to track change, together with pupil and reflex checks
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} glucose, electrolytes, liver and kidney function, infection markers, blood gases, and drug screens
    \item \textbf{Brain imaging:} urgent CT scan to detect bleeding, stroke, swelling, or tumour; MRI may add detail later
    \item \textbf{Electroencephalogram (EEG):} records brain electrical activity and can detect seizures or patterns suggesting awareness
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} repeated examinations by a specialist team over days to weeks to look for any signs of improvement
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Emergency stabilisation:} securing the airway, ensuring breathing and circulation, and treating the cause (e.g.\ glucose for hypoglycaemia, antidotes for overdose, surgery for a brain bleed)
    \item \textbf{Intensive care:} ventilation, control of brain pressure, seizure treatment, and careful blood pressure management
    \item \textbf{Preventing complications:} regular turning to prevent pressure sores, physiotherapy for the lungs and joints, eye and mouth care, and prevention of blood clots
    \item \textbf{Nutrition:} feeding through a tube into the stomach once the gut is working, with fluids and calories matched to needs
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} specialist teams in neurology, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, speech therapy, and psychology work to find and develop any response
    \item \textbf{Prognostication:} careful, repeated assessment over months, usually in a specialist centre, before conclusions about the chance of recovery are drawn
    \item \textbf{Communication and support:} honest, compassionate discussion with family, who may also be offered counselling and support groups
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pneumonia, especially from food or fluid entering the lungs (aspiration)
    \item Pressure sores and skin breakdown from prolonged immobility
    \item Blood clots in the legs (deep vein thrombosis) and their complications
    \item Muscle wasting, joint contractures, and spasticity
    \item Constipation, bowel or bladder problems, and urinary infections
    \item Seizures and recurrent infections such as chest and urine infections
    \item Severe psychological distress in family members and carers
    \item Disagreements within families and between families and clinicians about ongoing treatment and care goals
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Outcomes depend on the cause, the extent of brain damage, and the person's age. Coma that follows a drug overdose or metabolic upset may resolve completely in days as the cause is treated. After cardiac arrest or severe head injury, recovery is uncertain and often slow: most improvement, when it happens, occurs in the first three to six months. A small proportion of people regain awareness, and a smaller number go on to a good functional recovery; a significant number of those who recover are left with permanent physical or cognitive disability. If a vegetative state has persisted for more than a year after trauma, or several months after other causes, meaningful recovery is extremely unlikely, although exceptions are reported. Each case is assessed individually, and families should receive specialist, honest guidance.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wear seatbelts and helmets, and follow road safety rules to prevent head injury
    \item Prevent falls in older people by removing trip hazards, improving lighting, and reviewing medicines that cause dizziness
    \item Manage diabetes, high blood pressure, and heart disease to reduce the risk of stroke
    \item Call emergency services immediately for chest pain or stroke symptoms, because early treatment protects the brain
    \item Never leave someone unconscious or drowsy after a head injury or overdose without urgent assessment
    \item Store medicines, including strong painkillers and sedatives, safely and out of reach of children
    \item Keep a list of the person's conditions and medicines to give to ambulance crews
    \item Support families through rehabilitation services and carer organisations
\end{itemize}
