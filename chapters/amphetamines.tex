\chapter{Amphetamines}
\index{Amphetamines}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Amphetamines are a group of stimulant drugs that increase alertness, energy, and concentration by boosting the activity of dopamine, noradrenaline, and serotonin in the brain. They have legitimate medical uses, principally in the treatment of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and narcolepsy, at low, carefully controlled doses. The same drugs are also widely misused for their euphoric and performance-enhancing effects, and related substances include methamphetamine (crystal meth or ice) and the party drug MDMA (ecstasy). Misuse of amphetamines is associated with dependence, overdose, psychosis, and significant physical harm, including cardiac and neurological damage. The distinction between prescribed therapeutic use and recreational misuse is central to understanding their benefits and risks.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Amphetamines are among the most widely used illicit drugs worldwide after cannabis. Tens of millions of people report using amphetamines, methamphetamine, or ecstasy each year, and use is most common in young adults, particularly men. Prescribed use of stimulants for ADHD has risen substantially in many countries over recent decades. Dependence develops in a minority of users, but emergency presentations with agitation, psychosis, and cardiac complications have increased, particularly in regions where high-potency methamphetamine is readily available. Methamphetamine use is associated with markedly higher rates of severe harm than pharmaceutical amphetamines.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Amphetamines exert their effects by increasing the concentration of monoamine neurotransmitters in the brain:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} the drugs release dopamine, noradrenaline, and serotonin from nerve endings and block their reuptake, amplifying signalling.
    \item \textbf{Effects:} this produces arousal, euphoria, increased energy and focus, and appetite suppression, with sympathetic effects on the heart and blood vessels.
    \item \textbf{Medical use:} low, controlled doses improve attention, impulse control, and wakefulness in ADHD and narcolepsy.
    \item \textbf{High-dose misuse:} large doses or repeated bingeing overstimulate the cardiovascular and nervous systems, with risk of toxicity and overdose.
    \item \textbf{Neurotoxicity:} chronic high-dose use can damage dopamine and serotonin nerve endings over time, with lasting cognitive and mood effects.
    \item \textbf{Risk factors for misuse:} a family history of substance use disorders, mental illness, early exposure to drugs, and peer and social factors all increase risk.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The effects depend on the dose, route, and pattern of use:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intoxication:} euphoria, increased energy and talkativeness, heightened alertness, and a sense of wellbeing.
    \item \textbf{Physical signs:} raised heart rate and blood pressure, dilated pupils, sweating, tremor, and a dry mouth.
    \item \textbf{Psychological effects:} agitation, anxiety, irritability, paranoia, and sometimes psychosis with hallucinations.
    \item \textbf{Other effects:} poor appetite, difficulty sleeping, and teeth grinding (bruxism).
    \item \textbf{Severe toxicity:} chest pain, palpitations, very high body temperature, seizures, and loss of consciousness.
    \item \textbf{Withdrawal:} extreme tiredness, increased appetite and sleep, depression, irritability, and intense craving, typically beginning as the drug wears off.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Stimulant intoxication can be confused with psychiatric and other medical conditions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Other stimulant drugs, including cocaine, MDMA, caffeine excess, and prescribed stimulants.
    \item Psychiatric disorders, including mania, anxiety, panic disorder, and schizophrenia.
    \item Hyperthyroidism, which causes tremor, sweating, and tachycardia.
    \item Heart rhythm disorders and other cardiac problems.
    \item Infection or fever causing agitation and tachycardia.
    \item Serotonin syndrome and neuroleptic malignant syndrome with other drugs.
    \item Withdrawal states, low blood sugar, and depression, which can mimic the crash phase.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek help if a person is using amphetamines heavily, experiencing anxiety, paranoia, depression, or psychosis, or struggling to cut down. Prescribed stimulant doses should be reviewed regularly by the prescribing doctor, and any significant adverse effects reported promptly.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, palpitations, or collapse after taking stimulants.
    \item Severe agitation, paranoia, or hallucinations.
    \item Very high temperature, profuse sweating, or seizures.
    \item Loss of consciousness.
    \item Suicidal thoughts or talk of self-harm.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment combines history, mental state examination, and physical checks:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the pattern, dose, and route of use (swallowed, inhaled, smoked, or injected), other drugs taken, and any history of mental or physical illness.
    \item \textbf{Mental state examination:} assessment of mood, agitation, paranoia, psychosis, and risk to self or others.
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, and pupillary signs.
    \item \textbf{Electrocardiogram (ECG):} to check for heart rhythm abnormalities.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} electrolytes, kidney function, and muscle enzymes in severe toxicity.
    \item \textbf{Drug screening:} urine or blood tests may confirm the drug and guide management.
    \item \textbf{Assessment of related harms:} testing for hepatitis B and C and HIV in people who inject.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on whether the presentation is acute toxicity, withdrawal, or dependence:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Toxicity:} urgent hospital care with sedation, active cooling, and treatment of hypertension, arrhythmias, and seizures.
    \item \textbf{Acute agitation and psychosis:} a calm, low-stimulation environment, reassurance, and short-term medication under medical supervision.
    \item \textbf{Withdrawal:} supportive care; symptoms are distressing but rarely medically dangerous, though depression during withdrawal requires monitoring.
    \item \textbf{Dependence treatment:} counselling and behavioural therapies are the mainstay, including cognitive behavioural therapy and contingency management; no medication is approved to substitute for amphetamines.
    \item \textbf{Mental health care:} treatment of coexisting depression, anxiety, and psychosis.
    \item \textbf{Harm reduction:} needle exchange programs, supervised injecting services, and safer-use advice.
    \item \textbf{Medical use:} prescribed stimulants are taken at low, carefully adjusted doses with specialist review.
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} ongoing support and relapse-prevention programs reduce relapse and overdose risk.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Heavy and long-term use carries serious physical and mental harms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Dependence and addiction.
    \item Stimulant psychosis, which can persist after stopping the drug.
    \item Heart attack, stroke, and cardiac rhythm problems.
    \item Very high body temperature, seizures, and kidney damage in severe overdose.
    \item Malnutrition and significant weight loss.
    \item Dental damage, known as meth mouth, from dry mouth and poor self-care.
    \item Infections with injecting use, including hepatitis B and C, HIV, and skin abscesses.
    \item Damage to relationships, employment, and finances.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people who use amphetamines occasionally do not become dependent, but heavy and compulsive use carries a high risk of harm. Dependence is difficult but treatable: many people achieve abstinence or substantial reduction in use with counselling and support, although relapse is common. Psychosis and cardiac complications generally improve when use stops, though some effects may persist. Medical use of stimulants for ADHD is generally safe and effective when supervised, and it does not carry the same risks as recreational misuse.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Harm reduction and early support are the main preventive strategies:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Never share needles or injecting equipment.
    \item Keep doses low and avoid mixing with other drugs, especially alcohol.
    \item Drink water and avoid overheating while using.
    \item Seek help early for mental health problems rather than self-medicating.
    \item Talk openly with a doctor about stimulant use to access support and treatment.
    \item Follow prescribed stimulant doses exactly and never share prescribed medicines.
    \item Store prescription stimulants securely to prevent misuse by others.
\end{itemize}
