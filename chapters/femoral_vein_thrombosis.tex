\chapter{Femoral Vein Thrombosis}
\index{Femoral Vein Thrombosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Femoral vein thrombosis is a blood clot (deep vein thrombosis, DVT) that forms in the femoral vein, one of the large deep veins of the thigh. A DVT can occur in any deep vein, but clots in the thigh and groin, sometimes called proximal DVTs, are particularly important because they are more likely to break off and travel to the lungs, causing a pulmonary embolism, which can be fatal. Femoral vein thrombosis usually causes pain and swelling of the leg, though it can occur silently. It is a medical emergency that requires urgent diagnosis and treatment with blood-thinning medicines. This chapter explains the causes, symptoms, diagnosis, and treatment of this potentially dangerous condition.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Deep vein thrombosis affects around 1--2 people per 1,000 each year, and DVTs of the thigh veins are among the most common serious presentations. The incidence rises steeply with age, and risk is higher after surgery, during hospital admission, with cancer, and during pregnancy and the postpartum period. Proximal DVTs such as femoral vein thrombosis are more dangerous than calf-vein clots: if untreated, a substantial proportion can extend or embolise. Pulmonary embolism from a proximal DVT is a leading preventable cause of hospital death, which is why hospitals use routine prevention measures and why urgent treatment of DVT is standard.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Virchow's triad:} clots form when there is stasis of blood flow, damage to the vein wall, and a tendency to clot excessively (hypercoagulability)
    \item \textbf{Immobility:} prolonged bed rest, long-haul travel, plaster casts, and paralysis slow blood flow in the leg veins
    \item \textbf{Surgery and trauma:} tissue injury and immobility after operations, especially hip and knee surgery, provoke clotting
    \item \textbf{Cancer:} malignancy activates the clotting system and is a major risk factor
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy and the pill:} hormonal changes, including combined oral contraceptives and HRT, increase clotting tendency
    \item \textbf{Inherited thrombophilia:} genetic conditions such as factor V Leiden increase risk
    \item \textbf{Previous DVT or family history:} the strongest predictors of a first or recurrent clot
    \item \textbf{Mechanism of harm:} the clot obstructs venous return, causing leg swelling and pain; if it detaches, it travels through the heart to the lung arteries as a pulmonary embolism
    \item \textbf{Chronic damage:} even after treatment, valve damage can cause the post-thrombotic syndrome of chronic swelling and skin changes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unilateral leg swelling, most often of the whole leg below the groin
    \item Pain, heaviness, or tenderness in the calf or thigh
    \item A feeling of warmth and red or dusky discolouration of the skin
    \item A visibly larger calf or thigh compared with the other leg
    \item A tense, shiny appearance of the skin in severe cases
    \item Sometimes no symptoms at all, with the clot found incidentally
    \item Symptoms of pulmonary embolism, which can be the first presentation: sudden breathlessness, chest pain, coughing blood, or collapse
    \item The affected leg may be pale or blue in extensive obstruction (phlegmasia), a limb-threatening emergency
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Leg pain and swelling have many causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Muscle strain, tears, and haematoma
    \item A ruptured Baker's cyst behind the knee
    \item Cellulitis, a bacterial skin infection
    \item Lymphoedema, chronic lymphatic swelling
    \item Superficial thrombophlebitis, a clot in a surface vein
    \item Heart, kidney, or liver failure causing generalised fluid retention
    \item Chronic venous insufficiency with varicose veins
    \item A popliteal aneurysm or other vascular conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with sudden, one-sided leg swelling or pain should be assessed urgently, as DVT needs rapid diagnosis and treatment. Seek medical care the same day, through a GP, urgent care, or emergency department.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden breathlessness, chest pain, or coughing blood, which may indicate a pulmonary embolism
    \item Collapse, fainting, or a racing heart
    \item A very swollen, painful, pale, or blue leg (possible extensive clot threatening the limb)
    \item Sudden severe headache or weakness, which may indicate a clot elsewhere
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} the doctor applies a clinical probability score (such as the Wells score) based on risk factors and examination
    \item \textbf{D-dimer blood test:} a negative D-dimer reliably excludes DVT in low-risk patients; a positive result is not specific and requires imaging
    \item \textbf{Venous ultrasound (Doppler):} the main diagnostic test, showing the clot and its extent in the deep veins
    \item \textbf{CT pulmonary angiography:} for suspected pulmonary embolism
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, kidney function, and investigations for underlying causes
    \item \textbf{Thrombophilia testing:} considered in younger patients, recurrent clots, or a strong family history, usually after treatment
    \item \textbf{Cancer screening:} based on age and risk factors, because a first unprovoked DVT can reveal malignancy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anticoagulant medicines:} the cornerstone of treatment, given as injections (such as enoxaparin), tablets (rivaroxaban, apixaban), or warfarin, usually for at least 3 months
    \item \textbf{Compression stockings:} reduce swelling and the risk of post-thrombotic syndrome
    \item \textbf{Leg elevation and early mobilisation:} once anticoagulation is started, walking is encouraged rather than bed rest
    \item \textbf{Thrombolysis:} clot-dissolving drugs for extensive clots threatening the limb, given in intensive care settings
    \item \textbf{Filter in the inferior vena cava:} a device that catches emboli, used only when anticoagulation is not possible
    \item \textbf{Treat the cause:} stop hormonal contraceptives where implicated, treat cancer, and address immobility
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} repeat ultrasound where needed, monitoring of anticoagulation, and review of duration of treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pulmonary embolism, which can be fatal or cause lasting lung damage
    \item Post-thrombotic syndrome: chronic leg swelling, pain, and skin changes from valve damage
    \item Bleeding from anticoagulant treatment, including serious bleeding requiring urgent care
    \item Recurrent DVT, especially when treatment is stopped early or the cause persists
    \item Limb-threatening venous obstruction in the rare, severe form called phlegmasia
    \item Chronic venous ulcers in the long term
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt anticoagulant treatment, most femoral vein thromboses resolve and the risk of pulmonary embolism falls quickly. Treatment is usually continued for 3--6 months, and longer when the clot was unprovoked, recurrent, or in a high-risk patient. Around a third of patients develop some degree of post-thrombotic syndrome, but this is minimised by compression stockings and early mobilisation. The risk of recurrence after finishing treatment is roughly 10--15\% in the first year for unprovoked clots and lower for provoked ones. Overall, with treatment and appropriate follow-up, the outlook for femoral vein thrombosis is very good.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item After surgery or hospital admission, follow the hospital's DVT prevention plan, including anticoagulant injections and early mobilisation
    \item During long travel, stand, walk, and do calf exercises regularly, and stay well hydrated
    \item Report sudden one-sided leg swelling or pain immediately
    \item Take anticoagulant medicines exactly as prescribed, and never miss doses
    \item Know the signs of bleeding on anticoagulants and seek urgent care for severe bleeding or head injury
    \item Wear compression stockings as advised after a DVT
    \item Discuss contraception and HRT options with a doctor if you have had a clot
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and stay active
\end{itemize}
