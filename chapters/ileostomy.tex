\chapter{Ileostomy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An ileostomy is a surgically created opening (stoma) on the surface of the abdomen through which the small intestine (ileum) is brought to the skin to divert faeces into a collection bag. It is formed when the large intestine (colon) must be bypassed or removed, allowing the small intestine to empty directly and continuously. An ileostomy may be permanent, when the colon and rectum are removed, or temporary, when it protects a healing join lower down while recovery takes place. With modern stoma care, most people with an ileostomy adapt well and return to a full and active life, including work, sport, and travel.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bowel surgery resulting in a stoma is common, with several thousand ileostomies and colostomies created in Australia each year. The most frequent reasons are inflammatory bowel disease, particularly ulcerative colitis, bowel cancer, and emergency surgery for conditions such as bowel obstruction, perforation, or severe infection. A significant proportion of ileostomies are temporary, created to allow a bowel join to heal after surgery, and are reversed in a later, smaller operation. People of all ages are affected, but stomas are most commonly created in middle-aged and older adults.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
An ileostomy is not a disease but a treatment, created when the normal route for faeces is no longer safe or functional. The most common indications are removal of the colon in ulcerative colitis or familial adenomatous polyposis, removal of rectal cancer requiring excision of the anal region, and diversion of the faecal stream to protect a surgically created join in the bowel (anastomosis) from leakage while it heals. Because the ileum does not absorb water as efficiently as the colon, output from an ileostomy is looser, more frequent, and contains digestive enzymes, making skin protection around the stoma a central part of ongoing care.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A pink, moist stoma protruding slightly from the abdominal wall, usually on the right side of the abdomen
    \item Output of semi-liquid faeces that occurs several times a day, often after meals
    \item The stoma has no nerve endings on its surface and is not painful to touch
    \item Continuous rather than voluntary passage of output, which is collected in an adhesive pouch
    \item The need for a pouch system that must be changed regularly, typically every 1--3 days
    \item Common challenges: leakage, skin irritation, odour, and adjustment to body image
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The main issues in people with an ileostomy are not diagnostic but functional, and common problems should be distinguished from each other.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High output:} excessive fluid loss that can cause dehydration and electrolyte disturbance
    \item \textbf{Stomal blockage:} cramping pain, reduced output, and swelling, often from food blockage or adhesions
    \item \textbf{Parastomal hernia:} a bulge around the stoma caused by weakness in the abdominal wall
    \item \textbf{Stomal prolapse or retraction:} the stoma protrudes excessively or sinks below skin level
    \item \textbf{Skin problems:} excoriation from leakage versus fungal infection
    \item \textbf{Recurrent disease:} a return of the underlying condition, such as Crohn's disease involving the small intestine
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with a new ileostomy should be reviewed by their stomal therapy nurse and surgical team in the weeks after surgery. A stoma that has been working well but suddenly changes, skin problems that do not settle, or difficulty with fitting appliances all warrant review. Anyone with persistent abdominal pain, vomiting, or reduced output should be assessed promptly.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item No output from the stoma for more than 6--8 hours accompanied by cramping pain, nausea, or vomiting
    \item Sudden severe abdominal pain, abdominal distension, or a tense, tender abdomen
    \item Signs of dehydration: dizziness, reduced urine, dry mouth, and weakness
    \item A dusky, dark, or blue discolouration of the stoma, which may indicate reduced blood supply
    \item Fresh bleeding from the stoma that does not stop with gentle pressure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stomal assessment:} inspection of the stoma's size, colour, and position, and of the surrounding skin
    \item \textbf{Fluid balance monitoring:} daily output volumes, urine output, and weight in the early weeks
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} electrolytes, urea, and creatinine to detect dehydration or imbalance with high output
    \item \textbf{Abdominal imaging:} X-ray or CT if obstruction is suspected
    \item \textbf{Endoscopy:} examination of the stoma and small bowel where recurrence of disease or bleeding is a concern
    \item \textbf{Radiological contrast studies:} to check the integrity of a distal join before reversal of a temporary stoma
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stoma care:} a stomal therapy nurse teaches fitting, changing, and disposing of pouches, and the use of skin barriers and pastes
    \item \textbf{Skin protection:} a correctly sized flange and gentle removal techniques prevent leakage and excoriation
    \item \textbf{Diet and fluids:} chewing food well, eating regularly, and drinking adequate fluids; output usually thickens as the bowel adapts
    \item \textbf{High-output management:} oral rehydration solutions, loperamide to slow transit, and dietary adjustment
    \item \textbf{Odour and gas control:} pouch deodorants and avoidance of gas-producing foods
    \item \textbf{Exercise and activity:} gradual return to normal activities, with advice about heavy lifting and parastomal hernia risk
    \item \textbf{Reversal:} a temporary ileostomy is reversed in a small operation once healing is confirmed
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} counselling and peer support from organisations such as Stoma Australia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dehydration and electrolyte disturbance from high output, especially in hot weather or with illness
    \item Skin breakdown and infection around the stoma
    \item Parastomal hernia, which may require a support belt or surgery
    \item Stomal prolapse, retraction, or stenosis
    \item Intestinal obstruction from food blockage or adhesions
    \item Bleeding from the stoma or the underlying bowel disease
    \item Vitamin and mineral deficiencies with long-term high output
    \item Psychological distress and altered body image
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people adjust well to an ileostomy, and studies show that quality of life after the initial adjustment period is generally good. Output gradually becomes more manageable as the small intestine adapts, and most people achieve reliable pouch wear times of several days. Temporary ileostomies are usually reversed within a few months of the original surgery. Long-term outcomes depend largely on the underlying disease, but the stoma itself does not prevent a full and active life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Master stoma care with the stomal therapy nurse before leaving hospital and keep their contact details
    \item Keep a spare pouch and barrier products with you at all times
    \item Drink plenty of fluids and increase salt intake if output is high
    \item Chew food thoroughly, especially fibrous foods, nuts, and corn, which can cause blockage
    \item Avoid fizzy drinks and gas-forming foods if they cause troublesome bloating
    \item Exercise gently at first and protect the abdominal wall when lifting
    \item Attend regular follow-up with the surgical and stoma care team
    \item Connect with support groups and, if needed, counselling to help with adjustment
\end{itemize}
