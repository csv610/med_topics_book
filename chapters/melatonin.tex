\chapter{Melatonin}
\index{Melatonin}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Melatonin is an endogenous neurohormone produced and secreted primarily by the pineal gland in a circadian pattern, playing a fundamental role in the regulation of the sleep-wake cycle and seasonal biorhythms. Synthesised from tryptophan via serotonin, its secretion is directly regulated by light-dark inputs received from the retina via the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the hypothalamus. Exogenous melatonin is widely utilised as a pharmacological agent or dietary supplement to manage circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorders, primary insomnia (especially in older adults), jet lag, and sleep disturbances associated with shift work or neurodevelopmental disorders.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The clinical and self-directed use of exogenous melatonin has grown substantially worldwide. In many countries, melatonin was historically available only by prescription, but regulatory changes have permitted over-the-counter access for individuals aged 55 and older for the short-term treatment of primary insomnia. The prevalence of melatonin use is high among frequent international travellers, shift workers, and paediatric populations with neurodevelopmental disorders (such as autism spectrum disorder and ADHD) who exhibit persistent sleep onset difficulties.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Melatonin's physiological functions are governed by receptor activation, environmental light cues, and chemical synthesis.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Circadian rhythm regulation:} Light exposure stimulates melanopsin-containing retinal ganglion cells, sending inhibitory signals to the SCN, which suppresses pineal melatonin release. In darkness, this inhibition is removed, leading to a surge in melatonin secretion (the ``vampire hormone'') that signals biological night.
    \item \textbf{Receptor activation:} Melatonin binds to two high-affinity G-protein-coupled receptors: MT1 (which suppresses SCN neuronal firing, promoting sleep onset) and MT2 (which shifts the phase of the circadian clock).
    \item \textbf{Synthesis pathway:} Tryptophan is hydroxylated and decarboxylated to serotonin, which is then converted to N-acetylserotonin by the rate-limiting enzyme arylalkylamine N-acetyltransferase (AANAT), and finally methylated to melatonin.
    \item \textbf{Antioxidant activity:} In addition to receptor-mediated effects, melatonin acts as a direct free radical scavenger and stimulates the expression of antioxidant enzymes (such as superoxide dismutase), protecting tissues from oxidative stress.
    \item \textbf{Age-related decline:} Endogenous melatonin production peaks in early childhood and progressively declines with age, which correlates with the increased incidence of sleep architecture disruption and insomnia in older adults.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Therapeutic melatonin use is characterised by specific administration timings, sleep-onset effects, and minor transient side effects.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Circadian phase-shifting:} When administered in the afternoon or early evening, exogenous melatonin causes a phase advance (shifting the sleep cycle earlier); when taken in the morning, it causes a phase delay.
    \item \textbf{Sleep-onset facilitation:} Reduces sleep onset latency (the time taken to fall asleep) and increases total sleep time without causing the substantial hangover effect or muscle weakness associated with benzodiazepines.
    \item \textbf{Transient side effects:} Includes mild drowsiness, headache, dizziness, nausea, and vivid dreams or nightmares.
    \item \textbf{Lack of dependency:} Unlike standard sedative-hypnotics, melatonin does not cause physical dependence, tolerance, addiction, or withdrawal symptoms upon discontinuation.
    \item \textbf{Hypothermic effect:} Melatonin induces a slight decrease in core body temperature, which is a physiological prerequisite for initiating high-quality sleep.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before attributing sleep issues to a melatonin deficiency or treating them with exogenous melatonin, alternative sleep disorders and secondary causes must be considered.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA):} A structural airway disorder causing fragmented sleep, loud snoring, and daytime sleepiness, requiring continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) rather than sleep aids.
    \item \textbf{Restless legs syndrome (RLS):} A neurological disorder causing an irresistible urge to move the legs, often worse in the evening, treated with dopaminergic agents or iron supplementation.
    \item \textbf{Primary psychiatric disorders:} Severe insomnia secondary to major depressive disorder, generalised anxiety disorder, or bipolar mania, which require targeted psychiatric treatments.
    \item \textbf{Poor sleep hygiene:} Chronic sleep difficulties caused by excessive screen time, late-night caffeine or alcohol intake, and irregular sleeping schedules.
    \item \textbf{Medication-induced insomnia:} Insomnia arising as a side effect of stimulants (e.g.\ methylphenidate), beta-blockers, corticosteroids, or bronchodilators.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Individuals experiencing chronic, persistent sleep disturbances (lasting more than a month) should consult a general practitioner to identify the underlying cause.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, life-threatening symptoms associated with accidental overdose or severe allergic reactions (anaphylaxis) to melatonin preparations, presenting with dyspnoea, facial swelling, or cardiovascular collapse.
    \item Extreme, sudden drowsiness or confusion leading to accidents, particularly if the individual has operated heavy machinery or driven a vehicle after taking the hormone.
\end{itemize}
Other reasons to see a doctor: Consult your doctor if you experience persistent daytime fatigue, morning headaches, gasping for air during sleep, or if melatonin does not improve your sleep after a few weeks of use.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Evaluating sleep complaints before commencing melatonin therapy involves a thorough clinical assessment and sometimes specialised sleep studies.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detailed sleep diary:} A patient-kept log recording sleep onset, wake times, sleep duration, daytime naps, caffeine/alcohol intake, and medication use for 1 to 2 weeks.
    \item \textbf{Polysomnography (Sleep study):} Overnight monitoring of brain activity, eye movements, muscle tone, heart rate, and respiration to rule out sleep apnoea or periodic limb movement disorder.
    \item \textbf{Dim-light melatonin onset (DLMO) test:} A specialised laboratory test measuring melatonin levels in saliva or blood in dim light to determine the precise timing of endogenous melatonin release.
    \item \textbf{Actigraphy:} Wearing a wrist-worn accelerometer for several days to track activity and rest patterns, helping diagnose circadian rhythm disorders.
    \item \textbf{Screening for drug interactions:} Evaluating concurrent medications, as melatonin can interact with fluvoxamine, oral contraceptives, anticoagulants, and immunosuppressants.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Melatonin management involves selecting the appropriate formulation, determining the correct dosage, and timing the administration precisely.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Formulation selection:} Choosing between immediate-release formulations (preferred for sleep onset difficulties or jet lag) and prolonged-release formulations (e.g.\ Circadin, preferred for sleep maintenance in older adults).
    \item \textbf{Precise timing of dose:} Administering melatonin 30 to 60 minutes before the desired bedtime for insomnia, or adjusting the timing systematically to achieve a circadian phase shift.
    \item \textbf{Dosage guidelines:} Initiating therapy at low doses (e.g.\ 0.5 mg to 2 mg) to achieve physiological levels, as higher doses can cause supraphysiologic blood levels and worsen daytime sleepiness.
    \item \textbf{Paediatric management:} In children with ADHD or autism, using melatonin only under medical supervision and in combination with behavioural sleep interventions.
    \item \textbf{Integration with sleep hygiene:} Combining pharmacological therapy with strict sleep hygiene protocols, such as avoiding blue-light exposure from screens for 2 hours before bed.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While melatonin is highly safe, minor complications and interactions can occur with improper use.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Daytime somnolence:} Excessive sleepiness, grogginess, or slowed reaction times the morning after taking melatonin, especially if taken too late in the night or at high doses.
    \item \textbf{Drug-hormone interactions:} Concomitant use with fluvoxamine (a strong CYP1A2 inhibitor) can dramatically increase melatonin levels, while beta-blockers can suppress endogenous melatonin production.
    \item \textbf{Hormonal fluctuations:} In theory, very high doses could affect other endocrine systems, although clinical studies have not demonstrated significant reproductive or thyroid hormone changes with standard doses.
    \item \textbf{Risk in autoimmune diseases:} Because melatonin can stimulate immune function, it should be used with caution in patients with active autoimmune disorders.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for sleep disorders treated with melatonin is generally positive, especially when combined with behavioural changes. In older adults with primary insomnia, prolonged-release melatonin significantly improves sleep quality and morning alertness. For jet lag and delayed sleep phase syndrome, melatonin is highly effective at resetting the biological clock, with symptoms resolving within a few days of adjusting to the new time zone. Long-term use in paediatric neurodevelopmental populations is well-tolerated and leads to sustained improvements in child sleep and maternal wellbeing.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Supporting natural melatonin production and using supplements safely can improve sleep hygiene.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Expose eyes to morning sunlight:} Get 10 to 15 minutes of bright, natural light early in the day to help anchor the circadian clock and suppress daytime melatonin.
    \item \textbf{Limit evening screen use:} Turn off televisions, computers, and smartphones, or use blue-light-filtering software, at least 1 to 2 hours before bedtime.
    \item \textbf{Keep the bedroom dark:} Use blackout curtains and turn off all lights to ensure optimal conditions for endogenous pineal secretion.
    \item \textbf{Follow dosage instructions:} Take the lowest effective dose at the recommended time; taking melatonin in the middle of the night can disrupt the sleep cycle the following day.
    \item \textbf{Maintain a regular sleep schedule:} Go to bed and wake up at the same time every day, including on weekends, to support stable circadian rhythms.
\end{itemize}
