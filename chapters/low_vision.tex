\chapter{Low Vision}
\index{Low Vision}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Low vision is a significant, chronic visual impairment that cannot be fully corrected with conventional spectacles, contact lenses, medical treatment, or surgery. It interferes with an individual's ability to perform daily activities, such as reading, writing, shopping, or recognising faces. Low vision is defined by visual acuity less than 6/12 (or 20/40) up to blindness (less than 3/60 or 20/400) or a visual field loss of less than 20 degrees in the better-seeing eye. Low vision care focuses on maximizing the use of remaining vision through optical and non-optical aids and rehabilitation.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Low vision affects millions of people globally, with prevalence rising rapidly with age due to the increasing incidence of age-related eye diseases. In many countries, it is estimated that hundreds of thousands of people live with low vision or blindness. The leading causes of low vision in developed countries are age-related macular degeneration (AMD), cataracts, glaucoma, and diabetic retinopathy. Global and local statistics highlight that about 80\% of low vision is preventable or treatable if diagnosed and managed early in the course of the underlying disease.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Low vision is the clinical consequence of damage to various components of the visual pathway, including the cornea, lens, retina, optic nerve, or visual cortex.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Age-related macular degeneration (AMD):} progressive degeneration of the macula, leading to loss of central vision while peripheral vision remains intact.
    \item \textbf{Glaucoma:} progressive optic neuropathy characterized by damage to the optic nerve head and loss of retinal ganglion cells, leading to peripheral visual field loss.
    \item \textbf{Diabetic retinopathy:} microvascular damage to the retina causing haemorrhages, exudates, and macular oedema, resulting in patchy or blurred vision.
    \item \textbf{Cataracts:} gradual opacification of the crystalline lens, scatter of light, and generalized loss of contrast sensitivity and visual acuity.
    \item \textbf{Genetic disorders:} conditions such as retinitis pigmentosa (causing rod photoreceptor degeneration) or Stargardt disease.
    \item \textbf{Trauma or stroke:} mechanical injuries to the eye or cerebral infarctions affecting the visual pathways or occipital cortex.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The presentation depends on the underlying cause, but generally involves persistent difficulty performing visually guided tasks.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty reading standard print, writing, or viewing screens even with updated glasses
    \item Inability to recognise the faces of friends or family members from a distance
    \item Poor contrast sensitivity, making it hard to see steps, curbs, or low-contrast objects
    \item Difficulty navigating safely, particularly in unfamiliar or poorly lit environments
    \item Alterations in color perception or severe sensitivity to glare (photophobia)
    \item Visual field defects, such as central blind spots (scotomas) or peripheral field narrowing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The clinical task is to identify the primary cause of low vision and differentiate it from correctable refractive errors or reversible vision loss.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Uncorrected refractive error:} myopia, hyperopia, or astigmatism that can be fully corrected with standard glasses or contact lenses.
    \item \textbf{Reversible blindness/vision loss:} such as untreated cataracts or vitreous haemorrhage, which can be restored by surgery or medical therapy.
    \item \textbf{Cortical visual impairment:} vision loss caused by damage to the brain's visual cortex rather than the eye itself.
    \item \textbf{Functional or psychogenic vision loss:} non-organic vision loss where clinical examination reveals no structural or physiological cause.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Sudden or progressive changes in vision require urgent professional evaluation. Patients with known eye conditions should adhere to regular follow-up schedules.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, painful loss of vision in one or both eyes
    \item Vision loss accompanied by severe head trauma, sudden weakness, or speech difficulty (suggesting stroke)
    \item Sudden onset of flashing lights and showers of floaters (suggesting retinal detachment)
    \item Eye injury involving chemical exposure, penetration, or severe physical trauma
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Comprehensive assessment by an ophthalmologist or optometrist is required to diagnose low vision and establish its cause.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visual acuity testing:} measurement of distance and near vision using specialized charts (e.g., LogMAR or Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study charts).
    \item \textbf{Visual field testing (perimetry):} assessing the peripheral visual field to identify blind spots or narrowing.
    \item \textbf{Slit-lamp biomicroscopy:} examining the anterior and posterior segments of the eye for structural pathologies.
    \item \textbf{Optical coherence tomography (OCT):} high-resolution cross-sectional imaging of the retina to evaluate macular and optic nerve health.
    \item \textbf{Tonometry:} measuring intraocular pressure to screen for or monitor glaucoma.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The primary goal is rehabilitation to enhance the patient's functional independence and quality of life.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Optical magnifiers:} hand-held, stand, or pocket magnifiers to assist with near tasks like reading.
    \item \textbf{Telescopic systems:} distance magnifiers mounted on spectacles or hand-held to view signs or television.
    \item \textbf{Digital and electronic aids:} video magnifiers (CCTVs), screen-reading software, and text-to-speech devices.
    \item \textbf{Non-optical modifications:} enhanced lighting, high-contrast labels, and large-print materials.
    \item \textbf{Orientation and mobility training:} teaching safe travel techniques, including the use of a white cane or guide dog.
    \item \textbf{Occupational therapy:} home assessments to improve safety and lighting, reducing the risk of falls.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Increased risk of falls, hip fractures, and other physical injuries
    \item Depressive symptoms, anxiety, and social isolation due to loss of independence
    \item Inability to drive, leading to restricted employment and social opportunities
    \item Charles Bonnet syndrome, characterised by vivid visual hallucinations in patients with severe vision loss
    \item Decreased adherence to medication regimens due to difficulty reading labels
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Low vision is typically a chronic, permanent state, but the functional prognosis is greatly improved by early referral to low vision rehabilitation services. While the underlying pathology (like dry AMD or glaucoma damage) is often irreversible, the vast majority of patients can learn to utilize their remaining vision effectively. Adjusting to vision loss is a gradual psychological process, and supportive counselling significantly enhances coping mechanisms and long-term outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend regular comprehensive eye examinations, especially if over the age of 40 or with a family history of eye disease.
    \item Manage chronic conditions like diabetes and hypertension to prevent microvascular retinal damage.
    \item Protect the eyes from ultraviolet (UV) radiation by wearing sunglasses and wide-brimmed hats.
    \item Quit smoking, as smoking is a major modifiable risk factor for AMD and cataracts.
    \item Ensure adequate, glare-free lighting at home, particularly in task areas and stairways.
\end{itemize}
