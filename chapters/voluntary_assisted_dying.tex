\chapter{Voluntary assisted dying}
\index{Voluntary assisted dying}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Voluntary assisted dying (VAD) refers to the legal, medically regulated process that allows an eligible individual with a terminal, advanced, and progressive medical condition to request and receive medical assistance to end their life. The terminology surrounding this clinical practice varies globally, with terms such as medical aid in dying (MAiD) in Canada and the United States, voluntary euthanasia (where a clinician administers the lethal substance), and assisted suicide or physician-assisted suicide (where the patient self-administers the substance) being utilized. However, the term ``voluntary assisted dying'' has become the standard clinical and legislative nomenclature in jurisdictions such as some countries. VAD represents a profound paradigm shift in modern medicine, intersecting clinical practice, medical ethics, human rights, and patient autonomy.

The primary objective of VAD is to provide individuals experiencing intolerable, unrelievable suffering with a compassionate option to control the timing and manner of their death. The legal frameworks governing VAD are highly structured and incorporate rigorous eligibility criteria, mandatory assessment processes, and comprehensive reporting mechanisms to ensure patient safety and prevent abuse. VAD is distinct from the withholding or withdrawing of life-sustaining treatment (such as mechanical ventilation or artificial nutrition) and is also distinct from palliative sedation, where medications are titrated to induce a state of decreased consciousness to relieve refractory symptoms without the primary intent to shorten life. The ethical discourse surrounding VAD balances the principles of beneficence and patient autonomy against the duty to preserve life, highlighting the necessity of integrating VAD as a voluntary option within the broader continuum of high-quality end-of-life care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of voluntary assisted dying provides insight into the patient demographics, clinical indications, and geographic distribution of individuals who access these services. VAD is legally available in a growing number of jurisdictions worldwide, including all six local states, New Zealand, Canada, Spain, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Switzerland, Colombia, and several states in the United States (such as Oregon, Washington, California, and Colorado).

Statistically, VAD accounts for a small but stable percentage of all deaths in jurisdictions where it is legal. In long-established frameworks, such as in the Netherlands and Belgium, VAD deaths represent approximately 1.5\% to 4.5\% of total annual mortalities. In newer jurisdictions, such as the local states, VAD-related deaths typically account for 0.5\% to 1.5\% of overall deaths during the initial years of legislation, with a gradual plateau as awareness and clinical infrastructure develop.

Demographic data consistently demonstrate that the cohort of patients accessing VAD is characterised by specific clinical and socioeconomic profiles:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Underlying Diagnoses}: Malignant neoplasms (cancer) are the leading primary diagnosis, accounting for 70\% to 80\% of all VAD cases. Neurodegenerative disorders, particularly amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) or motor neurone disease (MND), constitute the second largest group (typically 6\% to 10\% of cases). Other common diagnoses include severe, end-stage chronic respiratory conditions (e.g., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) and advanced cardiovascular or renal failure.
    \item \textbf{Age Distribution}: The majority of individuals utilizing VAD are older adults, with the median age of recipients typically falling between 70 and 79 years. However, VAD is accessed by adults across the entire age spectrum from 18 years onward, depending on the legal framework of the jurisdiction.
    \item \textbf{Gender Balance}: Epidemiological analyses show a relatively equal distribution between males and females, with some jurisdictions reporting a minor male predominance (approximately 52\% to 55\% of cases).
    \item \textbf{Socioeconomic and Educational Background}: Patients who access VAD tend to have higher levels of formal education, are frequently enrolled in or receiving concurrent palliative care services, and often reside in metropolitan areas with greater access to specialised healthcare services.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
In the context of voluntary assisted dying, ``aetiology'' refers to the underlying clinical conditions and patient-reported motivations that prompt a request for VAD. ``Pathophysiology'' refers to the biological mechanisms through which the prescribed lethal substances induce physiological arrest.

The request for VAD is multi-factorial, stemming from a combination of physical, psychological, and existential distress:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Refractory Physical Symptoms}: Patients often experience severe, intractable symptoms that cannot be adequately palliated by standard medical interventions. These include severe neuropathic or visceral pain, progressive dyspnoea (air hunger), profound fatigue, dysphagia, nausea, bowel obstruction, and recurrent choking episodes, which are particularly common in advanced neurodegenerative diseases.
    \item \textbf{Loss of Autonomy and Dignity}: The progressive loss of independence, inability to participate in meaningful life activities, loss of control over bodily functions, and the perception of being a burden to family members represent significant psychosocial drivers.
    \item \textbf{Existential and Psychological Distress}: Anticipated future suffering, fear of the dying process, and existential distress regarding the loss of self-identity contribute substantially to the patient's determination that their quality of life has fallen below an acceptable threshold.
\end{itemize}

The pathophysiology of VAD involves the administration of a lethal dose of central nervous system (CNS) depressants, leading to sequential respiratory and cardiac arrest. The pharmacological agents are typically administered orally (patient self-administration) or intravenously (clinician administration):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Central Nervous System Depression}: The primary mechanism relies on high doses of barbiturates (such as pentobarbital) or multi-drug regimens (commonly containing combinations of diazepam, digoxin, morphine, and amitriptyline, abbreviated as DDMA). Barbiturates act as positive allosteric modulators of the gamma-aminobutyric acid type A (GABA-A) receptor, enhancing GABA-mediated chloride currents. This causes widespread, profound inhibition of neuronal activity within the cerebral cortex and brainstem.
    \item \textbf{Loss of Consciousness and Coma}: Within minutes of administration, the patient rapidly transitions from drowsiness to deep sedation, followed by a state of surgical anaesthesia and deep coma. Cortical electrical activity ceases, eliminating any conscious perception of pain or air hunger.
    \item \textbf{Respiratory Arrest}: As the pharmacological agents reach high concentrations in the brainstem, they suppress the respiratory control centres in the medulla oblongata and pons. This leads to progressive bradypnoea, hypoventilation, hypercapnia, and ultimately complete apnoea.
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular Collapse}: Systemic hypoxia secondary to respiratory arrest, combined with the direct cardiotoxic and vasodilatory effects of the high-dose medications, results in profound hypotension, myocardial ischemia, and cardiac arrest. In multi-drug regimens, cardiotonic agents like digoxin are explicitly included to induce fatal arrhythmias and accelerate myocardial standstill once the patient is deeply comatose.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features associated with voluntary assisted dying can be divided into the clinical presentation of the patient leading up to the VAD request, and the physiological stages observed during the active administration of the VAD substances.

The patient presenting for VAD assessment typically exhibits clinical signs and symptoms of end-stage terminal illness:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Functional Decline}: Severe physical disability, marked by a low Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status or Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) score, representing dependency in activities of daily living.
    \item \textbf{Neurological Deficits}: In patients with neurodegenerative disease, clinical signs include progressive muscle atrophy, fasciculations, spasticity, dysarthria, and dysphagia, leading to an inability to manage oral secretions.
    \item \textbf{Cachexia and Wasting}: Significant weight loss, muscle wasting, and severe anorexia secondary to advanced systemic malignancy or organ failure.
\end{itemize}

During the administration of the VAD substance, the clinical features present as a rapid, predictable sequence of physiological changes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Phase 1: Rapid Sedation}: Immediately following ingestion or injection of the VAD substance, the patient displays signs of rapid onset drowsiness, slurred speech, and mild ataxia, progressing to a complete loss of responsiveness within 2 to 10 minutes.
    \item \textbf{Phase 2: Deep Coma}: The patient enters a state of unresponsiveness to painful stimuli. Muscle tone becomes completely flaccid, corneal reflexes are lost, and pupillary constriction followed by dilatation occurs.
    \item \textbf{Phase 3: Respiratory Alterations}: Breathing patterns alter, displaying Cheyne-Stokes respiration, shallow breathing, and long periods of apnoea. Agonal breathing or snoring (due to upper airway relaxation) may occur briefly but is not perceived by the comatose patient.
    \item \textbf{Phase 4: Cyanosis and Death}: As respiratory function ceases, peripheral and central cyanosis becomes marked. Heart sounds become distant and irregular, culminating in a complete cessation of pulse and respiration. Death is clinically confirmed by persistent absence of breath sounds, absence of heart sounds on auscultation, fixed and dilated pupils, and lack of response to any external stimuli.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When evaluating a patient who requests voluntary assisted dying, clinicians must perform a careful differential diagnosis to distinguish a genuine, autonomous, and settled request for VAD from other clinical conditions or distress states that require alternative management:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Major Depressive Disorder (MDD)}: Clinical depression can impair cognitive function and lead to feelings of hopelessness, worthlessness, and suicidal ideation. Clinicians must differentiate between MDD and a rational, existential desire for a peaceful death in a terminally ill patient. While existential sadness and anticipatory grief are common in terminal illness, MDD is characterised by pervasive anhedonia, pathological guilt, and cognitive distortions that may impair decision-making capacity.
    \item \textbf{Delirium and Cognitive Impairment}: Acute delirium, dementia, or metabolic encephalopathy (common in end-stage liver or renal failure) can cause fluctuating capacity, confusion, and impulsive requests to end life. These cognitive changes must be actively screened for, as they temporarily or permanently render a patient ineligible for VAD due to lack of decision-making capacity.
    \item \textbf{Poorly Controlled Physical Symptoms}: An urgent request to die may be a manifestation of acute, inadequately treated pain, dyspnoea, or nausea. When these symptoms are optimized through aggressive palliative interventions, the patient's desire for VAD may resolve. Therefore, sub-optimal palliative care must be ruled out as the primary driver of the request.
    \item \textbf{Demoralisation Syndrome}: This clinical state is characterized by a loss of meaning, hopelessness, helplessness, and subjective incompetence. Unlike depression, demoralisation is heavily tied to situational factors and relational dynamics. It requires targeted psychological, spiritual, and social interventions rather than VAD.
    \item \textbf{Coercion and Elder Abuse}: A request for VAD may be covertly driven by external pressures, such as financial coercion, feeling like a burden to caregivers, or family conflict. Clinicians must ensure the patient's request is entirely voluntary and free from undue influence by conducting private interviews with the patient.
    \item \textbf{Acute Suicidal Ideation / Mental Health Crisis}: A request for VAD must be differentiated from acute suicidality arising from personal crises, trauma, or psychiatric illness. The latter requires psychiatric hospitalization, crisis intervention, and safety planning, whereas VAD is a structured, legal process for individuals with a terminal physical illness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Voluntary assisted dying is a planned, elective medical procedure, and patients undergoing this process are closely monitored by their clinical care team. However, there are specific situations during the planning phase, administration phase, or in the context of the underlying illness where immediate medical care must be sought.

During the active phase of VAD administration (particularly for self-administration at home), the patient's designated contact person or family members should contact the VAD Care Navigator, the coordinating medical practitioner, or emergency medical services under the following circumstances:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Incomplete Ingestion}: If the patient is unable to swallow the full dose of the oral medication, vomits within a short period after ingestion, or becomes unconscious before the full dose is administered.
    \item \textbf{Prolonged Coma without Death}: If the patient remains in a comatose state for an unexpectedly prolonged period (e.g., exceeding several hours for intravenous administration or 24 hours for oral administration) without progressing to respiratory arrest, causing distress to family members.
    \item \textbf{Acute Physical Distress during Ingestion}: If the patient experiences severe choking, airway obstruction, or acute pain during the administration process.
\end{itemize}

Additionally, if a patient is experiencing an acute, unexpected health crisis or severe distress related to their terminal illness, or is experiencing an acute psychiatric crisis with active suicidal ideation outside the regulated VAD pathway, immediate medical care must be sought:
\begin{itemize}
    \item For acute medical emergencies or life-threatening symptoms associated with the underlying illness, call emergency services immediately:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item 	extbf{Local crisis support}: Contact local emergency services
        \item \textbf{United States and Canada}: Call 911
        \item \textbf{United Kingdom}: Call 999 or 111
    \end{itemize}
    \item For acute psychological crises or urgent suicide prevention support:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item 	extbf{Local crisis support}: Contact a a local crisis service or the a local suicide-prevention service
        \item \textbf{United States and Canada}: Call or text the Suicide \& Crisis Lifeline, or use a local crisis service
        \item \textbf{United Kingdom}: Call Samaritans on 116 123
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
There is no diagnostic test or investigation that identifies a need for voluntary assisted dying; instead, ``diagnosis'' in this context refers to the comprehensive clinical and legal assessment process used to determine whether a patient meets the strict eligibility criteria established by law.

The assessment process is highly structured and typically involves the following clinical investigations and evaluations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary Eligibility Assessment}: Conducted by a coordinating medical practitioner who must be a registered doctor meeting specific experience and training requirements. The practitioner reviews the patient's medical history, confirms the primary diagnosis of a terminal illness, and verifies that the condition is advanced, progressive, and expected to cause death within the statutory timeframe (usually 6 months, or 12 months for neurodegenerative diseases like MND).
    \item \textbf{Secondary Independent Assessment}: Conducted by a consulting medical practitioner who independently reviews the patient, confirms the terminal diagnosis, and verifies all eligibility criteria. Both doctors must document their findings independently.
    \item \textbf{Review of Diagnostic Imaging and Pathology}: Clinicians must review objective medical evidence to confirm the stage and prognosis of the underlying disease. This includes computed tomography (CT) scans, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET) scans, histopathology reports, and laboratory blood panels (such as organ function tests and tumour markers).
    \item \textbf{Assessment of Decision-Making Capacity}: Clinicians must verify that the patient has the capacity to make decisions regarding VAD. The patient must be able to understand the information relevant to the decision, retain that information, weigh the information to make a decision, and communicate their decision. If cognitive impairment, delirium, or depression is suspected to affect capacity, the patient must be referred to a specialist, such as a geriatrician, psychiatrist, or neuropsychologist, for a formal cognitive evaluation (e.g., using the Mini-Mental State Examination [MMSE] or Montreal Cognitive Assessment [MoCA] alongside clinical interviews).
    \item \textbf{Voluntariness and Coercion Screening}: Clinicians must conduct separate, private consultations with the patient to ensure they are making the decision voluntarily, free from any coercion or elder abuse. The clinician documents the consistency and determination of the patient's requests over time.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The clinical management of voluntary assisted dying involves a multi-disciplinary approach that integrates pharmacological protocols, patient safety measures, administrative coordination, and psychological support. The process is designed to ensure a safe, effective, dignified, and legally compliant procedure.

The clinical management steps are structured as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological Protocol Planning}: The prescribing doctor determines the appropriate medication regimen based on the patient's physical state, swallowing ability, and personal preferences. The VAD substances are highly regulated and dispensed by a specialized state-wide pharmacy service.
    \item \textbf{Pre-medication Administration}: To ensure patient comfort and prevent complications such as emesis (vomiting) during oral administration, pre-medications are prescribed. Typically, a combination of antiemetics (e.g., metoclopramide 10 mg or ondansetron 8 mg) is administered 30 to 60 minutes prior to the VAD substance. Prophylactic prokinetic agents may also be used to facilitate rapid gastric emptying and absorption of the lethal dose.
    \item \textbf{Administration Pathways}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Self-Administration (Oral/Enteral)}: The patient swallows the prepared liquid medication themselves, or administers it through an existing enteral feeding tube (e.g., PEG tube). The patient must physically initiate the administration (e.g., drinking from a cup or pushing a syringe plunger).
        \item \textbf{Clinician Administration (Intravenous)}: If the patient is physically unable to self-administer (e.g., due to severe dysphagia, paralysis, or loss of manual dexterity), or if self-administration fails, an authorized medical practitioner can administer the substance intravenously. This involves placing an intravenous cannula and administering a rapid sequence of anaesthetics and neuromuscular blockers (e.g., propofol, rocuronium) or cardiotoxic agents.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Integrated Palliative Care Support}: VAD does not replace palliative care. Management includes ongoing coordination with palliative care teams to manage symptoms up to the moment of VAD administration, ensuring the patient remains as comfortable and pain-free as possible.
    \item \textbf{Psychological and Bereavement Support}: Provision of psychological counseling for the patient, their family members, and caregivers. This includes preparing the family for the physiological events of the dying process and arranging post-bereavement support services.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While voluntary assisted dying is designed to be a highly controlled and peaceful process, clinical complications can arise during the preparation, administration, or immediate post-administration phases. Clinicians and families must be prepared to manage these risks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nausea and Vomiting}: The ingestion of large volumes of concentrated oral substances can irritate the gastric mucosa, potentially causing emesis. Vomiting the VAD substance leads to incomplete drug absorption, which can delay or prevent death, and poses an aspiration risk. This is mitigated by mandatory pre-medication with antiemetics.
    \item \textbf{Regurgitation and Aspiration}: In patients with pre-existing dysphagia or impaired airway reflexes, oral administration can lead to coughing, choking, or aspiration of the fluid into the lungs, causing acute distress and respiratory panic.
    \item \textbf{Prolonged Interval to Death}: The time between ingestion of oral VAD substances and cardiac arrest can vary. In a small percentage of cases, the patient may remain in a comatose state for an extended period (12 to 24 hours, or occasionally longer). This prolonged survival can cause significant distress, anxiety, and exhaustion for the family members keeping vigil.
    \item \textbf{Failure to Induce Death}: On rare occasions, the administered dose may fail to cause cardiac arrest, resulting in the patient regaining consciousness. In jurisdictions where it is legally permitted, a back-up clinician administration protocol must be prepared to address this occurrence.
    \item \textbf{Difficulty with Intravenous Access}: For clinician-administered VAD, patients with advanced cachexia, history of chemotherapy, or severe dehydration may have extremely poor venous access. Difficulties in establishing or maintaining a patent intravenous line can cause physical discomfort and delay the procedure.
    \item \textbf{Institutional and Caregiver Distress}: Healthcare professionals and family members may experience moral distress, anticipatory grief, or post-traumatic stress symptoms. Access to debriefing and psychological support is essential to prevent long-term psychological sequelae.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of a patient who has requested and been approved for voluntary assisted dying is governed by the underlying terminal illness and the timeline of the VAD procedure.

Once the VAD substances are administered, the outcome is uniform and predictable, resulting in a rapid and peaceful death:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intravenous Administration Timeline}: Loss of consciousness occurs within 1 to 2 minutes of injection. Deep coma rapidly ensues, and respiratory arrest followed by cardiac arrest typically occurs within 10 to 30 minutes of administration.
    \item \textbf{Oral Administration Timeline}: The onset of drowsiness occurs within 5 to 15 minutes of ingestion. Complete loss of consciousness is typically achieved within 15 to 30 minutes. The interval from ingestion to death is more variable than the intravenous route, with the median time to death being approximately 1 to 3 hours, although it can range up to 6 hours or more depending on gastrointestinal absorption and metabolic factors.
\end{itemize}

The primary prognostic factor influencing the success and peace of the procedure is the physical preparation of the patient, including adequate hydration, proper administration of antiemetics, and a quiet, comfortable environment. For patients who undergo the assessment process but choose not to proceed with VAD, their prognosis is determined by the natural history of their underlying disease, with survival rates and symptom trajectories managed via standard palliative care protocols. Statistics indicate that approximately 20\% to 30\% of patients who receive a VAD permit never actually administer the medication, deriving comfort and a sense of control simply from knowing the option is available to them.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
In the context of voluntary assisted dying, ``prevention and self-care'' does not refer to preventing the death itself, but rather to the prevention of unnecessary distress, pain, and complications during the end-of-life journey, alongside the psychological self-care required by patients, families, and healthcare providers.

Key preventive and self-care strategies include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Early Advance Care Planning}: Engaging in open discussions regarding end-of-life preferences and documenting these in an Advance Care Directive or appointing a Medical Treatment Decision Maker. This prevents confusion, family conflict, and unwanted medical interventions if the patient loses decision-making capacity.
    \item \textbf{Optimization of Palliative Self-Care}: Utilizing non-pharmacological comfort measures, such as positioning to ease breathing, mouth care to prevent dryness, and relaxation techniques. Patients should actively report changes in symptom severity to their care team to prevent symptoms from becoming refractory.
    \item \textbf{Psychological and Spiritual Preparation}: Engaging in legacy work (e.g., writing letters, recording messages), spiritual counselling, or mindfulness practices to address existential anxiety and facilitate closure with loved ones.
    \item \textbf{Support Network Mobilisation}: Setting boundaries and determining who will be present during the final administration process. Creating a calming physical environment with familiar music, soft lighting, and comfortable seating for family members.
    \item \textbf{Post-VAD Care for Bereaved Families}: Arranging structured bereavement support, counseling, and support groups for family members to prevent complicated grief and post-traumatic stress.
    \item \textbf{Self-Care for Healthcare Providers}: Clinicians participating in VAD must engage in regular peer support, reflective practice, and professional debriefing to manage the emotional and moral load associated with assisted dying care.
\end{itemize}
