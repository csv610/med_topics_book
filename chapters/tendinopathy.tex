\section*{Definition and Overview}
Tendinopathy is the medical umbrella term for a painful, often stubbornly recurring condition of a tendon, the strong cord of tissue that attaches muscle to bone. It replaces older names such as tendonitis and tendinosis and covers the whole spectrum of tendon problems, from the tendon that is overloaded and painful after a sudden increase in activity to the chronically thickened, degenerate tendon that has troubled someone for months or years. The old assumption was that all tendon pain was due to inflammation, and that resting and taking anti-inflammatory medicines would fix it; modern understanding is more nuanced. In most persistent cases the tendon shows signs of failed healing and degeneration rather than classic inflammation, and the treatment that works best is not rest but carefully graded loading through specific exercises. Tendinopathy is extremely common: it affects active people of all ages, from weekend runners with Achilles pain to office workers with elbow pain from repetitive mouse use and older adults with shoulder tendon pain. The good news is that the majority of tendinopathies improve, although they typically take weeks to months and respond better to patience and structured exercise than to a quick fix. This chapter explains what tendons do, why they become painful, which tendons are most commonly affected, and how modern treatment with progressive loading can restore comfortable function.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Tendinopathy is among the commonest musculoskeletal problems seen in primary care, accounting for a large share of sports-injury consultations and a substantial amount of workplace-related pain. It becomes more common with age, and although it affects both sexes, men are over-represented in many studies, particularly for Achilles and patellar tendon problems. The most frequently affected sites are the Achilles tendon, the patellar tendon below the kneecap, the rotator cuff tendons around the shoulder, the tendons at the outer elbow, and the gluteal tendons at the hip. Certain groups are at high risk: runners and jumpers for lower-limb tendinopathy, swimmers and throwers for shoulder problems, people doing repetitive manual work for elbow and wrist pain, and older adults for shoulder and hip tendon disease. Once a tendon has been painful, recurrence is common, with many people reporting repeated episodes over years, and the burden is considerable in lost sport and work time and in the frustration of a problem that feels slow to resolve.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
A tendon is built from collagen fibres that lie parallel to the line of pull, along with cells and a ground substance, and it is designed to transmit force efficiently from muscle to bone. Tendons are living tissue that adapts to load: they become stronger with sensible loading and weaker with disuse. Tendinopathy develops when the demand placed on a tendon repeatedly exceeds its capacity, so that micro-damage accumulates faster than the tissue can repair.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Overload:} a sudden increase in the amount, intensity, or frequency of activity, such as jumping into a training program too quickly, is the commonest trigger
    \item \textbf{Repetitive strain:} repeated movements in sport or work, such as running, throwing, hammering, or using a computer mouse, load the same tendon many thousands of times
    \item \textbf{Deconditioning:} a sedentary period leaves tendons weaker, so a subsequent burst of activity overloads them; age-related tendon change increases this vulnerability
    \item \textbf{Altered biomechanics:} poor foot posture, hip or core weakness, muscle tightness, and faulty technique change how load is shared between tissues
    \item \textbf{Muscle weakness:} when the muscle that drives a tendon is weak, the tendon takes a disproportionate share of the load
    \item \textbf{Metabolic and systemic factors:} conditions such as diabetes, high cholesterol, and gout are associated with a higher rate of tendinopathy, and some antibiotics (quinolones) and steroid injections can damage tendons
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle factors:} smoking, poor sleep, and obesity are associated with slower tendon healing
\end{itemize}

The tissue response is central to understanding treatment. In acute, very recent injury there may be some true inflammation, but in most chronic cases the tendon shows disorganisation of collagen, increased cell numbers, and areas of degeneration, with impaired ability to repair. This is why rest alone often fails and why graded loading, which stimulates the tendon cells to rebuild orderly collagen, is the cornerstone of management.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tenderness:} pain that is felt directly over the tendon, such as at the back of the heel, below the kneecap, or at the outer side of the elbow
    \item \textbf{Pain with use:} pain that comes on with activity, especially with the first movements in the morning or after rest, and eases as the tendon "warms up"
    \item \textbf{Load-related pain:} pain reproduced by specific movements or resisted muscle contractions, such as standing on tiptoes for the Achilles or gripping for the elbow
    \item \textbf{Morning stiffness:} a stiff, sore feeling in the tendon area for the first half hour or so after waking
    \item \textbf{Thickening:} the tendon can feel thicker, firmer, or knobbly compared with the other side
    \item \textbf{Stiffness after inactivity:} pain and stiffness after sitting for long periods, which can make getting up painful
    \item \textbf{Progressive onset:} most often gradual, building over weeks, rather than following a single dramatic injury
    \item \textbf{Reduced performance:} difficulty with sport, stairs, carrying, gripping, or raising the arm, depending on the tendon affected
\end{itemize}

The pain can be surprisingly severe and persistent, and it is worth repeating that a painful tendon is not necessarily inflamed in the classic sense; the diagnosis and treatment follow from understanding load, not simply from the word "itis".

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tendon tear or rupture:} a partial or complete tear causes sudden weakness and sometimes a gap in the tendon; an Achilles rupture has a classic popping sensation and inability to stand on tiptoe
    \item \textbf{Bursitis:} inflammation of the fluid sacs that cushion tendons, such as retrocalcaneal bursitis behind the heel or subacromial bursitis at the shoulder, often coexists with tendinopathy
    \item \textbf{Arthritis:} joint pain from osteoarthritis or inflammatory arthritis can be mistaken for tendon pain, particularly at the shoulder and hip
    \item \textbf{Impingement:} shoulder impingement involves tendons, bursae, and bony anatomy and overlaps heavily with rotator cuff tendinopathy
    \item \textbf{Nerve pain:} referred pain from the neck or a nerve entrapment can mimic tendon pain; ulnar nerve problems can cause elbow pain, and sciatica can cause buttock and leg pain that resembles gluteal tendinopathy
    \item \textbf{Fracture:} an avulsion fracture at a tendon insertion, or a stress fracture in an active person, can mimic tendon pain and is suggested by marked point tenderness over bone
    \item \textbf{Infection:} rare infective tendon or joint problems cause severe pain with redness, swelling, and fever
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most early tendinopathy can be managed with sensible load modification and self-care, but a professional review is worthwhile when pain persists or function is limited.

Seek medical advice if you have:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain that does not improve with a few weeks of activity modification and self-care
    \item Pain that interferes with work, sport, sleep, or daily activities
    \item Sudden weakness or a popping sensation in a tendon, particularly the Achilles
    \item Swelling, redness, or fever, which can indicate infection
    \item Recurrent episodes, or a tendon problem in someone with diabetes, gout, or another chronic condition
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services or seek urgent care for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Inability to bear weight, or sudden inability to stand on tiptoe after a pop in the calf or heel, which suggests an Achilles rupture
    \item Severe pain with rapidly spreading redness, swelling, and fever
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of tendinopathy is usually clinical, and imaging is needed mainly to confirm the site of the problem, exclude a tear, or guide treatment in stubborn cases.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the pattern of pain, which activities provoke it, the training or work history, and any past injury
    \item \textbf{Examination:} palpating the tendon for tenderness and thickening, and testing the strength and load tolerance of the affected muscle with resisted movements
    \item \textbf{Functional tests:} for example, single-leg heel raises for the Achilles, single-leg squat for the patellar tendon, and resisted grip for the elbow
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} shows tendon thickening, disorganised structure, and tears, and is useful at the bedside; it also allows ultrasound-guided injections if needed
    \item \textbf{MRI:} provides detailed images of tendon structure and is used when ultrasound is inconclusive or a deep problem such as a partial tear is suspected
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} occasionally used to check for gout, inflammatory arthritis, diabetes, or other metabolic contributors
    \item \textbf{Referral:} persistent or complicated cases may be referred to a sports medicine physician or a physiotherapist specialising in tendinopathy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Modern management is built around education and graded loading. The tendon needs a period of reduced demand followed by a structured, progressive exercise program that stimulates repair.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Education and reassurance:} understanding that tendons recover slowly, over months, and that some discomfort during exercise is expected and not damaging
    \item \textbf{Relative rest and load management:} reduce, but do not completely stop, the aggravating activity; pain during activity should be kept at a manageable level rather than eliminated entirely
    \item \textbf{Isometric exercises:} pain-relieving holds of the affected muscle, such as wall sits or heel raises against resistance, which can settle pain within days
    \item \textbf{Isotonic strengthening:} slow, controlled strengthening exercises (eccentric, concentric, or heavy slow resistance) that load the tendon progressively and are the core of treatment
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy guidance:} a physiotherapist can design a graduated program and correct biomechanics, which improves outcomes and reduces recurrence
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} paracetamol or a short course of an anti-inflammatory medicine can help with early pain, but anti-inflammatories are not a long-term treatment and may hinder tendon repair in some cases
    \item \textbf{Adjunct therapies:} massage, dry needling, and shockwave therapy are used by some practitioners; evidence is strongest for shockwave therapy in certain stubborn cases
    \item \textbf{Injections:} corticosteroid injections give short-term relief but carry a risk of weakening the tendon and recurrence; platelet-rich plasma and other injections remain of unproven benefit
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} reserved for severe cases that fail a full rehabilitation program, with the specific operation depending on the site
    \item \textbf{Treat contributing factors:} improve foot posture, core and hip strength, technique, sleep, and any metabolic condition
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chronic pain and dysfunction:} untreated or mismanaged tendinopathy can become persistent, limiting sport, work, and daily life
    \item \textbf{Tendon tear:} a degenerate tendon is weaker and can tear, particularly the Achilles
    \item \textbf{Recurrence:} many people relapse when they return to full activity too quickly or abandon their exercise program
    \item \textbf{Secondary problems:} compensating movements can cause other muscle and joint pain, such as knee, hip, or back problems
    \item \textbf{Effects of medication:} repeated corticosteroid injections can weaken the tendon and increase the risk of rupture
    \item \textbf{Psychological effects:} prolonged pain and lost sport can cause frustration and low mood
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Tendinopathy is slow to settle, and the single most important message for patients is that it takes time: most people need three to six months of consistent, graded exercise to return to full activity, and some need longer, especially with longer-standing or more severe disease. Early cases treated with good load management and a proper strengthening program generally do very well, and outcomes depend more on adherence to the exercise program and on addressing the underlying cause of overload than on any single injection. Once recovered, tendons remain vulnerable to recurrence, so maintaining strength, building up training loads gradually, and protecting the tendon during returns to sport are the keys to staying pain-free. The overall message is optimistic: this is a treatable, mechanical problem that responds to understanding and effort, not a degenerative dead end.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Build up the intensity, duration, and frequency of exercise gradually, following the ten percent rule of increasing training load by no more than about ten percent per week
    \item Strengthen muscles progressively, including the muscle group around any tendon you rely on heavily for sport or work
    \item Warm up properly and cool down after activity, and allow adequate recovery days between intense sessions
    \item Correct technique and footwear, and address foot posture, hip, and core strength with professional help
    \item Take rest days and vary activity to avoid repetitive overload of a single tendon
    \item Maintain a healthy weight, good sleep, and good diabetes control, all of which support tendon health
    \item If tendon pain begins, act early: reduce the aggravating activity and start gentle strengthening rather than pushing through pain
\end{itemize}
