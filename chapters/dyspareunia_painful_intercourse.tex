\chapter{Dyspareunia (Painful Intercourse)}
\index{Dyspareunia (Painful Intercourse)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dyspareunia is the medical term for persistent or recurrent pain during or after sexual intercourse. It is a common and distressing problem that affects women more often than men, and it has physical, psychological, and relationship dimensions. The pain may be felt at the entrance of the vagina, deep inside the pelvis, or throughout intercourse, and the causes range from simple and easily treated problems such as dryness or infection to more complex conditions such as endometriosis or pelvic floor dysfunction. This chapter explains the causes of painful intercourse and the treatments available, which are effective for most women.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Painful intercourse affects a substantial proportion of women, with studies suggesting that one in ten to one in five women experiences dyspareunia at some stage, and it is more common around the menopause, after childbirth, and after pelvic surgery. Many women do not seek help, either from embarrassment or from believing the pain is unavoidable. The condition is associated with reduced sexual satisfaction, relationship strain, and psychological distress.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The causes of dyspareunia are diverse and often multiple.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vaginal dryness:} reduced lubrication from inadequate arousal, menopause, breastfeeding, or certain medications
    \item \textbf{Infection:} vaginal infections such as thrush, bacterial vaginosis, and sexually transmitted infections
    \item \textbf{Skin conditions:} vulval skin conditions such as lichen sclerosus and dermatitis
    \item \textbf{Vaginismus:} involuntary tightening of the pelvic floor muscles, making penetration painful or impossible
    \item \textbf{Pelvic conditions:} endometriosis, adenomyosis, fibroids, ovarian cysts, and pelvic inflammatory disease cause deep pain
    \item \textbf{Pelvic floor problems:} prolapse, pelvic floor dysfunction, and scarring after childbirth or surgery
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy and surgery:} treatment for gynaecological cancers can cause vaginal shortening, dryness, and scarring
    \item \textbf{Psychological factors:} anxiety, previous trauma, and relationship difficulties
    \item \textbf{In men:} pain is less common and may be due to infection, phimosis, or Peyronie's disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The character and timing of the pain provide important clues to the cause.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain at the entrance of the vagina, which may be a burning or tearing sensation
    \item Deep pain during thrusting, felt in the pelvis or lower abdomen
    \item Pain that begins only with penetration, or pain that persists after intercourse
    \item Dryness and soreness
    \item Itching, discharge, or burning suggesting infection
    \item Involuntary tightening of the pelvic floor muscles
    \item Avoidance of sexual activity because of anticipated pain
    \item In men, pain during intercourse or ejaculation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The pattern of pain directs the search for the underlying cause.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vaginismus and pelvic floor hypertonicity, with difficulty with penetration
    \item Vulvodynia, chronic vulval pain without an identifiable cause
    \item Infectious causes, with discharge, itching, or odour
    \item Endometriosis and other deep pelvic conditions, with deep pain
    \item Post-childbirth or post-surgical scarring
    \item Menopause-related atrophy of the vaginal tissues
    \item Psychological and relationship factors, which often coexist with physical causes
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any woman experiencing persistent pain during or after intercourse should see a GP. Early assessment identifies treatable causes and prevents the condition from becoming entrenched. Women should also be reviewed after pelvic surgery or cancer treatment if pain develops.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe pelvic pain during or after intercourse
    \item Heavy vaginal bleeding after intercourse
    \item Fever, chills, or a foul-smelling discharge
    \item Sudden collapse or severe faintness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis begins with a careful and sensitive history, followed by examination and targeted tests.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} location, timing, and character of pain, and its context and impact
    \item \textbf{Pelvic examination:} gentle examination to identify dryness, infection, skin conditions, tenderness, or masses
    \item \textbf{Swabs:} tests for thrush, bacterial vaginosis, and sexually transmitted infections
    \item \textbf{Pelvic ultrasound:} to assess the uterus, ovaries, and pelvic structures
    \item \textbf{Laparoscopy:} for suspected endometriosis
    \item \textbf{Psychological assessment:} exploring anxiety, trauma, and relationship factors
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is directed at the underlying cause and is often very effective.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lubricants and moisturisers:} water-based lubricants and vaginal moisturisers relieve dryness
    \item \textbf{Vaginal oestrogen:} low-dose treatment for menopausal atrophy
    \item \textbf{Treatment of infection:} antifungal or antibiotic treatment as appropriate
    \item \textbf{Pelvic floor physiotherapy:} exercises and techniques to relax and retrain the pelvic floor
    \item \textbf{Treatment of pelvic conditions:} hormonal or surgical treatment for endometriosis, fibroids, and other causes
    \item \textbf{Counselling and sex therapy:} addressing anxiety, trauma, and relationship issues
    \item \textbf{Cognitive behavioural therapy:} effective for chronic pain and for vaginismus
    \item \textbf{Graduated approaches:} for vaginismus, a programme of gradual desensitisation under guidance
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Avoidance of intimacy and reduced sexual satisfaction
    \item Relationship conflict and strain
    \item Anxiety, low mood, and reduced self-esteem
    \item Delayed diagnosis of underlying conditions
    \item Difficulty with gynaecological examinations and contraception use
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for dyspareunia is good when the cause is identified and treated. Dryness, infection, and menopausal causes respond well to simple treatment, while vaginismus and pelvic floor problems usually improve with physiotherapy and counselling. Women with endometriosis often improve with medical or surgical management. Seeking help early, and involving a partner where appropriate, improves outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use lubricant generously, especially after menopause, childbirth, or pelvic surgery
    \item Allow sufficient time for arousal before intercourse
    \item Communicate openly with your partner about comfort and preferences
    \item Maintain good vulval care: avoid harsh soaps and wear breathable underwear
    \item Practise relaxation techniques to reduce pelvic floor tension
    \item See a doctor early rather than enduring pain
    \item Seek pelvic floor physiotherapy after childbirth or pelvic surgery
\end{itemize}
