\chapter{Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hormone replacement therapy (HRT), also called menopausal hormone therapy, is treatment with oestrogen, with or without progestogen, to relieve the symptoms of menopause and protect against the long-term effects of oestrogen deficiency. It is the most effective treatment for hot flushes and night sweats, and it also improves sleep, mood, vaginal dryness, and bone health. Oestrogen is used alone in women without a uterus and combined with progestogen in those with a uterus to protect the uterine lining. HRT is individualised, and modern practice uses the lowest effective dose for the shortest appropriate time, with the risks and benefits discussed with each woman.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Up to three-quarters of women experience vasomotor symptoms at menopause, and around a quarter have symptoms severe enough to affect daily life. Despite the effectiveness of HRT, many women do not use it: Australian rates are modest, with fewer than one in five eligible women taking HRT, largely because of fear generated by early studies that overestimated risks. For most healthy women under 60 who start HRT near menopause, the benefits substantially outweigh the risks. Use of vaginal oestrogen for genitourinary symptoms is increasingly common and carries minimal systemic risk.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Menopause:} declining ovarian oestrogen production causes vasomotor instability, vaginal changes, sleep disturbance, and bone loss
    \item \textbf{Oestrogen:} stabilises the temperature-regulating centre, thickens the vaginal lining, supports bone, and improves mood and sleep
    \item \textbf{Progestogen:} protects the uterine lining from the growth-promoting effect of oestrogen in women with a uterus
    \item \textbf{Delivery routes:} tablets, patches, gels, and vaginal preparations differ in absorption and effects
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} replacing oestrogen reverses many of the physiological changes of menopause
    \item \textbf{Risk mechanisms:} oestrogen slightly increases clotting and breast cell activity in some women, underpinning the small increases in venous thrombosis, stroke, and breast cancer risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item HRT is considered for troublesome hot flushes, night sweats, and sleep disturbance
    \item Vaginal oestrogen treats vaginal dryness, discomfort, and recurrent urinary infections
    \item HRT helps prevent post-menopausal bone loss and fractures
    \item Symptoms improve within weeks for flushes and sleep
    \item Individual responses vary, and doses are adjusted
    \item Treatment is reviewed regularly and can be tapered when symptoms settle
    \item Women who have had a hysterectomy use oestrogen alone
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Menopausal symptoms overlap with thyroid disease, depression, and anaemia, which should be considered
    \item Vasomotor symptoms may be caused by other conditions and medicines
    \item The decision to use HRT is individualised by age, time since menopause, and risk profile
    \item Alternative options: non-hormonal medicines and cognitive behavioural therapy help some women
    \item Not all women with menopausal symptoms need or want HRT
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Women with troublesome menopausal symptoms should discuss HRT with their doctor, including the risks and benefits for their age and health profile. Any post-menopausal bleeding requires urgent review.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, sudden breathlessness, or a swollen painful leg (possible blood clot)
    \item Severe headache, visual disturbance, or signs of stroke
    \item Sudden heavy vaginal bleeding
    \item Collapse or fainting
    \item Breast lump or discharge with other concerning symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} symptom review and confirmation of menopausal status
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} blood pressure, weight, blood clot history, breast cancer risk, and cardiovascular risk
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} FSH may help in younger women or unclear cases
    \item \textbf{Bone density testing:} where osteoporosis risk is elevated
    \item \textbf{Breast screening:} routine mammography continues as recommended
    \item \textbf{Regular review:} annual assessment of symptoms, side effects, and ongoing need
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Systemic HRT:} oestrogen tablets, patches, or gels, with progestogen in women with a uterus
    \item \textbf{Vaginal oestrogen:} low-dose creams, pessaries, or rings for genitourinary symptoms
    \item \textbf{Regimens:} continuous or sequential progestogen depending on the woman's needs
    \item \textbf{Dosing:} the lowest effective dose, adjusted to symptoms
    \item \textbf{Duration:} often 2--5 years for symptoms, with longer use for bone protection in some women
    \item \textbf{Tapering:} gradual dose reduction when stopping
    \item \textbf{Non-hormonal options:} some antidepressants and CBT reduce flushes for women who cannot use HRT
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} exercise, calcium and vitamin D, and healthy weight support overall health
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Side effects: breast tenderness, nausea, bloating, and breakthrough bleeding early on
    \item Small increased risk of venous thromboembolism and stroke, mainly with tablets in older women
    \item Small increased risk of breast cancer with prolonged combined use, falling after stopping
    \item Gallbladder disease with tablets
    \item Vaginal bleeding, which requires review
    \item Risks are lower with patches and gels than tablets for clotting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For most healthy women who start HRT near menopause, the benefits outweigh the risks: symptoms are relieved, quality of life improves, and fracture risk falls. The absolute risks of serious harm are small, especially in women under 60, and decline after stopping. Women who cannot or choose not to use HRT have effective alternatives. The best outcomes follow individualised decision-making, regular review, and attention to overall cardiovascular and bone health.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discuss symptoms openly with your doctor rather than accepting them
    \item Ask about the risks and benefits of HRT for your age and health
    \item Use the lowest effective dose and review treatment regularly
    \item Report any unusual bleeding promptly
    \item Maintain physical activity, calcium, and vitamin D for bone health
    \item Manage cardiovascular risk: no smoking, healthy diet, and blood pressure control
    \item Consider non-hormonal options if HRT is not suitable
    \item Seek support from menopause information services
\end{itemize}
