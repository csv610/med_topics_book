\chapter{Iliac Vein Thrombosis}
\index{Iliac Vein Thrombosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Iliac vein thrombosis is a blood clot (deep vein thrombosis, DVT) that forms in one of the iliac veins, the large veins in the pelvis that drain blood from the legs back towards the heart. It is a form of proximal deep vein thrombosis that can obstruct the main venous drainage of an entire leg, causing sudden swelling, pain, and a feeling of heaviness. Iliac vein clots are at high risk of breaking off and travelling to the lungs, causing a pulmonary embolism, which can be life-threatening. They can also cause long-term damage to the leg veins, leading to chronic venous insufficiency and post-thrombotic syndrome. Prompt diagnosis and treatment with blood thinners are essential.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Deep vein thrombosis affects around 1 to 2 in 1000 people each year, and around a fifth to a quarter of these clots involve the iliac or femoral veins. Iliofemoral thrombosis is more common in younger people than more distal clots, partly because of conditions such as May--Thurner syndrome, and it is associated with pregnancy, the oral contraceptive pill, pelvic surgery, and cancer. The risk rises sharply after surgery, with long-haul travel, and with age and obesity. If left untreated, iliofemoral thrombosis carries a substantial risk of pulmonary embolism, and it is the pattern of clot most likely to leave long-term leg symptoms.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
As with all blood clots, iliac vein thrombosis usually results from a combination of factors described by Virchow's triad: reduced blood flow, injury to the vein wall, and a tendency to abnormal clotting. The iliac vein can be compressed where it crosses under the right iliac artery (May--Thurner syndrome), which narrows the vein and promotes clot formation, particularly in young women. Other important risk factors include surgery, especially of the hip, pelvis, or abdomen; immobility and bed rest; pregnancy and the post-partum period; cancer; hormone therapy including the combined oral contraceptive pill; obesity; and inherited or acquired clotting disorders such as Factor V Leiden. Many patients have more than one risk factor.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Swelling of the entire leg, often sudden in onset and greater than with more distal clots
    \item Pain, aching, heaviness, or a feeling of tightness in the affected leg
    \item Redness, warmth, and a shiny appearance of the skin
    \item In severe cases the leg may appear pale or blue and feel tense (phlegmasia), indicating severe obstruction
    \item Visible superficial veins in the thigh and lower abdomen that enlarge as collaterals develop
    \item Often the clot produces no symptoms and is found on imaging performed for other reasons
    \item A pulmonary embolism may be the first presentation: sudden breathlessness, chest pain, or collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of a swollen, painful leg have several other possible causes that should be distinguished from a clot.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Other deep vein thromboses:} clots in the femoral, popliteal, or calf veins
    \item \textbf{Musculoskeletal injury:} muscle strain, tear, or haematoma
    \item \textbf{Cellulitis:} bacterial skin infection causing redness, warmth, and swelling
    \item \textbf{Lymphoedema:} chronic swelling from lymphatic obstruction, often after cancer treatment
    \item \textbf{Ruptured Baker's cyst:} swelling behind the knee that can mimic a DVT
    \item \textbf{Superficial thrombophlebitis:} clot in a superficial vein, which is less serious but can extend
    \item \textbf{Compartment syndrome or severe arterial occlusion:} both emergencies with a tense, painful limb
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with new, unexplained swelling or pain in one leg, particularly with risk factors such as recent surgery, pregnancy, or cancer, should be assessed promptly. A D-dimer blood test and ultrasound can usually be arranged through a GP or emergency department. Seek urgent assessment if symptoms develop after long-haul travel or bed rest, or if the leg becomes very tense and discoloured.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden breathlessness, chest pain, coughing up blood, or collapse, which may indicate a pulmonary embolism
    \item A leg that becomes very tense, pale, blue, or cold, suggesting severe obstruction (phlegmasia)
    \item Sudden severe leg pain with loss of feeling or movement
    \item Fainting or a rapid heartbeat with breathing difficulty
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical probability assessment:} validated scores such as the Wells score combine risk factors and findings
    \item \textbf{D-dimer blood test:} a negative result in a low-risk person makes a clot unlikely; a raised level is not specific and needs imaging
    \item \textbf{Venous ultrasound:} the first-line imaging, showing clot in the iliac or femoral veins and whether it is compressible
    \item \textbf{Computed tomography venography (CTV) or magnetic resonance venography (MRV):} for better visualisation of the iliac veins when ultrasound is inconclusive
    \item \textbf{Chest imaging:} CT pulmonary angiography if a pulmonary embolism is suspected
    \item \textbf{Blood tests for clotting disorders:} thrombophilia screening in younger patients, those with recurrent clots, or a strong family history
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anticoagulation:} blood-thinning medicines are the cornerstone of treatment, usually starting with an injectable or oral anticoagulant and continuing for at least 3--6 months
    \item \textbf{Compression stockings:} fitted graduated compression reduces swelling and the risk of post-thrombotic syndrome
    \item \textbf{Elevation and activity:} keeping the leg elevated when resting and remaining mobile once anticoagulation is established
    \item \textbf{Catheter-directed thrombolysis:} in selected cases, clot-dissolving medicine is delivered directly into the vein to restore flow, particularly in young patients with extensive iliofemoral clots
    \item \textbf{Mechanical thrombectomy:} removal of the clot with a catheter device in severe cases
    \item \textbf{Stenting:} placement of a stent if the iliac vein is compressed (May--Thurner syndrome) after clot removal
    \item \textbf{Long-term follow-up:} review of anticoagulant duration and ongoing leg symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pulmonary embolism, which can be fatal
    \item Post-thrombotic syndrome: chronic pain, swelling, skin discolouration, and leg ulcers developing months to years later
    \item Phlegmasia: severe obstruction that threatens the limb and can require urgent intervention
    \item Bleeding from anticoagulant treatment
    \item Recurrent thrombosis if risk factors persist or anticoagulation is stopped too early
    \item Chronic venous insufficiency from damaged vein valves
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt anticoagulation, the immediate risk of pulmonary embolism falls dramatically and most clots gradually resolve or organise without complete recanalisation. Long-term outcome depends on how completely the vein recanalises and on the underlying cause. Post-thrombotic syndrome affects a substantial minority of people with iliofemoral thrombosis and can cause lasting leg symptoms, which is one reason early, aggressive treatment with clot removal is considered in suitable patients. Recurrent clot risk depends on whether the cause was temporary (such as surgery) or permanent (such as a clotting disorder or cancer).

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Move regularly, especially during long flights, car trips, and periods of illness or bed rest
    \item Stay well hydrated and avoid prolonged sitting without breaks
    \item After surgery, follow the team's advice about early mobilisation and any prescribed preventive blood thinners
    \item Wear compression stockings during pregnancy, long-haul travel, or after a clot as advised
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and stop smoking
    \item Know the signs of a new clot and seek early review if the leg swells again
    \item Take anticoagulants exactly as prescribed and attend blood monitoring where required
\end{itemize}
