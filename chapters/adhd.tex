\chapter{Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)}
\index{Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterised by persistent difficulties with attention, hyperactivity and impulsivity that are more severe than expected for a person's age and that interfere with everyday life. It usually begins in childhood and often continues into adolescence and adulthood. ADHD is not caused by poor parenting, too much sugar or too much screen time, and it is not a character flaw or a sign of low intelligence. There are three main presentations: predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, and combined, in which both patterns are present. With appropriate recognition and support, people with ADHD manage well and lead productive lives.

\section*{Epidemiology}
ADHD is one of the most common childhood neurodevelopmental disorders, affecting roughly one in twenty children, and it is diagnosed about twice as often in boys as in girls. Girls are more likely to have the inattentive form, which is easily missed because it is less disruptive. Symptoms persist into adolescence in many cases and into adulthood in a substantial minority of those affected. ADHD frequently coexists with learning difficulties, anxiety, depression, autism spectrum disorder and sleep problems. Reported diagnosis rates have risen over recent decades, in part because of better awareness and recognition by schools, families and clinicians.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
ADHD has a strong biological basis, and the exact cause is not fully understood.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} ADHD runs in families, and inherited factors play a large role
    \item \textbf{Brain differences:} imaging studies show differences in the size, timing of development and activity of brain regions involved in attention and self-control
    \item \textbf{Chemical messengers:} alterations in the brain chemicals dopamine and noradrenaline, which help regulate attention and impulse control
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy factors:} prematurity, low birth weight, and smoking or alcohol use in pregnancy are associated with a higher risk
    \item \textbf{Environment:} family stress and parenting style do not cause ADHD but can influence how symptoms are managed and experienced
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms must be present before the age of 12, occur in more than one setting (for example at home and at school), and cause real difficulty in daily functioning.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inattention:} trouble sustaining focus, careless mistakes, not listening when spoken to, losing belongings, avoiding tasks that need sustained concentration, being easily distracted and forgetfulness
    \item \textbf{Hyperactivity:} fidgeting or squirming, inability to stay seated, running or climbing at inappropriate times, being unable to play quietly, talking excessively and being constantly "on the go"
    \item \textbf{Impulsivity:} blurting out answers, difficulty waiting a turn, interrupting others and acting without considering the consequences
\end{itemize}
In adults, the same core difficulties present as poor time management, forgetfulness, restlessness and difficulty completing tasks.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Many other problems can look like ADHD, so a careful assessment is essential.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Normal variation in behaviour, or being young for the class group
    \item Anxiety and depression, which can cause poor concentration
    \item Learning disorders, such as dyslexia
    \item Autism spectrum disorder
    \item Sleep problems, including obstructive sleep apnoea, which cause daytime inattention
    \item Hearing or vision problems
    \item Oppositional defiant disorder and conduct disorder
    \item Bipolar disorder, when considering ADHD in adults
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a GP if inattention or hyperactivity is affecting learning, school performance, friendships, family life or safety. A diagnosis requires a thorough specialist assessment, so the GP usually refers to a paediatrician, child and adolescent psychiatrist, or clinical psychologist.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services or seek urgent help for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Talk of self-harm or suicide, especially in an adolescent with ADHD
    \item Severe agitation, aggression or behaviour that puts the person or others in danger
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
There is no single test for ADHD; the diagnosis is made clinically by an experienced specialist.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detailed history:} information from parents, the young person and often school teachers about behaviour across settings
    \item \textbf{Questionnaires:} standardised rating scales such as the Conners or Vanderbilt forms, completed by parents and teachers
    \item \textbf{Developmental history:} when symptoms began and whether they have been present consistently over time
    \item \textbf{Observation:} of behaviour during the assessment
    \item \textbf{Exclusion of other causes:} checks of hearing, vision and sleep, and sometimes blood tests (such as thyroid function) if another condition is suspected
    \item \textbf{Adult assessment:} usually with a psychiatrist or psychologist, involving a partner, parent or long-term friend to confirm symptoms in childhood and adulthood
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment combines education, behavioural strategies and, where appropriate, medication.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychoeducation:} learning about ADHD for the person and family, which reduces blame and improves coping
    \item \textbf{Behavioural parent training:} practical strategies for managing behaviour, often the first step for young children
    \item \textbf{School support:} classroom adjustments, structured routines and individualised learning plans
    \item \textbf{Psychological therapy:} cognitive behaviour therapy to build organisation, time management and coping skills, particularly in older children and adults
    \item \textbf{Medication:} stimulants such as methylphenidate and dexamphetamine, and non-stimulants such as atomoxetine, prescribed and monitored by a specialist
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} regular sleep, physical activity and predictable routines
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular review to monitor symptoms, side effects, growth in children and response to treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Without recognition and support, ADHD can have significant effects on life.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Academic underachievement and school dropout
    \item Low self-esteem and frequent criticism from others
    \item Anxiety and depression, which are more common in people with ADHD
    \item Increased risk of accidental injury
    \item Relationship and family conflict
    \item Higher rates of substance use problems
    \item Occupational difficulties in adulthood
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
ADHD is a lifelong condition for many people, but it is highly manageable. Hyperactivity and impulsivity often lessen with age, although difficulties with attention and organisation may persist. With appropriate treatment and support, most children learn to manage their symptoms, succeed at school and lead productive adult lives. Early recognition and consistent support are associated with the best outcomes, and many adults with ADHD develop effective coping strategies and successful careers.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
ADHD cannot be prevented in the usual sense, because it is largely inherited, but its impact can be reduced.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Avoid smoking and alcohol during pregnancy
    \item Recognise symptoms early and seek a proper assessment rather than waiting
    \item Build consistent routines for sleep, meals, homework and activities
    \item Use clear, short instructions and positive reinforcement at home
    \item Ensure regular physical activity and limit screen time before bed
    \item Work closely with school and health professionals
    \item Attend follow-up appointments and review treatment regularly
\end{itemize}
