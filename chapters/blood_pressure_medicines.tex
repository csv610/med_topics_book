\chapter{Blood Pressure Medicines}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Blood pressure medicines (antihypertensives) are medicines used to lower blood pressure in people with high blood pressure (hypertension). They work by relaxing blood vessels, reducing the volume of fluid in the body, slowing the heart, or blocking the hormones that raise blood pressure. Lowering blood pressure reduces the risk of heart attack, stroke, kidney damage, and other complications of hypertension. Treatment is usually lifelong, and many people need two or more medicines to reach their blood pressure target. The main classes are ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), calcium channel blockers, diuretics, and beta-blockers. Medicines work best alongside lifestyle measures such as salt reduction, weight loss, and regular exercise.

\section*{Epidemiology}
High blood pressure is one of the most common chronic conditions, affecting roughly one in three adults worldwide, and it becomes more common with age. Most people with hypertension do not feel unwell, which is why it is often called the silent killer -- it is frequently found only when blood pressure is checked. A large majority of people treated for hypertension need more than one medicine, and fixed-dose combination tablets are common. Because hypertension is so widespread, blood pressure medicines are among the most frequently prescribed medicines in the world, and their appropriate use is one of the most effective ways to prevent cardiovascular disease.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Blood pressure is determined by the strength of the heart's pumping, the width of the blood vessels, and the volume of fluid in the circulation. In high blood pressure, the blood vessels narrow or stiffen, the heart works harder, or the kidneys retain too much salt and fluid. The different classes of medicine act on these mechanisms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ACE inhibitors and ARBs:} block hormones that constrict blood vessels, so the vessels relax
    \item \textbf{Calcium channel blockers:} relax the muscle in the vessel walls and widen the vessels
    \item \textbf{Diuretics:} make the kidneys remove extra salt and water, reducing fluid volume
    \item \textbf{Beta-blockers:} slow the heart and reduce the force of its pumping
    \item \textbf{Combination therapy:} different classes used together work better than any one alone and allow lower doses
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Blood pressure medicines cause no symptoms when working well; the features that matter are the side effects, which vary by class:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Dry, persistent cough, most typical of ACE inhibitors
    \item Dizziness, light-headedness, or fainting, especially on standing, from blood pressure dropping too low
    \item Swollen ankles, common with calcium channel blockers
    \item Increased urination and changes in blood potassium with diuretics
    \item Fatigue, cold hands or feet, and a slower heart rate with beta-blockers
    \item Headaches or facial flushing early in treatment, often settling with time
    \item Swelling of the lips, tongue, or face (angioedema) -- a rare but serious reaction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
New symptoms while taking blood pressure medicines need careful sorting out:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A side effect of the medicine versus progression of the underlying heart or kidney disease
    \item A drug interaction versus an unrelated new condition
    \item Dizziness from low blood pressure versus inner ear problems, anaemia, or heart rhythm problems
    \item Cough from an ACE inhibitor versus a chest infection or asthma
    \item Swollen ankles from a calcium channel blocker versus heart failure or kidney disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if you have troublesome side effects, dizziness or fainting, ankle swelling, or difficulty keeping blood pressure within target. Do not stop a blood pressure medicine without medical advice, because stopping suddenly can cause a dangerous rebound rise in blood pressure.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Swelling of the face, lips, or tongue, or difficulty breathing (possible angioedema)
    \item Fainting, collapse, or a very slow heart rate
    \item Chest pain or a sudden, severe headache
    \item Severe weakness, numbness, or difficulty speaking -- possible stroke
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure measurement:} in the clinic and at home, including readings taken seated and standing
    \item \textbf{Medication review:} a full review of all medicines, including herbal and over-the-counter products, to check for interactions
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} kidney function, electrolytes, and potassium levels, especially with ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and diuretics
    \item \textbf{Blood sugar and cholesterol:} measured because they often accompany high blood pressure
    \item \textbf{Heart and kidney checks:} an ECG, and urine tests for protein, where indicated
    \item \textbf{Review of side effects:} to adjust the dose or change class when a medicine is not tolerated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Take as prescribed:} daily, at the time advised, and do not stop or change the dose on your own
    \item \textbf{Monitor:} keep a home blood pressure record and bring it to appointments
    \item \textbf{Combination therapy:} expect that more than one medicine may be needed to reach target
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} continue salt reduction, a healthy diet, exercise, weight loss, and limiting alcohol
    \item \textbf{Side effect management:} dose adjustment, switching class, or symptom treatment, under medical advice
    \item \textbf{Avoid interactions:} check with a pharmacist before adding new medicines, including anti-inflammatory pain relievers, which can raise blood pressure
    \item \textbf{Regular review:} blood pressure, kidney function, and electrolytes are checked at intervals
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy planning:} discuss with your doctor, as some blood pressure medicines are not suitable in pregnancy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Blood pressure falling too low, causing dizziness and falls
    \item Kidney and electrolyte problems, especially with ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and diuretics
    \item Angioedema, a rare but serious reaction requiring urgent care
    \item Drug interactions that raise blood pressure or cause toxicity
    \item Rebound hypertension if a medicine is stopped abruptly
    \item Poorly controlled hypertension continuing to damage the heart, brain, eyes, and kidneys
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Blood pressure medicines are very effective and, when taken as prescribed alongside lifestyle changes, greatly reduce the risk of stroke, heart attack, heart failure, and kidney disease. Most people tolerate treatment well once the right medicine or combination is found, and side effects can usually be managed. Treatment is generally lifelong, and blood pressure can rise again if medicines are stopped. The better the blood pressure control, the better the long-term outlook.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take your medicines every day, at the same time, and use a pill organiser if helpful
    \item Measure blood pressure at home as advised and record the readings
    \item Reduce salt, eat plenty of vegetables, fruit, and whole grains, and keep to a healthy weight
    \item Be physically active most days and limit alcohol
    \item Do not smoke
    \item Bring a complete list of medicines to every appointment and pharmacy visit
    \item Ask your doctor or pharmacist about any new symptom, and never stop treatment abruptly
    \item Attend scheduled checks of blood pressure, kidney function, and electrolytes
\end{itemize}
