\chapter{Movement disorders}
\index{Movement disorders}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Movement disorders are a group of neurological conditions characterised by abnormal, involuntary movements (known as hyperkinesia or dyskinesia) or a slowness and poverty of voluntary movements (bradykinesia or hypokinesia), in the absence of motor weakness or spasticity. These disorders arise from structural or functional dysfunction within the basal ganglia, a group of subcortical nuclei responsible for planning, initiating, smoothing, and executing motor programmes, or within their connecting cerebellar-thalamocortical networks. Common movement disorders include Parkinson's disease, essential tremor, dystonia, Huntington's disease, Tourette syndrome, and myoclonus. They present major clinical challenges, often causing progressive physical disability, social isolation, and reducing overall quality of life.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Movement disorders are highly prevalent, with their incidence rising significantly with age. Parkinson's disease is the second most common neurodegenerative disorder globally after Alzheimer's disease, affecting approximately 100,000 people, with about 30 new cases diagnosed daily. Essential tremor is the most common movement disorder, affecting up to 4\% to 5\% of adults over the age of 40. Restless legs syndrome, another common sensorimotor disorder, is estimated to affect up to 10\% of the population. Tic disorders, including Tourette syndrome, are paediatric-onset conditions, affecting approximately 1\% of school-aged children worldwide.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The underlying pathophysiology of movement disorders involves neurodegeneration, genetic mutations, receptor hypersensitivity, or drug-induced blockades within the extrapyramidal motor system.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Parkinson's disease (Hypokinetic):} Characterised by the progressive degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta. This leads to a severe depletion of dopamine in the striatum, causing over-activity of the indirect pathway and under-activity of the direct pathway within the basal ganglia loop, clinically presenting as bradykinesia and rigidity. The histological hallmark is the presence of Lewy bodies containing aggregated alpha-synuclein protein.
    \item \textbf{Huntington's disease (Hyperkinetic):} An autosomal dominant, progressive neurodegenerative disorder caused by a CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion in the \textit{HTT} gene on chromosome 4. This results in an abnormal huntingtin protein, leading to selective degeneration of GABAergic medium spiny neurons in the striatum, causing a loss of inhibitory control and presenting as chorea.
    \item \textbf{Dystonia:} Characterised by sustained or intermittent muscle contractions causing abnormal, repetitive twisting movements or postures. The pathophysiology involves reduced reciprocal inhibition at spinal, brainstem, and cortical levels, alongside abnormal basal ganglia plastic changes.
    \item \textbf{Essential tremor:} A slowly progressive postural and kinetic tremor, often autosomal dominant. The pathophysiology involves oscillation in the Guillain-Mollaret triangle, involving the inferior olive, red nucleus, and dentate nucleus of the cerebellum.
    \item \textbf{Drug-induced movement disorders:} Blockade of dopamine D2 receptors by antipsychotics or antiemetics (e.g., metoclopramide) can cause acute dystonia, akathisia (motor restlessness), drug-induced parkinsonism, or tardive dyskinesia (chronic, involuntary orofacial movements).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Clinical features are categorized into hypokinetic (slowness and poverty of movement) and hyperkinetic (excessive, involuntary movements) signs.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Parkinsonism:} A clinical syndrome characterised by bradykinesia (slowness of movement and progressive loss of amplitude on rapid repetitive tasks) combined with at least one of: a resting tremor (classic 4--6 Hz ``pill-rolling'' tremor), lead-pipe or cogwheel rigidity, and postural instability leading to falls. Other features include a shuffling gait, micrographia (small handwriting), and hypomimia (masked facies).
    \item \textbf{Tremor:} An involuntary, rhythmic, oscillatory movement of a body part. Essential tremor is typically bilateral, symmetric, postural (occurring when holding hands against gravity), and kinetic (occurring during action like writing or pouring water), improving with alcohol.
    \item \textbf{Chorea:} Involuntary, brief, random, irregular, purposeless movements that flow from one muscle group to another, often incorporated into semi-purposeful actions (parakinesia).
    \item \textbf{Dystonia:} Involuntary, sustained muscle contractions causing twisting or abnormal postures (such as cervical dystonia/torticollis, blepharospasm, or writer's cramp).
    \item \textbf{Myoclonus:} Sudden, brief, shock-like, involuntary muscle jerks originating from the central nervous system.
    \item \textbf{Tics:} Semi-voluntary, repetitive, stereotyped movements (motor tics) or sounds (vocal tics) that are preceded by an uncomfortable premonitory urge, which is temporarily relieved by performing the tic.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Distinguishing between primary neurodegenerative movement disorders and secondary or treatable conditions is a vital clinical task.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Idiopathic Parkinson's disease vs. atypical parkinsonism (Parkinson-Plus syndromes):} Atypical syndromes, such as Progressive Supranuclear Palsy (PSP) and Multiple System Atrophy (MSA), are characterised by early falls, vertical gaze palsy, prominent autonomic dysfunction, and a poor response to levodopa therapy.
    \item \textbf{Essential tremor vs. Parkinsonian tremor:} Essential tremor is kinetic/postural and worsens during activity, whereas Parkinsonian tremor is predominantly a resting tremor that resolves during voluntary movement.
    \item \textbf{Wilson's disease:} A rare, autosomal recessive disorder of copper metabolism that can present with tremor, dystonia, psychiatric features, and Keyser-Fleischer rings in the cornea. It must be ruled out in any patient under 40 presenting with a movement disorder.
    \item \textbf{Functional (psychogenic) movement disorders:} Involuntary movements that are inconsistent over time, distractible on examination, and do not align with established anatomical or physiological patterns of disease.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients experiencing new, progressive, or uncontrolled involuntary movements should seek neurological evaluation. Certain presentations represent medical emergencies.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS): A life-threatening reaction to dopamine antagonists, characterised by high fever, severe muscle rigidity (``lead-pipe''), altered consciousness, autonomic instability, and elevated creatine kinase.
    \item Status dystonicus: A severe, life-threatening exacerbation of generalized dystonia characterised by continuous, painful muscle contractions that can lead to rhabdomyolysis, respiratory failure, and hyperthermia.
    \item Sudden, severe difficulty swallowing (dysphagia) or breathing, posing an immediate risk of aspiration or airway compromise.
    \item Severe falls causing head trauma, fractures, or inability to stand.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of movement disorders is primarily clinical, supported by targeted investigations to rule out secondary causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive neurological examination:} Detailed assessment of muscle tone, power, coordination, deep tendon reflexes, cranial nerves, sensory systems, and formal gait and balance testing.
    \item \textbf{Brain MRI:} Recommended to exclude structural lesions (such as strokes, vascular disease, brain tumours, or normal pressure hydrocephalus) and identify specific neurodegenerative signatures (e.g., caudate atrophy in Huntington's disease or the ``hummingbird sign'' in PSP).
    \item \textbf{Dopamine Transporter Scan (DaTscan):} A SPECT imaging study that assesses the integrity of the presynaptic dopaminergic system, helping to differentiate essential tremor (normal scan) from degenerative parkinsonism (abnormal scan).
    \item \textbf{Biochemical screening:} Serum ceruloplasmin and 24-hour urinary copper levels to screen for Wilson's disease in young patients. Thyroid function tests (to rule out thyrotoxicosis-induced tremor) and serum electrolytes are also performed.
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} Genetic panels are available to confirm diagnoses of Huntington's disease (CAG repeat analysis), spinocerebellar ataxias, and hereditary dystonias.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is tailored to the specific disorder, focusing on symptomatic pharmacotherapy, surgical interventions, and physical rehabilitation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pharmacotherapy for Parkinson's disease:} Levodopa combined with a dopa-decarboxylase inhibitor (carbidopa or benserazide) remains the gold standard treatment. Other agents include dopamine agonists (pramipexole, rotigotine), MAO-B inhibitors (rasagiline), and COMT inhibitors (entacapone) to prolong levodopa efficacy.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacotherapy for tremors and chorea:} Beta-blockers (propranolol) or primidone (an anticonvulsant) for essential tremor. Vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2) inhibitors (tetrabenazine or deutetrabenazine) are used to treat Huntington's chorea and tardive dyskinesia.
    \item \textbf{Botulinum toxin injections:} The first-line therapy for focal dystonias (such as blepharospasm, hemifacial spasm, and cervical dystonia), injected directly into hyperactive muscles under electromyographic (EMG) guidance to cause temporary muscle relaxation.
    \item \textbf{Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS):} A highly effective surgical option involving the implantation of electrodes into specific brain targets (subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus internus for Parkinson's and dystonia; ventral intermediate nucleus of the thalamus for essential tremor), connected to an implantable pulse generator.
    \item \textbf{Allied health rehabilitation:} Ongoing physical therapy to improve gait, balance, and core strength; occupational therapy for home safety and assistive devices; and speech and language therapy (such as LSVT LOUD) to manage dysarthria and dysphagia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The progression of movement disorders is associated with significant physical, cognitive, and psychosocial complications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Falls and fractures:} Postural instability, gait freezing, and involuntary movements significantly increase the risk of recurrent falls, resulting in hip fractures and subdural haematomas.
    \item \textbf{Aspiration pneumonia:} Progressive dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) in late-stage Parkinson's and Huntington's disease can lead to silent aspiration of food or fluids into the lungs, a major cause of death.
    \item \textbf{Neuropsychiatric complications:} Severe depression, anxiety, apathy, visual hallucinations, and cognitive decline or dementia. Dopamine agonists can also cause impulse control disorders (e.g., pathological gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive buying).
    \item \textbf{Levodopa-induced dyskinesias:} Fluctuations in dopamine levels after long-term levodopa use can cause involuntary, choreiform movements (dyskinesias) during peak dose periods, making motor management highly complex.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of movement disorders varies widely depending on the underlying pathology. Essential tremor is slowly progressive but does not impact life expectancy. Parkinson's disease is a chronic, progressive condition; while it is not curable, modern medical and surgical treatments (like DBS) allow patients to maintain high levels of function and a near-normal life expectancy for many years. Huntington's disease is a progressive, neurodegenerative condition that is universally fatal, with a median survival of 15 to 20 years from the onset of motor symptoms. Focal dystonias do not reduce lifespan but often require lifelong botulinum toxin therapy to control symptoms.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Self-care and environmental adaptation are crucial to maintaining independence and preventing secondary injuries.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Falls prevention and home safety:} Remove loose rugs, secure electrical cords, install grab rails in the bathroom and shower, use non-slip mats, and ensure all living areas are well-lit.
    \item \textbf{Regular physical exercise:} Participate in activities that promote balance, flexibility, and strength. Activities such as Tai Chi, dancing, boxing programmes (specifically adapted for Parkinson's), and stationary cycling are highly beneficial.
    \item \textbf{Medication compliance:} Keep a detailed medication diary and take parkinsonian medications precisely on time. Avoid missing doses, as this can trigger severe motor fluctuations or ``off'' periods.
    \item \textbf{Dietary adjustments:} For patients with swallowing difficulties, modify food consistency (soft or pureed foods) and use thickening agents in liquids. Sit upright at a 90-degree angle during meals and remain upright for 30 minutes after eating.
    \item \textbf{Use assistive devices:} Utilise walking frames, weighted utensils, button hooks, and elastic shoelaces to maintain independence in daily activities.
\end{itemize}
