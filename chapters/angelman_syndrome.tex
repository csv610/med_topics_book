\chapter{Angelman Syndrome}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Angelman syndrome is a rare genetic condition that primarily affects the nervous system. It is characterised by severe developmental delay, intellectual disability, absent or severely limited speech, a characteristic happy and excitable demeanour, unsteady movement (ataxia), and, in many cases, epilepsy. The condition is named after the English paediatrician Harry Angelman, who first described three affected children in 1965.

Children with Angelman syndrome usually appear normal at birth, and the first signs typically emerge between around 6 and 12 months of age, when parents may notice delayed developmental milestones, a lack of babbling, and an unusually placid or happy temperament. The condition is present from conception, is not contagious, and does not worsen progressively over time. Although there is no cure, early diagnosis allows families and clinicians to put in place therapies and supports that substantially improve quality of life and functional outcomes.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Angelman syndrome is rare, affecting approximately 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 20,000 people worldwide, and it occurs equally in males and females and across all ethnic groups. Most cases arise as a spontaneous (de novo) genetic change and occur in families with no previous history of the condition. A small proportion of cases are inherited from a parent, and the risk of recurrence differs depending on the underlying genetic mechanism. Because milder cases can be difficult to recognise, and because the condition is under-diagnosed in some regions, the true prevalence is likely to be somewhat higher than reported figures suggest.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Angelman syndrome is caused by loss of function of the maternal copy of the \textit{UBE3A} gene on chromosome 15. This gene encodes an enzyme (a ubiquitin--protein ligase) that helps regulate the breakdown of proteins within neurons, a process essential for normal brain development and synaptic function.

In most body tissues both copies of \textit{UBE3A} are active, but in specific regions of the brain only the maternally inherited copy is expressed, a phenomenon known as genomic imprinting. Angelman syndrome therefore results when the maternal copy is absent or non-functional and the paternal copy is silenced in the brain, leaving the neuron with no working protein. The main genetic mechanisms are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Deletion:} loss of a segment of the maternal chromosome 15 (band 15q11.2-q13), accounting for roughly 70\% of cases and usually associated with the most severe phenotype
    \item \textbf{UBE3A mutation:} a pathogenic variant within the maternal \textit{UBE3A} gene itself
    \item \textbf{Paternal uniparental disomy:} the child inherits two copies of chromosome 15 from the father and none from the mother
    \item \textbf{Imprinting defect:} the maternal gene is present but incorrectly switched off because of an abnormality in the imprinting centre
\end{itemize}

In a small number of cases the genetic cause remains unidentified. Regardless of the mechanism, the final common pathway is reduced UBE3A protein activity in brain cells, which disrupts neuronal signalling and leads to the characteristic developmental and neurological features.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical picture is distinctive, although the severity varies between individuals, particularly according to the genetic cause. Typical features include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Developmental delay:} delayed sitting, crawling, and walking; a broad-based, stiff-legged gait with unsteadiness (ataxia)
    \item \textbf{Speech impairment:} little or no functional speech; most affected individuals understand more language than they can produce
    \item \textbf{Happy demeanour:} frequent smiling and laughter, an excitable personality, and easily triggered laughter
    \item \textbf{Seizures:} epilepsy of variable type begins in around 80--90\% of children, often before age three
    \item \textbf{Microcephaly:} a head circumference that falls behind as the child grows
    \item \textbf{Movement problems:} jerky, flapping limb movements and a fine tremor
    \item \textbf{Behavioural features:} hyperactivity, a short attention span, and difficulty sleeping
    \item \textbf{Fascination with water:} a strong attraction to water and shiny or crinkly objects
\end{itemize}

Feeding difficulties and constipation are common in early childhood, and some children have a degree of strabismus (squint) or other visual problems.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The combination of developmental delay, absent speech, ataxia, and a happy disposition is highly suggestive, but several other conditions can present similarly, particularly in early childhood:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rett syndrome:} another genetic neurodevelopmental disorder with loss of previously acquired skills, seen mostly in girls
    \item \textbf{Mowat--Wilson syndrome:} genetic disorder with distinctive facial features, microcephaly, and severe intellectual disability
    \item \textbf{Pitt--Hopkins syndrome:} a genetic condition with intellectual disability, breathing irregularity, and a happy affect
    \item \textbf{Prader--Willi syndrome:} the related chromosome 15 imprinting disorder, with hypotonia, obesity, and hyperphagia
    \item \textbf{Cerebral palsy:} especially ataxic or hypotonic forms with associated learning difficulties
    \item \textbf{Other causes of intellectual disability:} chromosomal anomalies, inborn errors of metabolism, or non-genetic causes such as birth asphyxia or fetal alcohol spectrum disorder
\end{itemize}

A careful developmental history, physical examination, and genetic testing are essential to distinguish these possibilities.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents should seek medical review if a young child is not reaching developmental milestones, is not babbling or speaking, walks unsteadily, or develops jerky movements or suspected seizures. Early referral to a paediatrician and a clinical geneticist is important for establishing a diagnosis and starting support.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A seizure lasting longer than 5 minutes, repeated seizures, or a seizure with a slow recovery
    \item Difficulty breathing, blue lips or face, or choking
    \item Loss of consciousness or unresponsiveness
    \item A head injury with vomiting, confusion, or unusual drowsiness, since falls are common because of poor balance
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is based on clinical features confirmed by genetic testing. Recommended steps include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical history and examination:} detailed developmental assessment of motor, speech, cognitive, and social skills, together with growth parameters and head circumference
    \item \textbf{DNA methylation analysis:} the first-line genetic test in most centres; it detects the four major mechanisms (deletion, uniparental disomy, imprinting defect, and mutation) in about 80--90\% of cases
    \item \textbf{Chromosomal microarray:} used to detect the size and extent of a chromosome 15 deletion
    \item \textbf{UBE3A sequencing:} to identify point mutations when methylation studies are normal
    \item \textbf{EEG (electroencephalogram):} a characteristic pattern of high-amplitude slow waves supports the diagnosis and helps guide antiepileptic treatment
    \item \textbf{Genetic counselling:} essential to explain the cause, the recurrence risk (which ranges from below 1\% to 50\% depending on the mechanism), and the options available to the family
\end{itemize}

Ongoing multidisciplinary review monitors development, seizures, feeding, vision, and behaviour.

\section*{Management}
There is no cure, and care aims to maximise development, control symptoms, and support the family through a coordinated team approach:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} to improve balance, walking, and motor coordination and to manage contractures
    \item \textbf{Speech and language therapy:} to build non-verbal communication through signing, picture cards, and augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) devices
    \item \textbf{Occupational therapy:} to develop self-care, fine motor, and daily living skills
    \item \textbf{Seizure management:} antiepileptic medicines such as valproate, clobazam, or levetiracetam, chosen in consultation with a paediatric neurologist
    \item \textbf{Behavioural and sleep support:} structured routines, positive behavioural strategies, and, where needed, short-term medication for severe insomnia
    \item \textbf{Feeding support:} management of reflux, constipation, and chewing difficulties; a feeding team can advise if weight gain is poor
    \item \textbf{Educational support:} individualised schooling with special education resources and regular review
    \item \textbf{Family and carer support:} respite care, counselling, and support groups to reduce carer burden
\end{itemize}

Clinical trials investigating gene-targeted and molecular therapies are underway, and families should discuss emerging options with their specialist team.

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Injury from falls caused by ataxia and poor coordination
    \item Prolonged seizures or status epilepticus
    \item Aspiration and recurrent chest infections in children with swallowing difficulties
    \item Gastro-oesophageal reflux and chronic constipation
    \item Scoliosis and, less often, joint contractures
    \item Chronic sleep disturbance, with associated daytime fatigue and behavioural difficulties
    \item Visual problems, including strabismus and hypermetropia (long-sightedness)
    \item Significant physical and emotional strain on families and carers
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people with Angelman syndrome live a normal or near-normal lifespan. With good support, the majority learn to walk, although often later than average and with a characteristic unsteady gait, and most communicate effectively using non-verbal methods. The happy, sociable temperament commonly persists into adult life, and many adults participate in structured daily activities, though they generally remain dependent on caregivers for daily living.

Outcomes vary with the underlying genetic cause: children with large deletions typically have the most severe motor, language, and seizure difficulties, whereas those with uniparental disomy or imprinting defects often have milder presentations. Seizures frequently become easier to control with age, and sleep often improves after childhood.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Angelman syndrome cannot be prevented in most cases because the majority arise spontaneously. For affected families the following measures are helpful:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend genetic counselling to clarify recurrence risk and reproductive options, including preimplantation or prenatal testing where appropriate
    \item Arrange early therapy services and appropriate educational placement as soon as possible after diagnosis
    \item Use soft flooring, padding, and close supervision to reduce fall-related injuries
    \item Maintain a written seizure management plan agreed with the treating team, including guidance on emergency medication
    \item Establish a consistent, calming bedtime routine and manage screen time to improve sleep
    \item Use communication aids consistently across home and school so the child can express wants and needs
    \item Seek carer support, respite care, and peer networks to prevent burnout and maintain family wellbeing
\end{itemize}
