\chapter{Hemophilia}
\index{Hemophilia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hemophilia is a rare inherited bleeding disorder in which the blood does not clot normally because of a deficiency of a specific clotting factor. Hemophilia A is caused by a lack of factor VIII, and hemophilia B by a lack of factor IX. The condition is caused by changes in genes on the X chromosome, so it affects almost exclusively males, while females are usually carriers. People with hemophilia bleed for longer after injury and may bleed spontaneously into joints and muscles. With modern treatment, including replacement of the missing clotting factor, most people with hemophilia live full, active lives.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hemophilia affects approximately one in 5,000--10,000 male births for hemophilia A and one in 30,000--50,000 for hemophilia B. Around 3,000 people in many countries live with hemophilia, and the condition occurs in all ethnic groups. Although there is no cure, treatment has transformed the condition: children born with hemophilia in many countries today can expect a near-normal life expectancy and quality of life, a dramatic change from past decades when bleeding into joints caused severe disability and life-threatening bleeds were common.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} hemophilia is inherited in an X-linked recessive pattern; the gene for factor VIII or IX is carried on the X chromosome
    \item \textbf{Inheritance:} affected males pass the gene to all daughters (who become carriers) and to no sons; carrier females have a 50\% chance of passing it to each child
    \item \textbf{New mutations:} around one-third of cases occur with no family history
    \item \textbf{Clotting cascade:} factors VIII and IX are essential links in the chain of reactions that form a stable blood clot
    \item \textbf{Severity:} classified by factor level as mild (5--40\% of normal), moderate (1--5\%), or severe (less than 1\%)
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} without adequate factor, bleeding continues and can occur spontaneously in severe disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Severe hemophilia:} spontaneous bleeding into joints, muscles, and soft tissues, beginning in early childhood
    \item \textbf{Moderate hemophilia:} bleeding after injury, with occasional spontaneous bleeds
    \item \textbf{Mild hemophilia:} bleeding mainly after surgery, dental work, or significant injury; often diagnosed late
    \item Joint bleeds cause pain, swelling, and stiffness, usually in knees, elbows, and ankles
    \item Prolonged bleeding after cuts, dental procedures, and immunisations
    \item Easy bruising and bleeding into muscles
    \item Bleeding after circumcision is often the first sign in babies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item von Willebrand disease, the most common inherited bleeding disorder, affects both sexes
    \item Other factor deficiencies, such as factors XI, VII, and fibrinogen
    \item Platelet disorders, which cause bruising and mucosal bleeding
    \item Acquired inhibitors and acquired bleeding disorders
    \item Child abuse may be suspected in children with unusual bruising, and should be excluded carefully
    \item Coagulation testing distinguishes hemophilia from these conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Families with a history of hemophilia should seek genetic counselling, and boys with bleeding symptoms need evaluation. Anyone with hemophilia should be managed by a specialist haemophilia treatment centre and seek prompt care for any bleed.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A blow to the head, however minor, with possible internal bleeding
    \item Bleeding into the neck, mouth, or throat
    \item Severe pain and swelling in a joint or muscle
    \item Bleeding that does not stop despite factor treatment
    \item Blood in the urine or stool, or vomiting blood
    \item Any injury in a person with severe hemophilia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coagulation tests:} a prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) with a normal prothrombin time raises suspicion
    \item \textbf{Factor assays:} specific measurement of factor VIII and IX levels confirms the diagnosis and severity
    \item \textbf{Family history:} an X-linked pattern of bleeding in male relatives supports the diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} identifies the gene change, enables carrier testing, and guides family planning
    \item \textbf{Prenatal testing:} available for families with known gene changes
    \item \textbf{Newborn screening:} testing after birth where there is a family history
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Factor replacement:} intravenous infusions of the missing factor, either on demand for bleeds or as regular prophylaxis to prevent them
    \item \textbf{Prophylaxis:} regular factor infusions, usually begun in early childhood, prevent joint damage and are the standard of care in severe disease
    \item \textbf{Extended half-life products:} newer factor concentrates allow less frequent infusions
    \item \textbf{Non-factor therapies:} newer agents that rebalance clotting reduce bleed rates with less frequent dosing
    \item \textbf{Home treatment:} most families learn to administer factor at home
    \item \textbf{Desmopressin:} boosts factor VIII in mild hemophilia A for some procedures
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive care:} treatment centres coordinate haematology, physiotherapy, dentistry, surgery, and psychological support
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Joint damage (haemophilic arthropathy) from repeated bleeds, causing chronic pain and disability
    \item Inhibitor development: antibodies that attack infused factor, making treatment difficult
    \item Life-threatening bleeding, including intracranial and gastrointestinal haemorrhage
    \item Bleeding after surgery and dental work
    \item Blood-borne infections from older plasma-derived products, now extremely rare with modern concentrates
    \item Psychosocial impact on the person and family
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With modern prophylaxis and comprehensive care, the outlook for hemophilia is excellent. Children who start regular factor replacement early have minimal joint damage and can participate in most activities safely. Life expectancy for people with hemophilia in many countries now approaches that of the general population. The main remaining challenges are inhibitors, which complicate treatment, and the need for lifelong adherence to therapy. Gene therapy, which provides a long-term source of the missing factor, is now approved in some countries and may transform treatment further.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend a specialist haemophilia treatment centre and follow its care plan
    \item Take prescribed prophylaxis regularly to prevent bleeds
    \item Treat bleeds early, even if mild, and contact the treatment centre
    \item Avoid high-risk contact sports, and use protective gear and physiotherapy to keep joints healthy
    \item Maintain good dental hygiene to reduce the need for dental procedures
    \item Carry a card or medical alert identifying the condition
    \item Immunise against hepatitis A and B
    \item Seek genetic counselling before having children
\end{itemize}
