\chapter{Cerebral Palsy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cerebral palsy is a group of permanent disorders of movement and posture caused by damage to the developing brain, usually before or around the time of birth. The word "cerebral" refers to the brain and "palsy" to weakness or problems with movement.

The condition varies widely: some children have mild clumsiness or a slight limp, while others have severe physical disability and may need help with all daily activities. Cerebral palsy is non-progressive, meaning the underlying brain damage does not worsen over time, although movement problems can change as a child grows.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cerebral palsy is the most common cause of physical disability in childhood, affecting roughly 1 in 500 to 1 in 400 children. It occurs in all parts of the world and in all social and ethnic groups.

Rates are higher in babies born prematurely and in those with low birth weight. Improved care of very premature babies has meant that more of them survive, and cerebral palsy has become relatively more common among this group, although overall rates have been fairly stable in recent decades.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Cerebral palsy results from injury to or abnormal development of the brain while it is still forming:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Premature birth:} the immature brain is vulnerable to bleeding and damage to the white matter that carries nerve signals
    \item \textbf{Birth-related events:} oxygen deprivation (asphyxia) around the time of labour, now uncommon with modern care
    \item \textbf{Infections in pregnancy:} such as rubella, cytomegalovirus, or toxoplasmosis
    \item \textbf{Genetic and developmental abnormalities:} malformations of the brain that form before birth
    \item \textbf{Infections in early infancy:} severe meningitis or encephalitis
    \item \textbf{Trauma or lack of oxygen in early life:} such as a severe head injury or near-drowning
    \item \textbf{Maternal health:} thyroid disease, severe pre-eclampsia, or multiple pregnancy
\end{itemize}

Damage to the parts of the brain that control movement results in abnormal muscle tone, poor coordination, or involuntary movements. The type of movement disorder depends on which brain regions are affected.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Cerebral palsy is usually noticed in the first months to years of life as delays in motor milestones:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Delays in sitting, crawling, standing, or walking
    \item Stiff, tight muscles (spasticity) with exaggerated reflexes
    \item Unusual floppiness (low muscle tone) in early infancy
    \item Involuntary, writhing movements (dystonia or athetosis)
    \item Poor balance and coordination (ataxia)
    \item Difficulty with feeding, swallowing, or excessive drooling
    \item Speech difficulties and, in some children, intellectual disability
    \item Associated problems: epilepsy, vision or hearing impairment, and learning difficulties
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions can cause similar movement problems in early childhood:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Inherited metabolic and genetic disorders, such as Rett syndrome
    \item Progressive neuromuscular conditions, such as muscular dystrophy or spinal muscular atrophy
    \item Genetic movement disorders mistaken for cerebral palsy
    \item Developmental delay from other causes, including intellectual disability alone
    \item Brain tumours or other progressive neurological disease
    \item Spinal cord disorders causing weakness or spasticity
    \item Complications of birth such as brachial plexus injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if a child is missing expected developmental milestones, has unusual muscle stiffness or floppiness, is not sitting by around 9 months, or is not walking by around 18 months.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A seizure lasting more than five minutes or repeated seizures
    \item Difficulty breathing or choking while feeding
    \item Loss of consciousness or a sudden collapse
    \item Sudden weakness or change in a child's usual state
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Developmental assessment:} review of milestones, movement, tone, and reflexes
    \item \textbf{Detailed history:} pregnancy, birth, and early health events
    \item \textbf{Brain imaging:} MRI is the most useful test and may show the pattern of injury
    \item \textbf{Hearing and vision testing:} to identify associated sensory problems
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} when a genetic cause is suspected
    \item \textbf{Multidisciplinary assessment:} paediatrician, physiotherapist, occupational therapist, and speech pathologist
    \item \textbf{Important:} diagnosis is clinical; tests support but do not replace careful observation over time
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Cerebral palsy cannot be cured, but treatment can improve comfort, function, and quality of life:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} stretches, strengthening, and positioning to maintain movement and prevent contractures
    \item \textbf{Occupational therapy:} help with daily tasks such as dressing, feeding, and writing
    \item \textbf{Speech and language therapy:} for communication and swallowing difficulties
    \item \textbf{Medication:} medicines to reduce spasticity, such as baclofen, and botulinum toxin injections into tight muscles
    \item \textbf{Orthoses and equipment:} splints, walking frames, wheelchairs, and communication aids
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} tendon lengthening or orthopaedic surgery to correct deformities, and selective procedures for severe spasticity
    \item \textbf{Feeding support:} dietary adjustment, and occasionally a feeding tube when swallowing is unsafe
    \item \textbf{Pain management and care of associated problems:} treatment of epilepsy, reflux, and hip problems
    \item \textbf{Family support:} respite, counselling, and connecting with support organisations
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Contractures: permanent tightening of muscles and joints that limit movement
    \item Hip dislocation and scoliosis (curvature of the spine)
    \item Feeding and swallowing problems leading to poor nutrition or aspiration pneumonia
    \item Recurrent chest infections and constipation
    \item Epilepsy, which affects a significant minority
    \item Chronic pain from muscle spasm, joint strain, or hip problems
    \item Dental problems and gastro-oesophageal reflux
    \item Pressure sores in people with reduced mobility
    \item Social, emotional, and educational challenges for the child and family
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Cerebral palsy is a lifelong condition, and the extent of disability is generally stable from childhood onward, though movement can change with growth and ageing. Many children with mild cerebral palsy walk independently and live full, productive lives. Outcomes depend on the severity of motor impairment and on associated problems such as intellectual disability or epilepsy. With good therapy, equipment, and support, most people maintain their abilities into adulthood, and the focus shifts over time to independent living, education, employment, and managing the effects of ageing.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Cerebral palsy cannot always be prevented, but some risks can be reduced:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Good antenatal care, including vaccination against rubella before pregnancy
    \item Avoidance of alcohol, smoking, and harmful substances in pregnancy
    \item Prompt treatment of infections and blood pressure problems in pregnancy
    \item Specialised neonatal care for premature and unwell babies
    \item For children with cerebral palsy: early therapy, regular stretching, and use of prescribed equipment
    \item Immunise children on schedule to prevent meningitis and encephalitis
    \item Support families with respite care, counselling, and community resources
\end{itemize}
