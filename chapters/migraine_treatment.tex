\chapter{Migraine Treatment}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Migraine treatment comprises abortive (acute) therapies to resolve individual migraine attacks and preventative (prophylactic) strategies to reduce the frequency, duration, and severity of future episodes. Migraine is a complex neurological disorder characterized by recurrent, moderate-to-severe throbbing headaches, often accompanied by sensory disturbances (aura), nausea, vomiting, photophobia, and phonophobia. A successful treatment paradigm requires an individualised approach that combines pharmacological interventions with lifestyle modifications, trigger identification, and non-pharmacological therapies. Proper management is essential to prevent the progression of episodic migraine into a chronic daily headache pattern and to reduce the overall burden of disability.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Migraine is one of the most prevalent neurological conditions globally, affecting approximately 15\% of the population, including over 4.9 million Australians. The demand for effective treatment is high, particularly among women, who are affected three times more frequently than men, primarily due to hormonal influences. The economic impact is substantial, driven by lost productivity, absenteeism, and direct healthcare costs. Approximately 1\% to 2\% of the population suffers from chronic migraine (defined as headaches on 15 or more days per month for at least 3 months, with at least 8 days meeting migraine criteria). Medication-overuse headache (MOH), resulting from the frequent or daily use of acute pain medications, complicates the management of up to 50\% of chronic migraine patients.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The choice of migraine treatments is guided by the underlying neurovascular mechanisms of the disease.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Trigeminovascular system activation:} The activation of trigeminal sensory afferents leads to the release of vasoactive neuropeptides, including calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and substance P, causing neurogenic inflammation and vasodilation.
    \item \textbf{Cortical spreading depression (CSD):} A wave of neuronal and glial depolarisation that propagates across the cerebral cortex, responsible for the migraine aura and subsequent activation of trigeminal nociceptors.
    \item \textbf{Central and peripheral sensitisation:} Repeated stimulation of trigeminal pathway neurons leads to hypersensitivity, manifesting as throbbing pain and allodynia (pain from normally non-painful stimuli).
    \item \textbf{Serotonergic pathway dysfunction:} Fluctuations in serotonin (5-HT) levels contribute to trigeminovascular activation; triptans target 5-HT1B/1D receptors to counteract this.
    \item \textbf{Hormonal triggers:} Fluctuations in oestrogen levels (e.g. during menstruation) serve as major triggers, requiring targeted hormonal management in some patients.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Migraine treatment selection varies depending on the specific clinical features of the patient's attacks.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Throbbing or pulsating headache, typically unilateral but can be bilateral, lasting 4 to 72 hours.
    \item Association with nausea, vomiting, and extreme sensitivity to light (photophobia) and sound (phonophobia).
    \item Transient focal neurological symptoms (aura), such as visual disturbances (scintillating scotomas), sensory numbness, or tingling, preceding the headache.
    \item Significant functional impairment, forcing the patient to seek rest in a dark, quiet room.
    \item Frequency of attacks ranging from occasional episodic events to daily chronic headaches.
    \item Allodynia, where touch, brushing hair, or wearing glasses is perceived as painful.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before initiating treatment, other causes of headache must be ruled out, particularly those requiring urgent intervention.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tension-type headache:} Presents with bilateral, band-like, non-throbbing pain without nausea or photophobia; managed with simple analgesics.
    \item \textbf{Cluster headache:} Severe, strictly unilateral orbital or temporal pain, lasting 15 to 180 minutes, associated with ipsilateral cranial autonomic symptoms; treated with high-flow oxygen and triptans.
    \item \textbf{Giant cell arteritis (GCA):} Inflammatory vasculitis in older adults presenting with headache, jaw claudication, and visual loss; requires immediate high-dose corticosteroids.
    \item \textbf{Subarachnoid haemorrhage:} Presents as a sudden, catastrophic "thunderclap" headache; represents a neurosurgical emergency.
    \item \textbf{Medication-overuse headache (MOH):} A secondary headache caused by the regular overuse of acute headache medications (e.g. triptans or simple analgesics for 10--15 or more days per month).
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients experiencing new, worsening, or atypical headaches should consult a general practitioner or neurologist to establish a formal diagnosis and treatment plan.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A sudden, extremely severe headache reaching peak intensity within seconds (thunderclap headache)
    \item Headache accompanied by fever, neck stiffness, confusion, or altered consciousness
    \item New headache with focal neurological signs, such as limb weakness, numbness, double vision, or difficulty speaking
    \item Headache following a recent head trauma
    \item New-onset headache in a patient over 50 years of age or with a history of cancer
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of migraine is primarily clinical, based on a detailed history and physical examination.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical history and diagnostic criteria:} Guided by the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3) criteria, documenting headache diaries to track frequency, triggers, and medication use.
    \item \textbf{Physical and neurological examination:} Essential to rule out secondary causes and confirm normal neurological function between attacks.
    \item \textbf{Neuroimaging (MRI or CT of the brain):} Indicated only if there are atypical features, "red flags", or focal neurological deficits on examination.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} Full blood count and inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP) to rule out giant cell arteritis or systemic infections in older patients.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management involves a combination of acute treatments, preventative medications, and non-pharmacological interventions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute (abortive) therapy:} Simple analgesics (aspirin, ibuprofen, or paracetamol) for mild-to-moderate attacks. Triptans (e.g. sumatriptan, zolmitriptan) are first-line for moderate-to-severe attacks, acting as selective 5-HT1B/1D receptor agonists. Antiemetics (e.g. metoclopramide, prochlorperazine) are used to treat nausea and enhance analgesic absorption.
    \item \textbf{Preventative (prophylactic) therapy:} Recommended for patients with 3 or more disabling attacks per month. Options include beta-blockers (e.g. propranolol), anticonvulsants (e.g. topiramate), tricyclic antidepressants (e.g. amitriptyline), and newer monoclonal antibodies targeting the CGRP pathway (e.g. erenumab, galcanezumab).
    \item \textbf{Hormonal management:} In menstrual migraine, perimenstrual oestrogen supplementation or continuous oral contraceptives may be used.
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle and trigger avoidance:} Identifying and avoiding triggers (such as stress, irregular sleep patterns, skipped meals, dehydration, and specific foods like aged cheeses or artificial sweeteners).
    \item \textbf{Non-pharmacological therapies:} Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) for stress management, biofeedback, relaxation techniques, and neuromodulation devices (e.g. Cefaly).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Medication-overuse headache (rebound headache) from overusing acute medications.
    \item Status migrainosus: A debilitating migraine attack lasting more than 72 hours, often requiring hospitalisation for intravenous hydration and therapy.
    \item Migrainous infarction: A rare complication where an aura symptom is accompanied by ischaemic stroke, confirmed by neuroimaging.
    \item Persistent aura without infarction: Aura symptoms that last for a week or more without evidence of stroke.
    \item Secondary psychological distress, including anxiety, depression, and social isolation due to chronic pain.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With an optimal, structured treatment plan, the vast majority of migraine patients achieve significant improvement in their symptoms and quality of life. Acute therapies are highly effective at aborting attacks when taken early in the headache phase. Preventative therapies typically require 2 to 3 months to show benefit, with the goal of reducing attack frequency by at least 50\%. Chronic migraine and medication-overuse headache require dedicated detoxification and multidisciplinary care, but carry a favourable prognosis for recovery once the offending acute medications are discontinued.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a consistent daily routine, including regular sleep schedules, meals, and hydration.
    \item Keep a detailed headache diary to monitor symptoms, triggers, and medication effectiveness.
    \item Limit the use of acute pain relief or triptans to no more than 2 to 3 days per week to avoid medication-overuse headaches.
    \item Engage in regular, moderate aerobic exercise, such as walking, swimming, or cycling.
    \item Practice stress-reduction techniques, including mindfulness, deep breathing exercises, or progressive muscle relaxation.
\end{itemize}
