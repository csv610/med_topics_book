\chapter{Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis (CVST)}
\index{Cerebral Venous Sinus Thrombosis (CVST)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST) is a rare type of stroke caused by a blood clot in the veins or venous sinuses that drain blood from the brain. When these channels are blocked, blood backs up, raising pressure inside the skull and sometimes causing bleeding into the brain. CVST can occur at any age but is most common in young adults, particularly women, often in relation to pregnancy, the contraceptive pill, or hormone treatment. It is a serious condition that requires urgent treatment, but with prompt anticoagulant therapy most people recover well.

\section*{Epidemiology}
CVST is uncommon, affecting about three to five people per million per year, and accounts for roughly 0.5--1\% of all strokes. It is more common in women, with a peak around age 30, largely because of the oral contraceptive pill, pregnancy, and the postpartum period. Other risk factors include infection, cancer, blood clotting disorders, dehydration, and head injury. While rare, CVST is important to recognise because it often affects young, otherwise healthy people, and early treatment markedly improves outcomes.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
CVST results from a combination of risk factors that promote blood clotting in the cerebral veins.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood clots:} a thrombus forms in a cerebral vein or venous sinus
    \item \textbf{Oestrogen:} the combined oral contraceptive pill, pregnancy, and hormone replacement therapy increase clotting risk
    \item \textbf{Inherited clotting disorders:} factor V Leiden, prothrombin gene mutation, and protein C or S deficiency
    \item \textbf{Infection:} sinusitis, mastoiditis, and middle ear infection can spread to the adjacent venous sinuses
    \item \textbf{Cancer and chemotherapy:} increase clotting tendency
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory disease:} lupus, vasculitis, and Beh\u00e7et's disease
    \item \textbf{Other triggers:} dehydration, trauma, lumbar puncture, and some medicines
    \item \textbf{Effect:} blocked drainage raises pressure and can cause venous bleeding or swelling of the brain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms often develop gradually over days to weeks.

\begin{itemize}
    \item The most common symptom is a severe, persistent headache
    \item Blurred vision, double vision, or visual loss from raised pressure
    \item Seizures
    \item Weakness or numbness on one side of the body
    \item Difficulty speaking
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or altered consciousness
    \item Nausea and vomiting
    \item Fever, ear pain, or sinus symptoms when infection is the cause
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms overlap with many other neurological conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Migraine and tension-type headache
    \item Subarachnoid haemorrhage and other strokes
    \item Meningitis and encephalitis
    \item Brain tumour
    \item Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (pseudotumour cerebri), which has a similar headache and visual loss
    \item Sinusitis and other infections
    \item Pregnancy-related conditions, such as eclampsia and posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
A sudden, severe, or persistent new headache, especially with neurological symptoms or risk factors for clotting, should be assessed urgently in an emergency department.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A sudden severe headache, described as the worst ever, or a headache with vomiting or neck stiffness
    \item Seizures
    \item Sudden weakness, numbness, or difficulty speaking
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or loss of consciousness
    \item Visual loss or double vision
    \item Headache in pregnancy or the weeks after birth
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
CVST is diagnosed with brain imaging and confirmed by venography.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} headache, neurological signs, and risk factors including the pill and pregnancy
    \item \textbf{CT venogram or MR venogram:} the key tests, imaging the cerebral veins and sinuses
    \item \textbf{CT or MRI of the brain:} to look for bleeding, swelling, or infarction
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, clotting studies, and D-dimer
    \item \textbf{Thrombophilia testing:} inherited and acquired clotting disorder screens
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture:} in selected cases, to measure pressure and exclude infection
    \item \textbf{Other investigations:} as directed by the likely cause
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment aims to dissolve or stop the clot and manage complications.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anticoagulation:} heparin, then warfarin or a DOAC, is the mainstay of treatment
    \item \textbf{Anticoagulant duration:} usually three to six months, or longer if a clotting disorder is found
    \item \textbf{Seizure treatment:} anticonvulsant medicines for seizures
    \item \textbf{Pressure management:} treatment of raised intracranial pressure, including acetazolamide or procedures in severe cases
    \item \textbf{Treatment of the cause:} stopping the combined pill, treating infection, and managing pregnancy-related cases
    \item \textbf{Thrombectomy:} removal of the clot in severe, deteriorating cases
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} fluids, pain relief, and monitoring in a stroke or neurology unit
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy, speech therapy, and occupational therapy as needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Raised intracranial pressure causing visual loss
    \item Bleeding into the brain (haemorrhagic transformation)
    \item Seizures, including status epilepticus
    \item Brain swelling and herniation in severe cases
    \item Hydrocephalus
    \item Death in a small proportion of cases
    \item Recurrence of the clot, especially if a clotting disorder persists
    \item Long-term neurological disability, including visual field loss and cognitive symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for CVST has improved dramatically with modern treatment. Around 80--90\% of people recover fully or with only minor disability, and death is now uncommon. Recovery can take weeks to months, and some people have persistent headache, visual symptoms, or cognitive difficulties. The risk of recurrence is low once the initial treatment is complete, unless an ongoing clotting disorder or risk factor remains. Seizures and bleeding complications are the main determinants of a less favourable outcome.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discuss the risks of the combined contraceptive pill with a doctor, especially if you smoke, are over 35, or have a family history of blood clots
    \item Seek prompt assessment of severe or persistent headache, particularly in pregnancy
    \item Keep well hydrated and mobile during long travel or illness
    \item Treat sinus, ear, and dental infections promptly
    \item Take anticoagulants exactly as prescribed and attend blood tests
    \item Know the signs of recurrent clotting and seek care early
    \item Attend follow-up and rehabilitation after the illness
\end{itemize}
