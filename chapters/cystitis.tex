\chapter{Cystitis}
\index{Cystitis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cystitis is inflammation of the bladder, most commonly caused by a bacterial urinary tract infection (UTI). It causes a frequent and urgent need to urinate, pain or burning when passing urine, and lower abdominal discomfort. Cystitis is very common, especially in women, and most cases are uncomplicated and easily treated with antibiotics. Simple cystitis can often be managed with self-care and pharmacy treatment, but recurrent, severe, or complicated infections need medical assessment. Less commonly, cystitis is caused by non-infectious factors such as irritants, radiation, or interstitial cystitis (bladder pain syndrome).

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cystitis is one of the most common infections seen in general practice. Around half of all women experience at least one urinary tract infection in their lifetime, and many have recurrent episodes. Risk increases with sexual activity, the use of spermicides, menopause, and anatomical factors. In men, cystitis is less common and more often indicates an underlying problem such as an enlarged prostate. Catheter use, diabetes, pregnancy, and immune suppression increase risk. Most infections are caused by bacteria from the bowel, particularly \textit{Escherichia coli}.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Most cystitis is caused by bacteria ascending the urethra into the bladder.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\textit{Escherichia coli}:} causes the great majority of uncomplicated infections
    \item \textbf{Other bacteria:} \textit{Staphylococcus saprophyticus}, \textit{Klebsiella}, \textit{Proteus}, and \textit{Enterococcus}
    \item \textbf{Ascending infection:} bacteria from the bowel colonise the urethra and bladder
    \item \textbf{Short urethra in women:} explains the higher rate in females
    \item \textbf{Sexual activity:} can push bacteria into the urethra
    \item \textbf{Incomplete emptying:} urine stasis allows bacteria to multiply
    \item \textbf{Non-infectious causes:} interstitial cystitis, irritants such as bubble bath, radiation, and chemotherapy drugs
    \item \textbf{Complicated infection:} infection with structural abnormalities, catheters, or resistant organisms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms typically develop quickly.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A burning or stinging pain when passing urine (dysuria)
    \item Passing urine more often than usual (frequency)
    \item A sudden, strong urge to urinate (urgency)
    \item Lower abdominal pain or discomfort
    \item Urine that looks cloudy, dark, or strong-smelling
    \item Blood in the urine in some cases
    \item In older adults or people with catheters: confusion, fever, or general deterioration
    \item No fever in uncomplicated cystitis; fever suggests kidney infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Similar symptoms can be caused by other conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Urethritis from sexually transmitted infections such as chlamydia or gonorrhoea
    \item Vaginitis or vulvitis causing burning on urination
    \item Kidney infection (pyelonephritis), with fever and flank pain
    \item Bladder stones or tumours
    \item Interstitial cystitis (bladder pain syndrome), causing chronic pain without infection
    \item Prostatitis in men
    \item Urethral irritation from soaps, bubble bath, or sex
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Simple cystitis in a healthy woman can sometimes be treated with pharmacy products and fluids, but see a doctor if symptoms are severe, persistent, or recurrent, or if there is fever, blood in the urine, or pregnancy.

\textbf{Seek medical care promptly if:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item You are pregnant
    \item There is fever, chills, or pain in the back or side (possible kidney infection)
    \item You are a man, a child, or an older adult
    \item You have diabetes, a catheter, or a weakened immune system
    \item There is blood in the urine
    \item Symptoms do not improve within a couple of days of treatment
    \item You have frequent recurrences
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:} high fever with vomiting or confusion, severe abdominal or flank pain, or the inability to pass urine at all.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is often clinical, with tests for complicated or recurrent cases.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} symptoms, risk factors, and past infections
    \item \textbf{Urine dipstick test:} detects nitrites and leucocytes, giving a rapid indication of infection
    \item \textbf{Urine culture:} identifies the bacteria and its antibiotic sensitivities, used for complicated or recurrent cases
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test:} in women of childbearing age
    \item \textbf{Imaging or cystoscopy:} for recurrent, atypical, or complicated cases
    \item \textbf{STI testing:} where urethritis is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on severity and risk.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} a short course, such as trimethoprim or nitrofurantoin, for bacterial cystitis
    \item \textbf{Delayed antibiotics:} some uncomplicated cases can be observed for a day or two with self-care
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} paracetamol or ibuprofen
    \item \textbf{Bladder anaesthetics:} products such as phenazopyridine can soothe burning
    \item \textbf{Fluids:} drink plenty of water
    \item \textbf{Treatment of complications:} intravenous antibiotics and hospital care for kidney infection
    \item \textbf{Recurrent cystitis:} preventive antibiotics, post-coital antibiotics, or vaginal oestrogen after menopause
    \item \textbf{Non-infectious cystitis:} specialist management of interstitial cystitis and avoidance of irritants
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Kidney infection (pyelonephritis), if bacteria ascend from the bladder
    \item Sepsis in severe or untreated infection
    \item Recurrent cystitis, affecting quality of life
    \item Antibiotic resistance
    \item Complications in pregnancy, including kidney infection and preterm birth
    \item Chronic bladder pain in interstitial cystitis
    \item Kidney damage in people with reflux or obstruction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most episodes of cystitis settle within a few days of antibiotic treatment, and symptoms often improve within 24--48 hours. Recurrence is common, affecting about a quarter of women within six months, and can be reduced with preventive strategies. Serious complications are rare in otherwise healthy people. Chronic bladder pain syndrome is a different condition that persists despite negative tests and needs specialised management.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Drink plenty of water throughout the day
    \item Urinate when you feel the need and empty the bladder fully
    \item Urinate after sex
    \item Wipe front to back after the toilet
    \item Avoid harsh soaps, bubble bath, and douching in the genital area
    \item Wear cotton underwear and avoid tight clothing
    \item Consider cranberry products or vaginal oestrogen after menopause, as advised
    \item See a doctor for frequent recurrences rather than repeated self-treatment
\end{itemize}
