\chapter{Head Injury}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A head injury is any damage to the scalp, skull, or brain from a blow or impact to the head. Head injuries range from minor bumps and bruises that need no treatment to severe brain injuries that are life-threatening. Concussion, a common form of mild traumatic brain injury, causes temporary symptoms such as headache, dizziness, and confusion. The main danger after a head injury is bleeding or swelling inside the skull, which can compress the brain and cause serious harm if not treated urgently. Most head injuries are minor and resolve with rest, but it is important to know the warning signs that require emergency care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Head injuries are among the most common reasons for emergency department visits. Around 1 in 5 Australians sustains a head injury at some point in their lives. Falls cause the most head injuries, particularly in young children and older adults; sport, road crashes, and assaults are also common causes. Around 80--90% of head injuries are minor, but severe traumatic brain injury remains a leading cause of death and disability in young people. The risk of complications after a minor head injury is low but increases with age, blood-thinning medicines, and loss of consciousness.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Head injury causes damage through direct and indirect mechanisms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blunt trauma:} a blow to the head bruises the scalp, may fracture the skull, and can injure the brain
    \item \textbf{Concussion:} acceleration and deceleration of the brain within the skull stretches nerve fibres, causing temporary dysfunction
    \item \textbf{Contusion:} bruising of brain tissue at the site of impact or opposite it
    \item \textbf{Epidural haematoma:} bleeding between the skull and the dura, often after a skull fracture, which can compress the brain rapidly
    \item \textbf{Subdural haematoma:} bleeding between the dura and the brain, more common in older people and on blood thinners
    \item \textbf{Intracerebral haemorrhage:} bleeding within the brain tissue
    \item \textbf{Raised intracranial pressure:} bleeding and swelling increase pressure inside the skull, reducing blood flow to the brain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features of a head injury depend on its severity.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A bump, swelling, bruise, or cut on the head
    \item Headache
    \item Dizziness or feeling dazed
    \item Nausea or vomiting
    \item Confusion, poor memory, or difficulty concentrating
    \item Brief loss of consciousness in concussion
    \item Blurred or double vision
    \item Sensitivity to light and noise
    \item Irritability, mood changes, and sleep disturbance
    \item Serious signs: worsening headache, repeated vomiting, drowsiness, seizures, weakness, unequal pupils, or a "lucid interval" followed by deterioration
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Conditions that mimic or accompany head injury should be considered.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cervical spine injury, which frequently accompanies head injury
    \item Fainting, seizures, or low blood sugar that caused the fall
    \item Alcohol or drug intoxication
    \item Stroke and other neurological events
    \item Migraine and other headaches
    \item Middle ear or balance problems
    \item Intracranial bleeding, which must be considered in at-risk people
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for any head injury in a baby, an older person, or a person on blood thinners, and for any injury with loss of consciousness, confusion, vomiting, or persistent symptoms.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Loss of consciousness, even briefly
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or difficulty waking
    \item Repeated or projectile vomiting
    \item A seizure or fit
    \item Weakness or numbness of an arm or leg
    \item Slurred speech
    \item Unequal pupils or vision changes
    \item Blood or clear fluid from the nose or ears
    \item In an infant: a bulging soft spot, high-pitched crying, poor feeding, or drowsiness
    \item Worsening symptoms after an initial improvement
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment focuses on severity and the need for imaging.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} how the injury happened, symptoms, and risk factors
    \item \textbf{Neurological examination:} level of consciousness (Glasgow Coma Scale), pupils, movement, and coordination
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} used when there are risk factors for bleeding, such as loss of consciousness, vomiting, older age, blood thinners, or neurological signs
    \item \textbf{Observation:} low-risk injuries are managed at home with instructions
    \item \textbf{Concussion testing:} standardised assessments of memory, balance, and reaction
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review for persistent symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management ranges from home care to emergency surgery.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Minor injuries:} ice, rest, simple pain relief, and observation
    \item \textbf{Concussion:} rest, avoiding screens and school or work initially, then gradual return to activity and sport
    \item \textbf{Hospital observation:} for moderate injuries and at-risk patients
    \item \textbf{Urgent surgery:} drainage of epidural and subdural haematomas
    \item \textbf{Intensive care:} for severe brain injury, with airway, breathing, and pressure management
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy, speech therapy, and cognitive support after severe injury
    \item \textbf{Return-to-play:} medical clearance before returning to contact sport
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} monitoring of ongoing symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Post-concussion syndrome with persistent headache, dizziness, and poor concentration
    \item Epidural or subdural haematoma requiring emergency surgery
    \item Brain swelling and raised intracranial pressure
    \item Seizures
    \item Long-term cognitive, behavioural, and physical disability after severe injury
    \item Second-impact syndrome if returning to sport too soon
    \item Psychological effects, including anxiety and depression
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most head injuries are minor, and the vast majority of people recover fully within days to weeks. Concussion symptoms usually settle within one to four weeks with appropriate rest. The outlook for severe head injury depends on its extent and the speed of treatment; with modern care, many people make good recoveries, though some have lasting effects. The most important measures are prompt assessment of warning signs, preventing a second injury, and a structured return to normal activity.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wear helmets for cycling, skating, skiing, and contact sport
    \item Wear seatbelts and drive safely
    \item Prevent falls with home safety measures, especially for children and older people
    \item Seek medical assessment after any concerning head injury
    \item Follow the observation instructions you are given
    \item Rest after a concussion and return to activity gradually
    \item Do not return to sport until cleared
    \item Be alert for warning signs in the first 24--48 hours
    \item Older people on blood thinners should be assessed after any head injury
\end{itemize}
