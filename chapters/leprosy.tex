\chapter{Leprosy}
\index{Leprosy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Leprosy, also known as Hansen's disease, is a chronic infectious disease caused by the acid-fast bacillus \textit{Mycobacterium leprae} and the closely related \textit{Mycobacterium lepromatosis}. The disease primarily affects the skin, peripheral nerves, mucosal surfaces of the upper respiratory tract, and the eyes. Left untreated, it can lead to progressive and permanent nerve damage, sensory loss, muscle weakness, and characteristic physical deformities. Despite its historical stigma, leprosy is curable with multidrug therapy (MDT), and early diagnosis is key to preventing long-term disability and secondary complications.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Globally, leprosy remains a public health concern, with over 200,000 new cases reported annually, predominantly in tropical and subtropical regions of Southeast Asia, South America, and Africa. India, Brazil, and Indonesia account for the majority of the global burden. In many countries, leprosy is rare; almost all cases are diagnosed in immigrants or refugees from endemic countries, although sporadic cases are occasionally reported in indigenous communities in northern some countries. The incubation period is exceptionally long, ranging from 9 months to 20 years, with an average of 5 years, making epidemiological tracking and contact tracing complex.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\textit{Mycobacterium leprae} is an obligate intracellular bacillus with a predilection for Schwann cells of peripheral nerves and macrophages in the skin. Transmission occurs via prolonged, close contact with untreated patients, likely through respiratory droplets. The clinical spectrum of leprosy is determined by the host's cell-mediated immune response.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mycobacterium leprae infection:} the causative pathogen, which thrives in cooler body temperatures, affecting extremities, ears, nose, and superficial nerves.
    \item \textbf{Host immune response:} patients with strong cell-mediated immunity develop tuberculoid leprosy (limited disease, few bacilli), whereas those with weak cell-mediated immunity develop lepromatous leprosy (widespread disease, high bacterial load).
    \item \textbf{Schwann cell invasion:} the bacilli enter Schwann cells, leading to demyelination, inflammation, and eventual fibrosis of peripheral nerves, causing sensory loss.
    \item \textbf{Leprosy reactions:} acute immunological inflammatory episodes (Type 1 or reversal reaction, and Type 2 or erythema nodosum leprosum) caused by changes in immunological status, leading to rapid nerve damage.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation spans a spectrum from tuberculoid (paucibacillary) to lepromatous (multibacillary) leprosy, depending on host immunity.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hypopigmented or erythematous skin lesions with partial or complete loss of sensation (tactile, thermal, or pain)
    \item Thickened, tender, or painful peripheral nerves, most commonly the ulnar, median, radial cutaneous, common peroneal, and posterior tibial nerves
    \item Muscle weakness, paralysis, or wasting in the hands and feet, leading to claw hand or foot drop
    \item Anhidrosis (lack of sweating) in the affected skin patches due to autonomic nerve damage
    \item Nasal congestion, epistaxis, or saddle-nose deformity in advanced lepromatous disease
    \item Ocular complications, including lagophthalmos (inability to close the eyelids), keratitis, and iridocyclitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Leprosy's diverse skin and neurological features can mimic many other conditions, often delaying correct diagnosis.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fungal infections:} tinea versicolor or dermatophytosis, which present with hypopigmented patches but retain normal sensation.
    \item \textbf{Psoriasis or vitiligo:} autoimmune skin conditions characterised by plaques or complete depigmentation, without sensory loss or nerve thickening.
    \item \textbf{Diabetic neuropathy:} a common cause of peripheral sensory loss and foot ulcers, but lacks the characteristic hypopigmented skin lesions.
    \item \textbf{Sarcoidosis:} a systemic granulomatous disease that can present with similar cutaneous lesions and neuropathy.
    \item \textbf{Cutaneous lupus erythematosus:} autoimmune skin lesions that may mimic erythematous leprosy plaques.
    \item \textbf{Lyme disease:} can present with erythema migrans and neurological deficits but has a different travel and exposure history.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone presenting with persistent, unexplained skin patches showing loss of sensation, or unexplained numbness or weakness in the hands or feet, should seek medical evaluation, particularly if they have lived in or travelled to endemic regions.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute severe breathing difficulty due to laryngeal spasm (a rare manifestation in advanced lepromatous disease)
    \item Severe systemic symptoms (high fever, severe joint pain, rapid nerve pain or swelling) indicating an acute leprosy reaction
    \item Severe, infected skin ulcers with signs of systemic sepsis (e.g., confusion, rapid heart rate, high fever)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A definitive diagnosis is established by clinical findings and laboratory confirmation in specialised laboratories.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} identifying characteristic anaesthetic skin patches and palpably enlarged peripheral nerves.
    \item \textbf{Skin smear (slit-skin smear):} taken from the earlobes and active lesions to look for acid-fast bacilli using Ziehl-Neelsen staining.
    \item \textbf{Skin or nerve biopsy:} histological examination showing granulomatous inflammation and presence of bacilli within Schwann cells or macrophages.
    \item \textbf{Polymerase chain reaction (PCR):} sensitive molecular testing to detect \textit{M. leprae} DNA in tissue samples.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is coordinated by infectious disease specialists and involves multi-drug regimens, along with wound and disability management.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Multidrug therapy (MDT):} standard WHO-recommended combination of rifampicin, dapsone, and clofazimine for multibacillary cases (12 months), or rifampicin and dapsone for paucibacillary cases (6 months).
    \item \textbf{Corticosteroids:} high-dose prednisolone to manage acute Type 1 and Type 2 leprosy reactions and prevent permanent nerve damage.
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitative surgery:} reconstructive procedures (such as tendon transfers) to restore function in cases of foot drop or claw hand.
    \item \textbf{Wound care and orthotics:} custom footwear, protective splints, and meticulous care of insensitive limbs to prevent ulceration.
    \item \textbf{Psychosocial support:} counselling and education to address the historical stigma and anxiety associated with the diagnosis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Permanent sensory loss leading to repetitive trauma, painless burns, and non-healing ulcers
    \item Muscle paralysis and permanent deformities, including claw hand, foot drop, and resorption of digits
    \item Blindness due to lagophthalmos, corneal ulceration, or chronic uveitis
    \item Chronic renal failure secondary to amyloidosis in long-standing lepromatous disease
    \item Severe physical, social, and psychological distress due to social exclusion and stigma
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt and complete multidrug therapy, the prognosis for leprosy is excellent. The infection is completely cured, and further nerve damage is halted. However, nerve damage and physical deformities present at the time of diagnosis are often irreversible, though they can be managed with rehabilitation and surgery. Relapse after completing MDT is rare, and patients are no longer infectious after the very first dose of rifampicin.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Meticulously inspect hands and feet daily for cuts, blisters, or redness if sensory loss is present.
    \item Use protective gloves when handling hot objects or sharp tools.
    \item Avoid walking barefoot and wear custom-fitted protective footwear.
    \item Moisturise the skin regularly to prevent cracking and secondary bacterial infections.
    \item Ensure household contacts of diagnosed patients undergo regular clinical screening.
\end{itemize}
