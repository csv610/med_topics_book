\chapter{C. Difficile Infection}
\index{C. Difficile Infection}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
C. difficile infection, short for \textit{Clostridioides difficile} infection, is a bacterial infection of the bowel that causes diarrhoea and inflammation of the colon (colitis). The bacterium is a spore-forming germ found widely in the environment, in soil, and in the gut of a small proportion of healthy people. It usually causes illness only when the normal protective bacteria of the bowel have been disturbed, most often by antibiotic use.

C. difficile is a leading cause of antibiotic-associated diarrhoea and a major infection control problem in hospitals and aged-care facilities. The severity of illness ranges from mild, self-limiting diarrhoea to severe, life-threatening colitis, and the infection has a strong tendency to recur after treatment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
C. difficile is the most common infectious cause of diarrhoea acquired in hospital. Roughly 1 in 100 hospital inpatients develops the infection, and older adults in hospitals and nursing homes are at greatest risk. Most cases follow antibiotic use in the preceding weeks, and the infection is uncommon in healthy children and younger adults outside healthcare settings. Reported rates have risen over recent decades, and hospital outbreaks are an important public health concern because the spores can survive for long periods on surfaces and resist alcohol-based hand sanitisers.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
C. difficile causes disease when the normal balance of bacteria in the colon is disrupted, usually by antibiotics, allowing the organism to multiply and produce toxins:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antibiotic exposure:} broad-spectrum antibiotics, especially clindamycin, penicillins, cephalosporins, and fluoroquinolones, deplete protective gut bacteria and allow C.\ difficile to overgrow
    \item \textbf{Spores and transmission:} the bacterium forms hardy spores that survive on surfaces, clothing, and hands and are spread to others by the faecal--oral route
    \item \textbf{Toxin production:} the organism releases toxins (toxin A and toxin B) that damage the lining of the colon and trigger intense inflammation
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} older age, hospitalisation, prolonged stays in healthcare facilities, a weakened immune system, stomach-acid suppressing medicines, and previous C.\ difficile infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms typically begin days to weeks after taking antibiotics and range from mild diarrhoea to severe colitis:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Watery diarrhoea, sometimes many times a day, often with a distinctive smell
    \item Abdominal cramping and pain
    \item Fever and a general feeling of being unwell
    \item Nausea and reduced appetite
    \item Dehydration, weakness, and dizziness from fluid loss
    \item In severe cases: persistent or bloody diarrhoea, severe abdominal tenderness, and signs of serious illness such as confusion or a very high fever
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Many conditions cause diarrhoea and abdominal pain, and the symptoms of C.\ difficile can resemble:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Other infectious causes of diarrhoea, such as \textit{Salmonella}, \textit{Campylobacter}, \textit{E.\ coli}, norovirus, and rotavirus
    \item Antibiotic-associated diarrhoea that is not due to C.\ difficile
    \item Irritable bowel syndrome or a flare of inflammatory bowel disease such as Crohn disease or ulcerative colitis
    \item Ischaemic colitis, caused by reduced blood flow to the bowel
    \item Side effects of medicines, laxative use, or food poisoning
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with diarrhoea that follows antibiotic treatment should see a doctor if the symptoms are severe, persist, or are worsening, and should take steps to avoid spreading the infection to others.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Blood in the stool or black, tarry stools
    \item Severe or worsening abdominal pain
    \item High fever, confusion, or severe drowsiness
    \item Signs of dehydration that is not improving, such as extreme thirst, little or no urine, sunken eyes, or fainting
    \item Diarrhoea so frequent that an adequate fluid intake becomes impossible
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis is confirmed by testing a stool sample for the bacterium and its toxins:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stool testing:} a sample of diarrhoea is tested for the C.\ difficile germ (glutamate dehydrogenase antigen) and its toxins, using enzyme immunoassay or molecular (PCR) methods
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count and electrolyte levels are checked to assess dehydration and inflammation
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} a CT scan or X-ray of the abdomen may be used in severe cases to look for thickening of the colon, toxic megacolon, or perforation
    \item \textbf{Endoscopy:} flexible sigmoidoscopy or colonoscopy may occasionally be performed to identify the yellowish plaques of pseudomembranous colitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Most people with mild infection recover with supportive care, but specific antibiotic treatment is usually required:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stop the trigger:} the offending antibiotic is stopped or changed wherever possible
    \item \textbf{Fluids:} oral rehydration is the mainstay of treatment, with intravenous fluids for severe dehydration
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics for C.\ difficile:} oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin are the standard treatments, and a longer course may be needed for recurrent infection
    \item \textbf{Avoid anti-diarrhoea medicines:} agents that slow the bowel, such as loperamide, are usually avoided because they may trap toxins in the colon
    \item \textbf{Infection control:} affected people are isolated in hospital, and staff and visitors use gloves and wash their hands with soap and water
    \item \textbf{Faecal microbiota transplantation:} in some centres, transplanting donor gut bacteria is used for repeated recurrences that will not settle
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} rarely needed to remove a severely damaged section of colon
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dehydration and electrolyte imbalance from heavy diarrhoea
    \item Toxic megacolon, a dangerous over-distension of the colon
    \item Perforation of the bowel and peritonitis
    \item Sepsis and septic shock
    \item Recurrent infection, which occurs in roughly one in five to one in six people after a first episode
    \item Longer hospital stays and slower recovery in frail and elderly patients
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people with C.\ difficile infection recover fully with prompt treatment, often within one to two weeks, and mild cases may settle without specific treatment once the trigger is removed. However, recurrence is common, and the infection can be serious or even life-threatening in older, frail, or hospitalised patients. With good supportive care and appropriate antibiotics, most people make a complete recovery and regain normal bowel function.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use antibiotics only when they are truly needed, and complete them exactly as prescribed
    \item Wash hands thoroughly with soap and water, especially after using the toilet, as alcohol-based hand sanitisers do not reliably kill C.\ difficile spores
    \item Clean contaminated surfaces with bleach-based disinfectants
    \item Stay home and avoid visiting hospitals or nursing homes while unwell with diarrhoea
    \item Drink plenty of fluids to replace those lost through diarrhoea
    \item Tell your doctor about any previous C.\ difficile infection, as this increases the risk of recurrence
\end{itemize}
