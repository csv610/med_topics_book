\chapter{Chilblains}
\index{Chilblains}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Chilblains, also called pernio, are small, painful, itchy red or purple swellings that develop on the skin after exposure to cold. They most often affect the fingers, toes, ears, and nose, appearing hours after the skin has been chilled and then re-warmed. Chilblains are a form of cold-induced skin injury but are not the same as frostbite, which damages the skin more deeply. They are uncomfortable but usually heal on their own within a few weeks and do not cause permanent damage. In most cases they are a nuisance rather than a serious medical problem, although recurrent or atypical lesions deserve medical assessment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Chilblains are most common in cold, damp climates and are seen mainly during the winter months. They affect women more often than men, and people who are thin, who smoke, or who have poor circulation are at greater risk. Anyone exposed to cold and damp without adequate protection can develop them, but they are unusual in warm climates unless the skin is cooled locally, for example by cold water. Many affected people experience chilblains every winter, and the condition tends to be more troublesome in those with Raynaud's phenomenon or other circulatory sensitivity.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cold exposure:} cold causes the small blood vessels in the skin of the extremities to narrow (vasoconstriction)
    \item \textbf{Abnormal vascular response:} in susceptible people, sudden or rapid re-warming causes the vessels to widen too quickly, allowing fluid to leak into the surrounding skin
    \item \textbf{Inflammation:} the leaked fluid triggers an inflammatory reaction that produces redness, swelling, and itching
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} smoking, poor circulation, low body weight, and conditions that affect the blood vessels such as lupus or Raynaud's phenomenon
    \item \textbf{Not frostbite:} chilblains affect only the superficial skin layers, and the tissues do not actually freeze
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Small red, blue, or purple swellings on the fingers, toes, ears, or nose
    \item Itching, burning, or a painful sensation as the skin re-warms
    \item Swelling and tenderness of the affected skin
    \item The skin may feel cold and look blotchy
    \item Blisters or shallow sores may develop in severe cases
    \item Symptoms usually begin several hours after cold exposure and can persist for one to three weeks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Frostbite -- a deeper, more serious cold injury with numbness and hardening of the skin
    \item Raynaud's phenomenon -- fingers turn white, then blue, then red, usually without persistent swellings
    \item Lupus erythematosus -- an autoimmune disease that can cause similar skin lesions
    \item Acrocyanosis -- persistent blue discoloration of the hands and feet in the cold
    \item Livedo reticularis -- a blotchy, lace-like skin pattern that appears in the cold
    \item Chilblain lupus -- a lupus variant that mimics chilblains and is worth considering if lesions recur or persist
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if chilblains are severe, do not heal within a few weeks, keep coming back, or are accompanied by other symptoms such as joint pain, fever, or fatigue, which can suggest an underlying condition.

\medskip
\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Skin that turns very pale, becomes numb and hard, or develops blisters (possible frostbite)
    \item Signs of infection: spreading redness, heat, increasing pain, or discharge
    \item Open sores that are not healing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} the appearance and location of the lesions in the setting of cold exposure is usually sufficient to diagnose chilblains
    \item \textbf{History:} recent cold exposure, smoking, circulation problems, and any family history of similar conditions
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} may be arranged to look for an underlying cause such as lupus or another autoimmune condition if lesions are atypical or recurrent
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} rarely needed, but a small skin sample can be examined if the diagnosis remains uncertain
    \item \textbf{Assessment of circulation:} examination of pulses and, where indicated, tests of blood flow in the extremities
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Warm the skin gradually:} avoid sudden heat, which makes symptoms worse
    \item \textbf{Keep the affected area dry and protected} with loose, warm clothing
    \item \textbf{Avoid scratching} to prevent breaks in the skin
    \item \textbf{Topical treatments:} soothing emollient creams and, in some cases, prescribed creams that reduce inflammation
    \item \textbf{Medication:} in persistent cases, a doctor may prescribe medicines that relax the blood vessels or reduce inflammation
    \item \textbf{Stop smoking:} smoking narrows the blood vessels and slows healing
    \item \textbf{Treat underlying conditions:} such as lupus or Raynaud's phenomenon if present
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection of the skin if lesions break and become contaminated
    \item Ulceration or slow-healing sores in severe or repeated cases
    \item Scarring or permanent skin discoloration after large or deep lesions
    \item Recurrent chilblains each winter, affecting comfort and quality of life
    \item An underlying disease being missed if the chilblains are assumed to be the only problem
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Chilblains usually heal completely within one to three weeks once the skin is kept warm and protected, and they do not cause lasting damage in most people. They tend to recur every winter in susceptible individuals. The outlook is generally excellent, and long-term problems are uncommon unless the skin becomes infected or an underlying circulatory or autoimmune condition is present. Avoiding cold and damp exposure is the single most important step in preventing them from returning.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep the hands, feet, ears, and nose warm with gloves, thick socks, a hat, and warm, loose footwear
    \item Warm cold skin gradually, never with direct heat or hot water
    \item Keep the whole body warm, since chilling the body narrows the blood vessels in the extremities
    \item Avoid smoking and limit alcohol, both of which affect the circulation
    \item Keep the skin dry and apply moisturiser to the extremities in winter
    \item Exercise regularly to improve circulation
    \item If chilblains recur frequently, discuss assessment for an underlying cause with a doctor
\end{itemize}
