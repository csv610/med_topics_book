\chapter{Counselling}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Counselling is a professional talking therapy in which a trained counsellor helps a person explore and work through difficulties in their life. It provides a confidential, non-judgemental space to talk about feelings, relationships, life changes, loss, and stress. Counselling differs from clinical psychology in that it focuses on supporting people through current problems and life transitions rather than treating mental illness, though the two overlap. Counsellors do not give advice or solve problems for clients; instead, they help people understand their own thoughts and feelings and find their own ways forward.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Mental health difficulties and life stressors are extremely common. A substantial proportion of adults experience a period in which counselling would help, including after bereavement, relationship breakdown, job loss, or major illness. Anxiety and depression affect around one in five Australians in any given year, and many people access counselling as a first step. Demand for counselling has grown steadily, and services are provided through private practice, general practice, community health centres, schools, workplaces, and employee assistance programs.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
People seek counselling for a wide range of concerns.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bereavement and loss:} grief after the death of a loved one, or after other losses such as divorce or job loss
    \item \textbf{Relationship difficulties:} conflict, communication problems, or breakdown
    \item \textbf{Life transitions:} retirement, parenthood, moving, or children leaving home
    \item \textbf{Stress and burnout:} workplace stress, caregiving, or financial strain
    \item \textbf{Anxiety and low mood:} worry, sadness, or adjustment difficulties
    \item \textbf{Trauma:} past abuse, accidents, or violence
    \item \textbf{Health concerns:} coping with a new diagnosis, chronic illness, or a terminal illness
    \item \textbf{Personal growth:} self-esteem, identity, and life direction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Counselling addresses problems rather than medical symptoms, but several signs suggest it may help.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Feeling overwhelmed, anxious, or persistently low
    \item Difficulty coping with a major life event
    \item Relationship conflict or communication problems
    \item Grief that feels stuck or overwhelming
    \item Work stress, burnout, or difficulty balancing responsibilities
    \item Low self-esteem or uncertainty about life direction
    \item A desire to talk things through in a safe, confidential setting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A skilled counsellor recognises when more specialised care is needed.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Clinical depression, anxiety disorders, or post-traumatic stress disorder needing diagnosis and treatment by a GP, psychiatrist, or psychologist
    \item Severe or complex mental illness, such as bipolar disorder or schizophrenia
    \item Suicidal thoughts, which need urgent assessment
    \item Substance misuse, which may need specialist addiction treatment
    \item Domestic violence or abuse, needing specialised safety planning
    \item Physical symptoms that may have a medical cause
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone can see a counsellor directly, or ask a GP for a referral and a recommendation. A GP can also check whether symptoms such as persistent low mood, anxiety, or sleep problems need medical assessment or a psychology referral.

\textbf{Seek urgent help if:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item You have thoughts of harming yourself or ending your life
    \item You are unable to cope with daily activities
    \item You are experiencing severe symptoms of depression, anxiety, or psychosis
    \item Someone you care for is in immediate danger
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:} immediate danger of self-harm or suicide, or if someone is a danger to others. For crisis support in Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14 or Suicide Call Back Service on 1300 659 467.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Counselling begins with a structured assessment rather than medical tests.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Initial assessment:} discussion of the presenting problem, history, goals, and expectations
    \item \textbf{Goal setting:} identifying what the person wants to achieve from counselling
    \item \textbf{Safety screening:} assessment for self-harm, suicide risk, and severe distress
    \item \textbf{Referral pathways:} direction to a GP, psychologist, or psychiatrist where needed
    \item \textbf{Confidentiality agreement:} clear explanation of the limits of confidentiality
    \item \textbf{Review:} regular review of progress towards goals
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Counselling is delivered through a structured but flexible relationship.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Individual counselling:} one-on-one sessions, usually weekly or fortnightly
    \item \textbf{Couples or family counselling:} joint sessions for relationship difficulties
    \item \textbf{Group counselling:} sessions with others facing similar issues, such as grief or addiction
    \item \textbf{Approaches:} person-centred, cognitive behavioural, solution-focused, and psychodynamic counselling
    \item \textbf{Online and telephone counselling:} accessible options for many people
    \item \textbf{Integration with other care:} working alongside a GP, psychologist, or psychiatrist
    \item \textbf{Duration:} short-term (six to twelve sessions) or longer-term, depending on need
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Counselling can bring up painful feelings and memories before they resolve
    \item A poor match between client and counsellor can limit progress
    \item Delayed referral of a serious mental illness if it is not recognised
    \item Reliance on counselling without medical treatment where medication is needed
    \item Unqualified practitioners causing harm, so choosing a registered counsellor matters
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people who engage in counselling report improved coping, reduced distress, and better relationships. The benefits depend on the strength of the therapeutic relationship and the person's willingness to engage. Short-term counselling is effective for many adjustment problems and life stressors, while longer-term work may help with deeper or longstanding issues. Where mental illness is present, counselling combined with medical treatment is usually more effective than either alone.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek help early rather than waiting until a crisis
    \item Choose a counsellor registered with a professional body, such as the Australian Counselling Association
    \item Ask a GP for a referral and recommendations
    \item Be open about your goals and give the process time
    \item Practise self-care: sleep, activity, social connection, and time for rest
    \item Use crisis lines if you need immediate support
    \item Review progress regularly and change counsellor or approach if it is not helping
\end{itemize}
