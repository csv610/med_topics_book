\chapter{Anal Injury}
\index{Anal Injury}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An anal injury is damage to the anus, the anal canal, or the surrounding skin and sphincter muscles. Injuries range from minor superficial tears or bruises to deep lacerations involving the sphincter muscles that control bowel function.

Because the anus is used for passing stool and sits close to the rectum and perineum, injuries in this area can be painful, may bleed, and in more serious cases can affect bowel control. Most anal injuries are treatable, but severe ones need prompt medical assessment to protect sphincter function and prevent infection.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Anal injuries are not common enough to be reported widely, but they occur across all ages. They are more frequent in adults than in children, and the risk is higher with certain activities, such as anal intercourse, the use of anal toys or other inserted objects, straddle-type falls onto hard objects, and childbirth.

Older people and those taking blood-thinning medicines may bruise or tear more easily. Injuries caused by deliberate trauma or assault affect both sexes and are often under-reported, which makes reliable figures difficult to obtain.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blunt trauma:} falls onto the buttocks, straddle injuries, kicks, or blows can crush or bruise the anal and perineal tissues
    \item \textbf{Penetrating injury:} impalement on a sharp object or the insertion of foreign bodies, whether accidental or deliberate, can pierce the anal canal or rectum
    \item \textbf{Shearing or stretching:} anal intercourse, insertion of objects, or childbirth can tear the lining and the sphincter muscle fibres
    \item \textbf{Other causes:} self-inflicted injury, sexual assault, burns, and bites
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} torn tissue bleeds and swells, and bacteria from the bowel can enter deeper tissues; infection and abscess formation are therefore the main concerns after the initial injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain at the anus that is often worse when sitting, passing stool, or wiping
    \item Bright red bleeding from the anus or on the toilet paper
    \item Bruising, swelling, or a visible tear around the anus
    \item A lump or area of tenderness at the anal margin
    \item Difficulty or pain passing stools
    \item In more severe injuries, an inability to hold in gas or stools (loss of bowel control)
    \item Signs of deep infection if treatment is delayed, including fever, increasing pain, and discharge
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Anal fissure, a painful tear usually caused by passing a hard stool rather than by injury
    \item Haemorrhoids (piles), swollen veins that can bleed and cause a lump
    \item Perianal abscess or anal fistula, causing infection-related pain and discharge
    \item Fissures or ulceration due to inflammatory bowel disease, such as Crohn's disease
    \item A prolapsed internal haemorrhoid or rectal prolapse
    \item Sexually transmitted infections causing anal ulceration, such as herpes
    \item Anal cancer, which can mimic a chronic injury or ulcer
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for any anal injury that causes significant pain, ongoing bleeding, a visible tear, or concern about possible contamination of a deep wound.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, uncontrollable bleeding from the anus
    \item An object that is deeply embedded, or a penetrating wound to the area
    \item Signs of shock, including faintness, paleness, a rapid heart rate, or collapse
    \item Severe abdominal or perineal pain after injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} how the injury happened, when, and what was inserted, if anything
    \item \textbf{Examination:} gentle inspection of the anal area for tears, bruising, swelling, or retained foreign bodies
    \item \textbf{Digital rectal examination:} to assess sphincter tone and check for damage inside the anal canal (may be deferred in children or if it is too painful)
    \item \textbf{Anoscopy or proctoscopy:} a small instrument used to look directly at the anal canal and lower rectum
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} X-ray or CT for suspected foreign bodies, deep penetration, or associated pelvic injury
    \item \textbf{Blood tests and swabs:} if infection, significant blood loss, or a sexually transmitted infection is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Self-care for minor injuries:} rest, warm baths (sitz baths), simple pain relief, and gentle hygiene
    \item \textbf{Wound care:} cleaning the area, dressing minor wounds, and keeping the skin dry
    \item \textbf{Stool management:} laxatives or stool softeners so that passing stool does not re-tear the healing wound
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} for contaminated or infected wounds, as prescribed by a doctor
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} repair of deep lacerations or sphincter muscle tears, removal of foreign bodies, and drainage of collections
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review to check healing and, where sphincter function is affected, assessment by a colorectal specialist or pelvic-floor physiotherapist
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Anal fissure or a chronic non-healing ulcer
    \item Perianal abscess or anal fistula from bowel bacteria entering deeper tissues
    \item Anal stenosis, a narrowing of the anal canal from scarring
    \item Faecal incontinence from damage to the sphincter muscles
    \item Infection spreading into the pelvic tissues or bloodstream in severe cases
    \item Psychological distress, especially when the injury follows assault
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most minor anal injuries heal fully within a few days to weeks with simple measures, and sphincter function usually recovers completely. Deeper tears that are repaired early tend to heal well, although some people develop long-term scarring or a degree of incontinence. Injuries that are treated late, become infected, or involve the sphincter muscle carry a higher risk of ongoing symptoms. With appropriate follow-up, most people return to normal bowel and sexual function.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use plenty of lubricant and proceed gently during anal intercourse or the insertion of toys
    \item Avoid inserting hard, sharp, or non-body-safe objects into the anus
    \item Prevent falls and straddle injuries around the home, at work, and in sport
    \item Treat constipation so that hard stools do not tear the anal lining
    \item Keep tetanus vaccination up to date for any wound that breaks the skin
    \item Seek help promptly for any injury that bleeds, is painful, or is slow to heal
\end{itemize}
