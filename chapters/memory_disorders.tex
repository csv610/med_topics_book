\chapter{Memory disorders}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Memory disorders are a group of cognitive impairments characterised by progressive or acute deficits in the ability to encode, store, retrieve, or recognise information. These conditions range in severity from mild cognitive impairment (MCI)—where minor memory lapses do not significantly impair daily functioning—to various forms of dementia, which are chronic, progressive, and terminal neurodegenerative syndromes. The most common cause of progressive memory loss is Alzheimer's disease, but other etiologies include vascular dementia, dementia with Lewy bodies, frontotemporal dementia, and amnestic syndromes such as Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome. Because memory is fundamental to human identity and daily functioning, these disorders have a profound clinical and psychological impact, requiring comprehensive diagnostic evaluation, medical management, and extensive caregiver support.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Memory disorders represent a major public health challenge, particularly in nations with ageing populations. Globally, over 55 million people are estimated to live with dementia, a figure projected to rise to 139 million by 2050. In Australia, dementia is the second leading cause of death overall and the leading cause of death for Australian women, affecting more than 400,000 individuals. Mild cognitive impairment is even more prevalent, affecting approximately 10\% to 20\% of adults aged 65 years and older, with an estimated 10\% to 15\% of these individuals progressing to dementia each year. While age is the strongest risk factor, dementia is not a normal part of ageing, and younger-onset dementia (occurring before age 65) represents a significant cohort.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The underlying mechanisms of memory disorders involve neurodegenerative proteins, cerebrovascular pathology, nutritional deficits, or structural brain injuries.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurodegenerative protein neuropathology:} In Alzheimer's disease, the accumulation of extracellular beta-amyloid plaques and intracellular hyperphosphorylated tau neurofibrillary tangles leads to synaptic dysfunction and widespread neuronal death, particularly in the hippocampus. Dementia with Lewy bodies is characterised by abnormal aggregation of alpha-synuclein proteins.
    \item \textbf{Cerebrovascular damage:} Vascular dementia is caused by ischaemic or haemorrhagic brain injuries, such as large-vessel strokes, multiple microinfarcts (multi-infarct dementia), or subcortical small-vessel disease, which disrupt subcortical white matter pathways.
    \item \textbf{Nutritional and metabolic deficiencies:} Severe thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency, typically secondary to chronic alcohol misuse or malabsorption, leads to the destruction of the mammillary bodies and dorsomedial thalamic nuclei, resulting in Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome.
    \item \textbf{Reversible metabolic states:} Conditions such as hypothyroidism, vitamin B12 deficiency, and chronic hypercalcaemia impair cerebral metabolism, leading to cognitive slowing and memory deficits.
    \item \textbf{Normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH):} An abnormal accumulation of cerebrospinal fluid in the brain's ventricles, causing a classic triad of memory impairment, gait disturbance, and urinary incontinence.
    \item \textbf{Traumatic and structural lesions:} Chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) resulting from repetitive head injuries, subdural haematomas, or primary brain tumours compressing memory circuits.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The presentation of memory disorders varies depending on the specific cognitive domains affected and the rate of disease progression.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Short-term memory impairment:} Forgetting recent conversations, repeating questions, misplacing items, and relying heavily on memory aids (e.g. lists, reminders).
    \item \textbf{Disorientation:} Getting lost in familiar environments, losing track of dates, seasons, or the passage of time.
    \item \textbf{Executive dysfunction:} Difficulty planning, organizing, or completing familiar sequential tasks, such as managing finances, cooking a recipe, or playing a game.
    \item \textbf{Language deficits (aphasia):} Difficulty finding the right words, substituting inappropriate words, or struggling to follow or participate in a conversation.
    \item \textbf{Behavioural and psychological symptoms:} Apathy, depression, anxiety, social withdrawal, agitation, or motor restlessness (wandering), which are often collectively termed BPSD.
    \item \textbf{Visual-spatial impairment:} Inability to judge distances, recognise familiar faces or objects, or coordinate movements.
    \item \textbf{Confabulation:} The fabrication of memories to fill in memory gaps, without the conscious intention to deceive, classically seen in Korsakoff's syndrome.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Clinicians must distinguish between normal age-related cognitive changes, psychiatric conditions, and different subtypes of cognitive decline.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal ageing vs.\ MCI vs.\ Dementia:} Normal ageing involves minor slowing of recall. MCI involves objective memory impairment without functional impairment. Dementia involves cognitive deficits that interfere with independent daily living.
    \item \textbf{Dementia vs.\ Delirium:} Delirium is an acute, fluctuating state of confusion, inattention, and altered consciousness, usually triggered by an underlying medical issue (e.g. urinary tract infection). Dementia is chronic, progressive, and features clear consciousness.
    \item \textbf{Depressive pseudodementia vs.\ Primary dementia:} Severe depression in older adults can present with subjective memory complaints and poor cognitive performance, which resolves with appropriate antidepressant therapy.
    \item \textbf{Alzheimer's disease vs.\ Frontotemporal dementia (FTD):} FTD typically presents in younger individuals (aged 45 to 65) with early, profound personality changes, disinhibition, or language impairment, with relative preservation of memory in the early stages.
    \item \textbf{Dementia with Lewy Bodies (DLB) vs.\ Parkinson's disease dementia:} DLB features early visual hallucinations, fluctuating cognition, and parkinsonian motor symptoms developing before or concurrently with cognitive decline.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Individuals or family members should consult a general practitioner if they notice persistent, worsening memory lapses, difficulty performing daily tasks, or changes in personality or mood.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, acute onset of confusion, disorientation, or memory loss, which may indicate a stroke or acute delirium.
    \item Acute agitation, combativeness, or severe hallucinations that pose an immediate safety risk to the patient or others.
    \item Accidental ingestion of toxic substances or medication overdose due to memory confusion.
    \item High fever accompanied by confusion, headache, and neck stiffness, suggesting meningitis or encephalitis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A diagnosis of a memory disorder requires a multi-step evaluation to document cognitive decline and rule out reversible causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cognitive screening tests:} Administering standardized tests such as the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), or the Rowland Universal Dementia Assessment Scale (RUDAS) for culturally and linguistically diverse patients.
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive neuropsychological testing:} Detailed evaluation of multiple cognitive domains (memory, language, executive function, attention) to map the cognitive profile.
    \item \textbf{Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):} Performed to assess for structural changes, such as medial temporal lobe (hippocampal) atrophy in Alzheimer's, chronic vascular changes, or normal pressure hydrocephalus.
    \item \textbf{Metabolic and nutritional blood panel:} Testing serum vitamin B12, folate, thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), calcium, electrolytes, and renal/liver function to rule out correctable metabolic causes.
    \item \textbf{Advanced biomarkers:} Utilising FDG-PET imaging (to evaluate regional brain metabolism), amyloid-PET imaging, or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis for beta-amyloid and tau proteins.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is multi-disciplinary, incorporating pharmacological treatments, lifestyle adjustments, and extensive social and care support.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cholinesterase inhibitors:} Medications such as donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine, which increase acetylcholine levels and are indicated for mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's disease and Lewy body dementia.
    \item \textbf{NMDA receptor antagonists:} Memantine, which regulates glutamate activity, used for moderate-to-severe Alzheimer's disease, either alone or in combination with cholinesterase inhibitors.
    \item \textbf{Treatment of cardiovascular risk factors:} Strict control of blood pressure, diabetes, cholesterol, and smoking cessation to slow the progression of vascular dementia.
    \item \textbf{Non-pharmacological cognitive interventions:} Cognitive Stimulation Therapy (CST), occupational therapy to modify the home environment for safety, and structured exercise programs.
    \item \textbf{Management of BPSD:} Implementing non-pharmacological strategies first (sensory therapy, music, structured routine, addressing pain). Cautious, low-dose atypical antipsychotics (e.g. risperidone) are reserved for severe, distressing agitation or aggression where safety is compromised.
    \item \textbf{Social support and legal planning:} Connection with organisations like Dementia Australia, arranging respite care, and establishing Enduring Power of Attorney and Advance Care Plans early in the disease process.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The progression of memory disorders leads to severe functional impairment and physical vulnerability.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Loss of self-care capacity:} Eventual total dependence on others for feeding, bathing, dressing, and toileting.
    \item \textbf{Aspiration pneumonia:} Dysphagia (swallowing difficulty) in late-stage dementia leads to silent aspiration of food or saliva into the lungs, which is a leading cause of death.
    \item \textbf{Falls and fractures:} Instability, spatial disorientation, and osteopenia contribute to a high risk of falls and subsequent fractures (e.g. hip fractures).
    \item \textbf{Malnutrition and dehydration:} Forgetting to eat or drink, or refusing food due to cognitive confusion, leading to rapid weight loss and electrolyte imbalances.
    \item \textbf{Wandering and exposure:} Patients wandering out of their homes, getting lost, and being exposed to extreme weather conditions, traffic accidents, or physical trauma.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for progressive neurodegenerative dementias is poor, as there are currently no disease-modifying cures available. Alzheimer's disease has a slow, progressive course with an average survival of 4 to 8 years after diagnosis, though some patients may live for up to 20 years with supportive care. Vascular dementia often follows a step-wise decline, where cognitive function drops suddenly after each subclinical stroke or vascular event. Reversible causes of memory impairment (e.g. B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism) carry an excellent prognosis, with cognitive function often returning to baseline once the underlying medical issue is treated.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Evidence suggests that up to 40\% of dementia cases globally could be delayed or prevented by targeting modifiable risk factors throughout life.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular health management:} Maintaining optimal blood pressure, managing diabetes, and controlling lipid levels.
    \item \textbf{Regular physical exercise:} Engaging in at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise per week, which promotes neuroplasticity and brain perfusion.
    \item \textbf{Cognitive stimulation:} Keeping the brain active through lifelong learning, puzzles, reading, learning a new language, or playing musical instruments.
    \item \textbf{Social engagement:} Maintaining strong social connections with family, friends, and community groups to combat depression and cognitive isolation.
    \item \textbf{Healthy diet:} Adopting a Mediterranean-style diet rich in vegetables, fruits, whole grains, fish, and olive oil, while limiting processed foods and alcohol.
    \item \textbf{Hearing loss correction:} Using hearing aids to manage age-related hearing loss, which is a significant modifiable risk factor for cognitive decline.
\end{itemize}
