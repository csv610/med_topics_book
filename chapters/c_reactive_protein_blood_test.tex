\chapter{C-Reactive Protein Blood Test}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The C-reactive protein (CRP) blood test measures the level of CRP, a protein produced mainly by the liver that rises when there is inflammation somewhere in the body. CRP is an acute-phase reactant: its concentration climbs rapidly within hours of infection, injury, or tissue inflammation and falls again as the inflammation settles.

CRP is a general marker of inflammation rather than a specific test for a particular disease. It helps doctors detect the presence of inflammation, judge the severity of an illness, monitor the response to treatment, and, in its high-sensitivity form, assess low-grade inflammation related to cardiovascular risk. It is a simple, inexpensive test performed on a routine blood sample.

\section*{Epidemiology}
CRP testing is among the most commonly performed blood tests worldwide. It is used across all age groups in both hospital and community settings and is a routine part of evaluating fever, suspected infection, and chronic inflammatory conditions. High-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP) testing is used to assess low-level inflammation relevant to cardiovascular risk, and mildly raised levels are common in people who are overweight and in those with chronic conditions such as diabetes, arthritis, and inflammatory bowel disease. Because the test is so widely ordered, an understanding of how to interpret it matters to a large proportion of the population.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
CRP is produced mainly by the liver in response to inflammatory signals. When tissue is damaged or infected, immune cells release signalling proteins known as cytokines, which instruct the liver to increase CRP production. The level begins to rise within hours and peaks around 48 hours:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infection:} bacterial infections such as pneumonia, urinary tract infection, or sepsis typically produce large rises in CRP
    \item \textbf{Tissue injury:} surgery, trauma, burns, myocardial infarction, and pancreatitis trigger a rise in CRP
    \item \textbf{Chronic inflammation:} rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, and vasculitis cause persistently raised levels
    \item \textbf{Malignancy:} some cancers produce inflammation and an elevated CRP
    \item \textbf{Normal range:} healthy people usually have low levels, with minor variation detectable by high-sensitivity assays
\end{itemize}

CRP participates in the body's immune response by binding to damaged cells and activating complement, helping to clear infection and promote tissue repair.

\section*{Clinical Features}
A CRP test result does not itself cause symptoms; rather, it reflects what is happening in the body and is interpreted alongside the person's symptoms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal or low CRP:} makes significant bacterial infection or severe inflammation less likely
    \item \textbf{Moderately raised CRP:} common in viral illness, minor injury, or flare-ups of chronic inflammatory conditions
    \item \textbf{High CRP:} often seen in bacterial infection, pneumonia, or significant tissue damage
    \item \textbf{Very high CRP:} may occur in sepsis, severe infection, or major inflammatory diseases
    \item \textbf{Persistent mild elevation:} may point to ongoing chronic inflammation or cardiovascular risk factors
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A raised CRP is not a diagnosis, and many conditions can produce it. When interpreting the result, doctors consider:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bacterial versus viral infection, since bacterial infection usually raises CRP much higher
    \item Infection versus a non-infectious inflammatory condition such as gout, vasculitis, or inflammatory bowel disease
    \item Acute tissue injury, such as a heart attack, pancreatitis, or recent surgery
    \item Chronic conditions, including rheumatoid arthritis and other autoimmune diseases
    \item Malignancy, particularly lymphoma and advanced solid tumours
    \item Physiological variation or laboratory factors, as CRP is a non-specific test
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
The CRP test is part of an overall assessment, and the result alone does not usually determine whether urgent care is needed. If the test was ordered because of worrying symptoms, medical attention should be sought for:
\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever with difficulty breathing, chest pain, or a rapid heart rate
    \item Confusion, severe drowsiness, or other signs of sepsis
    \item Severe abdominal pain or persistent vomiting
    \item Worsening illness despite treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
CRP is measured from a simple blood sample taken from a vein, usually together with a full blood count and other inflammatory markers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Standard CRP:} used to detect and monitor significant inflammation and infection
    \item \textbf{High-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP):} a more sensitive assay used mainly to assess cardiovascular risk
    \item \textbf{Full blood count:} taken at the same time to examine white cell levels, which helps distinguish bacterial from viral infection
    \item \textbf{Other markers:} tests such as the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and procalcitonin may be added
    \item \textbf{Cultures and imaging:} urine, blood, or sputum cultures and X-rays or scans may be needed to locate the source of inflammation
    \item \textbf{Repeat testing:} serial CRP measurements are often used to track the response to treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management depends on the cause of the raised CRP rather than the number itself:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Treat the underlying condition:} antibiotics for bacterial infection, anti-inflammatory medicines for inflammatory disease, or appropriate treatment for the identified cause
    \item \textbf{Observation:} in mild, self-limiting illness, CRP is often repeated to confirm it is returning to normal
    \item \textbf{Monitoring treatment:} falling CRP levels suggest that treatment is working, while a persistently high level may mean the treatment needs review
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular risk management:} a high hs-CRP result prompts lifestyle changes and treatment of risk factors rather than a specific CRP-lowering therapy
    \item \textbf{Further investigation:} if no obvious cause is found, additional tests may be arranged to look for hidden infection, inflammatory disease, or malignancy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A falsely reassuring normal result in early infection, when CRP has not yet had time to rise
    \item A raised CRP of uncertain cause, leading to unnecessary tests and anxiety
    \item Misinterpretation of a mildly raised result as serious, or of a high result without an obvious source
    \item Bruising or discomfort at the blood-taking site, which is uncommon and minor
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
CRP levels fall quickly once the underlying problem is treated, often returning to normal within days to a couple of weeks. In people with chronic inflammatory conditions, CRP may remain mildly raised between flare-ups. As a test, CRP is most useful for tracking the course of an illness: a steadily falling level is reassuring evidence that treatment is working, whereas a rising level may signal worsening infection or inflammation that requires review.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Follow your doctor's advice about the medicines and tests arranged as part of the assessment
    \item Take antibiotics exactly as prescribed and complete the full course if an infection is confirmed
    \item If you have a chronic inflammatory condition, keep taking your regular medicines and attend scheduled reviews
    \item Maintain a healthy weight, eat well, and exercise regularly to reduce low-level inflammation
    \item Ask your doctor to explain your CRP result and what it means for your care
\end{itemize}
