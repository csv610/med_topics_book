\chapter{Contraceptive diaphragm}
\index{Contraceptive diaphragm}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A contraceptive diaphragm is a reusable, dome-shaped silicone cup with a flexible rim that is inserted into the vagina prior to sexual intercourse. It acts as a mechanical barrier contraceptive, designed to cover the uterine cervix and prevent sperm from entering the upper female reproductive tract. To achieve optimal contraceptive efficacy, the diaphragm must always be used in conjunction with a spermicide or an acid-buffering contraceptive gel. The diaphragm represents a non-hormonal, user-controlled method of contraception that is inserted prior to intercourse and must be left in place for at least six hours following the last episode of intercourse.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The use of contraceptive diaphragms has declined globally over recent decades due to the widespread availability of highly effective hormonal contraceptives and long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs). In many countries, diaphragms are utilized by a very small percentage of the reproductive-aged population (typically less than 1\%). However, they remain a critical option for individuals seeking non-hormonal birth control, those who experience adverse effects from hormonal methods, or patients with absolute medical contraindications to oestrogen and progestogens. The introduction of single-size contoured diaphragms (such as Caya) has simplified access by reducing the historical requirement for clinical fitting and sizing procedures.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The contraceptive diaphragm prevents pregnancy through a combination of physical and chemical barriers within the vaginal vault.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cervical occlusion:} The silicone dome of the diaphragm physically covers the external os of the cervix, blocking the direct entry of ejaculated semen into the cervical canal.
    \item \textbf{Anatomical positioning:} The device is wedged securely between the posterior vaginal fornix and the recess behind the pubic symphysis, utilizing the vaginal muscle walls to remain in place during intercourse.
    \item \textbf{Spermicidal action:} Contraceptive gels containing lactic acid (which lowers vaginal pH) or traditional spermicides containing surfactants (such as nonoxynol-9) chemically immobilize or destroy sperm cells upon contact.
    \item \textbf{Gel reservoir function:} The design of the diaphragm holds the contraceptive gel in direct contact with the cervix, ensuring a continuous chemical barrier throughout the duration of use.
    \item \textbf{Non-systemic physiological status:} The method does not interfere with the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis, leaving ovulation, endometrial thickness, and the natural menstrual cycle unaltered.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Safe and effective use of the contraceptive diaphragm requires the user to understand its unique clinical characteristics and operational requirements.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{User-dependent compliance:} Efficacy is highly dependent on the user's motivation and ability to insert the diaphragm correctly before every sexual encounter.
    \item \textbf{Flexible insertion timing:} The diaphragm can be inserted up to two hours prior to intercourse, preventing the interruption of sexual spontaneity.
    \item \textbf{Contraceptive gel requirement:} Mandatory application of an appropriate contraceptive gel to the dome and rim of the device before insertion.
    \item \textbf{Absence of systemic side effects:} Complete avoidance of hormonal side effects, such as mood changes, weight gain, acne, or increased risk of venous thromboembolism.
    \item \textbf{Partner compatibility:} When fitted and placed correctly, neither the user nor their partner should experience discomfort or notice the presence of the device during intercourse.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
In contraceptive counseling, the "differential diagnosis" involves comparing the diaphragm to other barrier and non-hormonal methods to determine the most suitable option based on clinical eligibility and lifestyle factors.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Male or female condoms:} Barrier methods that provide highly effective protection against sexually transmitted infections (STIs), whereas the diaphragm provides no reliable protection against STIs.
    \item \textbf{Cervical cap:} A smaller silicone barrier device that fits directly over the cervix via suction, requiring precise clinical sizing and carrying a higher typical-use failure rate.
    \item \textbf{Copper intrauterine device (IUD):} A long-acting, highly effective non-hormonal option that is user-independent but requires clinical insertion and can cause heavier menstrual bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Fertility awareness-based methods:} Non-barrier family planning involving the tracking of physiological markers (such as basal body temperature and cervical mucus), which carries a higher typical-use failure rate and requires periods of abstinence.
    \item \textbf{Contraindications to diaphragm use:} Clinical conditions that preclude diaphragm use, including pelvic organ prolapse (which prevents secure device placement), a history of toxic shock syndrome, or recurrent urinary tract infections.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Users should consult a healthcare provider or sexual health clinic if they experience pain or irritation during use, have difficulty removing the device, notice signs of vaginal infection, or suspect the diaphragm has become damaged.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of toxic shock syndrome (TSS), such as a sudden high fever, vomiting, diarrhoea, a widespread sunburn-like rash, dizziness, or fainting.
    \item Severe, unexplained lower abdominal or pelvic pain.
    \item Symptoms of a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) to the silicone material or the contraceptive gel, including swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or difficulty breathing.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Before initiating use, a clinical consultation is recommended to assess anatomical suitability and instruct the patient on correct insertion techniques.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bimanual pelvic examination:} Performed by a clinician to assess vaginal tone, uterine position, and ensure there is no significant pelvic organ prolapse that would cause the device to slip.
    \item \textbf{Fitting and sizing (for traditional devices):} Utilizing fitting rings of various diameters (typically 60mm to 90mm) to select the correct size; single-size contoured diaphragms do not require sizing but still benefit from anatomical verification.
    \item \textbf{Position check and practice session:} A clinical teaching session where the patient practice insertion and removal, followed by a clinician examination to confirm correct placement over the cervix.
    \item \textbf{Urinalysis and urine culture:} Indicated if the patient develops dysuria or frequency, to rule out a urinary tract infection potentially caused by the diaphragm rim pressing on the urethra.
    \item \textbf{STI screening:} Recommended to ensure baseline sexual health and rule out undiagnosed infections that could cause localized inflammation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management involves prescribing the appropriate diaphragm, educating the patient on correct usage protocols, and advising on emergency procedures.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Device selection:} Discussing the options between a traditional fitted circular diaphragm or a single-size contoured diaphragm (e.g., Caya).
    \item \textbf{Insertion education:} Instructing the user to apply about one teaspoon of contraceptive gel inside the dome and around the rim, squeeze the rim to fold the device, and push it high into the vagina.
    \item \textbf{Post-intercourse removal timing:} Emphasising that the diaphragm must remain in the vagina for at least six hours after intercourse, but must be removed within 24 hours to prevent bacterial overgrowth.
    \item \textbf{Device care and hygiene:} Instructing the patient to wash the device with mild, unfragranced soap and warm water, air-dry it, and store it in its original case away from direct sunlight.
    \item \textbf{Emergency contraception advice:} Instructing the patient to access emergency contraceptive pills or an emergency copper IUD if the diaphragm is found to be displaced, torn, or removed too early.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While the diaphragm is a low-risk contraceptive option, several complications can occur, particularly with incorrect use or poor hygiene.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urinary tract infections (UTIs):} Pressure from the firm rim of the diaphragm against the urethra can obstruct urinary flow, leading to incomplete voiding and increased susceptibility to cystitis.
    \item \textbf{Vaginal irritation and ulceration:} Localised tissue irritation, allergic contact dermatitis, or superficial mucosal abrasions caused by the silicone material, chemical gels, or an ill-fitting device.
    \item \textbf{Unintended pregnancy:} The risk of contraceptive failure resulting from incorrect insertion, displacement during intercourse, failure to use contraceptive gel, or early removal.
    \item \textbf{Toxic shock syndrome (TSS):} A rare but life-threatening staphylococcal or streptococcal toxin-mediated infection associated with leaving the diaphragm in the vagina for longer than 24 hours.
    \item \textbf{Abnormal vaginal discharge and odour:} Caused by leaving the diaphragm in place for too long or inadequate cleaning of the device, leading to bacterial imbalances.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With perfect use (consistent and correct use with contraceptive gel), the contraceptive diaphragm has an efficacy rate of approximately 92\% to 96\% (a 4\% to 8\% annual failure rate). Under typical-use conditions, the efficacy falls to approximately 88\% (a 12\% annual failure rate), largely due to inconsistent use or incorrect placement. The diaphragm has no impact on future fertility; return to baseline fertility is immediate upon discontinuing use. If maintained according to manufacturer guidelines, a single silicone diaphragm can be used for up to two years.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Adhering to self-care and safety protocols is essential to optimise the efficacy and longevity of the contraceptive diaphragm.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Consistent gel application:} Ensuring contraceptive gel is applied before every single act of intercourse, adding more gel with an applicator if intercourse is repeated while the device is in place.
    \item \textbf{Routine damage inspections:} Inspecting the silicone dome before each insertion by filling it with water or holding it up to a light source to check for pinholes or tears.
    \item \textbf{Avoiding oil-based products:} Refraining from using oil-based vaginal lubricants, creams, or suppositories (such as certain yeast infection treatments), which can damage and degrade the silicone.
    \item \textbf{Prompt post-coital urination:} Urinating immediately after intercourse to help flush out any bacteria that may have been pushed into the urethra during intercourse.
    \item \textbf{Adherence to the 24-hour limit:} Always removing the device within 24 hours of insertion to prevent the risk of toxic shock syndrome.
\end{itemize}
