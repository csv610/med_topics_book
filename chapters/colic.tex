\section*{Definition and Overview}
Infant colic is a common condition in which a healthy baby, usually under four months of age, cries or fusses for long periods, often at the same time of day, for no apparent reason. It is diagnosed when crying lasts for more than three hours a day, on more than three days a week, for more than three weeks, in an otherwise well-fed and thriving baby. Colic is not an illness or a sign that something is wrong with the baby, and it settles on its own with time. The term "colic" is used because the crying is often thought to relate to the tummy, but the cause is not fully understood. It is distressing for parents even though the baby is well.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Colic is very common, affecting roughly one in five babies in the first few months of life. It typically begins in the second to fourth week, peaks at around six weeks, and nearly always resolves by three to four months of age. Boys and girls are affected equally. Colic is seen across all cultures and social groups. While it can occur in any baby, some studies suggest a slightly higher chance in first-born babies and in babies whose mothers smoked during pregnancy. It is important to recognise that colic is a phase of development, not a long-term problem.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The exact cause of colic is unknown, and it is likely that several factors combine to explain the excessive crying:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immature digestive system:} the gut, including the balance of bacteria, is still developing, which may cause discomfort or wind
    \item \textbf{Overstimulation:} a young baby's nervous system can be overwhelmed by noise, light, and handling, and crying may be a way of releasing tension
    \item \textbf{Immature regulation of crying:} babies vary in how easily they can be settled, and some need more soothing than others
    \item \textbf{Feeding issues:} swallowing air while feeding, or a fast or slow milk flow, may add to discomfort
    \item \textbf{Cow's milk protein:} in a small number of breastfed or formula-fed babies, a sensitivity to cow's milk protein may contribute to symptoms
    \item \textbf{Temperament:} some babies are simply more intense in their crying than others
\end{itemize}

Crucially, no serious medical problem is found in babies who meet the definition of colic, and they feed, gain weight, and develop normally.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Periods of intense, inconsolable crying that follow a predictable daily pattern, often in the late afternoon or evening
    \item The baby appears distressed, with a red face, clenched fists, and drawn-up legs
    \item Crying continues despite feeding, changing, and comforting
    \item The baby is otherwise well, feeding normally, and gaining weight
    \item Symptoms begin within the first few weeks of life and settle by three to four months
    \item Gas and a tense tummy may be noticed, though wind is not necessarily the cause of the crying
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Crying that is truly caused by an underlying illness looks different from colic. Conditions that can mimic colic include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Hunger, thirst, or an inadequate milk supply
    \item A soiled or wet nappy, or nappy rash
    \item Too hot or too cold environment
    \item Gastro-oesophageal reflux, which causes spitting up, back-arching, and feeding distress
    \item Milk protein allergy or intolerance, which causes poor weight gain, vomiting, diarrhoea, or blood in the stool
    \item Infection, such as a urinary tract infection, ear infection, or rarely meningitis
    \item A twisted bowel (volvulus) or other surgical emergency, which causes sudden severe distress with vomiting or a swollen tummy
    \item An injured or trapped body part, such as a strand of hair wrapped around a toe or finger
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Colic is a diagnosis made by a health professional after ruling out other causes. See a doctor if:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The crying pattern changes suddenly or becomes much worse
    \item The baby has a fever, is vomiting, or has blood in the stool
    \item The baby is not feeding, is losing weight, or is not gaining weight
    \item The baby seems lethargic, floppy, or unusually pale
    \item There is a swollen or tense tummy, or a visible bulge in the groin
    \item You are worried, exhausted, or finding it hard to cope with the crying
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 if the baby looks seriously ill, is floppy, has a high-pitched cry, or stops breathing.}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} detailed account of the crying pattern, feeding, nappies, weight gain, and any other symptoms, taken with the parents or carers
    \item \textbf{Examination:} a full check of the baby, including weight, growth charts, and a careful look for signs of illness
    \item \textbf{Rule out other causes:} the doctor will look for infections, feeding problems, reflux, or other conditions that can cause crying
    \item \textbf{Urine test:} may be done to exclude a urinary tract infection
    \item \textbf{Observation:} because colic follows a typical time course, a trial of careful symptom tracking can help confirm the pattern
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
There is no cure for colic, and treatment focuses on settling the baby and supporting the parents:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Settling techniques:} gentle rocking, carrying, patting, and swaddling; a dummy; white noise or gentle movement such as a pram ride
    \item \textbf{Feeding adjustments:} feed in a more upright position, burp the baby gently, and try paced feeding to reduce swallowed air
    \item \textbf{Pause before feeding:} the crying may not mean hunger, so try settling the baby first
    \item \textbf{Carrying the baby:} many babies are soothed by skin contact or being worn in a sling
    \item \textbf{Time out for parents:} if the crying becomes overwhelming, it is safest to place the baby in a cot, leave the room, and take a few minutes to calm down
    \item \textbf{Seek support:} share night-time duties, and ask family or friends for help
    \item \textbf{Medical options:} a short trial of a lactose-free or hydrolysed formula, or maternal avoidance of cow's milk, may be considered on medical advice; medicines for wind and colic are of limited and uncertain benefit
\end{itemize}

Never shake a baby. Shaking can cause serious brain injury or death, no matter how frustrated or exhausted you feel.

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Parental exhaustion, anxiety, and depression
    \item Strain on the parents' relationship
    \item Difficulty breastfeeding, sometimes leading to early weaning
    \item The most serious complication is shaken baby syndrome, when a frustrated parent shakes the baby; this can cause severe brain injury
    \item Repeated visits to the doctor or emergency department while parents seek reassurance
    \item Unnecessary changes of formula or treatment as parents look for a cause
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Colic is self-limiting and resolves in the great majority of babies by three to four months of age, regardless of treatment. It causes no lasting harm to the baby, who continues to feed and grow normally. The main challenge is the effect on parents, and most cope with reassurance, practical settling strategies, and support. Once the crying settles, babies and families generally return to normal. Crying in the first months of life is not associated with long-term behaviour problems in children.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Respond promptly to crying; a young baby cannot be "spoiled" by attention
    \item Feed when the baby is hungry, rather than at fixed intervals
    \item Burp gently during and after feeds
    \item Provide a calm, predictable environment with reduced stimulation
    \item Develop a settling routine and use techniques that work for your baby
    \item Take breaks, share the load, and ask for help when you need it
    \item Talk to your doctor or child health nurse for reassurance and support
    \item Remember that colic is a passing phase, not a reflection on your parenting
\end{itemize}
