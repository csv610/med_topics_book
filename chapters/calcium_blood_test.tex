\chapter{Calcium Blood Test}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The calcium blood test measures the amount of calcium in the blood. Calcium is essential for nerve and muscle function, blood clotting, and bone strength, and the body keeps its blood level within a very narrow range. The test is used to detect too much or too little calcium, to investigate symptoms such as muscle cramps, fatigue, or kidney stones, and to monitor conditions that affect calcium balance, including disorders of the parathyroid gland, the kidneys, and vitamin D.

The test is common in both routine health checks and hospital care. Because blood calcium is so tightly regulated, an abnormal result usually indicates a genuine underlying disorder rather than a dietary problem, and it always warrants an explanation of its cause.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Calcium blood tests are performed frequently, often as part of routine blood panels in adults. Abnormal results are common: mildly raised calcium is found in roughly 1 in 100 blood tests, while low calcium is more often seen in people with kidney disease, vitamin D deficiency, or low protein levels. High calcium is most often caused by an overactive parathyroid gland or by cancer affecting bone, while low calcium is typically due to vitamin D deficiency or kidney disease. Both high and low calcium become more common with age.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Blood calcium levels are controlled by parathyroid hormone (PTH), vitamin D, and calcitonin, acting on the gut, kidneys, and bones. When this control is disturbed, blood calcium rises or falls:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High calcium (hypercalcaemia):} most often from an overactive parathyroid gland (primary hyperparathyroidism) or from cancer; also from vitamin D excess, dehydration, and some medicines
    \item \textbf{Low calcium (hypocalcaemia):} commonly from vitamin D deficiency, chronic kidney disease, low magnesium, pancreatitis, or underactive parathyroid glands
    \item \textbf{Albumin effect:} about half of blood calcium is bound to the protein albumin, so the measured level is affected by changes in protein levels
    \item \textbf{Ionised calcium:} the free, active fraction of calcium, which is more directly relevant to symptoms
    \item \textbf{Bone store:} calcium is released from bone to defend blood levels, so blood tests can be normal even when bone stores are low
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
A calcium blood test does not cause symptoms; instead, the results are interpreted in the light of the person's health:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High calcium:} may explain thirst, frequent urination, constipation, nausea, weakness, or confusion
    \item \textbf{Low calcium:} may explain tingling in the fingers and around the mouth, muscle cramps and twitching, and fatigue
    \item \textbf{Normal calcium:} reassuring if symptoms were thought to be due to a calcium problem
    \item \textbf{Borderline results:} often need repeating, as levels vary with hydration and time of day
    \item \textbf{Incidental findings:} many abnormal results are found by chance and require further investigation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
An abnormal calcium result is a clue, not a diagnosis. The doctor considers what is driving it:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Overactive parathyroid gland versus cancer as the cause of high calcium
    \item Vitamin D deficiency versus kidney disease versus low protein as the cause of low calcium
    \item Laboratory error, dehydration, or a recent meal affecting the result
    \item Medicine effects, such as thiazide diuretics raising calcium or certain drugs lowering it
    \item Other mineral disturbances, such as abnormal magnesium or phosphate levels
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
A calcium result outside the normal range should be discussed with a doctor. Urgent assessment is needed for some symptoms:

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Confusion, severe drowsiness, or loss of consciousness
    \item Seizures or severe muscle twitching
    \item Chest pain or a very abnormal heart rhythm
    \item Repeated vomiting or an inability to keep fluids down with a high calcium level
\end{itemize}

Otherwise, people should book an appointment to review any abnormal calcium result and plan further tests.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The calcium blood test is the first step and is usually followed by other tests to find the cause:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Total calcium:} the standard measurement, which includes calcium bound to albumin
    \item \textbf{Albumin:} measured at the same time so that a corrected calcium can be calculated
    \item \textbf{Ionised calcium:} a more specialised test of the free, active calcium, used when results are confusing
    \item \textbf{Parathyroid hormone (PTH):} distinguishes overactivity of the parathyroid gland from other causes
    \item \textbf{Vitamin D:} checked because deficiency is a common cause of low calcium
    \item \textbf{Kidney function and electrolytes:} to detect kidney disease and magnesium problems
    \item \textbf{Imaging and other tests:} depending on the cause, tests such as ultrasound of the parathyroid glands or bone density scanning may be arranged
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is directed at the underlying cause of the abnormal result rather than the number alone:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High calcium:} treat the cause, for example parathyroid surgery or treatment of the responsible cancer or medicine; fluids are given for dehydration
    \item \textbf{Low calcium:} correct vitamin D deficiency and, where needed, give calcium; treat the underlying kidney or parathyroid condition
    \item \textbf{Observation:} mildly abnormal results without symptoms may simply be monitored and repeated
    \item \textbf{Diet and supplements:} a healthy diet and appropriate supplements may be advised where deficiency is the issue
    \item \textbf{Repeat testing:} confirm that treatment has returned calcium to a normal level
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} persistent or severe abnormalities are usually managed with an endocrinologist or other specialist
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A result that is hard to interpret, leading to unnecessary tests or anxiety
    \item A missed cause of an abnormal result, allowing a treatable condition to progress
    \item Kidney stones and bone loss from untreated high calcium
    \item Seizures or heart rhythm problems from untreated severe low calcium
    \item Bruising or discomfort at the blood-taking site, which is uncommon and minor
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Once the cause is identified, most calcium problems respond well to treatment and the blood level returns to normal. Mild high calcium due to an overactive parathyroid gland often remains stable for years and may need no more than monitoring. Low calcium from vitamin D deficiency corrects with replacement therapy. In most cases the outlook is excellent, and the test simply provides a snapshot that guides further care.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Have the test done as recommended, usually after fasting if your doctor advises it
    \item Tell your doctor about all medicines, supplements, and vitamins you take, as some affect calcium
    \item Drink enough fluids, since dehydration can temporarily raise calcium levels
    \item Follow up any abnormal result with the tests your doctor recommends
    \item Maintain a calcium-rich diet and adequate vitamin D, but do not take high-dose supplements without advice
    \item Attend scheduled repeat blood tests to monitor the trend over time
\end{itemize}
