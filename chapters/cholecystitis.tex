\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cholecystitis is inflammation of the gallbladder, a small organ that sits under the liver and stores bile. In most cases it happens suddenly (acute cholecystitis) when a gallstone blocks the outlet of the gallbladder, trapping bile inside and causing the organ to swell and become infected. It usually causes severe pain in the upper right side of the abdomen and needs urgent medical attention. The standard treatment is removal of the gallbladder, most often with keyhole (laparoscopic) surgery. Chronic cholecystitis refers to repeated milder attacks that cause long-standing inflammation.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cholecystitis is one of the most common causes of emergency abdominal surgery in adults. It usually affects people over the age of 40, and women are affected about twice as often as men, partly because gallstones are more common in women. The risk rises with age, pregnancy, obesity, and rapid weight loss. Because gallstones are present in around 1 in 10 adults, attacks of cholecystitis are a frequent reason for emergency department visits, and the condition recurs in a large proportion of people who do not have their gallbladder removed.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Cholecystitis is almost always caused by gallstones, although a small number of cases occur without stones.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stone blocking the cystic duct:} a gallstone becomes wedged in the duct that drains the gallbladder, so bile cannot escape
    \item \textbf{Bile trapped inside:} the trapped bile irritates the lining of the gallbladder, causing inflammation and swelling
    \item \textbf{Secondary infection:} bacteria can multiply in the stagnant bile, worsening the inflammation
    \item \textbf{Acalculous cholecystitis:} inflammation without stones, seen in seriously ill people in intensive care, or after major trauma or burns
    \item \textbf{Reduced blood flow:} in acalculous cases the gallbladder wall may be starved of blood
    \item \textbf{Chronic cholecystitis:} repeated minor attacks leave the gallbladder wall thickened and scarred
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
An attack of cholecystitis is usually severe and unremitting, unlike the shorter, wave-like pain of biliary colic.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Severe pain} in the upper right side or centre of the abdomen, often spreading to the back or right shoulder
    \item \textbf{Pain that lasts hours} and may be worse with breathing in deeply
    \item \textbf{Tenderness} over the right side of the abdomen
    \item \textbf{Fever and chills}
    \item \textbf{Nausea and vomiting}
    \item \textbf{Loss of appetite} and a feeling of being unwell
    \item \textbf{Jaundice} (yellowing of the skin) in a minority of cases, when the main bile duct is also involved
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of cholecystitis overlap with several other conditions that need to be excluded.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Biliary colic (gallstone pain without inflammation)
    \item Bile duct stones or cholangitis (infection of the bile duct)
    \item Acute pancreatitis
    \item Peptic ulcer disease, including a perforated ulcer
    \item Appendicitis, especially if the appendix is high-lying
    \item Kidney stones or pyelonephritis
    \item Hepatitis or liver abscess
    \item Right lower lobe pneumonia or pleurisy
    \item Heart attack (myocardial infarction) presenting with upper abdominal pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with sudden, severe upper abdominal pain should be assessed urgently, because cholecystitis can worsen quickly and other serious conditions can cause the same pain.

\textbf{Call 000 or attend an emergency department for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe or worsening abdominal pain that is not settling
    \item Fever, chills, or rigors
    \item Yellowing of the skin or eyes (jaundice)
    \item Vomiting that prevents keeping fluids down
    \item A swollen, rigid, or very tender abdomen
    \item Signs of sepsis: confusion, rapid breathing or heart rate, mottled skin, or reduced urine output
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Cholecystitis is diagnosed from the combination of symptoms, blood tests, and imaging.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the pattern of pain and tenderness over the gallbladder
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} raised white cell count and inflammatory markers (CRP); liver function tests to check the bile ducts
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} the key test -- it shows gallstones, a thickened gallbladder wall, and fluid around the gallbladder
    \item \textbf{HIDA scan:} a nuclear medicine scan that shows whether the gallbladder is draining, used when the diagnosis is unclear
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} may be used if the ultrasound is inconclusive or complications are suspected
    \item \textbf{MRCP:} if a stone in the main bile duct is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Acute cholecystitis is usually treated in hospital, and removal of the gallbladder is the definitive cure.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fluids and pain relief:} given intravenously while fasting
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} for the infection that often accompanies the inflammation
    \item \textbf{Laparoscopic cholecystectomy:} keyhole removal of the gallbladder, usually performed during the same admission or shortly afterwards
    \item \textbf{Open surgery:} used in some complicated cases
    \item \textbf{Gallbladder drainage:} a tube may be placed through the skin to drain a severely infected gallbladder in people too unwell for immediate surgery
    \item \textbf{ERCP:} if a bile duct stone is also present, it may be cleared before the gallbladder is removed
    \item \textbf{Delayed surgery:} for mild attacks, surgery is sometimes planned weeks later once the inflammation has settled
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Without prompt treatment, cholecystitis can lead to serious complications.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gallbladder perforation:} the inflamed wall bursts, spilling bile into the abdomen
    \item \textbf{Abscess} formation around the gallbladder
    \item \textbf{Gangrene of the gallbladder} (necrosis) due to blocked blood supply
    \item \textbf{Cholangitis:} infection spreading to the bile ducts -- a life-threatening emergency
    \item \textbf{Bile peritonitis:} inflammation of the lining of the abdominal cavity
    \item \textbf{Fistula:} an abnormal channel forming between the gallbladder and the bowel
    \item \textbf{Sepsis and septic shock} in severe or delayed cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With early treatment, most people with cholecystitis recover completely. The inflammation usually settles with fluids and antibiotics, and once the gallbladder is removed the problem is cured for most people. Recovery from keyhole surgery is quick, with most people home within days and back to full activity within a few weeks. The outlook is less favourable if treatment is delayed and complications such as perforation, cholangitis, or sepsis develop. People treated without surgery have a high chance of further attacks, so surgery is recommended in those fit for it.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek medical care promptly for gallbladder pain so attacks are treated before complications develop
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and avoid crash dieting, which can encourage gallstones
    \item Eat a diet rich in fibre, fruit, and vegetables, with a moderate amount of fat
    \item Stay physically active
    \item Stop smoking and limit alcohol
    \item After surgery, return to normal eating gradually and follow the surgeon's activity advice
    \item Attend follow-up appointments, including any planned surgery
\end{itemize}
