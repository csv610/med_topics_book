\chapter{Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)}
\index{Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A deep vein thrombosis (DVT) is a blood clot that forms in a deep vein, most commonly in the leg. The clot can partially or completely block the vein, causing pain, swelling, and redness. The most serious risk of a DVT is that part of the clot can break away and travel to the lungs, causing a pulmonary embolism, which can be life-threatening. DVT is a form of venous thromboembolism and is both preventable and highly treatable. This chapter covers the risk factors, symptoms, diagnosis, and treatment of DVT.

\section*{Epidemiology}
DVT affects approximately 1--2 people per 1,000 each year, and the incidence rises steeply with age. In many countries, thousands of people are hospitalised with venous thromboembolism annually, and it is a common and preventable cause of in-hospital death. Risk is increased by surgery, prolonged immobility, cancer, pregnancy, and the combined oral contraceptive pill or hormone therapy. Around one-third of people who have a DVT develop post-thrombotic syndrome with chronic leg swelling and discomfort, and recurrent clots occur in a significant minority.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Virchow's triad:} blood clots form through a combination of sluggish blood flow (stasis), damage to the vein wall, and changes that make the blood more likely to clot (hypercoagulability)
    \item \textbf{Immobility:} long flights, bed rest, or leg paralysis slow blood flow in the deep veins
    \item \textbf{Surgery and trauma:} injury and major operations, especially hip and knee surgery, provoke clotting
    \item \textbf{Hormonal factors:} the combined oral contraceptive pill, hormone replacement therapy, and pregnancy increase clotting tendency
    \item \textbf{Cancer:} malignancy activates the clotting system
    \item \textbf{Inherited disorders:} conditions such as factor V Leiden and deficiencies of protein C, protein S, or antithrombin
    \item \textbf{Previous DVT:} a personal or family history increases risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Swelling of one leg or arm, often the calf or thigh
    \item Pain, tenderness, or a feeling of heaviness in the affected limb
    \item Redness and increased warmth of the skin over the clot
    \item Dilated surface veins
    \item Symptoms may be mild or absent, especially in smaller clots
    \item Features of pulmonary embolism: sudden breathlessness, chest pain, cough, coughing up blood, or collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Muscle strain, tear, or haematoma
    \item Superficial thrombophlebitis, a clot in a vein near the skin surface
    \item Baker's cyst, a fluid-filled cyst behind the knee that can mimic a DVT
    \item Cellulitis or other skin infections
    \item Lymphoedema, chronic leg swelling from lymphatic problems
    \item Varicose veins and chronic venous insufficiency
    \item Heart, liver, or kidney failure causing bilateral leg swelling
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any sudden, unexplained swelling or pain in one leg warrants urgent assessment, because DVT cannot be reliably diagnosed by examination alone. People at high risk should be especially vigilant. Symptoms of a pulmonary embolism require immediate emergency care.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden breathlessness, chest pain, coughing up blood, or collapse (possible pulmonary embolism)
    \item Severe leg swelling with pale or blue discolouration and severe pain (possible massive clot)
    \item Fainting or signs of shock
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical probability assessment:} validated scores such as the Wells score estimate the likelihood of DVT
    \item \textbf{D-dimer blood test:} a sensitive but non-specific marker of clot breakdown; a negative result in a low-risk person helps exclude DVT
    \item \textbf{Venous ultrasound (duplex):} the main diagnostic test, showing the clot and whether veins compress normally
    \item \textbf{CT pulmonary angiography:} used when pulmonary embolism is suspected
    \item \textbf{Further tests:} blood counts, coagulation studies, and testing for inherited clotting disorders in younger patients or those with recurrent clots
    \item \textbf{Imaging for cancer:} age-appropriate screening where unprovoked DVT raises the possibility of underlying malignancy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anticoagulant medicines:} the cornerstone of treatment, preventing clot extension and new clots; options include direct oral anticoagulants such as rivaroxaban and apixaban, or warfarin
    \item \textbf{Initial treatment:} anticoagulation is started promptly, sometimes with low-molecular-weight heparin injections or fondaparinux
    \item \textbf{Duration:} treatment typically continues for 3--6 months, and longer for unprovoked or recurrent clots
    \item \textbf{Compression stockings:} reduce swelling and the risk of post-thrombotic syndrome
    \item \textbf{Mobilisation:} walking and normal activity are encouraged once anticoagulation is started
    \item \textbf{Thrombolysis or surgery:} reserved for massive clots causing severe symptoms or threatening the limb
    \item \textbf{Inferior vena cava filter:} considered when anticoagulation is not possible
    \item \textbf{Management of provoking factors:} addressing oral contraceptive use, cancer, or other triggers
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pulmonary embolism, which can be fatal
    \item Post-thrombotic syndrome with chronic pain, swelling, and skin changes
    \item Recurrent DVT
    \item Bleeding complications from anticoagulant therapy
    \item Rarely, phlegmasia, a massive clot that threatens the limb
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt anticoagulant treatment, DVT resolves in most people without serious complications, and the risk of pulmonary embolism falls dramatically within days. The risk of recurrence is low once the provoking factor is removed, but higher for unprovoked clots. Post-thrombotic syndrome affects a substantial minority and is reduced by compression stockings and mobilisation. Overall, the outlook for DVT is good with modern treatment, and prevention measures for high-risk situations significantly reduce its occurrence.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Move regularly on long journeys: walk, stretch, and exercise calf muscles; consider compression stockings and adequate hydration
    \item Mobilise early after surgery and follow hospital prevention protocols, including blood-thinning injections where prescribed
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and avoid smoking
    \item Discuss the risks of the combined oral contraceptive pill or hormone therapy with a doctor, especially if you have other risk factors
    \item Know the symptoms and seek urgent assessment for one-sided leg swelling or pain
    \item Take anticoagulants exactly as prescribed and attend monitoring appointments
\end{itemize}
