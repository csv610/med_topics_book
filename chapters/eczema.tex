\chapter{Eczema}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Eczema, also called atopic dermatitis, is a chronic inflammatory skin condition that causes dry, itchy, red, and sometimes weeping skin. It is the most common form of dermatitis and often begins in infancy or early childhood, although it can start at any age. The condition runs a relapsing course, with flare-ups triggered by irritants, allergens, heat, stress, and infections. Eczema is not contagious, and while there is no cure, most people achieve good control with moisturising, trigger avoidance, and appropriate treatment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Eczema is one of the most common chronic conditions of childhood, affecting up to one in five children in Australia and many adults. It is more common in people with a family history of eczema, asthma, or hay fever, and these conditions often occur together, reflecting a shared tendency to allergy. Most children with eczema improve with age, although a substantial proportion continue to have symptoms into adulthood.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Eczema results from a combination of an impaired skin barrier and an overactive immune response.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Skin barrier defect:} a genetic difference in the skin protein filaggrin makes the skin dry, leaky, and vulnerable to irritants
    \item \textbf{Immune response:} the immune system overreacts to triggers, causing inflammation and itching
    \item \textbf{Triggers:} soaps and detergents, wool and synthetic fabrics, heat and sweating, dust mites, pollen, pet dander, stress, and some foods
    \item \textbf{Infection:} broken skin becomes infected with bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus, worsening the eczema
    \item \textbf{The itch-scratch cycle:} itching leads to scratching, which damages the skin and causes more itching
    \item \textbf{Allergic tendency:} eczema commonly coexists with asthma, hay fever, and food allergy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Eczema appears differently at different ages and on different parts of the body.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Intense itching, often worst at night
    \item Dry, rough, and scaly skin
    \item Red, inflamed patches that may weep or crust when severe
    \item In infants: rash on the face, scalp, and outer surfaces of the arms and legs
    \item In older children and adults: rash in the skin creases, such as the inside of the elbows and behind the knees, and on the hands and neck
    \item Thickened, leathery skin from long-term scratching (lichenification)
    \item Sleep disturbance from itching
    \item Flare-ups and remissions over time
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other skin conditions can resemble eczema.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seborrhoeic dermatitis, which affects the scalp and face with greasy scales
    \item Contact dermatitis, caused by a specific irritant or allergen
    \item Psoriasis, with well-defined scaly plaques
    \item Scabies, with intense itching and burrows
    \item Fungal infections, such as tinea
    \item Ichthyosis and other dry skin conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a GP if eczema is severe, widespread, not responding to moisturisers, or affecting sleep and daily life. Seek review if the skin becomes infected, with weeping, crusting, spreading redness, or fever.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe spreading redness with fever, which may indicate a serious skin infection
    \item Eczema with signs of a widespread herpes infection, including clusters of small blisters and fever
    \item Difficulty breathing, which may occur with a severe allergic reaction
    \item Signs of a very unwell child, including lethargy or poor feeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is usually clinical.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the pattern of the rash, itch, and triggers
    \item \textbf{Allergy testing:} sometimes used to identify food or environmental triggers, guided by the history
    \item \textbf{Skin swabs:} to identify bacterial infection when the skin is weeping or crusted
    \item \textbf{Skin biopsy:} rarely needed, for atypical cases
    \item \textbf{Assessment of impact:} checking sleep, school, and quality of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is stepped and built around skincare and trigger control.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Emollients:} regular, generous moisturising is the foundation of treatment, applied daily even when the skin is clear
    \item \textbf{Topical corticosteroids:} creams and ointments applied to inflamed areas during flare-ups, in the lowest effective strength
    \item \textbf{Other topical treatments:} calcineurin inhibitors for sensitive areas such as the face
    \item \textbf{Trigger avoidance:} gentle cleansers, soft fabrics, and identification of specific triggers
    \item \textbf{Managing infection:} antibiotics for bacterial infection when present
    \item \textbf{Antihistamines:} can help with itch and sleep
    \item \textbf{Advanced treatments:} for severe eczema, phototherapy, immunosuppressant medicines, or the newer biologic drugs
    \item \textbf{Education:} understanding the condition and how to treat flare-ups early
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bacterial skin infections, including impetigo
    \item Eczema herpeticum, a serious infection with the herpes virus
    \item Sleep disturbance and fatigue
    \item Itch-scratch damage, including thickened and pigmented skin
    \item Psychological effects, including embarrassment, low self-esteem, and stress
    \item Effects on school, sport, and social life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Eczema is a chronic condition, but the outlook is good with consistent management. Most children improve substantially as they grow, and many outgrow it or have only mild symptoms. Flare-ups are common and should be treated early and vigorously. Newer treatments have transformed outcomes for people with severe eczema that does not respond to conventional therapy.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Moisturise daily, especially after bathing
    \item Use lukewarm water and soap-free cleansers
    \item Pat the skin dry and apply moisturiser within minutes
    \item Wear soft, breathable fabrics and avoid wool against the skin
    \item Keep nails short and consider cotton gloves at night
    \item Use treatments early during flare-ups
    \item Manage stress and seek support if eczema affects your wellbeing
\end{itemize}
