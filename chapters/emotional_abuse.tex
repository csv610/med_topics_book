\chapter{Emotional Abuse}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Emotional abuse, also called psychological abuse, is a pattern of behaviour by one person that controls, belittles, humiliates, or frightens another. It can occur in intimate relationships, within families, in the workplace, and in care settings, and it affects children and adults alike. Emotional abuse often goes unrecognised because it leaves no bruises, but its effects on mental and physical health are profound and long-lasting. It is a form of family violence, and it is against the law. This chapter explains the signs of emotional abuse, its effects, and how to get help.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Emotional abuse is common. A significant proportion of Australian women and men report having experienced emotional abuse from a partner, and many more experience it in childhood or in other settings. It frequently occurs alongside other forms of abuse, including physical and sexual abuse. Emotional abuse is under-reported because it is hard to identify, is often dismissed or minimised, and victims may not recognise that they are being abused.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Emotional abuse arises from the abuser's need for power and control over another person.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Control:} the abuser controls the victim's movements, finances, friendships, and decisions
    \item \textbf{Isolation:} the victim is cut off from family and friends, increasing dependence
    \item \textbf{Belittling:} constant criticism, name-calling, and humiliation damage self-esteem
    \item \textbf{Fear:} threats, intimidation, and unpredictable anger create constant anxiety
    \item \textbf{Gaslighting:} the abuser denies or distorts reality, making the victim doubt their own mind
    \item \textbf{Child abuse:} emotional abuse of children includes rejection, terrorising, and withholding love
    \item \textbf{Effects:} chronic stress affects the brain and body, causing anxiety, depression, and physical illness, and in children, affecting development
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The signs of emotional abuse can be subtle, especially from outside.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Constant criticism, insults, and put-downs
    \item Controlling behaviour over money, time, and relationships
    \item Isolation from family and friends
    \item Threats, intimidation, and outbursts of anger
    \item Blaming the victim for the abuse
    \item Gaslighting and denial of the abuse
    \item In victims: low self-esteem, anxiety, depression, and fear
    \item Physical symptoms including headaches, sleep problems, and fatigue
    \item In children: withdrawal, behaviour changes, and developmental regression
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Emotional abuse may be confused with other difficulties.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ordinary relationship conflict, which involves both partners, rather than one controlling the other
    \item Cultural or communication differences
    \item Mental illness, in either partner, which may or may not involve abuse
    \item Stress and life pressures, which do not excuse abuse
    \item In children: behaviour problems or mental health conditions that result from, rather than cause, the abuse
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who recognises the signs of emotional abuse, in themselves or someone they know, should seek help. GPs can provide support, refer to counselling and specialist services, and assist with safety planning. In an emergency, call the police.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Physical violence or threats of violence
    \item Thoughts of suicide or self-harm
    \item A child in immediate danger
    \item A situation where someone is being seriously harmed or threatened
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
There is no medical test for emotional abuse; recognition is the first step.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Recognition:} learning the signs and trusting that abuse is real and serious, even without physical evidence
    \item \textbf{Health assessment:} a GP can assess the effects on physical and mental health
    \item \textbf{Children:} if emotional abuse of a child is suspected, professionals have a duty to report concerns to child protection services
    \item \textbf{Safety assessment:} services can help assess risk and plan for safety
    \item \textbf{Documentation:} keeping a diary of incidents can be useful
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Recovery from emotional abuse is possible with support.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Safety planning:} plans for leaving, for protecting children, and for emergencies
    \item \textbf{Counselling:} therapy for the effects of abuse, including trauma-focused approaches
    \item \textbf{Legal protection:} apprehended violence orders and family law remedies
    \item \textbf{Support services:} specialist domestic and family violence services offer advocacy, housing, and legal help
    \item \textbf{Financial support:} help with money, housing, and employment
    \item \textbf{Support for children:} counselling and child-focused programs
    \item \textbf{Abuser accountability:} perpetrator programs, which can reduce reoffending
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder
    \item Low self-esteem and long-term psychological damage
    \item Physical illness from chronic stress
    \item Effects on children's development and mental health
    \item Social isolation and financial dependence
    \item Progression to physical violence
    \item Suicide risk, which is elevated in victims of abuse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
People can and do recover from emotional abuse. Safety, support, and therapy allow victims to rebuild their self-esteem, health, and lives. Children who are removed from abusive environments and given support can develop well. The outcomes are best when abuse is recognised early and victims are connected with specialist services.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Learn the signs of emotional abuse and trust your instincts
    \item Keep in touch with friends and family; isolation enables abuse
    \item Keep important documents, money, and a bag ready if planning to leave
    \item Seek counselling for your own wellbeing
    \item If you know someone being abused, stay in contact and offer practical help
    \item Teach children about respect, boundaries, and speaking up
    \item Call a support line for confidential advice
\end{itemize}
