\chapter{Flexible Cystoscopy (Female)}
\index{Flexible Cystoscopy (Female)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A flexible cystoscopy is a procedure in which a thin, bendable telescope is passed through the urethra into the bladder to examine the bladder lining and the urethra. In women, it is used to investigate symptoms such as blood in the urine, recurrent urinary tract infections, bladder pain, or problems passing urine, and to check the bladder after treatment for bladder disease. The flexible instrument makes the procedure more comfortable than older rigid versions, and in women it is usually quick, lasting only a few minutes, and performed in an outpatient or day-surgery setting, often without any anaesthetic. This chapter explains why a flexible cystoscopy may be needed, what happens during the procedure, and what to expect afterwards.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cystoscopy is one of the most common procedures in urology, and flexible cystoscopy is the standard way of examining the bladder. It is frequently performed to investigate haematuria (blood in the urine), which affects around 1 in 10 adults at some point, and to follow up bladder cancer, which is among the most common cancers and recurs frequently. Women undergo flexible cystoscopy for recurrent urinary tract infections, interstitial cystitis, and urethral problems. The procedure is safe and well tolerated: serious complications such as infection, bleeding, and bladder injury are uncommon, and most women return to normal activities the same day.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The procedure is diagnostic and therapeutic in several ways.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visualisation:} the flexible scope carries a light and camera, allowing the urologist to inspect the urethra and the entire bladder lining in detail
    \item \textbf{Diagnosis:} tumours, stones, inflammation, diverticula, and other abnormalities are seen directly
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} small tissue samples can be taken through the scope when abnormal areas are found
    \item \textbf{The female urethra:} short and straight, which makes the procedure quicker and more comfortable than in men
    \item \textbf{Lubrication and anaesthetic gel:} numbing gel is used to make passage through the urethra more comfortable
    \item \textbf{Filling the bladder:} sterile water or saline is instilled to expand the bladder so its lining can be seen
    \item \textbf{Comfort during the test:} in women, flexible cystoscopy is often performed without anaesthetic, with only topical gel
    \item \textbf{Why symptoms occur:} bladder tumours and stones can cause blood and pain; infection causes inflammation; and the test finds these causes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features are those of the procedure itself and of the recovery.
\begin{itemize}
    \item The procedure takes about 5--10 minutes in an outpatient clinic or day unit
    \item You lie on your back with knees bent and apart, and the scope is gently passed into the bladder
    \item A feeling of needing to pass urine, mild pressure, or discomfort during the test
    \item Local anaesthetic gel numbs the urethra, reducing discomfort
    \item Results are usually discussed immediately or soon after, with biopsy results later
    \item Afterwards: mild burning on passing urine for a day or so, and occasionally a little blood in the urine
    \item Most women return to normal activities immediately or the next day
    \item Symptoms that prompted the test, such as blood in the urine, guide the diagnosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The test is performed to distinguish the causes of urinary symptoms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bladder cancer and other bladder tumours
    \item Urinary tract infection and inflammation
    \item Bladder stones
    \item Interstitial cystitis (bladder pain syndrome)
    \item Urethral stricture, polyps, and other urethral conditions
    \item Overactive bladder and other functional bladder problems
    \item The cause of blood in the urine, which can also lie in the kidneys, requiring additional imaging
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with blood in the urine, persistent bladder symptoms, recurrent infections, or pain should see a doctor, who will arrange investigations. Flexible cystoscopy is an outpatient procedure, but the symptoms that prompt it can be serious and should not be ignored.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The inability to pass urine after the procedure
    \item Heavy bleeding, large clots, or bleeding that continues beyond a day or two
    \item Fever, chills, or severe pain, which may indicate infection
    \item Severe pain or symptoms of a punctured bladder (rare), including severe lower abdominal pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urine tests:} dipstick and microscopy for blood, infection, and abnormal cells
    \item \textbf{Urine culture:} to identify infection and guide antibiotics
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} ultrasound, CT urogram, or X-rays to examine the kidneys and ureters, since blood can come from anywhere in the urinary tract
    \item \textbf{The cystoscopy itself:} the definitive examination of the bladder lining, with biopsy of any suspicious area
    \item \textbf{Biopsy and histology:} tissue sent for pathology when tumours are seen
    \item \textbf{Follow-up tests:} repeated cystoscopy at intervals when bladder cancer is treated, because it can recur
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Before the procedure:} no special preparation is usually needed; the bladder is emptied before the test, and the doctor will advise about medicines and blood thinners
    \item \textbf{During the procedure:} local anaesthetic gel, gentle technique, and clear communication; you can ask to pause or stop at any time
    \item \textbf{After the procedure:} drink extra fluids to dilute the urine and ease any burning
    \item \textbf{Pain and burning:} usually settles within 24 hours and can be eased with paracetamol
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} sometimes prescribed after the procedure or for a detected infection
    \item \textbf{Treatment of findings:} small bladder tumours may be removed later under anaesthetic; infections are treated with antibiotics; stones may require further treatment
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} results discussed, and a plan made for monitoring or treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A urinary tract infection, affecting a small proportion of patients, with burning, frequency, and sometimes fever
    \item Blood in the urine for a day or two, usually minor
    \item Temporary difficulty passing urine
    \item Rarely, injury to the urethra or bladder
    \item Discomfort, which is usually mild
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Flexible cystoscopy is a safe, quick, and well-tolerated procedure, and most women have no significant side effects and return to normal activities immediately. Its value lies in diagnosis: it finds bladder tumours early, when treatment is most effective, clarifies the cause of blood and recurrent infection, and provides reassurance when the bladder is normal. For women with bladder cancer, regular surveillance cystoscopy is a key part of staying ahead of recurrence. The benefits of an accurate diagnosis far outweigh the brief discomfort of the test.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ask the doctor to explain the test, what it is for, and what it feels like before giving consent
    \item Empty the bladder before the procedure as instructed
    \item Tell the doctor about all medicines, including blood thinners
    \item Drink plenty of fluids after the procedure
    \item Report fever, heavy bleeding, inability to pass urine, or severe pain promptly
    \item Attend follow-up appointments and surveillance as scheduled
    \item Do not ignore blood in the urine, and seek assessment rather than waiting to see if it recurs
\end{itemize}
