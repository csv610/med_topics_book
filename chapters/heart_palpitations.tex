\chapter{Heart Palpitations}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Heart palpitations are the awareness of an abnormal or unusually strong heartbeat: a racing, fluttering, pounding, skipping, or "flip-flopping" sensation in the chest, throat, or neck. Palpitations are extremely common and are usually benign, triggered by stress, caffeine, alcohol, exercise, or hormonal changes. However, they can also be a symptom of a heart rhythm disorder (arrhythmia), including atrial fibrillation and more serious conditions. The task of assessment is to determine whether palpitations are harmless or reflect an arrhythmia that needs treatment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Most people experience palpitations at some point in life, and they are one of the most common reasons for visiting a GP and for referral to a cardiologist. They occur at all ages and are more frequently reported by women. Most palpitations are benign, but a significant minority reflect arrhythmias, particularly atrial fibrillation, which affects around 2--3\% of adults and increases with age. Serious arrhythmias are rare in young, healthy people but must be excluded, especially when palpitations occur with fainting or a family history of sudden death.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Benign triggers:} stress, anxiety, panic, caffeine, alcohol, nicotine, recreational drugs, and some medications
    \item \textbf{Hormonal factors:} pregnancy, the menstrual cycle, and menopause
    \item \textbf{Metabolic causes:} anaemia, thyroid overactivity, fever, dehydration, and low blood sugar
    \item \textbf{Heart conditions:} ectopic beats, atrial fibrillation and flutter, supraventricular tachycardia, and ventricular arrhythmias
    \item \textbf{Structural heart disease:} valve disease, cardiomyopathy, and previous heart attack predispose to arrhythmias
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} ectopic electrical impulses or re-entry circuits within the heart generate extra or rapid beats; the perceived sensation reflects the force and rhythm of contraction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A sensation of the heart racing, pounding, fluttering, or skipping a beat
    \item Symptoms may last seconds or minutes, or be sustained for hours
    \item Palpitations at rest, during exertion, or related to specific triggers
    \item Accompanying symptoms: light-headedness, breathlessness, chest discomfort, or sweating
    \item Fainting or near-fainting with palpitations is a red flag for a significant arrhythmia
    \item Some arrhythmias are entirely asymptomatic and detected incidentally
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ectopic beats:} benign premature beats are the commonest cause of the sensation of a missed or extra beat
    \item \textbf{Atrial fibrillation:} irregularly irregular palpitations, often persistent
    \item \textbf{Supraventricular tachycardia:} sudden-onset rapid regular palpitations
    \item \textbf{Ventricular arrhythmias:} rare but serious; more likely with structural heart disease
    \item \textbf{Anxiety and panic attacks:} palpitations with other anxiety symptoms
    \item \textbf{Hyperthyroidism, anaemia, and stimulant use:} systemic causes of a fast heartbeat
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with new, frequent, or concerning palpitations should be assessed by a doctor, who will take a careful history, examine the heart, and usually arrange an ECG. Palpitations that occur with fainting, chest pain, or breathlessness require urgent assessment.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Palpitations with fainting, collapse, or near-fainting
    \item Palpitations with chest pain, pressure, or severe breathlessness
    \item Palpitations that are sustained and rapid, especially with dizziness
    \item Palpitations after a heart attack or in someone with known heart disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} details of onset, triggers, duration, associated symptoms, and family history are the most important diagnostic tool
    \item \textbf{Electrocardiogram:} captures the rhythm at rest; abnormal in some, normal in many with intermittent symptoms
    \item \textbf{Holter monitor:} continuous ECG recording over 24--48 hours to capture episodes as they occur
    \item \textbf{Event recorder and implantable loop recorder:} longer-term monitoring for infrequent symptoms
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} thyroid function, iron studies, electrolytes, and full blood count
    \item \textbf{Echocardiogram:} assesses heart structure and function when an arrhythmia or heart disease is suspected
    \item \textbf{Exercise testing:} for palpitations provoked by exertion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reassurance:} most palpitations are benign, and explaining this is often the main treatment
    \item \textbf{Trigger avoidance:} reducing caffeine, alcohol, stimulants, and stress
    \item \textbf{Treating underlying causes:} thyroid disease, anaemia, and other conditions
    \item \textbf{Rate and rhythm control:} medicines for atrial fibrillation and other arrhythmias
    \item \textbf{Cardioversion:} restoring normal rhythm with electric shock or medication
    \item \textbf{Catheter ablation:} curative treatment for many tachycardias by destroying the abnormal electrical pathway
    \item \textbf{Implantable devices:} pacemakers and defibrillators for slow rhythms and high-risk arrhythmias
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fainting and injuries from falls caused by arrhythmias
    \item Atrial fibrillation increases the risk of stroke fivefold if untreated
    \item Sustained fast arrhythmias can cause heart failure over time
    \item Ventricular arrhythmias can cause sudden cardiac death, particularly with structural heart disease
    \item Anxiety and health-related fear can persist even when palpitations are benign
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For the majority of people, palpitations are harmless and resolve with reassurance and trigger management. Where an arrhythmia is found, the outlook is generally good: most tachycardias can be controlled or cured with medication or ablation, and atrial fibrillation is managed effectively with treatment to control rhythm or rate and prevent stroke. The most important determinant of prognosis is the presence of structural heart disease, which is why assessment is essential before a benign explanation is assumed.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reduce caffeine, alcohol, and stimulant use and stop smoking
    \item Manage stress and anxiety with relaxation, mindfulness, and adequate sleep
    \item Stay well hydrated and avoid excessive fatigue
    \item Keep a diary of episodes, including triggers and associated symptoms, to share with your doctor
    \item Attend follow-up and monitoring as recommended
    \item Learn to check your pulse and seek advice about warning signs
    \item Ask about any family history of heart conditions or sudden death
\end{itemize}
