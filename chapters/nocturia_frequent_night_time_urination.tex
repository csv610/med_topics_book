\chapter{Nocturia (frequent night-time urination)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Nocturia is defined as the need for an individual to wake up one or more times during the night to void, with each urination preceded and followed by sleep. It is a highly prevalent and clinically significant lower urinary tract symptom (LUTS) that disrupts sleep architecture, impairs daytime functioning, and reduces overall quality of life. Nocturia is not a single disease entity but rather a multi-system symptom that can arise from urological, renal, cardiovascular, endocrine, or primary sleep disorders. Clinically, it is categorised into nocturnal polyuria (excessive urine production during sleep), global polyuria (excessive urine production over 24 hours), functional or structural reduction in bladder capacity, or a combination of these factors. 

\section*{Epidemiology}
The prevalence of nocturia increases progressively with age, affecting both men and women, though gender distributions vary. In younger cohorts (under 30 years), the symptom is slightly more common in women, often associated with pregnancy, childbirth, or recurrent urinary tract infections. In individuals aged 50 years and older, the prevalence rises sharply, with up to 60\% of men and women reporting at least two nocturnal voids per night. In Australia, nocturia is recognized as a major public health concern in the ageing population, representing a leading cause of sleep fragmentation and a significant independent risk factor for nocturnal falls, hip fractures, and institutionalisation in residential care.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The underlying mechanisms of nocturia are diverse, categorised by physiological abnormalities in fluid balance, bladder function, or sleep regulation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nocturnal polyuria:} Defined as a nocturnal urine output exceeding 20\% (in young adults) to 33\% (in older adults) of the total 24-hour urine volume. This is frequently driven by a loss of the normal circadian rhythm of arginine vasopressin (AVP) secretion, or by the recumbent redistribution of peripheral oedema associated with congestive heart failure or venous insufficiency.
    \item \textbf{Global polyuria:} Characterised by a 24-hour urine output exceeding 40 mL/kg. This is typically caused by poorly controlled diabetes mellitus (osmotic diuresis), diabetes insipidus (impaired antidiuretic hormone action), or primary polydipsia.
    \item \textbf{Reduced bladder capacity:} A reduction in the volume of urine the bladder can hold, which may be structural (due to benign prostatic hyperplasia, bladder tumours, or radiation cystitis) or functional (due to overactive bladder, neurogenic detrusor overactivity, or urinary tract infections).
    \item \textbf{Sleep disorders:} Conditions like obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) or insomnia. In OSA, repetitive upper airway collapse causes negative intrathoracic pressure, stretching the cardiac atria and stimulating the release of atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP), which promotes renal sodium and water excretion.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological and lifestyle factors:} Consuming caffeine, alcohol, or large volumes of fluid close to bedtime, and taking medications such as diuretics, calcium channel blockers, or selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Nocturia presents as a sleep interruption due to the urge to urinate, often accompanied by symptoms of the underlying systemic or urological disease:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nocturnal voiding episodes:} Waking up to urinate, where each episode is distinct and separated by periods of sleep.
    \item \textbf{Sleep disturbance:} Chronic fatigue, daytime somnolence, difficulty concentrating, mood irritability, and decreased cognitive performance due to fragmented sleep.
    \item \textbf{Storage and voiding symptoms:} Urinary urgency, frequency, a weak or intermittent urinary stream, hesitancy, and a feeling of incomplete bladder emptying.
    \item \textbf{Urine volume variation:} Large-volume voids indicating a systemic fluid issue versus frequent, small-volume voids indicating bladder storage dysfunction.
    \item \textbf{Systemic signs:} Bilateral lower limb swelling, elevated blood pressure, or symptoms of uncontrolled diabetes such as constant thirst (polydipsia) and dry mouth.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Diagnosing the cause of nocturia requires distinguishing between urological conditions and systemic medical diseases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH):} Causes bladder outlet obstruction in older men, leading to high post-void residual volumes and reduced functional bladder capacity.
    \item \textbf{Overactive bladder (OAB):} Characterised by urinary urgency, often with frequency and urgency incontinence, resulting in frequent, small-volume night-time voids.
    \item \textbf{Congestive heart failure (CHF):} Promotes nocturia through the mobilisation of interstitial fluid accumulated in the lower limbs during the day when the patient lies flat.
    \item \textbf{Diabetes mellitus:} Causes osmotic diuresis due to high blood glucose levels, resulting in constant thirst and excessive urination day and night.
    \item \textbf{Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA):} Disrupts sleep and increases nocturnal urine output via hypoxia-induced cardiac stress and ANP secretion.
    \item \textbf{Urinary tract infection (UTI) or cystitis:} Causes acute, painful urination (dysuria), urgency, and frequency, presenting with small-volume voids.
    \item \textbf{Chronic kidney disease (CKD):} Impairs the kidneys' ability to concentrate urine, leading to increased urine production overnight.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should seek medical evaluation if they consistently wake up two or more times per night to urinate, if sleep disruption is affecting their daytime well-being, or if they experience accompanying urinary symptoms.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Complete inability to pass urine (acute urinary retention) associated with severe lower abdominal pain
    \item Chest pain, severe shortness of breath, or sudden palpitations
    \item High fever, shaking chills, confusion, or severe flank pain, suggesting a serious kidney infection or sepsis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A comprehensive diagnostic evaluation is essential to identify the underlying pathway of nocturia:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Frequency-volume chart (bladder diary):} A detailed record kept for 72 hours documenting fluid intake, the timing and volume of all voids, and sleep times to calculate the nocturnal polyuria index.
    \item \textbf{Urinalysis and microscopy:} To screen for infection, haematuria, glycosuria, and proteinuria.
    \item \textbf{Serum chemistry:} Assessment of renal function (electrolytes, urea, creatinine, eGFR), fasting glucose, or HbA1c to screen for diabetes mellitus.
    \item \textbf{Bladder ultrasound:} Used to measure the post-void residual (PVR) volume to assess for bladder retention.
    \item \textbf{Uroflowmetry:} Evaluates the rate of urine flow to check for bladder outlet obstruction.
    \item \textbf{Polysomnography:} A sleep study indicated if the patient has clinical signs of obstructive sleep apnoea, such as loud snoring, choking during sleep, or witnessed apnoeic events.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is tailored to the specific underlying causes and typically requires a combination of lifestyle, behavioural, and medical therapies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle modifications:} Restricting fluid intake 2 to 4 hours before bedtime, and avoiding bladder irritants such as caffeine and alcohol in the afternoon and evening.
    \item \textbf{Fluid redistribution strategies:} Elevating the legs in the late afternoon or wearing compression stockings to promote venous return and fluid elimination before sleep.
    \item \textbf{Behavioural therapies:} Bladder training exercises to increase functional capacity and pelvic floor muscle training to control urgency.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacotherapy for urological causes:} Alpha-blockers (e.g., tamsulosin) or 5-alpha reductase inhibitors (e.g., dutasteride) for BPH; anticholinergic medications (e.g., solifenacin) or beta-3 agonists (e.g., mirabegron) for overactive bladder.
    \item \textbf{Antidiuretic therapy:} Desmopressin (a synthetic analogue of AVP) taken before bedtime to reduce overnight urine production, requiring close monitoring of serum sodium levels to prevent hyponatremia.
    \item \textbf{Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP):} Resolves nocturia in patients with obstructive sleep apnoea by preventing hypoxia-induced atrial natriuretic peptide release.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, chronic sleep deprivation leading to cognitive impairment, mood disturbances, depression, and anxiety
    \item Increased risk of nocturnal falls and fractures, particularly in older adults with mobility or vision impairments
    \item Cardiorespiratory strain due to disrupted sleep-wake cycles and sympathetic nervous system hyperactivation
    \item Reduced occupational safety, increased risk of motor vehicle accidents, and decreased daytime productivity
    \item Reduced quality of life and secondary sleep disruption for partners or family members
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of nocturia varies depending on the primary cause and the success of targeted therapies. Lifestyle and behavioural modifications, when strictly followed, can significantly reduce nocturia frequency and improve sleep quality for many patients. In cases where nocturia is driven by a single treatable condition, such as BPH, OSA, or diabetes, specific treatments frequently result in excellent symptom improvement. However, in older adults with multifactorial causes, complete cure may be difficult to achieve, and the goal of management is to reduce night-time voiding to a level that minimises sleep disruption and prevents falls.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Manage evening fluid intake:} Restrict water and other fluid intake in the hours leading up to bedtime.
    \item \textbf{Limit bladder irritants:} Avoid caffeine, alcohol, and carbonated drinks in the late afternoon and evening.
    \item \textbf{Double voiding:} Try to urinate, wait a couple of minutes, and then attempt to urinate again before getting into bed.
    \item \textbf{Optimise medication timing:} Take prescribed diuretic medications in the morning or early afternoon to avoid peaking overnight.
    \item \textbf{Elevate legs:} Put your feet up on a footstool or recliner for a few hours in the late afternoon to help clear fluid from your legs before bed.
    \item \textbf{Ensure bedroom safety:} Keep walkways clear of obstacles and install motion-activated night lights to prevent falls during night-time trips to the bathroom.
\end{itemize}
