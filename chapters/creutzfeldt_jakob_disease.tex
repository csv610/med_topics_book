\chapter{Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease}
\index{Creutzfeldt-Jakob Disease}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD) is a rare, rapidly progressive and fatal brain disorder caused by abnormal proteins called prions. It belongs to a group of conditions known as prion diseases or transmissible spongiform encephalopathies. CJD damages the brain, causing rapidly worsening dementia, personality change, movement problems, and loss of coordination, and most people die within a year of symptoms beginning. There is no cure, and treatment is supportive. Most cases are sporadic (arising without a known cause), while a small number are inherited or acquired through medical procedures.

\section*{Epidemiology}
CJD is very rare, affecting roughly one to two people per million per year worldwide, which translates to around ten to twenty new cases a year in many countries. The sporadic form accounts for about 85\% of cases and typically affects people in their sixties and seventies. Inherited forms are rarer and occur in families with gene mutations. Acquired forms include variant CJD (vCJD), linked to exposure to bovine spongiform encephalopathy (mad cow disease) in the 1980s--90s, and rare cases from contaminated medical products. The number of vCJD cases has fallen sharply since measures were introduced.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
CJD is caused by prions, infectious proteins that fold abnormally and trigger normal proteins to misfold in turn.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sporadic CJD:} arises spontaneously, without a known cause, in about 85\% of cases
    \item \textbf{Inherited CJD:} caused by mutations in the prion protein gene, passed down in families
    \item \textbf{Acquired CJD:} transmission through contaminated neurosurgical instruments, human growth hormone, or corneal and dura mater grafts (historic)
    \item \textbf{Variant CJD:} caused by exposure to bovine spongiform encephalopathy, mainly in the UK
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} misfolded prions accumulate in the brain, forming plaques and causing spongy degeneration of brain tissue
    \item \textbf{Progression:} the damage spreads rapidly through the brain, causing progressive neurological loss
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
CJD progresses quickly, with symptoms worsening over weeks to months.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Rapidly progressive dementia with confusion and memory loss
    \item Personality change, apathy, depression, and withdrawal
    \item Ataxia: unsteadiness, poor coordination, and difficulty walking
    \item Myoclonus: sudden, jerky muscle movements
    \item Visual disturbances, including double vision or visual hallucinations
    \item Difficulty speaking and swallowing
    \item Stiffness, weakness, and paralysis in late stages
    \item Rapid decline, with most people becoming bed-bound within months
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Many conditions cause rapidly progressive neurological decline, and CJD is diagnosed by exclusion and specific tests.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Alzheimer's disease and other dementias
    \item Vascular dementia from strokes
    \item Encephalitis from viral or autoimmune causes
    \item Autoimmune encephalitis, which is treatable and must not be missed
    \item Brain tumour or lymphoma
    \item Metabolic and toxic causes of encephalopathy
    \item Progressive supranuclear palsy and other movement disorders
    \item Huntington's disease and other inherited conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with rapidly progressive confusion, memory loss, or neurological symptoms should be assessed promptly by a doctor and referred to a neurologist.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A sudden change in consciousness or a seizure
    \item Sudden weakness, difficulty speaking, or facial drooping (possible stroke)
    \item Difficulty breathing or choking from swallowing problems
    \item A fall with a head injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is made by a neurologist using a combination of tests, and can rarely be confirmed without brain biopsy.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the pattern and speed of decline, family history, and past medical procedures
    \item \textbf{MRI of the brain:} shows characteristic changes in certain brain regions
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture:} analysis of cerebrospinal fluid, including the real-time quaking-induced conversion (RT-QuIC) test, which is highly specific
    \item \textbf{Electroencephalogram (EEG):} shows characteristic periodic complexes in many cases
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} to exclude other causes of rapid cognitive decline
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} for inherited prion disease, with counselling
    \item \textbf{Brain biopsy:} rarely needed, and only when the diagnosis cannot otherwise be made
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
There is no cure, and management focuses on comfort, dignity, and support for the family.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurological care:} specialist management by a neurology team
    \item \textbf{Symptom control:} medicines for myoclonus, agitation, pain, and seizures
    \item \textbf{Palliative care:} comprehensive support for the person and family
    \item \textbf{Swallowing and nutrition:} dietetic input, thickened fluids, and feeding support where needed
    \item \textbf{Therapy support:} physiotherapy and occupational therapy for comfort and positioning
    \item \textbf{Family support:} counselling and respite care
    \item \textbf{Prevention of transmission:} standard precautions in healthcare, and reporting to public health authorities
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pneumonia from aspiration or immobility
    \item Pressure sores
    \item Falls and injuries
    \item Difficulty swallowing, leading to choking and malnutrition
    \item Seizures
    \item Infections in late disease
    \item Severe distress for family members caring for a rapidly declining loved one
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for CJD is poor: the disease is invariably fatal, with most people surviving less than a year from symptom onset, and many considerably less. Sporadic CJD has the fastest course, while inherited forms and variant CJD may progress slightly more slowly. There is no treatment that slows the disease. Palliative care, however, can ensure that people die comfortably and with dignity, and families are supported through bereavement.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Understand that sporadic CJD cannot be prevented and is not related to lifestyle
    \item Follow infection control measures in healthcare settings
    \item Use single-use or appropriately decontaminated neurosurgical instruments
    \item Do not donate blood, organs, or tissue if you have a diagnosis or family risk of prion disease, as advised
    \item Seek genetic counselling if there is a family history of inherited prion disease
    \item Access support services and palliative care early for the person and family
\end{itemize}
