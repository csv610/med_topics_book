\chapter{Open TMJ surgery}
\index{Open TMJ surgery}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Open temporomandibular joint (TMJ) surgery, or arthrotomy, is an invasive surgical procedure performed to treat severe, refractory disorders of the temporomandibular joint that have failed to respond to comprehensive conservative therapies. The TMJ is a complex, bilateral ginglymoarthrodial (sliding hinge) joint that connects the mandible to the temporal bone of the skull, facilitating essential functions such as mastication, speech, and yawning. Open TMJ surgery involves making a cutaneous incision—typically preauricular, submandibular, or postauricular—to directly expose the joint capsule. This direct exposure allows the surgeon to visualize, palpate, and reconstruct the intra-articular components. Procedures performed via an open approach include disc repositioning (meniscopexy), discectomy with or without graft interposition, condylectomy, joint debridement, release of ankylosis, and total joint reconstruction (arthroplasty) using custom-made prostheses. It is reserved for advanced internal derangement, end-stage degenerative joint disease, bony ankylosis, trauma, or neoplasms.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Temporomandibular disorders (TMD) are common, affecting approximately 10--15\% of the adult population, with a marked female-to-male ratio ranging between 4:1 and 8:1. The condition most frequently presents during the reproductive years (ages 20 to 45). However, the vast majority of patients with TMD (over 95\%) are successfully managed with non-surgical interventions such as occlusal splints, physical therapy, pharmacotherapy, or minimally invasive procedures (arthrocentesis and arthroscopy). Open TMJ surgery is indicated in less than 1--2\% of all TMD cases. In many countries, the procedure is performed by oral and maxillofacial surgeons, often in specialized tertiary referral clinics. The incidence of open TMJ arthrotomies has stabilized due to the clinical efficacy of custom-fabricated TMJ replacements for patients with severe condylar resorption or ankylosis.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Advanced TMJ disorders requiring open surgical intervention are multifactorial, resulting from structural, mechanical, and systemic pathways.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Internal derangement:} Chronic, progressive anterior displacement of the fibrocartilaginous articular disc. Over time, the disc loses its elasticity, becomes deformed, perforates, or undergoes complete mechanical displacement, preventing normal condylar translation.
    \item \textbf{Degenerative joint disease:} Severe osteoarthritis causing progressive loss of articular cartilage, subchondral bone remodeling, osteophyte formation, and flattening of the condylar head.
    \item \textbf{Ankylosis:} Fibrous or bony fusion of the mandibular condyle to the glenoid fossa and articular eminence, typically secondary to condylar fractures (especially in paediatric patients) or untreated joint infections.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory arthropathies:} Rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, or ankylosing spondylitis causing autoimmune destruction of the synovial membrane, cartilage, and subchondral bone.
    \item \textbf{Trauma:} Displaced intracapsular or subcondylar fractures that cause mechanical obstruction, severe malocclusion, or are associated with soft-tissue damage.
    \item \textbf{Neoplasia:} Rare benign (e.g., osteoma, osteochondroma) or malignant tumors of the mandibular condyle or temporal bone.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Patients requiring open joint surgery typically present with a long-standing history of severe physical dysfunction and pain.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Persistent, severe pain:} Chronic, localized pain over the TMJ, exacerbated by mandibular function, which often radiates to the ear, temple, cheek, neck, or shoulders.
    \item \textbf{Trismus and restricted mobility:} Significant limitation in mouth opening, with a maximum interincisal opening often restricted to less than 25 mm (normal is 40--50 mm), or severe lateral deviation of the mandible during opening.
    \item \textbf{Joint locking:} Frequent, distressing episodes of the jaw catching or locking, either in the open position (inability to close) or closed position (inability to open).
    \item \textbf{Coarse joint noises:} Loud, grating sounds (crepitus) during jaw movement, signifying bone-on-bone friction due to disc perforation or severe degeneration.
    \item \textbf{Occlusal changes:} A progressive shift in the patient's bite (malocclusion), such as an anterior open bite, due to rapid condylar resorption or joint space collapse.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before undertaking open surgery, clinicians must distinguish intra-articular pathology from extra-articular and myofascial causes of facial pain.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Myofascial pain dysfunction syndrome (MPDS):} Extra-articular pain arising from the muscles of mastication, frequently associated with stress, bruxism (teeth grinding), and muscle spasm.
    \item \textbf{Otologic conditions:} Otitis media, otitis externa, or Eustachian tube dysfunction, which present with ear pain that mimics TMJ dysfunction.
    \item \textbf{Temporal arteritis:} A systemic vasculitis of older adults that presents with temporal headache, jaw claudication, and visual loss, requiring emergency steroid treatment.
    \item \textbf{Neuropathic pain:} Trigeminal neuralgia or atypical facial pain, characterized by sharp, shooting, or burning pain along cranial nerve pathways.
    \item \textbf{Dental infections:} Odontogenic pain (e.g., deep caries, pericoronitis of third molars) that radiates to the jaw and preauricular region.
    \item \textbf{Salivary gland disorders:} Parotitis, sialolithiasis, or parotid gland tumors causing pain and swelling in the preauricular area.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients with chronic jaw pain or clicking should seek an elective referral to an oral and maxillofacial surgeon. However, immediate medical evaluation is necessary for acute locking or postoperative complications.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Inability to breathe or swallow due to severe swelling in the lower face or neck following surgery.
    \item Active, rapid bleeding from the preauricular wound or ear canal.
    \item Sudden weakness or paralysis of the facial muscles (e.g., inability to close the eye or raise the eyebrow on the operated side).
    \item Signs of systemic infection, including high fever, severe chills, and purulent discharge from the wound.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A thorough diagnostic workup is essential to evaluate the structural integrity of both the soft and hard tissues of the joint.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical evaluation:} Detailed charting of mandibular range of motion (opening, protrusion, lateral excursions), palpation of the TMJs and masticatory muscles, and evaluation of occlusal stability.
    \item \textbf{Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):} The gold standard for assessing soft tissue components. It demonstrates disc position relative to the condylar head, disc morphology, ligamentous integrity, and joint effusion.
    \item \textbf{Computed tomography (CT) or Cone Beam CT (CBCT):} The preferred modality for evaluating osseous structures, displaying cortical erosion, subchondral cysts, osteophyte formation, and bony ankylosis.
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic nerve blocks:} Local anaesthetic infiltration into the joint space to confirm that the patient's pain is intra-articular rather than muscular in origin.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Open TMJ surgery comprises a range of surgical techniques tailored to the patient's specific pathology, always followed by structured rehabilitation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Disc repositioning and meniscopexy:} The disc is mobilized and secured back to its normal anatomical position on top of the condyle using sutures or anchors.
    \item \textbf{Discectomy with interpositional grafting:} Removal of a severely damaged, perforated, or necrotic disc, followed by reconstruction of the joint interface using autologous grafts (e.g., temporalis muscle, auricular cartilage) or alloplastic materials.
    \item \textbf{Arthroplasty and condylectomy:} Removal of osteophytes or resection of the diseased condylar head to relieve pain and restore joint space.
    \item \textbf{Total joint replacement:} Resection of the diseased joint and replacement with a custom-designed, patient-specific prosthetic fossa and condyle component (total TMJ reconstruction).
    \item \textbf{Postoperative physical therapy:} Structured, early mobilization exercises (using passive motion devices or active jaw stretching) are critical to prevent joint adhesions and maintain jaw opening.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Due to the complex anatomy surrounding the TMJ, open surgery carries unique anatomical risks.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Facial nerve injury:} Injury to the temporal or zygomatic branches of the facial nerve (CN VII) during dissection, resulting in temporary or permanent forehead paralysis and inability to close the eye.
    \item \textbf{Ear canal injury and hearing impairment:} Proximity of the incision to the external auditory meatus can cause auditory canal stenosis, tympanic membrane perforation, or temporary middle ear fluid accumulation.
    \item \textbf{Malocclusion:} Postoperative changes in dental occlusion (e.g., posterior open bite or deviation), which may require orthodontic or orthognathic correction.
    \item \textbf{Fibrous or bony re-ankylosis:} Recurrent stiffness and tissue fusion, particularly in patients who do not adhere to postoperative physiotherapy.
    \item \textbf{Prosthetic complications:} Component loosening, wear, or foreign-body giant cell reaction, which may necessitate revision surgery.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis after open TMJ surgery is generally favourable for pain reduction and functional improvement, with success rates of 70--80\%. However, patients must understand that the surgery is palliative rather than curative; complete pain eradication is rarely achieved, and the goal is to establish a functional, pain-manageable jaw range of motion. Custom total joint replacements demonstrate excellent, stable, long-term outcomes in patients with end-stage arthritis or severe ankylosis. Patient compliance with post-operative jaw exercises is the single most critical factor in achieving a satisfactory range of motion.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adhere to physical therapy:} Complete the prescribed jaw-stretching and mobilization exercises daily to prevent scarring and adhesions.
    \item \textbf{Follow a soft diet:} Eat a soft or pureed diet for the first 6--12 weeks post-surgery to minimize mechanical load on the healing joint structures.
    \item \textbf{Avoid jaw strain:} Limit extreme jaw opening (e.g., wide yawning, shouting) and avoid chewing hard, sticky, or chewy foods.
    \item \textbf{Manage bruxism:} Use a custom occlusal splint or nightguard to reduce stress on the joint if clenching or grinding teeth at night.
    \item \textbf{Apply localized therapy:} Use warm compresses or ice packs as directed to manage postoperative muscle spasms and swelling.
\end{itemize}
