\chapter{Blood in Stool}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Blood in stool (rectal bleeding) means that blood passes with, or instead of, a bowel motion. The appearance of the blood gives useful clues about where it originates. Bright red blood on the toilet paper or coating the surface of the stool usually comes from the anal canal or lower rectum, while dark, tarry, sticky stool (melaena) suggests bleeding higher in the stomach or small intestine, because the blood has been partially digested as it travels through the gut. Occasionally blood is invisible to the naked eye and is detected only by a screening test for hidden (occult) blood. Rectal bleeding is common and often has a benign cause, but it always warrants assessment, because it can be a sign of a serious condition such as bowel cancer.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bleeding from the back passage is very common. Haemorrhoids (piles) and anal fissures affect a large proportion of adults at some point in their lives, and both can cause bright red bleeding. Diverticular disease becomes more common with age and is a frequent cause of significant lower bowel bleeding. Bowel (colorectal) cancer is one of the most common cancers in developed countries, and its risk rises steeply after age 50, which is why blood in stool is an important symptom and why national screening programmes target this age group. Inflammatory bowel disease typically affects younger adults, and peptic ulcer bleeding is an important cause of melaena at all ages.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Blood enters the stool from any level of the digestive tract. Bleeding low down in the anus or rectum appears fresh and red because little digestion has occurred. Bleeding from higher in the colon is usually mixed with the stool and may be dark red, while bleeding from the stomach or small intestine produces black, tarry melaena once the blood has been digested. Common causes include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Haemorrhoids (piles):} swollen veins in the lower rectum or anus
    \item \textbf{Anal fissure:} a tear in the lining of the anus, often after passing a hard stool
    \item \textbf{Diverticular disease:} small pouches (diverticula) in the colon wall that bleed
    \item \textbf{Bowel polyps and bowel cancer:} abnormal growths that bleed as they enlarge
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory bowel disease:} ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease cause inflamed, bleeding bowel lining
    \item \textbf{Peptic ulcers:} stomach or duodenal ulcers that bleed and cause melaena
    \item \textbf{Angiodysplasia:} abnormal blood vessels in the bowel wall, mainly in older people
    \item \textbf{Infections:} gastroenteritis from bacteria such as \textit{Shigella}, \textit{Salmonella}, or \textit{Campylobacter}
    \item \textbf{Upper gastrointestinal sources:} oesophagitis, gastritis, or oesophageal varices
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bright red blood on the toilet paper, on the stool surface, or in the toilet bowl
    \item Dark red blood mixed through the stool
    \item Black, tarry, sticky stool (melaena)
    \item Pain on passing stool, suggesting an anal fissure
    \item Itching, soreness, or a lump at the anus (haemorrhoids)
    \item Change in bowel habit: looser, more frequent, or narrower stool
    \item Abdominal pain, bloating, or cramps
    \item Unexplained weight loss, fatigue, or pallor (possible anaemia)
    \item Mucus in the stool, common in inflammatory bowel disease
    \item Dizziness, faintness, or rapid pulse with significant blood loss
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Not everything that looks like blood in stool is true bleeding:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Haemorrhoids, anal fissure, and anal skin tags
    \item Bowel cancer and bowel polyps
    \item Inflammatory bowel disease (ulcerative colitis, Crohn's disease)
    \item Diverticular bleeding
    \item Peptic ulcer or gastritis (causing melaena)
    \item Bright red foods that mimic blood, such as beetroot, or red food colouring
    \item Iron supplements, bismuth medicines, or dark foods that turn the stool black
    \item Swallowed blood from a nosebleed or from coughing up blood
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any episode of blood in stool should be reported to a doctor, even if it stops, because it can be the only sign of a serious condition. Seek attention promptly for recurrent or persistent bleeding, melaena, or bleeding accompanied by a change in bowel habit, weight loss, or abdominal pain.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Large amounts of blood from the back passage, or bleeding that does not stop
    \item Fainting, dizziness on standing, or collapse
    \item Vomiting blood along with dark, tarry stool
    \item Severe abdominal pain, fever, or signs of shock (pallor, rapid pulse, confusion)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} amount, colour, and frequency of bleeding; current medicines such as aspirin or anticoagulants; family history of bowel cancer
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} abdominal examination and a digital rectal examination
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count for anaemia, iron studies, and clotting tests where relevant
    \item \textbf{Colonoscopy:} camera examination of the large bowel; polyps can be removed at the same time
    \item \textbf{Gastroscopy:} camera examination of the stomach and upper bowel if melaena is present
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT scan or CT colonography in selected cases
    \item \textbf{Faecal immunochemical test (FIT):} screening test for hidden blood in the stool
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Haemorrhoids:} increased fibre, fluids, and stool softeners; banding or minor procedures if persistent
    \item \textbf{Anal fissure:} soothing creams, laxatives to soften stool, and avoiding straining
    \item \textbf{Diverticular disease:} a high-fibre diet and, for severe bleeding, hospital care
    \item \textbf{Polyps:} removal during colonoscopy prevents progression to cancer
    \item \textbf{Bowel cancer:} surgery, often combined with chemotherapy or radiotherapy
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory bowel disease:} anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive medicines under specialist gastroenterology care
    \item \textbf{Peptic ulcer:} acid-suppressing medicines and eradication of \textit{H. pylori} infection if present
    \item \textbf{Severe bleeding:} hospital admission, intravenous fluids, transfusion, and endoscopic or surgical control of bleeding
    \item \textbf{Medicines review:} aspirin and anticoagulants are adjusted only under medical advice
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Iron deficiency anaemia from slow, ongoing blood loss
    \item Severe blood loss leading to hypovolaemic shock and requiring urgent transfusion
    \item A bowel cancer being missed or diagnosed late if bleeding is dismissed
    \item Complications of investigation, such as bleeding after polypectomy or, rarely, a perforated bowel
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most causes of blood in stool are benign: haemorrhoids and fissures settle with simple measures, and even diverticular bleeding usually stops on its own. Polyps removed at colonoscopy are cured, and bowel cancer detected at an early stage has a good outlook. Persistent bleeding that is investigated thoroughly carries a favourable prognosis overall. The key message is that blood in stool is never normal, and prompt assessment is what makes serious causes treatable.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take part in recommended bowel cancer screening (FIT) for your age group
    \item Eat a high-fibre diet with plenty of fruit, vegetables, and whole grains, and drink enough fluid
    \item Avoid straining on the toilet and prolonged sitting
    \item Stay physically active and maintain a healthy weight
    \item Limit alcohol and avoid smoking
    \item Report any blood in stool to your doctor promptly, especially after age 40 or with a family history of bowel cancer
    \item Keep aspirin and blood-thinning medicines under medical review
\end{itemize}
