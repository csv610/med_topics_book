\chapter{Tonsillitis}
\index{Tonsillitis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Tonsillitis is defined as the acute, recurrent, or chronic inflammation of the palatine tonsils, which are mucosal-associated lymphoid tissues located bilaterally within the tonsillar fossa of the lateral oropharynx. Positioned at the biological gateway of both the respiratory and digestive tracts, these organs are the primary line of defense against inhaled or ingested pathogens. They form part of Waldeyer's tonsillar ring, a ring of lymphoid tissue in the pharynx that also includes the nasopharyngeal tonsils (adenoids), tubal tonsils, and lingual tonsils. The palatine tonsils play an important role in the inductive phase of the mucosal immune system. They sample antigens from the environment via specialized reticular epithelium in the tonsillar crypts, initiating immune responses by presenting antigens to B-lymphocytes and T-lymphocytes. This activation triggers the local production of secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA), which acts to neutralize pathogens before they enter the systemic circulation or deeper mucosal tissues.

Despite their protective immunological function, the tonsils are vulnerable to infection. Tonsillitis is classified clinically into three categories: acute, recurrent, and chronic. Acute tonsillitis is characterized by the sudden onset of sore throat, fever, dysphagia, and tonsillar swelling, with symptoms typically resolving within seven to ten days, though they may persist for up to two weeks. Recurrent tonsillitis is defined by multiple distinct episodes of acute tonsillitis occurring within a specified timeframe, often diagnosed using the Paradise criteria (such as seven episodes in one year, five episodes per year for two consecutive years, or three episodes per year for three consecutive years). Chronic tonsillitis is characterized by persistent, low-grade inflammation of the tonsils lasting longer than three months, often associated with halitosis, the formation of tonsilloliths (tonsil stones), and persistent cervical lymphadenopathy. Tonsillitis is a major healthcare concern, representing a common reason for primary care consultations, antibiotic prescriptions, and surgical interventions.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Tonsillitis is a highly prevalent condition that affects millions of individuals worldwide each year. While it can occur at any age, it is primarily a disease of childhood and young adolescence. The peak incidence of acute tonsillitis occurs in children between the ages of five and fifteen years, corresponding to the period of peak tonsillar immunological activity and high social contact in school environments. Bacterial tonsillitis, particularly cases caused by Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Streptococcus), is concentrated in this school-aged population, accounting for 15\% to 30\% of all childhood sore throat presentations. In contrast, bacterial tonsillitis is less common in adults, representing only 5\% to 15\% of acute pharyngotonsillitis cases, with the remainder being viral.

Viral tonsillitis exhibits a broader age distribution and is the predominant form of the disease in children under the age of five years, for whom streptococcal infections are relatively rare. In temperate climates, tonsillitis displays a distinct seasonal variation, with incidence peaking in the late winter and early spring months. This seasonality aligns with increased indoor crowding and the transmission patterns of common respiratory viruses and Group A Streptococcus.

There is no significant difference in incidence based on gender or ethnicity. However, socioeconomic factors such as household crowding, poor ventilation, and close contact in school or daycare environments are strongly associated with higher transmission rates. Environmental exposures, particularly secondhand cigarette smoke, have been linked to a higher frequency and severity of tonsillitis episodes in children. The economic burden of tonsillitis is significant, involving direct costs for clinical care and medications, as well as indirect costs from lost productivity and school absenteeism.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The etiology of tonsillitis involves a range of viral and bacterial pathogens that colonize the tonsillar tissue and trigger a localized inflammatory response.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral Pathogens}: The majority of all tonsillitis cases (70\% to 80\%) are viral in origin. The most common viral agents include rhinoviruses, coronaviruses, adenoviruses, influenza A and B, parainfluenza viruses, and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV). The Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), the causative agent of infectious mononucleosis, is a notable viral cause that presents with severe exudative tonsillitis, prolonged fever, and splenomegaly. Viral infections occur when inhaled respiratory droplets colonize the epithelial cells of the oropharynx, leading to cytolysis, inflammation, and secondary bacterial susceptibility.
    \item \textbf{Bacterial Pathogens}: Streptococcus pyogenes, a Gram-positive, Group A beta-hemolytic bacterium, is the most common and clinically significant bacterial cause of tonsillitis. Other bacterial pathogens include Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, Moraxella catarrhalis, Staphylococcus aureus, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, and anaerobic bacteria. Bacterial tonsillitis occurs when these organisms colonize the deep tonsillar crypts, multiplying and producing toxins (such as streptolysins and pyrogenic exotoxins) that cause direct mucosal damage and stimulate a strong immune response.
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology of Inflammation}: When pathogens colonize the tonsillar tissue, antigen-presenting cells initiate an immune response. This leads to the release of inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-1, interleukin-6, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha. These chemical messengers cause local vasodilation, increased capillary permeability, and recruitment of neutrophils and lymphocytes to the tonsils. The resulting vascular congestion and fluid accumulation lead to tonsillar hypertrophy and erythema. The collection of dead bacteria, cellular debris, white blood cells, and fibrin within the tonsillar crypts forms the characteristic white or yellow follicular exudate.
    \item \textbf{Host Risk Factors}: Factors increasing susceptibility to tonsillitis include young age, exposure to air pollutants and secondhand tobacco smoke, systemic immunodeficiency, and chronic mouth breathing (which dries the oral mucosa and reduces the protective effects of saliva). Anatomical factors, such as deep, narrow tonsillar crypts, can also predispose individuals to the accumulation of food particles and bacteria, leading to chronic infection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features of tonsillitis vary depending on the age of the patient, the causative pathogen, and the severity of the inflammation. The onset of symptoms is typically acute, presenting with local pharyngeal discomfort and systemic signs of infection.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sore Throat (Pharyngalgia)}: A constant, severe pain in the throat is the primary symptom. The pain is localized to the oropharynx and is exacerbated by swallowing (odynophagia) or speaking.
    \item \textbf{Dysphagia}: Swelling of the tonsils can make swallowing solid foods, liquids, and sometimes saliva difficult. In severe cases, this can lead to decreased oral intake, dehydration, and drooling.
    \item \textbf{Fever and Systemic Symptoms}: Acute tonsillitis is often accompanied by a sudden onset of fever, chills, malaise, headache, generalized muscle aches (myalgia), and fatigue. Fever is generally higher and more abrupt in bacterial tonsillitis.
    \item \textbf{Referred Otalgia}: Pain originating in the tonsils is frequently referred to the ears on the affected side. This referred pain is mediated by the tympanic branch of the glossopharyngeal nerve (cranial nerve IX), which shares sensory pathways with the tonsillar tissue.
    \item \textbf{Tonsillar Erythema and Hypertrophy}: Visual inspection reveals red, swollen tonsils. Tonsillar swelling can be graded from stage 1+ to stage 4+ (where the tonsils meet in the midline, potentially causing upper airway obstruction).
    \item \textbf{Tonsillar Exudate}: White, yellow, or grey patches of pus are often present in the tonsillar crypts. The presence of exudate is a key indicator of inflammation, though it does not reliably distinguish bacterial from viral infection.
    \item \textbf{Cervical Lymphadenopathy}: The anterior cervical lymph nodes, particularly the jugulodigastric nodes located at the angle of the jaw, are typically enlarged, firm, and tender to palpation.
    \item \textbf{Halitosis and Voice Changes}: The accumulation of bacteria and cellular debris in the tonsillar crypts causes bad breath. Severe swelling of the tonsils and soft palate can produce a muffled, ``hot potato'' voice.
    \item \textbf{Pediatric Presentations}: In young children, tonsillitis may present as irritability, poor feeding, drooling, vomiting, and abdominal pain (associated with mesenteric adenitis).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When evaluating a patient with suspected tonsillitis, it is important to consider alternative diagnoses that present with sore throat, pharyngeal inflammation, or airway obstruction.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infectious Mononucleosis}: Caused by the Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), this condition features severe exudative tonsillitis, high fever, and sore throat. It is differentiated from bacterial tonsillitis by severe fatigue, generalized lymphadenopathy (affecting the posterior cervical nodes), splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, and the development of a maculopapular rash if treated with amoxicillin.
    \item \textbf{Peritonsillar Abscess (Quinsy)}: A suppurative complication of tonsillitis characterized by a collection of pus in the space between the tonsillar capsule and the pharyngeal muscles. Symptoms include severe unilateral throat pain, deviation of the uvula to the contralateral side, drooling, and trismus (difficulty opening the mouth).
    \item \textbf{Acute Epiglottitis}: A life-threatening inflammation of the epiglottis, typically caused by Haemophilus influenzae type b in unvaccinated individuals. Symptoms include sudden, severe sore throat, high fever, drooling, dysphagia, and stridor, with a normal-appearing oropharynx. Direct throat examination is contraindicated as it can cause complete airway occlusion.
    \item \textbf{Retropharyngeal and Parapharyngeal Abscesses}: Deep neck space infections that cause severe throat and neck pain, neck stiffness (torticollis), dysphagia, stridor, and respiratory distress. These require urgent diagnostic imaging and surgical evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Pharyngeal Diphtheria}: Caused by Corynebacterium diphtheriae, this rare condition is characterized by a low-grade fever, sore throat, and a thick, grey, adherent pseudomembrane on the tonsils and pharynx that bleeds when removed.
    \item \textbf{Herpangina and Herpetic Gingivostomatitis}: Viral infections caused by Coxsackievirus and herpes simplex virus, respectively. They present with painful vesicular and ulcerative lesions on the soft palate, tonsillar pillars, and oral mucosa.
    \item \textbf{Vincent's Angina}: A painful, unilateral necrotizing tonsillar ulceration caused by fusiform bacilli and spirochetes, associated with poor oral hygiene and severe halitosis.
    \item \textbf{Non-Infectious Causes}: Pharyngeal irritation from gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), chemical irritants, tobacco smoke, dry air, allergic rhinitis with post-nasal drip, or foreign bodies can mimic tonsillitis but present without fever or purulent exudate.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most cases of tonsillitis resolve with supportive care, but a medical consultation is necessary to determine if antibiotic treatment is indicated, especially to prevent complications associated with Group A Streptococcus.
Patients should consult a physician if they experience:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A sore throat that lasts longer than 48 hours without improvement.
    \item A fever that remains high or persists for several days.
    \item Visible white or yellow patches (exudate) on the tonsils.
    \item Severe throat pain that prevents swallowing fluids, raising the risk of dehydration.
    \item A sore throat accompanied by joint pain, shortness of breath, or a new rash.
\end{itemize}

Immediate emergency medical attention must be sought if any of the following warning signs occur, as they indicate potential airway compromise or a rapidly spreading deep tissue infection:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, rapid respiration, or gasping for air.
    \item Stridor, which is a high-pitched, squeaking sound heard during inhalation, indicating upper airway obstruction.
    \item An inability to swallow liquids or saliva, resulting in drooling.
    \item Trismus, which is the inability to fully open the mouth, suggesting a peritonsillar abscess.
    \item Significant swelling on one side of the throat or visual deviation of the uvula to one side.
    \item A stiff neck, severe headache, or confusion.
    \item Signs of severe dehydration, such as producing little to no urine, extreme lethargy, or dizziness when standing.
\end{itemize}
If any of these life-threatening symptoms occur, call emergency services immediately by dialing local emergency services in many countries, 911 in the United States and Canada, 999 in the United Kingdom, or 112 in Europe.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of tonsillitis is established through a detailed clinical history and physical examination, with diagnostic tests used to identify the causative pathogen.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical Examination}: Visual inspection of the oropharynx is performed to assess tonsillar size, erythema, and the presence of exudate. The neck is palpated to detect enlargement and tenderness of the anterior and posterior cervical lymph nodes.
    \item \textbf{Clinical Scoring Systems}: Algorithms like the Centor Criteria or the McIsaac Score are used to estimate the probability of Group A beta-hemolytic streptococcal (GABHS) infection. Points are assigned for fever, absence of cough, tender anterior cervical nodes, and tonsillar exudate, along with age adjustments. High scores guide the use of diagnostic tests.
    \item \textbf{Rapid Antigen Detection Test (RADT)}: A point-of-care throat swab test that detects the presence of Group A streptococcal antigens. It provides results within 10 to 15 minutes, with a high specificity (95\% or higher) but lower sensitivity (70\% to 90\%). In children, a negative RADT is typically confirmed with a throat culture.
    \item \textbf{Throat Culture}: The diagnostic gold standard. Swabs are taken from the tonsillar pillars and posterior pharynx, avoiding the tongue and saliva, and cultured on blood agar plates. It has a high sensitivity (90\% to 95\%) and specificity but requires 24 to 48 hours for incubation.
    \item \textbf{Complete Blood Count (CBC)}: A CBC is not routinely performed but can be helpful in severe or prolonged cases. Leukocytosis with a high percentage of neutrophils suggests a bacterial infection, while lymphocytosis with atypical lymphocytes indicates viral mononucleosis.
    \item \textbf{Serological Testing}: If infectious mononucleosis is suspected, a heterophile antibody test (Monospot) or specific EBV antibody titers are performed.
    \item \textbf{Imaging}: Computed Tomography (CT) of the neck with contrast is indicated only if deep neck space infections or abscesses are suspected.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of tonsillitis depends on the causative agent (viral versus bacterial) and the severity of symptoms. The goals are to relieve symptoms, prevent complications, and reduce pathogen transmission.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Supportive Care}: Essential for all cases, particularly viral infections.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Hydration}: Consuming cool fluids, ice chips, popsicles, or warm broths prevents dehydration and soothes the throat.
        \item \textbf{Rest and Nutrition}: Adequate rest and a diet of soft, non-acidic foods are recommended.
        \item \textbf{Humidification}: Using a cool-mist humidifier or inhaling steam helps keep the airway moist.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological Therapy}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Analgesics and Antipyretics}: Acetaminophen (paracetamol) or ibuprofen are used to manage pain and reduce fever. Aspirin is contraindicated in children and adolescents due to the risk of Reye's syndrome.
        \item \textbf{Antibiotics}: Indicated only for confirmed or highly suspected GABHS tonsillitis. The standard treatment is a ten-day course of oral Penicillin V. For patients with a history of penicillin allergy, first-generation cephalosporins (e.g., cephalexin) or macrolides (e.g., azithromycin) are used.
        \item \textbf{Corticosteroids}: A single dose of oral dexamethasone may be considered in cases of severe tonsillar swelling to reduce inflammation and provide rapid pain relief.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgical Management}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Tonsillectomy}: Surgical excision of the palatine tonsils.
        \item \textbf{Indications}: Reserved for patients with recurrent tonsillitis meeting the Paradise criteria, obstructive sleep apnea due to tonsillar hypertrophy, recurrent peritonsillar abscesses, or suspected malignancy.
        \item \textbf{Techniques}: Performed under general anesthesia using cold knife dissection, electrocautery, coblation, or harmonic scalpel.
        \item \textbf{Postoperative Risks}: Recovery takes seven to fourteen days. The primary risk is post-tonsillectomy hemorrhage, which requires immediate medical evaluation.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Complications of tonsillitis can be local (suppurative) or systemic and immune-mediated (non-suppurative).
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Suppurative Complications}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Peritonsillar Abscess (Quinsy)}: The spread of infection beyond the tonsillar capsule, forming a collection of pus that requires incision and drainage.
        \item \textbf{Deep Neck Space Infections}: Retropharyngeal or parapharyngeal abscesses that can compress the airway and require urgent intervention.
        \item \textbf{Otitis Media and Sinusitis}: Extension of infection to the middle ear or paranasal sinuses.
        \item \textbf{Lemierre's Syndrome}: A rare complication involving jugular vein thrombophlebitis and septic emboli, caused by Fusobacterium necrophorum.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Non-Suppurative Complications} (GABHS immune-mediated sequelae):
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Acute Rheumatic Fever (ARF)}: An inflammatory disease affecting the heart, joints, skin, and brain, preventable with prompt antibiotic therapy.
        \item \textbf{Post-Streptococcal Glomerulonephritis (PSGN)}: Kidney inflammation causing hematuria, hypertension, and edema. Antibiotic treatment of tonsillitis does not prevent PSGN.
        \item \textbf{PANDAS}: The sudden onset or exacerbation of obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) or tic disorders in children following a GABHS infection.
        \item \textbf{Streptococcal Toxic Shock Syndrome (STSS)}: A rare, life-threatening systemic infection causing rapid onset of shock and organ failure.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for acute tonsillitis is excellent. Most cases of viral tonsillitis resolve spontaneously within seven to ten days. Bacterial tonsillitis responds rapidly to antibiotics, with symptoms improving within 24 to 48 hours. Completing the full ten-day course of penicillin is essential to prevent recurrence and eliminate the risk of acute rheumatic fever. For patients undergoing tonsillectomy, long-term outcomes are highly favorable, showing a substantial reduction in the frequency and severity of throat infections and improved sleep parameters in cases of obstructive sleep apnea. Chronic and recurrent tonsillitis carry a good prognosis once appropriate medical or surgical therapies are implemented.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Prevention of tonsillitis involves reducing the transmission of pathogens through hygiene practices, while self-care focuses on symptom management.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hand Hygiene}: Wash hands frequently with soap and water for at least 20 seconds, or use an alcohol-based hand sanitizer.
    \item \textbf{Respiratory Hygiene}: Cover coughs and sneezes with a tissue, and discard the tissue immediately.
    \item \textbf{Avoid Sharing Personal Items}: Do not share cups, eating utensils, or toothbrushes.
    \item \textbf{Replacing Toothbrushes}: Replace toothbrushes after recovering from bacterial tonsillitis to prevent reinfection.
    \item \textbf{Avoid Irritants}: Avoid exposure to secondhand tobacco smoke and air pollutants.
    \item \textbf{Warm Saltwater Gargle}: Older children and adults can gargle with warm saltwater (half a teaspoon of salt in a glass of warm water) to soothe throat pain.
    \item \textbf{Lozenges}: Use throat lozenges or anesthetic sprays for temporary pain relief (avoiding in children under four years).
    \item \textbf{Hydration}: Consuming adequate fluids prevents dehydration and maintains mucosal moisture.
    \item \textbf{Stay Home When Ill}: Sick individuals should stay home from school or work. Bacterial cases are generally non-contagious after 24 hours of antibiotic therapy.
\end{itemize}
