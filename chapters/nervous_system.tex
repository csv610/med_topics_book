\chapter{Nervous system}
\index{Nervous system}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The nervous system is a complex, highly specialised network of cells, tissues, and organs that coordinates, regulates, and controls bodily functions. It serves as the primary communication and information processing infrastructure of the human body. Anatomically divided into the central nervous system (CNS)---comprising the brain and spinal cord---and the peripheral nervous system (PNS)---comprising nerves and ganglia---it is responsible for sensory perception, motor control, cognitive functions, memory, emotion, and homeostatic regulation via autonomic mechanisms. The system is structurally sustained by neurons, which transmit electrical impulses, and glial cells, which provide support, protection, and nourishment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Conditions affecting the nervous system are among the leading causes of disability and mortality globally and in many countries. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), neurological disorders contribute to over 10\% of the global burden of disease. Cardiovascular-related neurological events, such as stroke, represent a leading cause of long-term adult disability. Chronic conditions like migraine, epilepsy, and neurodegenerative disorders (such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's diseases) affect millions of individuals. In many countries, dementia has become the second leading cause of death overall, and the leading cause of death among women, with increasing prevalence driven primarily by an ageing population.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The functioning of the nervous system can be disrupted by various structural, functional, or biochemical anomalies, leading to severe clinical manifestations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurodegenerative processes:} Accumulation of misfolded proteins (e.g.\ beta-amyloid plaques and tau tangles in Alzheimer's disease, and alpha-synuclein in Parkinson's disease) leading to progressive neuronal death.
    \item \textbf{Vascular insult:} Ischaemic or haemorrhagic disruptions to cerebral blood flow, causing rapid cellular hypoxia and infarction.
    \item \textbf{Infectious agents:} Viral, bacterial, fungal, or parasitic pathogens crossing the blood-brain barrier to cause inflammation (e.g.\ meningitis, encephalitis).
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune response:} Immune-mediated demyelination of central axons (as seen in multiple sclerosis) or autoimmune destruction of peripheral nerves (as in Guillain-Barré syndrome).
    \item \textbf{Genetic and congenital anomalies:} Inherited gene mutations or chromosomal disorders affecting neural development, as seen in Huntington's disease or cerebral palsy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Clinical features of nervous system dysfunction depend entirely on the anatomical location, size, and nature of the lesion or neurochemical imbalance.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cognitive and psychiatric symptoms:} Memory loss, disorientation, confusion, mood lability, and language deficits such as aphasia or dysphasia.
    \item \textbf{Motor dysfunction:} Muscle weakness (paresis), complete paralysis, tremors, incoordination (ataxia), spasticity, and gait disturbances.
    \item \textbf{Sensory disturbances:} Numbness, tingling (paraesthesia), hyperalgesia, neuropathic pain, and loss of proprioception.
    \item \textbf{Autonomic instability:} Fluctuations in blood pressure, orthostatic hypotension, bowel or bladder incontinence, and sweating anomalies.
    \item \textbf{Cranial nerve signs:} Visual disturbances (diplopia, hemianopia), dysphagia (difficulty swallowing), dysarthria (slurred speech), facial asymmetry, and hearing impairment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Given the systemic manifestations of neurological diseases, it is essential to differentiate primary nervous system disorders from systemic or metabolic conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Metabolic encephalopathy:} Systemic electrolyte imbalances, uraemia, hepatic failure, or hypoglycaemia presenting with altered mental state and confusion.
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular syncope:} Transient cerebral hypoperfusion mimicking transient ischaemic attacks (TIAs) or seizure activity.
    \item \textbf{Functional neurological disorder:} Neurological symptoms (e.g.\ weakness, non-epileptic seizures) without an identifiable organic or structural cause.
    \item \textbf{Endocrine disorders:} Myxoedema madness in severe hypothyroidism, or thyrotoxicosis presenting with neurological tremors and hyperarousal.
    \item \textbf{Systemic rheumatological diseases:} Lupus cerebritis or systemic vasculitis presenting as focal neurological deficits or psychiatric symptoms.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should seek medical evaluation for any new, progressive, or unexplained neurological symptoms.
\hfill \break
\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden weakness or numbness in the face, arm, or leg, especially on one side of the body (signs of stroke).
    \item Sudden difficulty speaking, slurred speech, or difficulty understanding speech.
    \item Sudden onset of severe headache ("thunderclap" headache).
    \item Unexplained loss of consciousness, new-onset seizure activity, or sudden loss of balance and coordination.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis relies on a meticulous clinical history and physical examination, supplemented by advanced imaging and neurophysiological studies.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurological examination:} Comprehensive assessment of mental status, cranial nerves, motor systems, sensory modalities, reflexes, and gait.
    \item \textbf{Neuroimaging (MRI and CT):} Visualisation of structural lesions, infarctions, haemorrhages, demyelination, or tumours.
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture and CSF analysis:} Measurement of opening pressure and analysis of cerebrospinal fluid for infection, inflammation, or oligoclonal bands.
    \item \textbf{Electroencephalography (EEG):} Evaluation of cortical electrical activity to diagnose seizures, sleep disorders, and encephalopathies.
    \item \textbf{Electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction studies (NCS):} Assessment of peripheral nerve and muscle electrical activity to identify neuropathies or myopathies.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is highly individualised, requiring multidisciplinary coordination to arrest disease progression, alleviate symptoms, and optimise functional status.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological therapy:} Antiepileptics for seizures, immunomodulators for multiple sclerosis, levodopa for Parkinson's disease, and thrombolysis/anticoagulation for acute ischaemic stroke.
    \item \textbf{Surgical intervention:} Neurosurgical decompression, tumour resection, or deep brain stimulation (DBS) for movement disorders.
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation services:} Intensive physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and speech pathology to retrain neural pathways and maintain independence.
    \item \textbf{Symptom-specific management:} Pharmacotherapy for neuropathic pain (e.g.\ gabapentinoids), spasticity (e.g.\ baclofen), or autonomic dysfunction.
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle modifications:} Optimising cardiovascular health, regular cognitive stimulation, and diet adjustments.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Permanent motor disability, paraplegia, or tetraplegia.
    \item Cognitive decline, dementia, and loss of independence.
    \item Aspiration pneumonia secondary to dysphagia.
    \item Chronic neuropathic pain, anxiety, and depression.
    \item Neurogenic bladder leading to recurrent urinary tract infections.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of nervous system disorders varies widely based on the underlying pathology, the location of the lesion, and the speed of treatment. Acute conditions like stroke require rapid intervention to limit tissue damage, whereas chronic neurodegenerative diseases are generally progressive, focusing management on maintaining quality of life and delaying functional decline. Neuroplasticity allows for substantial recovery in many non-degenerative cases through structured rehabilitation.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Engage in regular cardiovascular exercise to maintain cerebral perfusion and neuroplasticity.
    \item Eat a Mediterranean-style diet high in antioxidants, healthy fats, and omega-3 fatty acids.
    \item Keep intellectually active and socially engaged to build cognitive reserve.
    \item Control cardiovascular risk factors, including hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidaemia.
    \item Avoid smoking, limit alcohol consumption, and protect against head trauma by wearing helmets and seatbelts.
\end{itemize}
