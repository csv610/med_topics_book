\chapter{Liver cancer}
\index{Liver cancer}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Liver cancer refers to any malignant neoplasm arising in the liver tissues, categorised as primary or secondary (metastatic). Primary liver cancer originates within the cells of the liver. The most common type is hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), which accounts for approximately 85\% of primary liver malignancies and arises from hepatocytes. Other forms include intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (bile duct cancer) and rare tumours such as angiosarcoma or hepatoblastoma. Secondary liver cancer, which occurs when cancer spreads to the liver from other primary sites (such as the bowel, breast, or lung), is significantly more common than primary liver cancer in Western countries. Primary liver cancer is highly aggressive and is strongly associated with chronic liver inflammation and cirrhosis.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Globally, primary liver cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer-related death, accounting for more than 800,000 deaths annually. In many countries, the incidence of liver cancer has risen rapidly over the past three decades, making it one of the fastest-growing causes of cancer death. This trend is driven by an increase in chronic hepatitis B and C infections, alongside growing rates of obesity, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). Liver cancer is approximately three times more common in males than in females, and the risk increases progressively with age, with the majority of cases diagnosed in individuals aged 60 years and older.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The vast majority of primary liver cancers develop on a background of chronic liver injury and cirrhosis. The key aetiological factors and mechanisms include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chronic viral hepatitis:} Persistent infection with hepatitis B virus (HBV) or hepatitis C virus (HCV) drives chronic hepatic necroinflammation, fibrogenesis, and oncogenesis. HBV can integrate directly into the host genome, predisposing patients to HCC even in the absence of cirrhosis.
    \item \textbf{Hepatic cirrhosis:} A state of advanced diffuse fibrosis and regenerative nodule formation; approximately 80\% to 90\% of HCC cases arise in a cirrhotic liver, regardless of the underlying cause.
    \item \textbf{Chronic alcohol abuse:} Long-term heavy alcohol consumption causes direct toxic damage to hepatocytes, leading to alcoholic steatohepatitis, cirrhosis, and subsequent malignant transformation.
    \item \textbf{Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD):} Severe hepatic steatosis with inflammation (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis or MASH) promotes progressive fibrosis and increases the risk of HCC, which can occur without established cirrhosis.
    \item \textbf{Aflatoxins and environmental carcinogens:} Chronic ingestion of dietary aflatoxin B1 (produced by Aspergillus molds contaminating stored grains in tropical regions) causes specific DNA mutations in the TP53 tumour suppressor gene.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Early-stage primary liver cancer is frequently asymptomatic, meaning the diagnosis is often delayed unless caught during regular surveillance. As the tumour grows, clinical features include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Constitutional symptoms:} Unintentional and rapid weight loss, loss of appetite (anorexia), early satiety, and profound fatigue.
    \item \textbf{Abdominal pain:} A dull, aching pain or discomfort in the right upper quadrant of the abdomen or epigastric region, sometimes radiating to the right shoulder.
    \item \textbf{Jaundice:} Yellowing of the skin and the sclera of the eyes, accompanied by dark urine and pale, clay-coloured stools due to biliary obstruction or advanced liver impairment.
    \item \textbf{Ascites and fluid retention:} The sudden accumulation of fluid in the peritoneal cavity causing abdominal distension, and peripheral oedema in the lower limbs.
    \item \textbf{Signs of portal hypertension:} Palpable splenomegaly, prominent abdominal wall veins (caput medusae), and muscle wasting.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Distinguishing primary liver cancer from other focal liver lesions or acute hepatic decompensation is critical:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Metastatic liver disease:} Secondary cancers from colorectal, pancreatic, gastric, or breast carcinomas, which typically present as multiple lesions scattered throughout both hepatic lobes.
    \item \textbf{Benign liver tumours:} Lesions such as hepatic haemangiomas, focal nodular hyperplasia (FNH), or hepatocellular adenomas, which can be distinguished using contrast-enhanced imaging.
    \item \textbf{Hepatic abscess:} A pyogenic or amoebic infectious collection presenting with high fever, rigours, leukocytosis, and tender hepatomegaly.
    \item \textbf{Decompensated cirrhosis:} Acute liver failure or portal hypertensive crisis without an underlying malignancy, though the two conditions frequently coexist.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients with known risk factors (such as cirrhosis or hepatitis B) must undergo regular surveillance and seek prompt medical attention if new symptoms arise.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Haematemesis or melaena:} Vomiting blood or passing black, tarry stools, indicating life-threatening gastrointestinal haemorrhage (often from ruptured oesophageal varices).
    \item \textbf{Severe, acute abdominal pain:} Sudden onset of intense abdominal pain, which may indicate spontaneous tumour rupture and internal bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Acute hepatic encephalopathy:} Sudden confusion, severe lethargy, personality changes, or a flapping tremor (asterixis) due to liver failure.
    \item \textbf{Severe shortness of breath:} Difficulty breathing caused by severe abdominal distension (ascites) pressing on the diaphragm.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is established through a combination of serum biomarkers and advanced radiological imaging, often bypassing the need for a biopsy:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP):} A glycoprotein biomarker elevated in 60\% to 70\% of HCC cases; serial measurements help monitor response to therapy.
    \item \textbf{Multiphasic contrast-enhanced CT or MRI:} The primary diagnostic modalities for HCC, showing diagnostic features of intense arterial enhancement followed by rapid washout in the portal venous and delayed phases.
    \item \textbf{Abdominal ultrasound:} Used as a screening tool every 6 months, combined with AFP, in patients with established cirrhosis or high-risk hepatitis B.
    \item \textbf{Liver biopsy:} Percutaneous core needle biopsy, reserved for atypical imaging findings or when histopathological confirmation is necessary to guide systemic therapy.
    \item \textbf{Liver function and coagulation tests:} Tests including albumin, bilirubin, transaminases, and international normalised ratio (INR) to evaluate liver reserve (e.g., Child-Pugh class).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment decisions are complex and depend on the stage of the cancer, the patient's performance status, and the severity of the underlying liver disease:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgical resection (hepatectomy):} Surgical removal of the tumour, suitable for patients with single tumours, no extrahepatic spread, and preserved liver function without significant portal hypertension.
    \item \textbf{Liver transplantation:} The preferred curative option for patients who meet specific criteria (such as the Milan criteria), as it removes both the tumour and the diseased cirrhotic liver.
    \item \textbf{Locoregional therapies:} Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) or microwave ablation for small tumours; transarterial chemoembolisation (TACE) or selective internal radiation therapy (SIRT) for intermediate, unresectable disease.
    \item \textbf{Systemic therapy:} First-line immunotherapy combinations (e.g., atezolizumab plus bevacizumab) or oral multikinase inhibitors (e.g., sorafenib or lenvatinib) for advanced disease.
    \item \textbf{Supportive and palliative care:} Management of ascites (diuretics or paracentesis), pain control, nutritional support, and care focused on quality of life.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The progression of liver cancer and its underlying chronic liver disease can lead to several severe complications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Liver failure:} Progressive loss of hepatic synthetic and metabolic function, leading to hepatic encephalopathy, coagulopathy, and metabolic derangements.
    \item \textbf{Spontaneous tumour rupture:} A life-threatening emergency where the tumour ruptures, causing massive intra-abdominal bleeding and shock.
    \item \textbf{Portal vein thrombosis:} Tumour invasion into the portal vein system, leading to sudden worsening of portal hypertension, ascites, and variceal bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Metastasis:} Haematogenous spread of cancer cells, most commonly to the lungs, regional lymph nodes, and bones.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for liver cancer depends heavily on the stage at diagnosis and the severity of the underlying cirrhosis. When diagnosed early through surveillance, curative therapies like resection or transplantation yield 5-year survival rates of 60\% to 70\%. However, because the disease is often clinically silent in its early stages, many patients present with advanced disease. For advanced HCC, the historical median survival was less than a year; however, modern systemic immunotherapies are improving survival outcomes, although the overall 5-year survival rate remains low at around 20\% in many countries.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Prevention strategies are crucial in reducing the global and individual burden of primary liver cancer:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hepatitis B immunisation:} Universal vaccination of newborns and high-risk groups to prevent chronic hepatitis B infection.
    \item \textbf{Antiviral therapy:} Prompt and sustained treatment of chronic hepatitis B and curative direct-acting antiviral therapy for hepatitis C to halt progression to cirrhosis.
    \item \textbf{Limiting alcohol consumption:} Avoiding heavy alcohol use to prevent development of alcoholic liver disease and subsequent cirrhosis.
    \item \textbf{Weight management and metabolic health:} Adopting a balanced diet, exercising regularly, and managing diabetes to prevent MASLD and its inflammatory progression.
    \item \textbf{Regular surveillance:} Adhering strictly to 6-monthly ultrasound screenings for patients diagnosed with liver cirrhosis or chronic hepatitis B.
\end{itemize}
