\chapter{Limb numbness}
\index{Limb numbness}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Limb numbness is a sensory symptom characterised by a partial or complete loss of sensation, a feeling of 'pins and needles' (paresthesia), or an altered perception of touch in the arms, hands, legs, or feet. Numbness arises from a disruption anywhere along the somatosensory pathway, which transmits sensory information from the skin's peripheral receptors, through peripheral nerves, up the spinal cord, and into the brain. It can affect a single nerve distribution, a dermatome, or occur symmetrically. Limb numbness can be a transient and benign occurrence (such as temporary compression from an awkward sleeping position) or a key clinical indicator of a serious underlying neurological, systemic, or vascular condition that requires thorough diagnostic evaluation.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Limb numbness is a highly prevalent clinical complaint in primary care and emergency departments worldwide, including in many countries. It can affect individuals of all ages, though specific causes correlate with particular demographics. For instance, entrapment neuropathies like carpal tunnel syndrome are common in working-age adults, while degenerative spinal conditions and diabetic polyneuropathy are more frequent in older populations. Peripheral neuropathy affects up to 50\% of patients with long-term diabetes mellitus, representing a major source of morbidity and chronic disability in the local community.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The pathways leading to limb numbness involve mechanical, ischaemic, inflammatory, or metabolic insults to the nervous system. The primary causes and risk factors include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Peripheral nerve compression:} Localised entrapment of a nerve, such as the median nerve in the wrist (carpal tunnel syndrome) or the ulnar nerve at the elbow (cubital tunnel syndrome).
    \item \textbf{Radiculopathy:} Compression or irritation of a spinal nerve root at the intervertebral foramen, commonly caused by a herniated disc, spondylosis, or osteophytes.
    \item \textbf{Metabolic and nutritional neuropathies:} Symmetrical damage to peripheral nerves due to chronic hyperglycaemia (diabetic neuropathy), vitamin B12 deficiency (pernicious anaemia or dietary insufficiency), or chronic alcohol misuse.
    \item \textbf{Demyelinating diseases:} Autoimmune attack on the myelin sheath of axons, as seen in multiple sclerosis (central nervous system) or Guillain-Barré syndrome (peripheral nervous system).
    \item \textbf{Ischaemic stroke or TIA:} Acute disruption of cerebral blood flow to the somatosensory cortex, leading to contralateral limb or facial numbness.
    \item \textbf{Vascular insufficiency:} Reduced arterial perfusion to limbs, such as in peripheral arterial disease (PAD) or vasospastic episodes (Raynaud's phenomenon).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Limb numbness may present in isolation or be accompanied by other sensory, motor, or autonomic symptoms depending on the location of the lesion. Clinical features include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sensory deficits:} Altered perception of light touch, pinprick, temperature, vibration, or proprioception (joint position sense) in the affected limb.
    \item \textbf{Paresthesia:} Spontaneous, abnormal sensations described as tingling, prickling, or a 'crawling' feeling.
    \item \textbf{Neuropathic pain:} Burning, shooting, or electric shock-like sensations that may accompany sensory loss.
    \item \textbf{Distribution patterns:} Unilateral dermatomal patterns (suggesting radiculopathy), focal nerve distributions (suggesting entrapment), or bilateral 'glove-and-stocking' distributions (suggesting systemic polyneuropathy).
    \item \textbf{Associated motor signs:} Muscle weakness, clumsiness, difficulty performing fine motor tasks, or gait instability (sensory ataxia).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Determining the source of limb numbness requires differentiating central nervous system pathologies from peripheral nerve and vascular causes. Differential diagnoses include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Peripheral neuropathy vs.\ acute stroke:} Symmetrical, slowly progressive sensory loss in the feet and hands vs.\ hyperacute, unilateral numbness involving the face and upper/lower limbs, often accompanied by motor weakness or speech difficulty.
    \item \textbf{Multiple sclerosis vs.\ cervical radiculopathy:} Disseminated, transient sensory disturbances across multiple body areas, often accompanied by visual changes, vs.\ localized, reproducible pain and numbness radiating down a specific arm dermatome.
    \item \textbf{Peripheral arterial disease vs.\ neuropathy:} Limb numbness and pain brought on by walking and relieved by rest (vascular claudication), associated with cold extremities and weak pulses, vs.\ continuous numbness with normal vascular perfusion.
    \item \textbf{Guillain-Barré syndrome vs.\ chronic neuropathy:} Rapidly ascending, progressive bilateral numbness and motor weakness starting in the lower limbs, often following a gastrointestinal or respiratory illness, vs.\ slow, insidious sensory decline.
    \item \textbf{Raynaud's phenomenon vs.\ nerve entrapment:} Transient, temperature-dependent colour changes (white, blue, red) and numbness in the fingers or toes vs.\ constant or posture-induced numbness without skin colour changes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
New-onset or progressive limb numbness should always be evaluated by a healthcare professional to identify the cause.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden onset of numbness in one side of the body (face, arm, or leg).
    \item Numbness accompanied by sudden weakness, difficulty speaking, slurred speech, confusion, dizziness, or facial droop.
    \item Numbness in the saddle region (groin, buttocks, perineum) accompanied by sudden bowel or bladder incontinence or inability to urinate (suggesting cauda equina syndrome).
    \item Numbness that spreads rapidly up the legs or arms.
    \item Numbness that begins immediately after a significant head, neck, or back injury.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A thorough diagnostic workup is required to localise the site of nerve dysfunction and identify any reversible systemic causes. Investigations include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detailed neurological examination:} Evaluating cranial nerves, motor strength, deep tendon reflexes, and testing sensory modalities (pain, light touch, vibration, joint position).
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} Full blood count, fasting blood glucose, HbA1c (to screen for diabetes), vitamin B12 levels, folate, thyroid function tests (TSH), and kidney and liver function panels.
    \item \textbf{Nerve conduction studies (NCS) and electromyography (EMG):} Electrophysiological testing to measure nerve conduction velocities and muscle responses, confirming the presence of peripheral neuropathy or nerve entrapment.
    \item \textbf{Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):} Performed of the brain or spinal cord if a central demyelinating disease, stroke, spinal cord compression, or nerve root impingement is clinically suspected.
    \item \textbf{Duplex ultrasound of the limbs:} Utilised if peripheral arterial disease or vascular insufficiency is suspected as the primary cause of limb numbness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is directed at treating the underlying cause, relieving symptoms, and preventing secondary injuries. Strategies include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Targeted medical therapy:} Optimising glycaemic control in diabetes, administering vitamin B12 injections or oral supplements, or adjusting medications causing drug-induced neuropathies.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological pain management:} Prescribing neuropathic agents such as gabapentin, pregabalin, amitriptyline, or duloxetine to alleviate painful paresthesias.
    \item \textbf{Physical and occupational therapy:} Tailoring exercise programmes to improve balance, strength, and coordination, alongside home assessments to reduce fall risks.
    \item \textbf{Splinting and orthotics:} Implementing wrist splints to keep the wrist in a neutral position for carpal tunnel syndrome, or using ankle-foot orthoses (AFOs) to manage foot drop.
    \item \textbf{Surgical decompression:} Indicated when conservative measures fail for mechanical compressions, such as carpal tunnel release, cubital tunnel release, or spinal discectomy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Chronic limb numbness can lead to significant secondary complications, especially if protective sensation is impaired:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Unnoticed injuries and burns:} Cut, blisters, or thermal burns occurring on numb skin without the patient realising, which can lead to severe infections.
    \item \textbf{Neuropathic foot ulcers:} Particularly in diabetic patients; sensory loss prevents early detection of pressure injuries, leading to deep ulcers, osteomyelitis, and potentially amputation.
    \item \textbf{Falls and fractures:} Loss of proprioception in the lower limbs causes balance impairment and unstable gait, increasing the risk of falls.
    \item \textbf{Chronic pain syndromes:} Transition of paresthesia into persistent, treatment-resistant neuropathic pain.
    \item \textbf{Joint damage (Charcot joint):} Progressive destruction of a weight-bearing joint due to repetitive trauma in the absence of pain sensation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of limb numbness depends entirely on the aetiology and how early treatment is initiated. Transient compression neuropathies have an excellent prognosis, resolving rapidly. Systemic neuropathies (such as diabetic neuropathy) can be stabilized with tight metabolic control, but established nerve damage may be permanent. Prompt treatment of vitamin B12 deficiency can lead to complete reversal of symptoms, whereas delay may result in permanent subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord. Recovery from central nervous system insults (e.g.\ stroke) is variable and requires long-term rehabilitation.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Patients experiencing chronic limb numbness can employ self-care practices to prevent complications and protect their limbs:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Daily visual foot checks:} Inspecting the feet daily for redness, swelling, cuts, or blisters using a mirror, and keeping feet clean and moisturised (avoiding the spaces between toes).
    \item \textbf{Wear appropriate footwear:} Always wearing protective, well-fitting shoes and socks, and avoiding walking barefoot, even indoors.
    \item \textbf{Maintain glycemic control:} Regularly checking blood glucose and adhering to diabetic dietary plans.
    \item \textbf{Avoid repetitive strain and pressure:} Taking regular breaks during repetitive tasks, adjusting ergonomics, and avoiding sitting with crossed legs or resting on elbows.
    \item \textbf{Test water temperatures:} Using an elbow or thermometer to check bath or shower water temperature before stepping in to prevent scalding.
\end{itemize}
