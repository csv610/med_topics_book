\section*{Definition and Overview}
Telogen effluvium is a temporary, generalised form of hair loss that occurs when a large number of hair follicles are pushed into their resting (telogen) phase at the same time and then shed together. It is one of the commonest causes of diffuse hair thinning, and it differs from pattern baldness in that the shedding is spread evenly across the whole scalp rather than concentrated at the crown or hairline, and it is almost always triggered by a specific physical or emotional event a few months earlier. The classic history is dramatic: a person who had a serious illness, a high fever, an operation, a crash diet, or the birth of a baby finds, roughly two to three months later, that their hair is coming out in handfuls in the shower or on the brush. The condition is intensely worrying because of the volume of hair shed, yet the prognosis is excellent: the hair cycle normally restores itself, and full regrowth occurs within six to twelve months once the trigger resolves. Understanding the normal hair cycle is the key, because telogen effluvium is essentially a timing problem within that cycle rather than permanent damage to the hair-producing follicles. This chapter explains why the shedding happens, how it is recognised and distinguished from other causes of hair loss, which tests are useful, and what can be done to support recovery. The reassuring practical point is that shedding following a known trigger is not usually a sign of serious internal disease, the scalp is not damaged, and with patience the hair almost always grows back.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Telogen effluvium is common at all ages and in both sexes, although it is reported more often in women, partly because women are more likely to notice and seek help for diffuse hair loss. It can follow many everyday events, which is why most people experience at least one episode in a lifetime. Post-partum shedding affects a large proportion of women in the months after childbirth, typically peaking around three to four months after the birth, as the high hormone levels of pregnancy that kept hair growing fall away. The condition also follows major illnesses, high fevers, surgery, and severe psychological stress, and it was observed widely after severe viral infections, including COVID-19. Because the trigger can be very common, the exact incidence is hard to measure, and most episodes are managed at home without ever reaching a doctor. In general, the milder and shorter the stressor, the milder the shedding, although the time lag between trigger and hair fall is remarkably constant at about two to three months, and this delay is a central clue to the diagnosis.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Hair grows in a cycle with three phases. The anagen phase is the active growing phase, lasting two to six years, during which the follicle produces hair. The catagen phase is a brief transitional stage. The telogen phase is the resting phase, lasting about three months, at the end of which the resting hair is shed and a new anagen hair begins. At any one time, roughly 85 to 90 percent of scalp hairs are in anagen and about 10 percent in telogen. In telogen effluvium, a stressful event causes an abnormally large proportion of anagen follicles to switch prematurely into telogen. About two to three months later, these resting hairs are shed at the same time, producing the characteristic diffuse shedding.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physiological triggers:} childbirth (post-partum shedding), severe illness with high fever, major surgery, and general anaesthesia
    \item \textbf{Emotional stress:} bereavement, divorce, job loss, exams, and other psychological stressors can trigger a wave of shedding
    \item \textbf{Metabolic change:} rapid weight loss, crash dieting, low protein or iron intake, and eating disorders
    \item \textbf{Nutritional deficiency:} iron deficiency, low ferritin, and low vitamin B12 or zinc are commonly associated, though cause and effect can be hard to separate
    \item \textbf{Medication:} some drugs, including certain blood-pressure medicines, antidepressants, retinoids, and hormonal agents, can provoke shedding
    \item \textbf{Hormonal events:} stopping the combined oral contraceptive pill, and thyroid disease (underactive or overactive), both disrupt the hair cycle
    \item \textbf{Chronic disease:} poorly controlled diabetes, kidney or liver disease, and chronic infection can suppress hair growth
    \item \textbf{COVID-19 and other viral illness:} post-viral shedding is well described, typically beginning one to three months after the infection
\end{itemize}

The follicles themselves are not destroyed, which is why the process is reversible. The shedding can sometimes trigger a second problem: if the person responds with severe anxiety about the hair loss, that anxiety can itself prolong or renew the shedding, creating a loop that continues until the cycle is broken.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diffuse shedding:} hair comes out evenly from all over the scalp, rather than from one area; patients often report handfuls of hair in the drain, pillow, or hairbrush
    \item \textbf{Onset two to three months after a trigger:} the single most characteristic feature, and the clue that separates telogen effluvium from most other causes
    \item \textbf{Sudden onset:} the shedding usually begins abruptly and can be dramatic, which is what drives most people to seek advice
    \item \textbf{No bald patches:} the scalp is thinned overall rather than showing discrete bald spots, which helps distinguish it from alopecia areata
    \item \textbf{Normal scalp:} there is no redness, scaling, pain, or itching in typical cases
    \item \textbf{Hair-pull test positive at the edge of shedding:} a clinician gently pulling a small tuft of hair may dislodge several loose telogen hairs when the shedding is active
    \item \textbf{General health otherwise normal:} apart from the stressor, most people feel well, and there are no systemic symptoms
    \item \textbf{Recovery phase:} shedding gradually reduces over three to six months, and new growth is usually visible within six to twelve months
\end{itemize}

The condition is uncomfortable emotionally but is not medically dangerous, and the key message is that shedding that follows a known trigger and then slows down is behaving exactly as expected.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Female and male pattern hair loss (androgenetic alopecia):} a gradual, progressive thinning over the crown or parting that is not linked to a specific trigger; telogen effluvium can be superimposed on it, and a biopsy or trichoscopy may be needed to separate them
    \item \textbf{Alopecia areata:} produces well-defined, smooth bald patches rather than diffuse shedding, though a rare diffuse form exists
    \item \textbf{Thyroid disease:} hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism both cause diffuse shedding; blood tests distinguish them from telogen effluvium
    \item \textbf{Iron deficiency and anaemia:} can cause or contribute to diffuse hair loss and should be checked, particularly in women with heavy periods
    \item \textbf{Syphilis (secondary):} causes patchy "moth-eaten" hair loss along with rash and systemic symptoms; blood testing settles the question in at-risk groups
    \item \textbf{Cicatricial (scarring) alopecia:} rare inflammatory conditions that scar the scalp and cause permanent loss; suggested by pain, redness, or scaling and by permanent loss
    \item \textbf{Traction alopecia:} hair loss from tight hairstyles, braids, or extensions, distributed along the affected edge rather than diffusely
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most episodes of telogen effluvium resolve without treatment, but a medical review is wise to confirm the diagnosis and check for treatable causes.

Seek medical advice if you:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Are losing large amounts of hair and there is no obvious trigger
    \item Have shedding that continues for more than six months or is getting worse
    \item Notice bald patches, redness, scaling, pain, or itching of the scalp
    \item Have other symptoms such as fatigue, weight change, or changes in periods, which suggest thyroid or other disease
    \item Are losing hair along with poor general health or an underlying illness
    \item Have a child with sudden hair loss
\end{itemize}

There is no emergency from telogen effluvium itself. However, sudden hair loss accompanied by severe fatigue, fevers, or other new systemic symptoms warrants prompt medical assessment. The condition can be emotionally distressing, and it is entirely appropriate to seek help for the worry as well as the hair.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of telogen effluvium is usually made on history and examination, and for most people no tests are needed.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detailed history:} the timing of the shedding, any events in the preceding three months, recent weight changes, diet, medicines, childbirth, and family history of hair loss
    \item \textbf{Hair-pull test:} gently pulling a small tuft of hairs to see how many come away; several telogen hairs falling together supports active shedding
    \item \textbf{Scalp examination:} assessing the pattern and distribution of thinning and checking for scaling, redness, or bald patches
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count and iron studies (ferritin), plus thyroid function tests; vitamin B12, zinc, and other tests are added if the history suggests deficiency
    \item \textbf{Trichoscopy:} a hand-held microscope examination of the scalp and hair shafts, used to distinguish telogen effluvium from pattern hair loss
    \item \textbf{Hair sample and trichogram:} laboratory analysis of plucked hairs showing the proportion in telogen; occasionally used in specialist clinics
    \item \textbf{Scalp biopsy:} rarely needed, but can separate telogen effluvium from scarring or other forms of hair loss when the diagnosis is unclear
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reassurance:} the single most important treatment; understanding that the condition is temporary and self-limiting greatly reduces the distress it causes
    \item \textbf{Identify and address the trigger:} treating the underlying illness, improving diet, managing stress, or adjusting medicines is the foundation of recovery
    \item \textbf{Nutritional correction:} restoring iron, ferritin, vitamin B12, zinc, and protein intake to normal levels, under guidance, if a deficiency is found
    \item \textbf{Gentle hair care:} avoid harsh chemicals, tight styling, and vigorous brushing during the shedding phase to reduce breakage
    \item \textbf{Avoid obsessive checking:} reducing the daily ritual of counting hairs helps break the anxiety cycle that can prolong shedding
    \item \textbf{Minoxidil in selected cases:} a topical scalp treatment that can help some people, particularly when telogen effluvium is layered on pattern hair loss; it needs a medical recommendation
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} counselling or stress management where stress, bereavement, or body-image concerns are significant
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} a review in three to six months to confirm the shedding has settled and regrowth is occurring
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Emotional distress:} the dramatic shedding causes anxiety, embarrassment, and lowered self-esteem, which can persist even after regrowth begins
    \item \textbf{Anxiety loop:} stress about hair loss can itself perpetuate shedding, delaying recovery
    \item \textbf{Repeated episodes:} successive triggers can cause another wave of shedding
    \item \textbf{Unmasking pattern hair loss:} the shedding can expose or accelerate an underlying hereditary thinning
    \item \textbf{Missed underlying diagnosis:} if a thyroid or iron condition is overlooked, shedding may continue
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for telogen effluvium is excellent. Once the triggering event is over, the hair cycle usually normalises on its own, shedding slows within a few months, and full regrowth is expected within six to twelve months. People often notice a period of improvement followed by a plateau, and some report that the new hair is of a different texture or has more "baby hair" at the hairline; this settles over time. The outcome is less certain when the trigger is ongoing, such as chronic stress, a persistent medical condition, or an unaddressed nutritional deficiency, and the shedding may continue or recur until the underlying problem is treated. It is also common for telogen effluvium to unmask an underlying hereditary tendency, so that a person notices pattern thinning they did not have before. The essential message is one of patience: hair grows slowly, and it takes months for the cycle to return to balance. With correct diagnosis, treatment of any contributing cause, and gentle hair care, the overwhelming majority of people make a complete recovery with no permanent loss of hair.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a balanced diet with adequate protein, iron, and vitamins, and seek a check of iron stores if you have heavy periods or fatigue
    \item Treat the underlying cause of stress, illness, or weight change promptly rather than waiting for the hair to recover on its own
    \item Be kind to your hair: avoid tight styles, extensions, harsh dyes, and excessive heat during and after an episode
    \item Keep expectations realistic and give the hair six to twelve months to regrow; month-to-month changes are slow
    \item Reduce the habit of pulling at or counting loose hairs, which feeds anxiety
    \item Use gentle brushes and combs, and wash and condition with mild products
    \item See a doctor early if shedding persists, has no clear trigger, or is associated with other symptoms
\end{itemize}
