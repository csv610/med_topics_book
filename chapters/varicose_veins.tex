\chapter{Varicose veins}
\index{Varicose veins}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Varicose veins represent a highly prevalent and clinically significant manifestation of chronic venous disease. By definition, varicose veins are subcutaneous, permanently dilated, elongated, and tortuous veins that measure 3 mm or more in diameter when evaluated with the patient in an upright, standing position. They typically affect the superficial venous system of the lower extremities, most commonly involving the great saphenous vein, the small saphenous vein, and their respective tributaries. It is essential to distinguish varicose veins from other venous abnormalities such as telangiectasias (commonly known as spider veins, which are intradermal dilated venules measuring less than 1 mm in diameter) and reticular veins (dilated bluish subdermal veins measuring between 1 mm and 3 mm in diameter).

The venous system of the lower limb is divided into three primary components: the deep venous system (which lies beneath the muscular fascia and carries the vast majority of blood back to the heart), the superficial venous system (located in the subcutaneous tissue above the fascia), and the perforating veins (which penetrate the muscular fascia to connect the superficial and deep systems). In a healthy individual, a series of one-way bicuspid valves ensures that blood flows unidirectionally from the superficial veins to the deep veins and then cranially toward the heart, overcoming the effects of gravity. When these valves fail or when the vein walls become pathologically distended, blood flows retrograde, leading to venous pooling, chronic venous hypertension, and the clinical presentation of varicose veins. Far from being a cosmetic concern, varicose veins represent a progressive vascular disorder that forms part of the chronic venous insufficiency spectrum, which can lead to debilitating symptoms, skin breakdown, and chronic ulceration if left unmanaged.

\section{*{Epidemiology}}
The epidemiological landscape of varicose veins is characterized by a high prevalence in industrialized nations, carrying significant public health and economic implications. Epidemiological studies, including the landmark Edinburgh Vein Study and the Framingham Heart Study, demonstrate that varicose veins affect approximately 20\% to 30\% of the global adult population, with some estimates exceeding 40\% when including milder forms of venous reflux. The prevalence is heavily influenced by age, gender, and lifestyle factors.

Age is one of the most prominent demographic risk factors. The condition is relatively rare in individuals under the age of 30, affecting less than 10\% of this demographic. However, the prevalence increases progressively with advancing age, climbing to over 50\% in individuals aged 70 and older. This rise is attributed to the cumulative effects of gravity, age-related degeneration of venous wall collagen and elastin, and the gradual loss of skeletal muscle tone in the lower extremities.

Gender distribution shows a historical predominance in females. In younger cohorts, women are affected at a ratio of approximately 2:1 to 3:1 compared to men, which is primarily driven by pregnancy, cyclic progesterone exposures, and the use of exogenous hormones (such as oral contraceptives or hormone replacement therapy). However, as the population ages, this gender gap narrows significantly, and in elderly demographics, the prevalence among men approaches that of women. Geographically, varicose veins are much more common in Western and European populations than in developing nations in Africa or Asia. This variation is hypothesized to stem from differences in dietary fiber intake, physical activity levels, occupational posture (long periods of static sitting or standing), and obesity rates.

\section{*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}}
The development of varicose veins is a multifactorial process involving a complex interplay of genetic predisposition, mechanical factors, and microscopic alterations in the vein wall structure. The primary underlying pathophysiology involves venous hypertension, which is driven by valvular incompetence and intrinsic venous wall weakness.

The mechanisms, risk factors, and pathways include the following:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Valvular Incompetence}: The primary pathological event in many cases is the failure of the one-way bicuspid valves situated within the saphenous veins and perforators. When these valves fail to close completely, blood is allowed to flow retrograde (reflux) down the leg during diastole or muscular relaxation. This reflux increases the hydrostatic pressure within the distal superficial venous segments, causing them to dilate and become tortuous.
\item \textbf{Venous Wall Weakness}: Research indicates that wall weakness often precedes valvular dysfunction. Quantitative and qualitative changes in the extracellular matrix of the vein wall---specifically a decrease in collagen type I, an increase in collagen type III, and the fragmentation of elastic fibers---compromise the structural integrity of the vessel. This makes the vein abnormally distensible, allowing it to dilate even under normal physiological pressures, which subsequently pulls the valve leaflets apart and induces secondary valvular incompetence.
\item \textbf{Genetic Predisposition}: Heredity is a major predisposing factor. Epidemiological data show that if both parents suffer from varicose veins, the probability of their offspring developing the disease is over 80\%. If only one parent is affected, the risk is approximately 60\% for females and 25\% for males. If neither parent has the condition, the risk drops to approximately 20\%.
\item \textbf{Hormonal Influences}: Female sex hormones, particularly progesterone, have a profound relaxing effect on vascular smooth muscle. Progesterone increases the distensibility of the venous wall, making it susceptible to stretch. This explains the high incidence of varicose veins during pregnancy, when hormone levels are significantly elevated, and during the administration of hormone replacement therapies.
\item \textbf{Increased Intra-Abdominal Pressure}: Conditions that raise pressure within the abdominal cavity obstruct the venous return from the lower limbs. Chronic constipation, pelvic tumors, obesity, and pregnancy all increase intra-abdominal pressure, forcing the femoral and iliac veins to work against higher resistance, which propagates pressure backward into the superficial system.
\item \textbf{Microvascular Inflammatory Cascade}: Chronic venous hypertension alters the shear stress experienced by the venous endothelium. This downregulates protective endothelial factors and upregulates adhesion molecules (such as ICAM-1 and VCAM-1). Leukocytes adhere to the endothelium and migrate into the vein wall, releasing inflammatory cytokines, metalloproteinases, and growth factors. This chronic inflammatory state digests the extracellular matrix, further weakening the vein wall and destroying the valve leaflets.
\item \textbf{Calf Muscle Pump Dysfunction}: The calf muscle pump is the primary engine of venous return. During walking, contraction of the gastrocnemius and soleus muscles compresses the deep veins, forcing blood upward. If a person leads a sedentary lifestyle, has limited ankle mobility, or stands statically for long periods, the pump is underutilized, leading to venous stasis and elevated venous pressures.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of varicose veins is highly variable, ranging from asymptomatic cosmetic concerns to severe, chronic pain and debilitating dermatological changes. The symptoms and signs are typically exacerbated by prolonged standing, warm weather, and at the end of the day, and they are classically relieved by leg elevation, walking, or the use of compression garments.

The subjective symptoms reported by patients include:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Aching and Heaviness}: A dull, persistent ache or feeling of weight in the lower legs, which worsens as the day progresses.
\item \textbf{Fatigue and Soreness}: An overall sense of muscle fatigue in the calves, even after minimal physical exertion.
\item \textbf{Paresthesias and Burning}: Sensations of tingling, itching, or burning over the course of the affected veins.
\item \textbf{Muscle Cramping}: Nocturnal calf cramps are frequently reported, thought to be related to venous congestion and localized metabolic accumulation.
\item \textbf{Restless Legs}: A subjective urge to move the limbs, often associated with venous congestion and relieved by movement.
\end{itemize}

The objective clinical signs observed during examination are categorized using the international CEAP (Clinical, Etiological, Anatomical, Pathophysiological) classification system:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C1 (Telangiectasias or Reticular Veins)}: Thread-like red or blue vessels less than 3 mm in size, representing early microvascular congestion.
\item \textbf{C2 (Varicose Veins)}: Tortuous, dilated superficial veins measuring 3 mm or more, visible and palpable, particularly when standing.
\item \textbf{C3 (Oedema)}: Pitting swelling of the lower leg, ankle, and foot, typically unilateral or asymmetrical, worsening in the evening.
\item \textbf{C4a (Pigmentation or Eczema)}: Hyperpigmentation of the skin, typically rusty-brown in color due to the deposition of haemosiderin (from extravasated red blood cells), and venous eczema (stasis dermatitis) characterized by red, itchy, scaling skin.
\item \textbf{C4b (Lipodermatosclerosis or Atrophie Blanche)}: Lipodermatosclerosis refers to chronic inflammation and fibrosis of the subcutaneous fat, causing the lower leg to harden and shrink, resulting in an ``inverted champagne bottle'' contour. Atrophie blanche appears as smooth, white, avascular scar-like areas surrounded by dilated capillaries.
\item \textbf{C5 (Healed Venous Ulcer)}: Evidence of past ulceration, presenting as thin, scarred skin vulnerable to recurrent breakdown.
\item \textbf{C6 (Active Venous Ulcer)}: Open, painful, chronic wounds, typically located near the medial malleolus, exhibiting irregular borders and a red, granulating base with variable amounts of exudate.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When evaluating a patient with suspected varicose veins and lower extremity symptoms, it is crucial to consider a wide range of vascular, systemic, and musculoskeletal conditions that can present with similar signs of pain, swelling, or skin discoloration.

Alternative diagnoses to consider include:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)}: Unlike the gradual, bilateral, or chronic nature of varicose veins, DVT typically presents as acute, sudden-onset, unilateral leg pain, swelling, and warmth. DVT represents a medical emergency due to the high risk of pulmonary embolism and must be ruled out using compressive duplex ultrasound and D-dimer testing.
\item \textbf{Peripheral Arterial Disease (PAD)}: PAD causes lower extremity pain due to arterial stenosis and ischemia. However, arterial pain (intermittent claudication) is provoked by walking and relieved by rest, whereas venous pain is relieved by walking and worsened by standing. Patients with PAD display cool limbs, diminished pulses, and trophic changes (hair loss, shiny skin), and compression therapy is strictly contraindicated in these patients.
\item \textbf{Lymphedema}: This is a chronic swelling caused by lymphatic drainage dysfunction. Lymphedema typically involves the dorsum of the foot and toes (producing a positive Stemmer's sign, where the skin over the second toe cannot be pinched), presents with non-pitting, woody oedema in its chronic stages, and lacks the hyperpigmentation and venous tortuosity characteristic of varicose veins.
\item \textbf{Congestive Heart Failure (CHF)}: CHF causes bilateral, symmetrical lower extremity pitting oedema due to systemic fluid overload. It is differentiated from venous disease by the presence of systemic symptoms such as dyspnoea on exertion, orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, jugular venous distension, and cardiomegaly on imaging.
\item \textbf{Hepatic or Renal Failure}: Chronic kidney disease, nephrotic syndrome, and cirrhosis can all cause bilateral leg oedema due to hypoalbuminaemia and fluid retention. Urinalysis, renal function tests, and liver function panels easily distinguish these conditions.
\item \textbf{Cellulitis}: An acute bacterial infection of the dermis and subcutaneous tissue that presents with unilateral erythema, warmth, pain, and systemic symptoms like fever and chills. While it can mimic acute stasis dermatitis, stasis dermatitis is typically bilateral, afebrile, and associated with chronic venous changes.
\item \textbf{Myofascial Pain or Osteoarthritis}: Degenerative joint disease of the knee or hip, lumbar radiculopathy (sciatica), or spinal stenosis can cause lower limb pain. These conditions are differentiated by localized joint tenderness, neurological deficits, pain related to back extension or joint loading, and normal venous duplex examinations.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
While varicose veins are often chronic and slow-progressing, certain symptoms warrant prompt medical evaluation to prevent serious complications. Patients should consult a primary care physician or a vascular specialist if they experience progressive, painful swelling of the lower leg, persistent aching that interferes with daily activities, or skin changes such as redness, hardening, or darkening around the ankles.

Immediate emergency medical attention must be sought in the following circumstances:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Spontaneous or Traumatic Bleeding}: A varicose vein can rupture through thin, overlying skin, leading to profuse, rapid blood loss. If a varicose vein bleeds, the patient must immediately lie flat on the ground, elevate the leg as high as possible above the heart, apply direct, firm pressure to the bleeding site with a clean cloth or bandage, and call emergency services (e.g., local emergency services in many countries, 999 in the United Kingdom, or 911 in the United States).
\item \textbf{Signs of Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)}: The sudden onset of severe swelling, pain, warmth, or redness in one leg (especially if concentrated in the calf) requires urgent hospital emergency department evaluation, as it may indicate a blood clot that could embolize.
\item \textbf{Symptoms of Pulmonary Embolism (PE)}: If leg pain or swelling is accompanied by sudden shortness of breath, sharp chest pain that worsens when breathing in, rapid heart rate, unexplained coughing (which may produce bloody sputum), or lightheadedness, the patient must immediately call emergency services (e.g., 000, 999, or 911) as this is a life-threatening emergency.
\item \textbf{Signs of Infection}: The development of spreading redness, intense warmth, severe tenderness, or pus-like drainage from the leg, particularly around an open sore or ulcer, accompanied by fever or chills, requires prompt medical assessment and antibiotic therapy.
\item \textbf{Non-Healing Ulcers}: Any open sore or break in the skin of the lower leg or ankle that does not show signs of healing within two weeks should be evaluated by a healthcare professional to initiate specialized wound care and address the underlying venous reflux.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of varicose veins is clinical, but a comprehensive diagnostic workup is essential to map the venous anatomy, localize the source of reflux, and plan appropriate interventions. The diagnostic protocol combines a detailed history, a structured physical examination, and non-invasive vascular imaging.

The diagnostic process includes:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Clinical History and Physical Examination}: The clinician evaluates the onset, duration, and nature of symptoms, occupational factors, and family history. The physical exam is performed with the patient standing to ensure maximal venous distension. The doctor inspects the limbs for venous distribution, oedema, skin discoloration, lipodermatosclerosis, and active or healed ulcers. Palpation helps identify fascial defects (where perforating veins emerge), localized tenderness, and the presence of superficial cords indicating thrombophlebitis.
\item \textbf{Duplex Ultrasound (DUS)}: Duplex ultrasound is the absolute gold standard for diagnosing venous reflux and planning treatment. It combines B-mode (gray-scale) imaging, which visualizes the anatomical structure of the veins, presence of thrombi, and compressibility, with spectral and color Doppler, which measures blood flow velocity and direction.
    \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reflux Criteria}: Venous reflux is defined as retrograde flow in the opposite direction of normal venous return. In the superficial veins (great and small saphenous veins) and accessory veins, reflux lasting longer than 0.5 seconds is considered pathologically significant. In the deep venous system (femoral and popliteal veins), the threshold is longer than 1.0 second. For perforating veins, reflux lasting longer than 0.35 seconds is diagnostic.
    \item \textbf{Anatomical Mapping}: The ultrasound scan maps the saphenofemoral junction (SFJ), the saphenopopliteal junction (SPJ), the diameter of the saphenous trunks, the presence of accessory veins, and the locations of incompetent perforators. It also rules out deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and post-thrombotic syndrome.
    \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Photoplethysmography (PPG)}: An infrared sensor is placed on the skin above the ankle to measure changes in skin blood volume during calf-muscle exercise. It calculates the venous refilling time (VRT). A VRT of less than 20 seconds indicates venous reflux, while a normal VRT is greater than 20 seconds. Applying a tourniquet can help differentiate superficial from deep venous reflux.
\item \textbf{Advanced Cross-Sectional Imaging}: In cases of suspected pelvic venous disorders, iliac vein compression (May-Thurner syndrome), or recurrent varicose veins with complex anatomy, Computed Tomography Venography (CTV) or Magnetic Resonance Venography (MRV) is indicated. These modalities provide excellent visualization of the pelvic and abdominal venous structures.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of varicose veins is tailored to the patient's symptoms, clinical stage (CEAP classification), anatomical pattern of reflux, and personal preferences. Treatment strategies range from conservative self-care measures to minimally invasive endovenous interventions and surgical procedures.

The therapeutic options include:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Conservative and Lifestyle Measures}: This is the first-line approach for all patients, particularly those with mild symptoms, those who are pregnant, or those who are unfit for interventional therapy.
    \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Graduated Compression Therapy}: Compression stockings (usually knee-high or thigh-high, providing 20--30 mmHg of pressure) are the cornerstone of conservative management. They apply external pressure to the tissues, decreasing the diameter of dilated veins, reducing venous reflux, facilitating the calf muscle pump, and reducing lower leg oedema.
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle Changes}: Regular exercise (such as walking or swimming), weight loss to decrease intra-abdominal pressure, elevating the legs above heart level for 15--20 minutes several times daily, and avoiding prolonged static sitting or standing.
    \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Pharmacological Therapy (Venoactive Drugs)}: While not a cure, certain venoactive agents (e.g., micronized purified flavonoid fraction (MPFF), calcium dobesilate, and horse chestnut seed extract) can be used as adjuvant therapy. They work by improving venous tone, reducing capillary hyperpermeability, and diminishing leukocyte-mediated inflammation, thereby reducing symptoms of heaviness, aching, and swelling.
\item \textbf{Endovenous Thermal Ablation (EVTA)}: This has largely replaced open surgery as the standard of care for saphenous vein incompetence. Performed under ultrasound guidance and tumescent local anaesthesia, these procedures are minimally invasive and have short recovery times.
    \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Radiofrequency Ablation (RFA)}: A specialized catheter is inserted into the incompetent saphenous vein. The catheter uses radiofrequency energy to deliver controlled thermal energy to the vein wall, causing collagen denaturation, vessel contraction, and fibrotic occlusion.
    \item \textbf{Endovenous Laser Therapy (EVLT)}: Similar to RFA, EVLT utilizes a laser fiber to deliver thermal energy. The laser energy heats the blood and the vein wall, causing endothelial damage, thrombosis, and eventual fibrotic closure of the vein.
    \item \textbf{Tumescent Anaesthesia}: A mixture of dilute local anaesthetic (lidocaine) and epinephrine is injected into the perivenous space. This protects surrounding nerves and tissues from heat damage, compresses the vein to ensure contact with the catheter, and provides intraoperative and postoperative analgesia.
    \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Non-Thermal Endovenous Ablation}: These techniques eliminate the need for tumescent local anaesthesia, further reducing the risk of nerve injury and procedural discomfort.
    \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cyanoacrylate Glue Embolisation}: A medical-grade adhesive (glue) is injected segmentally into the vein through a catheter under ultrasound guidance. The vein is compressed manually, causing immediate closure and subsequent fibrotic encapsulation.
    \item \textbf{Mechanochemical Ablation (MOCA)}: A device with a rotating wire tip is inserted into the vein to mechanically damage the endothelium while simultaneously infusing a liquid sclerosant (such as polidocanol). This combination leads to rapid spasm and occlusion of the vessel.
    \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Sclerotherapy}: This involves injecting a chemical sclerosant directly into the target veins.
    \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Liquid and Foam Sclerotherapy}: Polidocanol or sodium tetradecyl sulfate is injected as a liquid (for spider veins) or agitated with air/gas to form a foam (for larger varicose tributaries and reticular veins). Foam displaces blood within the vein, allowing the sclerosant to contact the endothelium directly, causing chemical phlebitis, thrombosis, and fibrosis.
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound-Guided Foam Sclerotherapy (UGFS)}: Used to treat deeper, non-visible varicose tributaries and source veins.
    \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Surgical Treatments}: Reserved for patients with very large, tortuous veins, specific anatomical variations, or failed endovenous interventions.
    \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High Ligation and Stripping}: Under general or regional anaesthesia, the saphenofemoral or saphenopopliteal junction is ligated, and the incompetent saphenous trunk is physically removed (stripped) from the leg using a specialized wire.
    \item \textbf{Ambulatory Phlebectomy}: Multiple micro-incisions (1--2 mm) are made over superficial varicose clusters, and the veins are extracted using specialized phlebectomy hooks under local anaesthesia. This is often performed in conjunction with thermal ablation.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated varicose veins can progress from a simple cosmetic concern to a complex chronic disease with serious local and systemic complications. Additionally, while modern treatments are highly safe, they carry specific procedural risks that must be understood.

Complications of untreated chronic venous disease include:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Superficial Thrombophlebitis}: The slow, turbulent blood flow within varicose veins predisposes to spontaneous clot formation and localized inflammation. This presents as a painful, warm, red, indurated cord along the course of the vein. While often self-limiting, if the thrombus propagates close to the saphenofemoral or saphenopopliteal junction, it can progress to deep vein thrombosis (DVT).
\item \textbf{Spontaneous Variceal Hemorrhage}: As the pressure in the varicose veins remains chronically high, the overlying skin can thin and erode. Minor trauma or even a hot shower can cause the vein to rupture, leading to profuse, painless bleeding that can be life-threatening if not managed immediately.
\item \textbf{Chronic Venous Insufficiency (CVI)}: Over time, the persistent venous hypertension causes damage to the microcirculation. Red blood cells extravasate into the surrounding tissue, where they break down and release iron-rich haemosiderin, leading to permanent hyperpigmentation and chronic skin inflammation.
\item \textbf{Venous Stasis Eczema}: A chronic inflammatory dermatitis characterized by severe itching, scaling, erythema, and occasional weeping of the skin of the lower leg.
\item \textbf{Lipodermatosclerosis}: This represents advanced tissue scarring where the skin and subcutaneous tissues of the lower leg become hard, indurated, and fibrotic, causing pain and constriction of the leg, which takes on the characteristic ``inverted champagne bottle'' appearance.
\item \textbf{Venous Ulceration}: The final and most debilitating stage of CVI. The compromised microcirculation leads to tissue ischemia and necrosis, resulting in open, painful ulcers that are slow to heal and highly susceptible to secondary bacterial infections (such as cellulitis or osteomyelitis).
\end{itemize}

Complications associated with treatments include:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) and Pulmonary Embolism (PE)}: A rare but serious complication (incidence less than 1\%) occurring when thrombus propagates from the treated superficial vein into the deep venous system (e.g., endovenous heat-induced thrombosis or EHIT).
\item \textbf{Nerve Injury}: Thermal or surgical trauma to nerves adjacent to the veins (specifically the saphenous nerve next to the great saphenous vein, or the sural nerve next to the small saphenous vein) can cause temporary or permanent numbness, paresthesia, or burning pain.
\item \textbf{Hyperpigmentation and Skin Burns}: Sclerotherapy can lead to skin staining (hemosiderin deposition) along the treated vein, which usually resolves within a year but can be permanent. Thermal ablation can cause localized skin burns if tumescent anaesthesia is inadequate.
\item \textbf{Neovascularisation}: The growth of new, fragile blood vessels at the site of surgical ligation, which can contribute to the recurrence of varicose veins.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals with varicose veins is generally excellent, particularly given the efficacy of modern, minimally invasive treatment modalities. However, because chronic venous disease is a chronic and progressive condition, ongoing management and self-care are often required.

The natural history of untreated varicose veins is characterized by slow, progressive deterioration. Over a period of several years, a significant proportion of patients will progress from simple varicose veins (CEAP C2) to showing signs of chronic venous insufficiency, such as persistent oedema (C3), skin pigmentation (C4), and eventually venous ulceration (C6). The rate of progression varies widely and is accelerated by persistent risk factors such as obesity, occupational standing, and lack of physical activity.

With intervention, the short-term and long-term outcomes are highly favorable. Modern endovenous thermal ablation techniques (RFA and EVLT) boast initial technical success rates (complete vein occlusion) of 95\% to 99\%. Long-term studies demonstrate that these occlusion rates remain high, between 90\% and 95\% at 5 years post-procedure. Compared to traditional open surgery (ligation and stripping), endovenous techniques offer significantly lower rates of postoperative pain, faster recovery times (returning to work in 1--2 days versus 2--3 weeks), and lower complication rates.

Recurrence remains the primary long-term challenge in venous disease management. Approximately 20\% to 30\% of patients will develop recurrent varicose veins within 5 years of treatment. Recurrence can be due to tactical failure (e.g., recanalisation of the treated trunk), technical failure (incomplete ablation), or, most commonly, disease progression (the development of reflux in previously healthy superficial veins or tributaries). Compliance with post-procedure compression therapy, regular exercise, and maintaining a healthy weight are critical factors that improve long-term outcomes and reduce the likelihood of recurrence.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
While genetic predisposition to varicose veins cannot be altered, the onset, progression, and severity of symptoms can be significantly influenced by adopting proactive lifestyle habits and self-care practices. These measures focus on improving venous return, reducing venous hypertension, and protecting the skin of the lower extremities.

Preventative measures and self-care strategies include:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Physical Activity}: Engaging in regular cardiovascular exercises that activate the calf muscle pump is the most effective way to promote venous return. Walking, cycling, jogging, and swimming are highly beneficial, as the repetitive contraction of the calf muscles pushes blood out of the lower limbs toward the heart.
\item \textbf{Postural Adjustments}: Avoid prolonged periods of static standing or sitting. If your job requires sitting at a desk, stand up every 30 to 45 minutes to walk around or perform calf raises. Avoid crossing your legs at the knees or ankles, as this can compress the superficial veins and impede blood flow. When standing is unavoidable, periodically shift weight from one foot to the other.
\item \textbf{Leg Elevation}: Make it a daily habit to elevate your legs above the level of your heart for 15 to 20 minutes, three to four times a day. This utilizes gravity to drain pooled blood from the lower extremities, relieving congestion and reducing ankle swelling.
\item \textbf{Weight Management}: Maintain a healthy body mass index (BMI). Excess weight increases intra-abdominal pressure, which puts mechanical pressure on the pelvic veins and hampers the upward flow of blood from the legs, exacerbating venous hypertension.
\item \textbf{Dietary Modifications}: A high-fiber, low-sodium diet is recommended. Fiber helps prevent constipation, which is a known cause of transiently increased intra-abdominal pressure that strains the venous valves. Reducing sodium intake helps minimize systemic water retention, which reduces lower leg swelling.
\item \textbf{Appropriate Footwear and Clothing}: Avoid wearing high heels for extended periods, as they restrict the range of motion of the ankle joint and reduce the efficiency of the calf muscle pump. Opt for flat or low-heeled shoes that allow the calf muscle to fully contract and relax. Avoid wearing tight clothing around the waist, groin, or thighs, as it can act as a tourniquet and obstruct venous drainage.
\item \textbf{Skin Care and Hygiene}: For those with advanced venous disease, keeping the skin of the lower legs clean, hydrated, and intact is critical. Apply a high-quality emollient daily to prevent dryness, cracking, and eczema. Inspect your legs daily for any minor cuts, scratches, or color changes, and seek prompt care for any non-healing lesions to prevent the development of a venous ulcer.
\end{itemize}
