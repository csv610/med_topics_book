\section*{Definition and Overview}
A wasp sting injects venom into the skin. Unlike many honeybees, wasps can sting more than once. Most stings cause a local reaction; a small number cause a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis).
\section*{Clinical Features}
Expect immediate sharp pain, redness, swelling, itching, and warmth. Swelling may enlarge over 24--48 hours and last several days. Widespread hives, swelling of the tongue or throat, wheeze, breathing difficulty, dizziness, collapse, or vomiting may indicate anaphylaxis.
\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
If anaphylaxis is suspected, use the person's adrenaline/epinephrine auto-injector immediately if available, call emergency services, and follow the device instructions. A second dose may be needed if symptoms do not improve and emergency guidance allows it. Seek urgent care for a sting in the mouth or throat, multiple stings, rapidly worsening swelling, or symptoms in a young child or person with a severe allergy.
\section*{Management}
Move away from the insect, wash the area, check for a retained stinger if the insect may have been a bee, and apply a cold pack wrapped in cloth. Elevate the limb when practical. An oral antihistamine or simple analgesic may help if safe for the individual. Do not squeeze, cut, or apply unproven substances to the sting.
\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Avoid disturbing nests, keep food and drinks covered outdoors, wear shoes, and use prescribed adrenaline without delay for future reactions. People with anaphylaxis should receive allergy evaluation and an emergency action plan.
