\chapter{Blood Poisoning (Sepsis)}
\index{Blood Poisoning (Sepsis)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Blood poisoning is the everyday term for what doctors call sepsis or bacteraemia. Bacteraemia means that bacteria are present in the bloodstream, usually originating from an infection elsewhere in the body. Sepsis is the more serious condition that follows: the body's immune response to an infection becomes dysregulated and overwhelming, damaging its own organs and tissues. Sepsis is a medical emergency. Recognising it early and obtaining urgent hospital treatment makes a critical difference, because untreated sepsis can progress quickly to septic shock, multi-organ failure, and death. Public awareness of the warning signs is essential, as sepsis can develop from an apparently minor infection within hours.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Sepsis is a leading cause of death and serious illness worldwide and is estimated to be involved in a large share of deaths in hospitals and intensive care units. It can affect anyone, but the very young, older adults, pregnant women, people with chronic disease, and those with weakened immune systems are at highest risk. Sepsis often begins from a common infection -- a chest infection, urinary tract infection, wound, or skin infection -- that then spreads into the bloodstream. Because it progresses rapidly, recognising the warning signs and acting quickly are central to improving outcomes.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Sepsis begins when an infection releases bacteria or their toxins into the bloodstream, or when the body's reaction to an infection becomes exaggerated. The immune system releases chemicals that normally fight infection, but in sepsis these cause widespread inflammation, leaky blood vessels, and the formation of tiny blood clots that block blood flow to organs.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bacteria:} the most common cause, including \textit{Staphylococcus aureus}, \textit{Streptococcus pneumoniae}, \textit{Escherichia coli}, and \textit{Neisseria meningitidis}
    \item \textbf{Sources of infection:} pneumonia, urinary tract infection, abdominal infection, skin or wound infection, infected intravenous lines or catheters, and meningitis
    \item \textbf{Viruses and fungi:} less common causes, especially in people with weakened immune systems
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} very young or old age, diabetes, cancer and its treatment, kidney or liver disease, recent surgery, and immune-suppressing medicines
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} widespread inflammation, falling blood pressure, poor blood flow to organs, and clotting abnormalities that starve tissues of oxygen
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The warning signs reflect the body's response to a serious infection. Look for any combination of the following:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever, or conversely a dangerously low body temperature, with shaking chills
    \item A very fast heart rate or fast breathing
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or difficulty waking
    \item Severe pain, discomfort, or a general feeling of being extremely unwell
    \item Cold, clammy, mottled, or pale skin
    \item A rash that does not fade when a glass is pressed against it
    \item Not passing urine, or passing very little
    \item Dizziness, light-headedness, or fainting -- signs of falling blood pressure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Many conditions can resemble sepsis, and the priority is to identify who needs emergency treatment:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Other causes of shock: major bleeding, heart attack, severe dehydration, or anaphylaxis
    \item Diabetic emergencies, such as very high blood sugar with ketoacidosis
    \item Pancreatitis or a perforated bowel causing similar abdominal pain and severe illness
    \item Severe asthma or other lung conditions causing fast breathing and breathlessness
    \item Blood cancers and other haematological disorders that cause fever and severe illness
    \item Heat stroke and other environmental emergencies
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek urgent medical attention immediately if you or someone else has an infection and develops any of the warning signs above, or simply feels severely unwell, confused, or is breathing fast. When in doubt, call for help -- sepsis can evolve within hours.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or difficulty waking
    \item Difficulty breathing or very fast breathing
    \item Cold, clammy, mottled, or blue-tinged skin
    \item A rash that does not fade when pressed
    \item Not passing urine, or collapse and loss of consciousness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
In hospital, sepsis is diagnosed and investigated rapidly, often at the same time as treatment begins:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} vital signs -- temperature, heart rate, breathing rate, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation
    \item \textbf{Blood cultures:} to grow and identify the bacteria causing the infection
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, infection markers such as lactate and C-reactive protein, and tests of kidney, liver, and clotting function
    \item \textbf{Urine tests:} urinalysis and culture if a urinary source is suspected
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} chest X-ray, ultrasound, or CT scan to find the source of infection
    \item \textbf{Other samples:} sputum, wound swabs, or cerebrospinal fluid if meningitis is suspected
    \item \textbf{Screening tools:} validated scoring systems help identify patients at high risk and trigger timely treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Sepsis is treated in hospital, usually in an intensive care or high-dependency setting, with time-critical measures:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Early antibiotics:} broad-spectrum antibiotics given quickly, covering the likely source and bacteria
    \item \textbf{Intravenous fluids:} to support blood pressure and organ blood flow
    \item \textbf{Oxygen:} to maintain adequate oxygen levels in the blood
    \item \textbf{Source control:} draining an abscess, removing an infected line or catheter, or surgery to deal with an infected area
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} vasopressor medicines to support blood pressure, and organ support including kidney dialysis or mechanical ventilation if needed
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} continuous observation of vital signs, urine output, and blood tests
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} rehabilitation and review for survivors, who may experience lasting effects
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Septic shock: dangerously low blood pressure despite fluid resuscitation
    \item Organ failure: the kidneys, lungs, liver, or heart may fail
    \item Disseminated intravascular coagulation: severe combined clotting and bleeding problems
    \item Tissue damage and gangrene, sometimes leading to amputation of fingers, toes, or limbs
    \item Long-term effects (post-sepsis syndrome): fatigue, memory problems, anxiety, and depression
    \item A high risk of death, especially if treatment is delayed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outcome of sepsis depends strongly on how quickly treatment begins; with early recognition and treatment, many people make a full recovery. Sepsis remains life-threatening, and even with optimal care some patients die, particularly the frail, the very old, or those with severe underlying illness. Survivors may take months to recover fully, and some are left with lasting physical or psychological problems. Every episode of suspected sepsis is treated as an emergency because speed saves lives.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep vaccinations up to date, including those against influenza, pneumococcal disease, and meningitis where recommended
    \item Wash hands regularly and practise good hygiene to prevent infections
    \item Clean and cover cuts and wounds, and seek care for infected wounds
    \item Treat infections such as urinary or chest infections promptly
    \item Manage chronic conditions such as diabetes well
    \item Be alert to the warning signs of sepsis and seek urgent help if they appear
    \item Follow hospital advice about removing lines, catheters, and drains as soon as they are no longer needed
\end{itemize}
