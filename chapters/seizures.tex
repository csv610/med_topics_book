\chapter{Seizures}
\index{Seizures}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A seizure is a brief episode caused by abnormal electrical activity in the brain. It may change awareness, movement, sensation, behaviour, or emotions. A seizure can occur for many reasons and does not always mean that a person has epilepsy. Epilepsy is a neurological disorder in which a person has a continuing tendency to have recurrent unprovoked seizures.

Most seizures stop on their own, but a seizure lasting 5 minutes or longer, or repeated seizures without recovery, is a medical emergency. The safest immediate response is to protect the person from injury, time the seizure, and obtain emergency help when the warning signs below are present.

\section*{Types and Clinical Features}
The features depend on where the seizure starts and how widely it spreads:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Focal aware seizure:} unusual sensations, emotions, smells, tastes, visual changes, or jerking in one part of the body while awareness is preserved
    \item \textbf{Focal impaired-awareness seizure:} reduced awareness, staring, and repetitive movements such as lip-smacking or fumbling
    \item \textbf{Generalised tonic--clonic seizure:} loss of consciousness followed by stiffening and rhythmic jerking, then confusion or sleepiness
    \item \textbf{Absence seizure:} a brief pause in awareness, often with staring, usually lasting only seconds
    \item \textbf{Myoclonic seizure:} sudden, brief muscle jerks
    \item \textbf{Atonic seizure:} sudden loss of muscle tone, which may cause a fall
\end{itemize}
Some people notice a warning (aura) before a focal seizure. After a convulsive seizure, confusion, headache, fatigue, or muscle soreness may last minutes to hours.

\section*{Causes and Triggers}
Possible causes include stroke, head injury, brain infection, a brain tumour or developmental brain abnormality, low blood sugar, electrolyte disturbance, genetic conditions, and autoimmune disease. Fever can cause febrile seizures in young children. Alcohol withdrawal, some medicines or drugs, missed antiseizure doses, sleep deprivation, and flashing lights can trigger seizures in susceptible people. A trigger is not necessarily the underlying cause.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who has a first seizure should receive prompt medical assessment. Seek urgent advice if seizures are becoming more frequent, recovery is unusually slow, or prescribed rescue medicine is needed. Contact your local emergency services immediately if:
\begin{itemize}
    \item the seizure lasts 5 minutes or longer, or another seizure starts before recovery;
    \item there is difficulty breathing, serious injury, or a seizure in water;
    \item the person is pregnant, has diabetes, or the seizure followed a head injury.
\end{itemize}

\section*{First Aid}
If someone is having a seizure:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Stay with them and note the time the seizure starts.
    \item Move nearby hard or sharp objects away, cushion the head, and loosen tight clothing around the neck.
    \item Do not restrain the person and do not put food, drink, fingers, or any object in their mouth.
    \item When the jerking stops, place them on their side in the recovery position if possible, and check that they are breathing normally.
    \item Do not give food, drink, or tablets until they are fully awake. Follow a prescribed rescue-medicine plan only if trained to do so.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services immediately if:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item the seizure lasts 5 minutes or longer, or another seizure starts before recovery;
    \item it is the person's first known seizure, or the person does not return to their usual state;
    \item there is difficulty breathing, serious injury, or a seizure in water;
    \item the person is pregnant, has diabetes, or the seizure followed a head injury.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Assessment and Diagnosis}
After a first seizure, urgent medical assessment is needed. A clinician will ask the person and witnesses what happened before, during, and after the episode; a phone video can be helpful if it was recorded safely. Assessment may include blood glucose, electrolytes, an electrocardiogram to look for fainting-related causes, an EEG, and brain imaging (usually MRI, or urgent CT when appropriate). Tests are selected according to the history and examination; a normal EEG does not by itself rule out epilepsy.

\section*{Management}
Treatment addresses the cause. Some provoked seizures need treatment of the underlying metabolic, infectious, or toxic problem rather than long-term antiseizure medicine. When epilepsy or a high risk of recurrence is diagnosed, a clinician chooses an antiseizure medicine based on seizure type, age, pregnancy potential, other medicines, and personal circumstances. Some people with difficult-to-control epilepsy may be offered surgery, device treatment, or a supervised dietary therapy. Prolonged seizures require emergency treatment, including rescue medicine prescribed in advance for some people.

Do not stop an antiseizure medicine suddenly. Safety advice for bathing, swimming, heights, machinery, sport, work, and driving should be individualised; driving and reporting requirements depend on local law and current medical advice.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take prescribed medicines consistently and keep a seizure diary.
    \item Prioritise regular sleep, avoid harmful alcohol or drug use, and learn personal triggers.
    \item Tell family, friends, school, or work colleagues how to give seizure first aid.
    \item Use sensible precautions around water, heights, heat, and machinery; do not swim alone.
    \item Attend follow-up appointments and discuss pregnancy, contraception, mental health, and medicine side effects with a clinician.
\end{itemize}
