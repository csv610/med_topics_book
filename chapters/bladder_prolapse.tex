\chapter{Bladder Prolapse}
\index{Bladder Prolapse}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bladder prolapse (cystocele) is the downward bulging of the bladder into the anterior wall of the vagina, occurring when the supporting muscles, ligaments, and connective tissue of the pelvic floor weaken. It is one form of pelvic organ prolapse, a term that covers prolapse of the bladder, uterus, rectum, or the top of the vagina. Prolapse is graded from mild, in which the bladder dips only slightly into the vagina, to severe, in which the bulge reaches or protrudes through the vaginal opening. Many women with a bladder prolapse have no symptoms at all, while others experience bothersome pressure, urinary difficulties, and leakage.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Pelvic organ prolapse is very common, and bladder prolapse is the most frequently encountered type. It is estimated that up to half of women who have given birth have some degree of prolapse on examination, although only a minority develop symptoms that interfere with daily life. The lifetime risk of surgery for prolapse is around 10--12\%. The condition becomes more common with increasing age and after the menopause, and risk is strongly related to childbirth. It can affect women of all ages, and a minority of cases occur in women who have never given birth.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The pelvic floor is a hammock of muscles (chiefly the levator ani) and connective tissue that normally holds the bladder and other organs in position within the pelvis. Damage or stretching of these supports allows the bladder to be pushed downwards by intra-abdominal pressure. Contributing factors include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vaginal childbirth:} the strongest risk factor, especially with large babies, prolonged or instrumental deliveries, and repeated births
    \item \textbf{Menopause:} falling oestrogen levels reduce the strength and elasticity of pelvic connective tissue
    \item \textbf{Chronic straining:} from long-standing constipation
    \item \textbf{Heavy lifting} and occupations or activities involving repeated straining
    \item \textbf{Chronic cough:} from smoking, asthma, or chronic lung disease
    \item \textbf{Obesity:} increased abdominal pressure is transmitted to the pelvic floor
    \item \textbf{Previous pelvic surgery:} particularly hysterectomy
    \item \textbf{Genetic and constitutional factors:} family history and inherited connective-tissue weakness, such as in Ehlers--Danlos syndromes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms vary widely, and the degree of the bulge correlates poorly with how much trouble it causes. The most common complaints are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A bulge or lump:} a sensation of something coming down in the vagina, which may be visible at the opening or protrude with straining
    \item \textbf{Pelvic pressure or dragging:} a feeling of heaviness that typically worsens after standing for long periods and eases when lying down
    \item \textbf{Urinary problems:} a weak or intermittent stream, difficulty emptying the bladder, a need to strain to void, urgency, and frequency
    \item \textbf{Leakage of urine:} stress incontinence on coughing or effort, or less commonly overflow dribbling from a poorly emptying bladder
    \item \textbf{Recurrent urinary tract infections} related to incomplete bladder emptying
    \item \textbf{Discomfort during sexual activity}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A bulge or pressure in the pelvis can have several causes, and more than one type of prolapse may coexist.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Uterine prolapse, rectocele (bowel prolapse), or enterocele (prolapse of the top of the vagina)
    \item Vaginal cysts, such as a Gartner duct cyst, or benign growths
    \item Uterine fibroids or other pelvic masses causing a sense of pressure
    \item Stress urinary incontinence without any prolapse
    \item Overactive bladder syndrome
    \item Other causes of a swelling at the vaginal opening, including a urethral caruncle or carcinoma
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any new sensation of a bulge in the vagina, worsening pelvic pressure, troublesome urinary symptoms, or pelvic pain that interferes with daily life warrants assessment. Seek help promptly, as early intervention with pelvic floor physiotherapy is more effective than treatment of established prolapse.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe pelvic or lower abdominal pain
    \item Complete inability to pass urine (acute urinary retention)
    \item A prolapse that has become trapped and cannot be replaced, with severe pain, marked swelling, or discolouration (incarceration -- a rare emergency)
    \item High fever with pelvic pain, suggesting infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis is primarily clinical and is made on pelvic examination.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} childbirth history, nature and onset of symptoms, impact on bladder, bowel, and sexual function, and any aggravating activities
    \item \textbf{Pelvic examination:} the prolapse is assessed at rest and with straining or coughing, using the standardised POP-Q grading system, to determine its type and severity
    \item \textbf{Pelvic floor muscle assessment:} strength and endurance of the levator muscles are evaluated
    \item \textbf{Bladder ultrasound:} measures the residual urine volume after voiding to detect incomplete emptying
    \item \textbf{Urinalysis:} to exclude infection in women with urinary symptoms
    \item \textbf{Urodynamic studies:} reserved for women with complex urinary symptoms or when surgery is being considered
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} to a urogynaecologist or gynaecologist for complicated or recurrent cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is individualised according to the severity of symptoms, the degree of prolapse, the woman's age and wishes regarding future fertility, and her general health. Asymptomatic prolapse often requires no treatment other than reassurance.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pelvic floor muscle training:} the first-line treatment for symptomatic prolapse, ideally supervised by a physiotherapist, and effective in reducing symptoms and preventing worsening
    \item \textbf{Vaginal pessary:} a silicone ring or support device inserted and fitted by a trained clinician to hold the bladder up; it provides immediate symptom relief and suits many women, particularly those not wishing surgery
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle modification:} avoiding heavy lifting, treating constipation and chronic cough, losing excess weight, and using a healthy posture during exertion
    \item \textbf{Vaginal oestrogen:} cream or pessaries in postmenopausal women, on medical advice, to strengthen the vaginal tissues
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} anterior vaginal wall repair (colporrhaphy), sometimes with mesh or additional suspension procedures, for bothersome prolapse unresponsive to conservative care; mesh use is now carefully regulated because of complications
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular review for pessary users to ensure correct fit and to check the vaginal wall, and surveillance after surgery for recurrence
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Incomplete bladder emptying with a high residual urine volume and chronic urinary retention
    \item Recurrent urinary tract infections from urine stasis
    \item Ulceration, bleeding, or discharge of the protruding vaginal wall
    \item Gradual worsening if risk factors, such as heavy lifting or constipation, continue
    \item Surgical complications, including bleeding, infection, injury to adjacent organs, recurrence of prolapse, and new or worsened urinary symptoms
    \item Problems related to mesh, including erosion, pain, and infection, in the small proportion of women who have mesh surgery
    \item Psychological distress, embarrassment, and reduced quality of life and sexual function
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Mild prolapse often requires no treatment, and symptoms can fluctuate over time without progressing. Many women improve significantly with supervised pelvic floor physiotherapy and lifestyle change. Vaginal pessaries relieve symptoms in most women who try them and can be used for many years. Surgery is successful in the majority of women, although prolapse can recur years later, and a small proportion require further procedures. Pregnancy and childbirth after treatment can also cause recurrence, which is relevant to the timing of surgery in younger women.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Practise pelvic floor muscle exercises regularly, including during and after pregnancy, to maintain support
    \item Maintain a healthy body weight
    \item Avoid heavy lifting, or use safe lifting techniques and exhale on exertion
    \item Prevent constipation with a high-fibre diet, adequate fluid, and regular activity
    \item Manage chronic cough, and stop smoking
    \item Seek help early for a sensation of a bulge, pelvic pressure, or urinary symptoms, rather than waiting
    \item If a pessary is used, attend scheduled reviews and report any discomfort, discharge, or bleeding
\end{itemize}
