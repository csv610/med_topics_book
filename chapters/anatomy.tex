\chapter{Anatomy}
\index{Anatomy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Anatomy is the study of the structure of the human body -- its organs, bones, muscles, blood vessels, and nerves, and the way they fit together and relate to one another. It is the foundation on which medical understanding is built: to understand disease, clinicians must first know what the healthy body looks like and where each structure sits.

In a health reference, basic anatomy helps people understand symptoms, investigations, and treatments. The body is organised into levels, from cells and tissues up to organs and organ systems, and anatomy is closely linked to physiology, which is the study of how these structures work. The two disciplines are complementary: structure determines function, and disease is often best understood as a change in either.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Anatomy itself is not a disease, but the structures it describes vary from person to person. Structural differences are common: many people have minor variations in blood vessel pathways that are found incidentally during imaging, and no two bodies are exactly alike.

Congenital abnormalities, such as a cleft palate, a heart defect, or an extra kidney, occur in roughly 2 to 3\% of newborns and range from harmless to serious. Later in life, wear and tear, injury, and ageing change body structures, which is why conditions such as osteoarthritis, spinal disc problems, and organ enlargement become more common with age. Understanding normal variation is essential to avoid mistaking a harmless difference for disease.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal variation:} minor differences in organ position, blood vessel branching, and muscle attachment are common and usually harmless
    \item \textbf{Developmental (congenital) causes:} errors during formation in the womb can lead to missing, duplicated, or malformed structures
    \item \textbf{Genetic causes:} inherited conditions that affect structure, such as Marfan syndrome, which causes a tall build with loose joints and heart problems, or achondroplasia, which causes short-limbed dwarfism
    \item \textbf{Trauma:} fractures, dislocations, and soft-tissue injuries that change the shape or alignment of structures
    \item \textbf{Degeneration and ageing:} thinning of cartilage, loss of bone density, and stiffening of blood vessels alter anatomy over time
    \item \textbf{Disease effects:} tumours, swelling, or organ enlargement can displace or compress neighbouring structures, which explains many symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Problems related to body structure present in characteristic ways:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A lump, swelling, or visible asymmetry of the body
    \item Pain or tenderness related to a specific structure, such as a bone, joint, or organ
    \item Reduced movement, weakness, or altered sensation when nerves or muscles are affected
    \item Symptoms that arise because a structure compresses something near it, such as a disc pressing on a nerve
    \item Findings on examination, including an enlarged organ, abnormal heart sounds, or a change in body contour
    \item Incidental findings: structures that look unusual on imaging but cause no symptoms at all
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Normal anatomical variation versus a true abnormality
    \item A benign finding, such as a cyst or a harmless variation, versus a serious one, such as a tumour
    \item Congenital versus acquired changes
    \item A structural cause of symptoms versus a functional cause with normal anatomy
    \item Localised disease versus a systemic disease affecting many structures
    \item A recent injury versus long-standing or age-related changes
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if you notice a new lump or swelling, persistent pain in a specific body region, or any change in how a part of the body looks or functions.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, difficulty breathing, or sudden weakness on one side of the body
    \item A sudden, severe injury with deformity or the inability to move a limb
    \item Heavy bleeding or a wound that exposes bone or deep tissue
    \item Signs of stroke, remembered with FAST: Face, Arms, Speech, Time
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} describing where and how the problem feels and locating it on the body are the first steps
    \item \textbf{Plain X-ray:} good for bones, joints, and the chest
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} images soft structures such as the liver, gallbladder, kidneys, and thyroid
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} detailed cross-sections of bones and organs, useful in trauma and for planning surgery
    \item \textbf{MRI:} the best method for soft tissues, including the brain, spinal cord, joints, and muscles
    \item \textbf{Endoscopy and biopsy:} looking inside hollow organs directly, and sampling tissue when structure alone cannot answer the question
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reassurance and observation:} many anatomical variations need no treatment at all
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy and exercise:} to strengthen and rehabilitate muscles, joints, and posture
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} to treat the disease process affecting the structure, such as anti-inflammatories for arthritis
    \item \textbf{Procedures:} drainage of fluid collections, injections, or minimally invasive surgery
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} repair, removal, or reconstruction of damaged or diseased structures
    \item \textbf{Prosthetics and assistive devices:} splints, braces, or artificial joints to replace lost structure and function
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive loss of function if a structural problem is not corrected
    \item Compression of nearby structures, such as nerves or blood vessels
    \item Complications of surgery, including infection, bleeding, and scarring
    \item Unsatisfactory healing, such as a fracture that heals in poor alignment
    \item Psychological effects of a change in body shape or function
    \item Limitations on daily activity, work, and quality of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends entirely on which structure is involved and why. Many anatomical variations never cause problems and need no treatment. Congenital abnormalities have very different outcomes: some correct themselves with growth, others need surgery, and a few are serious from birth. Acquired structural problems such as fractures usually heal well with appropriate care, and age-related changes are often managed rather than cured. Modern imaging and surgery mean that even complex structural problems can often be corrected or compensated for, with good long-term function.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep active to maintain strong bones, muscles, and joints
    \item Protect the body from injury with seatbelts, helmets, and safe techniques in sport and at work
    \item Maintain a healthy weight to reduce wear on joints and organs
    \item Avoid smoking and limit alcohol to protect blood vessels and organs
    \item Follow screening and check-up recommendations to detect structural problems early
    \item Seek prompt medical advice for new lumps, persistent pain, or changes in body function
\end{itemize}
