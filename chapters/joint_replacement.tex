\chapter{Joint Replacement}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Joint replacement is a surgical operation in which a damaged joint is removed and replaced with an artificial joint made of metal, plastic, or ceramic. It is most commonly performed on the hip and knee, and also on the shoulder, elbow, ankle, and smaller joints. Joint replacement is used when severe arthritis causes pain, stiffness, and disability that no longer respond to exercise, medicines, and other conservative treatment. The operation is highly successful, with most people experiencing major pain relief and improved mobility, and hip and knee replacements are among the most effective operations in all of medicine. Recovery involves hospital care, physiotherapy, and gradual return to activity.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Around 100,000 hip and knee replacements are performed in Australia each year, and the numbers continue to rise as the population ages and surgery becomes safer. Osteoarthritis is the reason for the great majority of replacements, with rheumatoid arthritis, fracture, and avascular necrosis accounting for most of the rest. The average age for hip replacement is around 68 and for knee replacement around 69, although younger people with severe joint damage also have surgery. Most replacements last 15 to 25 years, and revision surgery is needed in a minority.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Joint replacement is performed when the joint surface has been destroyed by disease. In osteoarthritis, cartilage wears away until bone rubs on bone, causing pain, deformity, and stiffness. In rheumatoid arthritis, inflammation destroys cartilage and bone. When conservative management no longer controls pain, the surgeon removes the damaged surfaces and replaces them: in a hip replacement, the femoral head and socket are replaced; in a knee replacement, the ends of the femur and tibia and the kneecap surface are resurfaced. The artificial joint is fixed to the bone, restoring smooth, pain-free movement. Success depends on good bone, correct alignment, and a well-fitting implant.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe joint pain that limits daily activities and sleep
    \item Pain that has failed to improve with exercise, weight control, and medicines
    \item Stiffness and reduced range of movement
    \item Difficulty walking, climbing stairs, and getting out of chairs
    \item Deformity and instability of the joint
    \item X-ray changes of advanced arthritis
    \item After surgery: a hospital stay of a few days, followed by physiotherapy
    \item Most people walk with assistance within a day and return to normal activity within weeks to months
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The decision to offer joint replacement follows full assessment.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conservative treatment:} exercise, weight loss, physiotherapy, and medicines
    \item \textbf{Joint injections:} steroids and lubricants
    \item \textbf{Other causes of pain:} referred pain from the spine or hip, bursitis, and vascular disease
    \item \textbf{Other surgical options:} arthroscopy, osteotomy, and partial replacement
    \item \textbf{Fitness and expectations:} the benefits and risks are weighed for each person
    \item \textbf{Alternatives:} walking aids and pacing when surgery is not appropriate
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with severe joint pain should be assessed to determine whether conservative treatment can help and whether joint replacement is appropriate. Urgent review is needed for a hot, swollen joint with fever, sudden deformity, or inability to bear weight. Anyone considering surgery should discuss the procedure, recovery, and risks with an orthopaedic surgeon.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A hot, swollen, painful joint with fever, which may indicate infection
    \item Sudden severe pain or inability to move a joint after injury
    \item Chest pain or breathlessness after surgery, which may indicate a blood clot
    \item A wound that is bleeding heavily, or signs of a severe reaction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the impact of pain on daily life and the joint's function
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} the primary imaging, showing the severity of arthritis
    \item \textbf{MRI:} in selected cases for soft tissue and early damage
    \item \textbf{Preoperative assessment:} blood tests, heart and lung review, and medicines optimisation
    \item \textbf{Bone density:} where bone quality is a concern
    \item \textbf{Assessment of expectations:} discussing realistic outcomes and recovery
    \item \textbf{Review by a multidisciplinary team:} including physiotherapy and occupational therapy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Preoperative preparation:} exercise, weight loss, and optimisation of health
    \item \textbf{The operation:} removal of damaged surfaces and placement of the artificial joint
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} pain management, early mobilisation, and prevention of complications
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} exercises from day one, with graded rehabilitation
    \item \textbf{Home recovery:} walking aids, wound care, and gradual return to activity
    \item \textbf{Complication prevention:} blood thinners, antibiotics, and early mobilisation
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review of wound, function, and the implant
    \item \textbf{Revision surgery:} for the minority whose replacement fails years later
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection of the joint, a serious but uncommon complication
    \item Blood clots in the legs or lungs, reduced by preventive treatment
    \item Bleeding, bruising, and wound problems
    \item Dislocation of the new hip in the early months
    \item Nerve and blood vessel injury, which is rare
    \item Different leg lengths and stiffness
    \item Loosening, wear, or fracture of the implant over years
    \item Anaesthesia risks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Joint replacement is one of the most successful operations in medicine. Around 90 per cent of people report excellent or good outcomes, with dramatic pain relief and improved mobility, and most implants last 15 to 25 years. Complications are uncommon and largely preventable. Recovery takes commitment, with physiotherapy and gradual return to activity over three to six months. For people with severe arthritis, joint replacement restores quality of life and independence.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and stay active before surgery
    \item Prepare the home for recovery: clear paths, raised seating, and aids
    \item Stop smoking and optimise health before surgery
    \item Follow fasting and medicine instructions before the operation
    \item Do physiotherapy exercises faithfully after surgery
    \item Attend follow-up appointments
    \item Protect the joint and seek advice before returning to sport
    \item Report fever, wound problems, or new pain promptly
    \item Take preventive medicines for blood clots as advised
\end{itemize}
