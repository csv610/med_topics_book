\section*{Definition and Overview}
A submental abscess is a collection of pus that forms in the midline of the neck, beneath the chin, in the space between the chin, the floor of the mouth, and the hyoid bone. It is a deep neck space infection and, like abscesses in the adjacent submandibular and sublingual spaces, it usually begins as a dental infection, an infected lymph node, or infection of the skin of the chin and lower lip. The submental space sits at the front of the neck, immediately in front of the airway, which gives the condition its importance: as pus collects and pressure rises, the infection can spread backward and upward into the spaces surrounding the airway and the floor of the mouth, where it can obstruct breathing.

Submental abscesses are less common than submandibular abscesses, but they behave in the same way and demand the same respect. They present as a painful, tense swelling under the chin, often with fever and difficulty swallowing, and they progress quickly if untreated. Because the infection travels along defined planes of the neck, a small swelling can become a dangerous collection within a day or two. This chapter covers how these abscesses arise, the people most at risk, the features that distinguish them from other neck lumps, and the modern treatment, which combines antibiotics with surgical drainage. It also explains the warning signs that call for urgent care, because with deep neck infections, safety depends on acting early.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Submental abscesses are uncommon relative to dental abscesses as a whole, but they are a recognised and important part of the spectrum of deep neck space infections seen in local emergency departments. They occur at all ages, from children, where they typically follow infected lymph nodes draining dental or throat infections, to older adults, where dental disease and poor oral hygiene predominate. Men are affected somewhat more often than women, consistent with the pattern of deep neck space infections generally, and the condition is seen more frequently in people with poor access to dental care, diabetes, smoking, and alcohol use, all of which increase the burden of dental disease.

The public health importance of these infections is out of proportion to their frequency. A submental abscess that spreads can become Ludwig's angina, a bilateral infection of the whole floor of the mouth that threatens the airway, and a proportion of patients still require intensive care. Trends over recent decades in developed countries show a fall in the most severe presentations, thanks to antibiotics and emergency dental services, but the condition has not disappeared, and hospital series continue to report admissions for submental and submandibular infections each year. The message is the same as it has always been: deep neck space infection is preventable with good dental care and early treatment of tooth and mouth infections.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The submental space lies in the midline beneath the chin, bounded above by the muscles of the floor of the mouth, below by the skin and fascia, and on either side by the anterior bellies of the digastric muscles. It is a potential space, normally containing lymph nodes that drain the central lower lip, the front of the gums, the tip of the tongue, and the floor of the mouth. When infection reaches these nodes or spreads directly from adjacent tissues, inflammation, pus, and swelling accumulate, and the collection then extends along the fascial planes to involve the floor of the mouth and the airway region.

The bacteria responsible are those that normally live in the mouth, a mixture of anaerobic and aerobic species. Because the mouth is rich in bacteria and the neck fascial planes offer little resistance, an untreated dental focus can seed the space readily. The progression from a small infected node to a pus-filled collection is driven by the continued production of infected material and by the body's inflammatory response, which seals the collection within the fascial boundaries and creates the characteristic tense, tender swelling.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dental infection:} the commonest cause in adults, with infection from the lower front teeth or the roots of the lower molars tracking toward the midline
    \item \textbf{Lymph node suppuration:} infected submental lymph nodes, often draining a tooth, mouth, or throat infection, enlarge and turn into an abscess
    \item \textbf{Skin and lip infections:} infection of the chin, lower lip, or a skin wound spreads to the submental nodes
    \item \textbf{Sialadenitis:} infection of a sublingual salivary gland can involve the midline floor of the mouth
    \item \textbf{Trauma:} penetrating injury or surgery in the region can introduce infection
    \item \textbf{Immune compromise and diabetes:} poor immune control makes infections more likely to develop and spread
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Swelling under the chin:} a tense, rounded swelling in the midline that may be fluctuant as pus accumulates
    \item \textbf{Pain and tenderness:} pain over the swelling that is worse with swallowing, chewing, or moving the neck
    \item \textbf{Fever:} raised temperature with malaise and a general feeling of being unwell
    \item \textbf{Difficulty swallowing:} pain on swallowing and drooling in more severe cases
    \item \textbf{Redness and warmth:} the skin over the swelling becomes red and hot as the infection progresses
    \item \textbf{Neck stiffness and restricted movement:} the person holds the neck still because movement hurts
    \item \textbf{Speech changes:} a muffled voice as the floor of the mouth is involved
    \item \textbf{Later airway signs:} progressive difficulty breathing and a feeling of throat tightness indicate the infection is threatening the airway
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Submandibular abscess:} an abscess off the midline, under the jaw, usually from a lower molar tooth; the two can coexist as infection spreads across the neck
    \item \textbf{Ludwig's angina:} bilateral, rapidly progressive infection of the whole floor of the mouth with the tongue pushed upward and the airway at risk; an extension of the same process and managed with equal urgency
    \item \textbf{Submental lymphadenitis:} enlarged infected lymph nodes without a true abscess; usually resolves with antibiotics, though it can progress
    \item \textbf{Dermoid and epidermoid cysts:} slow-growing, painless midline lumps under the chin that can become infected and resemble an abscess
    \item \textbf{Thyroglossal duct cyst:} a midline cyst that moves when the tongue is protruded, usually with no fever until it becomes infected
    \item \textbf{Salivary gland disease:} sublingual or submandibular gland infection or a ranula, a mucus-filled cyst of the floor of the mouth
    \item \textbf{Lipoma or benign neck lump:} a soft, slow-growing, non-tender swelling with no features of infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
A person with swelling under the chin and fever, tooth pain, or difficulty swallowing should be assessed promptly by a general practitioner, dentist, or emergency department. Early antibiotics and dental treatment can resolve the infection before a true abscess forms, and early diagnosis makes treatment simpler and safer.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing or a sensation that the throat is closing
    \item Rapid swelling that spreads across the whole neck, protrudes the tongue, or rises into the face
    \item A muffled voice with drooling and difficulty swallowing
    \item High fever with confusion, drowsiness, or feeling seriously unwell
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} assessment of the swelling for tenderness, fluctuance, and extent, examination of the mouth and teeth, and a check for airway and swallowing involvement
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} a full blood count and inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein assess the severity of infection
    \item \textbf{Computed tomography (CT) scan:} the definitive imaging test, defining the abscess, its position, and its spread into surrounding neck spaces
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} useful for confirming whether a superficial swelling contains pus and for guiding aspiration
    \item \textbf{Microbiological culture:} pus obtained at surgery is cultured to identify the organisms and guide antibiotic treatment
    \item \textbf{Dental imaging:} panoramic X-rays or dental photographs identify the source tooth
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} ear, nose, and throat (ENT) and oral and maxillofacial surgeons manage the drainage and source control
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intravenous antibiotics:} broad-spectrum antibiotics are started immediately to cover the mouth bacteria, then refined once culture results return
    \item \textbf{Surgical drainage:} the definitive treatment; the pus is drained under general anaesthesia through a small incision in the midline, and a drain is often placed
    \item \textbf{Source control:} the infected tooth or dental focus is treated by extraction or root canal, at the time of drainage or soon after
    \item \textbf{Airway management:} because the swelling sits in front of the airway, plans for securing the airway during anaesthesia are made in advance; severe cases may need an awake breathing tube or tracheostomy
    \item \textbf{Pain relief and fluids:} analgesia and intravenous fluids are given until the person can eat and drink comfortably
    \item \textbf{Mouth care:} warm saline rinses and gentle oral hygiene promote healing after surgery
    \item \textbf{Underlying health review:} blood glucose control in diabetes and assessment of immune function if infections are recurrent
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Airway obstruction:} the most dangerous complication, as infection spreads backward from the submental space toward the throat
    \item \textbf{Spread to the floor of the mouth:} progression to Ludwig's angina, with bilateral swelling that elevates the tongue and threatens the airway
    \item \textbf{Spread into the chest:} infection tracking down the neck causes mediastinitis, a life-threatening infection around the heart and lungs
    \item \textbf{Septicaemia:} bacteria in the bloodstream cause sepsis with organ failure
    \item \textbf{Jugular vein thrombosis:} infection of the neck veins with risk of blood clot spread
    \item \textbf{Recurrence:} incomplete drainage or failure to treat the source tooth allows the abscess to reform
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for submental abscess is very good when it is recognised and treated promptly. Most patients who undergo antibiotics and surgical drainage recover fully within one to two weeks, with the swelling settling and swallowing returning to normal. The factors that matter most are the speed of diagnosis, the presence of airway compromise at presentation, and whether the source of the infection is dealt with. Patients treated early, before the infection spreads, typically have an uncomplicated recovery. Those who present late, with spreading infection or breathing difficulty, face a longer and more serious illness requiring intensive care, but even in this group the combination of surgery, antibiotics, and modern respiratory support produces good outcomes for most people. Long-term recovery is determined largely by dental health: treating the source tooth and improving oral hygiene prevent recurrence and protect against future episodes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Brush twice daily, floss regularly, and see a dentist for routine check-ups
    \item Treat toothache and dental decay early instead of waiting until pain becomes severe
    \item Do not ignore swelling under the chin, particularly with fever or pain on swallowing
    \item If antibiotics are prescribed for a mouth or dental infection, complete the full course
    \item Manage diabetes and other conditions that weaken the immune system carefully
    \item Keep the mouth clean after any dental procedure, and seek review if swelling develops afterwards
    \item Follow up after surgery as arranged, and report any return of swelling, pain, or fever promptly
\end{itemize}
