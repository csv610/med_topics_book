\chapter{Child Immunization}
\index{Child Immunization}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Child immunization is the process of administering vaccines to infants, children, and adolescents to protect them against serious infectious diseases. Vaccines work by exposing the immune system to a weakened, inactivated, or partial form of a germ so that the body produces protective antibodies and immune memory without developing the full disease. If the child later encounters the real infection, the immune system responds rapidly and usually prevents the illness or greatly reduces its severity. Childhood vaccines are delivered according to a national immunization schedule that begins shortly after birth and continues through the teenage years. The schedule is designed so that protection is in place as early in life as possible, because several vaccine-preventable diseases are most dangerous in infancy.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Vaccination is one of the most effective public health measures in history. High childhood immunization rates have dramatically reduced death and disability from polio, measles, whooping cough, diphtheria, tetanus, hepatitis B, and bacterial meningitis. In high-income countries, around 9 in 10 children are fully immunized at each scheduled age, although coverage is lower in some regional, remote, and disadvantaged communities. Outbreaks of measles and whooping cough still occur when immunization rates fall, particularly where vaccine hesitancy is common. Measles is among the most contagious human infections, so a high proportion of the community must be immune (often above 95\%) to prevent sustained spread. Global immunization programs have eradicated smallpox and brought wild poliovirus to the brink of elimination.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Vaccines stimulate the immune system in a controlled way so that protection develops without causing the disease itself:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Live attenuated vaccines} contain a weakened form of the virus or bacterium that multiplies briefly but rarely causes illness (for example, MMR and chickenpox)
    \item \textbf{Inactivated vaccines} contain killed germs or parts of germs and cannot cause infection (for example, whooping cough and hepatitis A)
    \item \textbf{Subunit and toxoid vaccines} contain specific pieces of the germ or an inactivated toxin (for example, tetanus and diphtheria)
    \item \textbf{mRNA and viral-vector vaccines} instruct cells to produce a harmless piece of the germ, triggering immunity without using the live organism
    \item \textbf{Combined vaccines} protect against several diseases in one injection, reducing the number of needles a child needs
    \item \textbf{Oral and nasal vaccines} (for example, rotavirus and live influenza vaccine) are given by routes that mimic natural exposure
\end{itemize}

After a dose, the immune system takes about two weeks to develop a full response, which is why several vaccines require multiple doses or booster injections to achieve long-lasting protection. Boosters re-expose the immune system later in life, maintaining antibody levels and immune memory for years or decades.

\section*{Clinical Features}
A child who is unwell after a vaccine usually develops only mild, short-lived symptoms while the immune system responds:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Soreness, redness, or swelling at the injection site
    \item Low-grade fever lasting a day or two
    \item Mild irritability, drowsiness, or poor appetite
    \item Headache or muscle aches in older children
    \item A mild rash or swollen glands after some live vaccines (such as MMR)
    \item Mild stomach upset after the oral rotavirus vaccine
\end{itemize}

These features typically begin within hours of vaccination and settle within one to three days. More serious reactions, such as a severe allergic reaction or a high fever with a febrile convulsion, are rare. They usually occur within minutes to hours of vaccination, which is why a short period of observation may be recommended after the injection.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A fever or rash after a vaccine can be mistaken for an intercurrent viral illness, because many children acquire everyday infections around the time they are vaccinated
    \item A swollen limb or injection-site reaction can occasionally resemble cellulitis or an abscess, particularly if the area becomes increasingly red and tender
    \item In a child who is genuinely unwell, a coincidental vaccine reaction should not mask the diagnosis of conditions such as influenza, meningitis, or measles
    \item Persistent or unusual symptoms should be assessed for causes other than vaccination
    \item Severe fussiness and a bulging fontanelle in an infant could indicate meningitis rather than a simple vaccine reaction
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most vaccine reactions are mild and settle within a day or two. See a doctor if a child has a high fever lasting more than a couple of days, a severe or spreading rash, marked swelling of the face or throat, worsening symptoms, or a seizure at any time after vaccination.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, wheezing, or hoarseness after vaccination
    \item Swelling of the face, lips, or tongue
    \item Collapse, floppiness, or a convulsion
    \item Any sudden, severe reaction within minutes to hours of a vaccine
    \item An unusually high fever with lethargy, difficulty waking, or a stiff neck
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immunization history check:} review of the child's record to identify which vaccines are due, overdue, or missed
    \item \textbf{Assessment of reactions:} careful documentation of symptoms, their timing relative to vaccination, and a full examination
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} antibody levels may be checked in special situations, such as suspected poor response or after exposure to a vaccine-preventable disease
    \item \textbf{Notifiable disease reporting:} suspected cases of vaccine-preventable infections are reported to health authorities so that outbreaks can be investigated
    \item \textbf{Allergy evaluation:} children who have had a suspected severe reaction may be referred for specialist assessment before further doses
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Catching up on missed vaccines:} a doctor or nurse can plan a schedule to bring an overdue child up to date, respecting minimum intervals between doses
    \item \textbf{Symptom relief:} paracetamol or ibuprofen at a weight-appropriate dose for fever or injection-site discomfort; cool compresses for swelling
    \item \textbf{Documented medical exemptions:} children with genuine medical contraindications, such as a severe allergy to a vaccine component, can be formally exempted
    \item \textbf{Management of severe reactions:} anaphylaxis is treated as an emergency with intramuscular adrenaline and urgent medical care
    \item \textbf{Advice and reassurance:} doctors, nurses, and immunization clinics can answer concerns about vaccine safety and side effects
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Most children experience no significant complications from vaccination
    \item Rare severe allergic reactions (anaphylaxis) occur in roughly 1 in a million doses
    \item High fever with a febrile convulsion is uncommon and usually has no long-term consequences
    \item The greater risk is \textbf{not} vaccinating: unimmunized children can contract measles, whooping cough, or meningitis, which may cause pneumonia, brain damage, or death
    \item In rare cases, live vaccines can cause disease in children with severely weakened immune systems, which is why such children may require specialist advice
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Children who are immunized on schedule are protected against a wide range of serious diseases, and protection usually lasts for years or decades. Most vaccine reactions are brief and fully resolve without treatment. Very rare side effects are investigated by national safety monitoring systems so that patterns can be detected and acted upon. When immunization rates are high across a community, diseases can be controlled or even eliminated, as has happened with smallpox globally and with polio in most of the world. Some infections, such as whooping cough and measles, still circulate, so keeping boosters up to date remains important throughout life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Follow the national immunization schedule from birth through adolescence
    \item Keep an up-to-date record of your child's vaccinations and the reason for any missed doses
    \item If a vaccine is missed, book a catch-up appointment as soon as possible
    \item Tell the nurse or doctor about any previous vaccine reactions, allergies, or immune problems
    \item Give paracetamol or ibuprofen for mild fever or soreness only if needed
    \item Ask trusted sources, such as your doctor or immunization clinic, about any concerns rather than relying on social media
    \item Encourage good hygiene and stay home when sick, since vaccines do not cover every infection
\end{itemize}
