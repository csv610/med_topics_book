\chapter{Gallbladder and Biliary Disease}
\index{Gallbladder and Biliary Disease}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The biliary system is the network of organs and ducts that produces, stores, and delivers bile, the digestive fluid that helps break down fats. It includes the liver, which makes bile; the gallbladder, which stores and concentrates it; and the bile ducts, which carry it to the small intestine. Diseases of this system are common and include gallstones (cholelithiasis), inflammation of the gallbladder (cholecystitis), inflammation of the bile ducts (cholangitis), and obstruction of the ducts by stones or tumours. Symptoms typically include pain in the upper right abdomen, nausea, and jaundice. Many biliary conditions are managed with diet and medicines, while others need surgery or procedures. This chapter provides an overview of gallbladder and biliary disease.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Gallstones are the most common biliary disease, affecting around 10--15% of adults, and they become more common with age, affecting up to a third of people over 60. Women are affected about twice as often as men, particularly during and after pregnancy, and risk is higher with obesity, rapid weight loss, and a family history. Most people with gallstones never develop symptoms, but around 1--4% per year develop biliary pain or complications such as cholecystitis, cholangitis, or pancreatitis. Gallbladder surgery is one of the most common operations in many countries, with tens of thousands performed each year.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bile composition:} bile contains cholesterol, bile salts, and bilirubin; stones form when these precipitate out of solution
    \item \textbf{Cholesterol stones:} the most common type, forming when bile contains too much cholesterol relative to bile salts, as with obesity, rapid weight loss, and some medicines
    \item \textbf{Pigment stones:} form from excess bilirubin, as in haemolysis and some liver diseases
    \item \textbf{Gallbladder stasis:} a sluggish gallbladder, from fasting, pregnancy, or parenteral nutrition, allows stones to form
    \item \textbf{Biliary colic:} a stone temporarily blocks the cystic duct, causing pain that comes in waves
    \item \textbf{Cholecystitis:} persistent blockage causes inflammation and infection of the gallbladder wall
    \item \textbf{Cholangitis:} a stone blocking the common bile duct allows bacteria to infect the bile ducts, a life-threatening emergency
    \item \textbf{Pancreatitis:} a stone blocking the shared pancreatic duct inflames the pancreas
    \item \textbf{Jaundice:} obstruction of bile flow backs up bilirubin, yellowing the skin and eyes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Biliary colic: severe pain in the upper right abdomen or between the shoulder blades, coming in waves, often after fatty meals, lasting minutes to hours
    \item Nausea and vomiting with the pain
    \item Cholecystitis: persistent severe pain, fever, and tenderness in the right upper abdomen
    \item Cholangitis: fever, jaundice, and abdominal pain (the classic trio), with severe illness
    \item Pancreatitis: severe upper abdominal pain radiating to the back, with vomiting
    \item Jaundice with dark urine and pale stools when the ducts are blocked
    \item Indigestion and discomfort after fatty meals, though many people with stones have no symptoms
    \item The complications present as acute emergencies needing urgent care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Upper abdominal pain has many possible causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Peptic ulcer disease and gastro-oesophageal reflux
    \item Heart attack and other cardiac causes
    \item Pancreatitis and liver disease
    \item Irritable bowel syndrome and functional dyspepsia
    \item Kidney stones and urinary causes
    \item Pneumonia and pleurisy
    \item Imaging and blood tests distinguish biliary disease from these
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with recurrent upper abdominal pain after meals should be assessed. Sudden severe pain, fever, jaundice, or pain with vomiting requires urgent care.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain that does not settle, especially with fever
    \item Jaundice with fever and shaking chills, which may indicate cholangitis
    \item Signs of pancreatitis: severe pain radiating to the back with vomiting
    \item Chest pain or breathlessness, which may mimic or accompany biliary disease
    \item Collapse or confusion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the pattern of pain, and tenderness in the right upper abdomen
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} the first-line test, showing gallstones, gallbladder wall thickening, and dilated ducts
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} liver function tests and bilirubin, inflammatory markers, and pancreatic enzymes
    \item \textbf{MRCP:} magnetic resonance imaging of the bile ducts for suspected duct stones
    \item \textbf{ERCP:} an endoscopic procedure that both images and treats duct stones
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} for complications and in emergencies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asymptomatic gallstones:} usually no treatment; surveillance and lifestyle measures
    \item \textbf{Biliary colic:} analgesia, a lower-fat diet, and planned gallbladder removal if attacks recur
    \item \textbf{Cholecystectomy:} surgical removal of the gallbladder, usually laparoscopically, for symptomatic stones and cholecystitis
    \item \textbf{Cholecystitis:} antibiotics, fluids, and surgery, often early
    \item \textbf{Duct stones:} ERCP to remove the stones from the duct, before or after gallbladder surgery
    \item \textbf{Cholangitis:} an emergency with intravenous antibiotics, fluids, and urgent duct drainage
    \item \textbf{Pancreatitis:} hospital care with fluids, pain relief, and treatment of the cause
    \item \textbf{Diet:} a healthy, lower-fat diet, though it does not dissolve existing stones
    \item \textbf{Other treatments:} ursodeoxycholic acid for selected small cholesterol stones, and lithotripsy in some centres
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute cholecystitis with gangrene or perforation of the gallbladder
    \item Cholangitis, a life-threatening infection of the bile ducts
    \item Acute pancreatitis, which can be severe
    \item Obstructive jaundice and liver damage
    \item Gallstone ileus, a rare bowel obstruction from a large stone
    \item Complications of surgery, including bile duct injury
    \item Increased risk of gallbladder cancer with very long-standing untreated disease, though rare
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most biliary disease is successfully treated. Symptomatic gallstones are cured by gallbladder removal, which is safe and performed as keyhole surgery in the vast majority of cases, with most people home the same day or next day. Duct stones are removed by ERCP, and cholecystitis and pancreatitis resolve with prompt treatment. Serious complications such as cholangitis are dangerous but respond well to urgent care. After gallbladder removal, most people digest food normally, with some needing only to moderate fatty meals.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and avoid rapid weight loss
    \item Eat a diet high in fibre and low in saturated fat
    \item Exercise regularly
    \item Seek early assessment for recurrent upper abdominal pain
    \item Attend urgent care for severe pain, fever, or jaundice
    \item Follow the post-operative diet advice after gallbladder surgery
    \item Do not ignore jaundice or dark urine
\end{itemize}
