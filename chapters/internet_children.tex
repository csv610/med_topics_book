\chapter{Internet and Children}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The internet is an integral part of childhood and adolescence, used for learning, connection, entertainment, and creativity. This chapter addresses how to support children to use the internet safely, the benefits and risks of online life, and how parents and carers can set boundaries and respond to problems. While the online world offers enormous opportunities, it also carries risks, including exposure to inappropriate content, cyberbullying, online grooming, excessive screen time, and the effects of social media on mental health. A balanced approach combines clear family rules, open communication, privacy protection, and active involvement in children's online lives.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Australian children are online from an early age, with most using screens daily and many having their own device by primary school age. Around one in five Australian children has experienced cyberbullying, and social media use is near-universal among teenagers, with many reporting negative effects on sleep, body image, and mood alongside its benefits of connection and support. Excessive screen time is associated with poorer sleep, less physical activity, and lower wellbeing in some studies, while online education and creative tools benefit learning. The risks are greatest for vulnerable children, and parental involvement is strongly protective.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The internet affects children's health and development through multiple pathways. Excessive screen time displaces sleep, physical activity, and face-to-face interaction, which are essential for development. Social media platforms are designed to maximise engagement, and their use can drive comparison, validation-seeking, and fear of missing out, which contribute to anxiety and low mood in susceptible young people. Exposure to harmful content, including violence, self-harm, and grooming, can traumatise or manipulate children, while cyberbullying can follow children home and cause persistent distress. Protective factors include age-appropriate access, parental monitoring, media literacy, and strong offline relationships.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Excessive or compulsive use of screens and gaming
    \item Disrupted sleep, tiredness, and poor school performance
    \item Withdrawal, irritability, or distress when offline
    \item Secrecy about online activity or reluctance to discuss it
    \item Signs of cyberbullying: withdrawal, falling grades, or reluctance to attend school
    \item Exposure to inappropriate content, sometimes shared by peers
    \item Changes in mood, body image concerns, and anxiety linked to social media
    \item Positive features: learning, creativity, and connection with friends
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Problems that emerge around internet use may have other or additional causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mental health conditions:} anxiety, depression, and ADHD may present with heavy screen use
    \item \textbf{Learning difficulties:} may be masked or exacerbated by screen habits
    \item \textbf{Sleep disorders:} poor sleep from screens versus other causes
    \item \textbf{Cyberbullying versus other bullying:} both require the same protective response
    \item \textbf{Family conflict:} arguments about screens may reflect broader issues
    \item \textbf{Social difficulties:} children may retreat online when struggling socially offline
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents should seek professional help if a child's online use is affecting their health, schoolwork, or relationships; if there are concerns about self-harm, depression, or anxiety; or if there has been exposure to grooming or harmful content. Support is available from GPs, school counsellors, and child and adolescent mental health services. Cyberbullying should be reported to the school and, where serious, to the eSafety Commissioner.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Immediate risk of self-harm or suicide
    \item A child who has been threatened with violence or is in immediate danger
    \item A child who is severely distressed, confused, or unsafe
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Family discussion:} exploring screen use, what the child does online, and any concerns
    \item \textbf{Assessment of health:} sleep, mood, school performance, and physical activity
    \item \textbf{Screening for mental health problems:} with a GP or psychologist
    \item \textbf{School involvement:} discussing behaviour and friendship issues at school
    \item \textbf{Reporting tools:} the eSafety Commissioner for serious online harms
    \item \textbf{Device and privacy review:} checking settings, apps, and parental controls
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Family media plan:} agreed rules on screen time, apps, and device-free times such as meals and bedtime
    \item \textbf{Parental controls and privacy settings:} filtering, time limits, and age-appropriate access
    \item \textbf{Co-use and open conversation:} using media together and talking regularly about online life
    \item \textbf{Bedroom and bedtime rules:} keeping devices out of bedrooms and away at night
    \item \textbf{Teaching media literacy:} helping children recognise advertising, misinformation, and harmful content
    \item \textbf{Responding to cyberbullying:} supporting the child, documenting the abuse, and reporting it
    \item \textbf{Professional help:} counselling for mental health impacts and problematic use
    \item \textbf{Modelling:} parents demonstrating healthy screen habits
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sleep deprivation and chronic tiredness
    \item Reduced physical activity, obesity, and poor posture
    \item Anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem
    \item Cyberbullying, grooming, and exposure to harmful content
    \item Poor school performance and disengagement
    \item Problematic or addictive patterns of gaming and social media
    \item Damage to family relationships from conflict over screens
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With sensible boundaries and involved parenting, most children use the internet happily and safely, gaining real benefits in learning, creativity, and connection. Problems such as cyberbullying and excessive use respond well to early, consistent intervention, and most children's wellbeing recovers when sleep, activity, and offline relationships are restored. Children who develop mental health problems benefit from professional support, and the outcomes for children whose difficulties are recognised early are good.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Start early: set clear rules and stay involved in your child's online life
    \item Keep devices out of bedrooms and away from the dinner table
    \item Agree on screen time limits that leave room for sleep, activity, and homework
    \item Use parental controls but also teach your child to make good choices
    \item Talk regularly and without judgement about what your child does online
    \item Learn the apps and platforms your child uses
    \item Encourage offline friendships, sport, and hobbies
    \item Model healthy screen habits yourself
    \item Report serious online harms to the eSafety Commissioner
    \item Seek help early if you are worried about your child's wellbeing
\end{itemize}
