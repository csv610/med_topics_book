\chapter{Golfer's Elbow}
\index{Golfer's Elbow}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Golfer's elbow, medically known as medial epicondylitis, is a painful condition affecting the tendons that attach the forearm muscles to the inner side of the elbow. It is an overuse injury in which repeated gripping, wrist flexion, or forearm activity causes small tears and inflammation at the attachment point of the common flexor tendon. Despite the name, it affects golfers but also occurs in people who do repetitive activities such as gardening, carpentry, racket sports, and computer work. Golfer's elbow causes pain and tenderness on the inner elbow that can radiate into the forearm. It usually improves with rest, activity modification, physiotherapy, and graduated strengthening.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Golfer's elbow is less common than its counterpart tennis elbow (lateral epicondylitis), affecting around 1% of the population, with the highest rates in people aged 40--60. It affects men and women equally and is common in occupations and sports involving repetitive wrist and finger flexion or gripping. Golfers, particularly those who play frequently or with poor technique, are at risk, as are tradespeople, musicians, and office workers. Most cases are mild and respond to conservative treatment, though symptoms can persist for months in some people.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Golfer's elbow results from repetitive strain on the tendons of the forearm flexor muscles.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Attachment:} the common flexor tendon attaches the muscles that flex the wrist and fingers to the medial epicondyle, the bony bump on the inner elbow
    \item \textbf{Overload:} repetitive or forceful wrist flexion, gripping, and forearm rotation place excessive load on the tendon
    \item \textbf{Microtears:} repeated strain causes small tears in the tendon
    \item \textbf{Degeneration:} with ongoing overload, the tendon undergoes degenerative change rather than simple inflammation, with disorganised collagen and reduced healing capacity
    \item \textbf{Contributing factors:} poor technique, sudden increases in activity, weak shoulder and wrist muscles, and inadequate warm-up
    \item \textbf{Other names:} medial epicondylitis and "climber's elbow"
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Pain develops gradually and is related to activity.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain and tenderness on the inner side of the elbow, over the medial epicondyle
    \item Pain that radiates down the inner forearm to the wrist
    \item Pain worsened by gripping, squeezing, lifting, or flexing the wrist against resistance
    \item Pain when shaking hands, turning a key, or picking up objects
    \item Stiffness and reduced grip strength
    \item In golfers, pain when gripping the club and during the swing
    \item Symptoms that may worsen over weeks to months if activity continues
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of inner elbow pain should be considered, especially when symptoms are atypical or persistent.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cervical spine problems with referred pain into the arm
    \item Ulnar nerve entrapment (cubital tunnel syndrome), which causes numbness and tingling in the ring and little fingers
    \item Medial collateral ligament injury of the elbow, often from throwing
    \item Elbow arthritis, including osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis
    \item Olecranon bursitis, causing swelling over the back of the elbow
    \item Fracture of the medial epicondyle, especially after acute injury
    \item Thoracic outlet syndrome
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor or physiotherapist if elbow pain persists beyond a couple of weeks, is severe, or limits daily activities. Seek review if there is associated numbness or weakness, or if the elbow locks, gives way, or is swollen after an injury.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe elbow pain with deformity, inability to move the joint, or numbness below the elbow, which may indicate a fracture or dislocation
    \item Signs of a serious infection: severe pain, swelling, redness, and fever
    \item Sudden weakness or loss of function of the hand
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Golfer's elbow is usually diagnosed clinically, with tests reserved for atypical cases.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} tenderness over the medial epicondyle reproduced by resisted wrist flexion and gripping
    \item \textbf{Resisted wrist flexion test:} pain when the wrist is flexed against resistance
    \item \textbf{Grip strength testing:} documents weakness
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} may show tendon thickening, tears, or degeneration
    \item \textbf{MRI:} used when the diagnosis is uncertain or surgery is considered
    \item \textbf{Nerve conduction studies:} if ulnar nerve involvement is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Most cases of golfer's elbow improve with conservative treatment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Relative rest:} reduce or modify the aggravating activities, but do not completely immobilise the arm
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} paracetamol or anti-inflammatory medicines for short-term relief
    \item \textbf{Ice:} applying ice to the inner elbow for 15 minutes after activity
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} stretching and graduated strengthening of the forearm muscles, plus technique advice
    \item \textbf{Bracing:} a counterforce strap or elbow brace can reduce strain on the tendon
    \item \textbf{Activity modification:} changing grip, equipment, or technique in sport and work
    \item \textbf{Injections:} corticosteroid injections give short-term relief; other injections such as platelet-rich plasma are used in some cases
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} considered only for persistent cases after many months of treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic pain and disability lasting months to years
    \item Reduced grip strength affecting work, sport, and daily tasks
    \item Tendon rupture in severe untreated cases, which is rare
    \item Recurrence when activity resumes too quickly
    \item Complications of injections, including tendon weakening with repeated steroid use
    \item Surgical complications, including nerve injury and infection, though these are uncommon
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for golfer's elbow is good. Around 80--90% of people improve with conservative management, usually within three to six months, though symptoms can take up to a year or more to fully settle in some cases. Physiotherapy and graduated strengthening are the most effective long-term treatment. Surgery is successful in around 70--90% of the small minority who need it. The key to recovery is identifying and modifying the activities that overload the tendon, rather than simply resting until the pain goes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Warm up before sport, including gentle wrist and forearm stretches
    \item Strengthen the forearm muscles gradually to build capacity
    \item Use proper technique in golf, racket sports, and work tasks
    \item Use appropriate equipment, including grips suited to your size
    \item Take regular breaks from repetitive gripping and wrist movements
    \item Increase training loads gradually rather than suddenly
    \item Use an elbow brace early if symptoms begin
    \item Seek physiotherapy advice early rather than waiting for symptoms to settle
\end{itemize}
