\chapter{Brain Fog}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Brain fog is a popular, non-medical term for a subjective feeling of mental cloudiness: difficulty concentrating, poor memory, slow or fuzzy thinking, and a sense that the mind is not working as clearly as usual. It is not a disease in itself but a symptom that can arise from a wide range of physical, psychological, and lifestyle causes -- from a single poor night's sleep to a chronic illness. People commonly describe it as feeling "spaced out", struggling to find the right words, forgetting appointments, or having to work far harder than normal to complete a familiar task. Because brain fog is a symptom rather than a diagnosis, the key to resolving it is identifying and addressing the underlying cause rather than treating the fog itself.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Brain fog is extremely common and is reported at all ages, although it becomes more frequent with increasing age. It appears in a remarkably wide range of settings: after viral illnesses, in chronic fatigue and chronic pain syndromes, during and after cancer treatment, around hormonal transitions such as pregnancy and menopause, and in people who are chronically sleep-deprived, stressed, or taking medicines that affect the brain. Its prevalence rose sharply during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, when persistent mental cloudiness became one of the most frequently reported features of long COVID. Population surveys suggest that substantial numbers of adults experience at least transient episodes of cognitive fuzziness in any given year, making it one of the most common reasons for self-reported cognitive concern in primary care.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Brain fog has many possible triggers, and more than one is often at work simultaneously:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sleep disturbance:} insufficient or fragmented sleep, insomnia, and obstructive sleep apnoea
    \item \textbf{Psychological factors:} prolonged stress, anxiety, and depression
    \item \textbf{Medications:} sedatives, some antihistamines, opioids, anticholinergic drugs, and other centrally acting agents
    \item \textbf{Hormonal change:} pregnancy, the menopause transition, and hypothyroidism
    \item \textbf{Nutritional deficiencies:} iron, vitamin B12, vitamin D, or folate deficiency
    \item \textbf{Chronic illness:} myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome, fibromyalgia, chronic pain, and autoimmune diseases such as lupus and multiple sclerosis
    \item \textbf{Infections:} including persistent symptoms after COVID-19, glandular fever, and other viral illnesses
    \item \textbf{Cancer treatment:} chemotherapy-related cognitive impairment, sometimes called "chemo brain"
    \item \textbf{Head injury:} the mental cloudiness of concussion and post-concussion syndrome
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle factors:} dehydration, low blood sugar, excessive alcohol, and recreational drug use
\end{itemize}

The underlying mechanism is incompletely understood but is thought to involve a combination of neuroinflammation, altered levels of brain signalling chemicals, reduced cerebral blood flow, disturbed sleep architecture, and the direct effects of hormones and medicines. In most cases brain fog reflects the brain operating under strain rather than suffering permanent damage.

\section*{Clinical Features}
People with brain fog typically describe:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty concentrating or sustaining attention on a task
    \item Forgetfulness, losing track of items, and missing appointments
    \item Slowed thinking and a general sense of mental sluggishness
    \item Word-finding difficulty and trouble following conversations or instructions
    \item Mental fatigue that builds during the day and after exertion
    \item Feeling detached, "fuzzy", or as if there is a barrier between thought and action
    \item Reduced performance at work or study and an increased tendency to make errors
\end{itemize}

The symptoms are characteristically variable: they may fluctuate through the day, worsen with fatigue or stress, and improve at other times, which helps distinguish brain fog from the more persistent and progressive decline of dementia.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Depression and anxiety, which commonly present with poor concentration and memory
    \item Mild cognitive impairment or early dementia, particularly in older people
    \item Adult attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)
    \item Sleep disorders, especially obstructive sleep apnoea
    \item Hypothyroidism or vitamin B12, iron, or vitamin D deficiency
    \item Medication side effects and interactions
    \item Post-concussion syndrome after head injury
    \item Long COVID and other post-infectious syndromes
    \item Substance use, including alcohol, cannabis, or withdrawal states
    \item Chronic fatigue syndrome and fibromyalgia
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if brain fog is persistent, worsening, or interfering with work, study, or daily life, or if it is accompanied by low mood, headache, unrefreshing sleep or loud snoring, or unexplained fatigue. Because several easily treatable causes -- thyroid disease, anaemia, vitamin deficiency, and sleep apnoea -- are common, a simple evaluation is usually worthwhile even when the symptoms seem mild.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden confusion or a sudden, marked change in mental state
    \item Difficulty speaking, weakness, or numbness -- possible stroke
    \item A seizure or fitting episode
    \item Head injury followed by confusion, vomiting, or loss of consciousness
    \item Severe drowsiness or difficulty waking a person
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Brain fog is diagnosed mainly from the history, with investigations directed at the probable cause:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detailed history:} a careful account of sleep, stress, medications, illnesses, lifestyle, and the timing and pattern of symptoms
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, thyroid function, iron studies, vitamin B12, vitamin D, fasting blood glucose, and inflammatory markers
    \item \textbf{Sleep assessment:} validated questionnaires and, where apnoea is suspected, a sleep study
    \item \textbf{Mental health screening:} structured tools for depression, anxiety, and stress
    \item \textbf{Medication review:} a careful check of prescribed and over-the-counter medicines for cognitive side effects
    \item \textbf{Cognitive screening:} office-based tests of memory and attention, with formal neuropsychological assessment in selected cases
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT or MRI only when the history or examination suggests a structural or inflammatory cause
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment targets the underlying cause, and many people improve substantially once it is identified and addressed:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sleep:} improving sleep hygiene and treating sleep apnoea or insomnia if present
    \item \textbf{Treating physical causes:} thyroid hormone replacement, iron or vitamin supplementation, and adjustment of medications that contribute to cognitive symptoms
    \item \textbf{Mental health care:} psychological therapy and, where appropriate, medication for depression or anxiety
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} regular exercise, adequate hydration, regular meals, and moderation of alcohol and caffeine
    \item \textbf{Cognitive strategies:} lists, reminders, routines, and breaking large tasks into smaller steps to work with reduced mental energy
    \item \textbf{Pacing and occupational support:} balancing activity and rest, adjusting workloads, and requesting reasonable workplace or study accommodations
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} scheduled review to monitor progress and adjust the plan if symptoms persist or worsen
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reduced productivity and achievement at work, school, or home
    \item Increased risk of errors and accidents, including driving incidents
    \item Relationship strain, withdrawal from social activity, and isolation
    \item Anxiety about memory, including fear of early dementia
    \item Difficulty adhering to medical advice for coexisting health conditions
    \item Persistent or worsening symptoms when the underlying cause goes unaddressed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook is generally favourable. When brain fog is due to a reversible factor -- poor sleep, stress, a deficiency, or a medication -- it typically clears once the cause is corrected, often within weeks. Where it is part of a longer-term condition such as long COVID, chronic fatigue syndrome, or chemotherapy-related cognitive change, it may persist for months or years, although many people improve gradually with pacing, lifestyle measures, and supportive strategies. Brain fog is only rarely a sign of progressive or permanent brain damage, and most affected people recover fully or adapt successfully with the right support.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Prioritise good, regular sleep and seek early treatment for sleep problems
    \item Stay physically active and eat a balanced diet with enough fluids
    \item Manage stress with relaxation, mindfulness, or talking therapies
    \item Limit alcohol and avoid recreational drugs
    \item Keep mentally stimulated through reading, puzzles, learning, and social activity
    \item Have blood tests if brain fog persists, so that easily treatable causes are not missed
    \item During periods of mental fatigue, use lists and routines and allow rest rather than pushing through
\end{itemize}
