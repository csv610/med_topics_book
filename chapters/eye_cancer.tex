\chapter{Eye Cancer}
\index{Eye Cancer}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Eye cancer is a rare cancer that can develop in the eye or its surrounding structures. The most common primary eye cancer in adults is melanoma of the uvea (the middle layer of the eye), while in children retinoblastoma is the main type. Cancers can also spread to the eye from elsewhere, and skin cancers can affect the eyelids. Because eye cancer is rare, it is often diagnosed during routine eye examinations. Treatment has advanced considerably, and modern care aims to save both the eye and the sight while treating the cancer.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Eye cancer is rare, with a small number of new cases each year in many countries. Uveal melanoma is the most common primary eye cancer in adults and is more common in fair-skinned people and with increasing age. Retinoblastoma is a childhood cancer that is often detected by parents noticing a white pupil. With early diagnosis and modern treatment, survival from eye cancer is high.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The causes of eye cancer depend on the type.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Uveal melanoma:} arises from the pigment-producing cells of the uvea; risk factors include fair skin, light eyes, and sun exposure, though much of the cause is unclear
    \item \textbf{Retinoblastoma:} a genetic cancer of the retina in children, caused by a mutation in the RB1 gene; it may be inherited or arise as a new mutation
    \item \textbf{Eyelid and conjunctival cancers:} skin cancers such as basal cell carcinoma and melanoma
    \item \textbf{Metastatic cancer:} spread from other cancers, particularly breast and lung cancer
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} cancer cells grow within the eye, affecting vision and, in some types, spreading to other organs
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms vary with the type and location.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A change in vision, blurring, or a shadow in the vision
    \item A visible dark spot on the iris
    \item A white pupil or a squint in a child, which may indicate retinoblastoma
    \item Pain, redness, and glaucoma in advanced cases
    \item A lump or sore on the eyelid that does not heal
    \item Floaters and flashes, which can occur with uveal melanoma
    \item Many eye cancers are found incidentally during an eye examination
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other eye conditions can be mistaken for cancer.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Benign eye lesions, including choroidal naevi (freckles)
    \item Macular degeneration and other causes of vision loss
    \item Cataract and retinal detachment
    \item Eye infections and inflammation
    \item Floaters from other causes
    \item Benign eyelid lumps
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with new or changing vision symptoms, a visible eye lesion, or a white pupil in a child should have an eye examination. Eye cancer can be found on routine checks, so regular eye examinations matter.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden loss of vision
    \item Sudden severe eye pain
    \item A red eye with severe pain and nausea, which may indicate acute glaucoma
    \item Sudden new floaters with flashes, which may indicate retinal detachment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is made by an ophthalmologist.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Eye examination:} examination of the retina and uvea through a dilated pupil
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound of the eye:} measures the tumour
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} OCT, fluorescein angiography, and sometimes MRI
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} rarely needed; most eye cancers are diagnosed by their appearance
    \item \textbf{Staging:} tests to check for spread, including ultrasound of the liver
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} for retinoblastoma and some uveal melanomas
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment aims to control the cancer while preserving vision.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} plaque brachytherapy (radioactive discs placed on the eye) or proton beam therapy, the main treatments for uveal melanoma
    \item \textbf{Laser treatment:} for small tumours
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} removal of the eye (enucleation) for large or advanced tumours
    \item \textbf{Retinoblastoma:} chemotherapy, laser, and other treatments, coordinated by specialist teams
    \item \textbf{Surveillance:} regular follow-up to detect recurrence and spread
    \item \textbf{Genetic counselling:} for inherited forms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Loss of vision in the affected eye
    \item Glaucoma and other eye complications of treatment
    \item Spread of melanoma to the liver and other organs
    \item The need for an artificial eye after enucleation
    \item The psychological impact of diagnosis and vision loss
    \item For retinoblastoma: effects of treatment on the child
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for eye cancer is generally good with early treatment. Uveal melanoma is curable in most cases, though it can spread years later, and retinoblastoma has a high survival rate with modern care. Treatment advances have made it possible to save the eye and vision in many cases. Regular follow-up is essential.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend regular eye examinations, particularly with age
    \item Report any new or changing vision symptoms promptly
    \item Protect the eyes from sun with sunglasses and hats
    \item For retinoblastoma families: genetic counselling and screening of children
    \item Follow the follow-up schedule after treatment
    \item Seek support for vision loss and the emotional impact
\end{itemize}
