\chapter{Mesothelioma (lung cancer)}
\index{Mesothelioma (lung cancer)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Malignant pleural mesothelioma is an aggressive, highly lethal malignancy arising from the mesothelial cells of the pleura—the protective double-layered membrane lining the lungs and thoracic cavity. Although frequently grouped with lung cancers due to its location in the chest and respiratory presentation, mesothelioma is biologically and histopathologically distinct from epithelial bronchogenic carcinomas. The disease is etiologically linked to prior exposure to asbestos fibres, which cause chronic pleural injury. Characterized by an exceptionally long latency period and poor responsiveness to traditional therapies, pleural mesothelioma represents a significant clinical challenge in respiratory oncology.

\section*{Epidemiology}
some countries have one of the highest incidence rates of mesothelioma globally, reflecting the affected region's extensive historical mining, manufacturing, and industrial use of asbestos during the mid-to-late 20th century. Approximately 700 to 800 new cases of mesothelioma are diagnosed in many countries annually. The disease predominantly affects males (approximately 80\% of cases), correlating with historical occupational exposures in construction, shipbuilding, railway workshops, and asbestos mining. The median age at diagnosis is 75 years, and the latency period between initial asbestos inhalation and the development of clinical disease is extremely long, typically spanning 20 to 50 years.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Mesothelioma pathogenesis is driven by the physical, chemical, and biological interactions of inhaled mineral fibres within the pleural space.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asbestos fibre inhalation:} Inhalation of microscopic asbestos fibres, particularly amphibole fibres (such as crocidolite or "blue asbestos" and amosite), which are highly biopersistent and penetrate deep into the distal airways to reach the pleura.
    \item \textbf{Chronic pleural inflammation:} The physical presence of biopersistent fibres in the pleura prevents clearance, leading to "frustrated phagocytosis" by macrophages, chronic tissue irritation, and the continuous release of mutagenic reactive oxygen species (ROS).
    \item \textbf{Fibrogenic cytokine release:} Chronic release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-$\alpha$) and interleukin-1$\beta$, which promote mesothelial cell survival and proliferation.
    \item \textbf{Somatic genetic alterations:} Accumulation of genetic mutations driven by chronic inflammation, characteristically involving deletions or inactivation of key tumour suppressor genes including BAP1, NF2 (merlin), and CDKN2A/p16.
    \item \textbf{Inhalation of non-asbestos fibres:} Exposure to other fibrous silicate minerals such as erionite, which has similar carcinogenic properties to asbestos.
    \item \textbf{Genetic susceptibility:} Germline mutations in the BAP1 gene predispose individuals to developing mesothelioma, even with minimal asbestos exposure.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features of pleural mesothelioma are primarily related to local intrathoracic tumor growth, pleural effusion, and invasion of adjacent mediastinal structures.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Progressive dyspnea:} Shortness of breath, initially on exertion and later at rest, caused by pleural effusion accumulation and the restriction of lung expansion by a fibrous pleural "rind."
    \item \textbf{Chest wall pain:} A persistent, dull, aching chest pain that is typically non-pleuritic and may radiate to the shoulder or back, caused by tumour invasion of the intercostal nerves, ribs, or chest wall.
    \item \textbf{Chronic dry cough:} Persistent cough caused by pleural irritation or airway compression.
    \item \textbf{Constitutional B-symptoms:} Unexplained weight loss, severe fatigue, anorexia, and recurrent night sweats.
    \item \textbf{Hemithoracic volume loss:} Asymmetrical chest expansion and flattening of the affected side of the chest in advanced stages due to diffuse pleural encasement.
    \item \textbf{Abdominal symptoms:} Ascites, abdominal pain, or distension indicating transdiaphragmatic extension of the tumour into the peritoneal cavity.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Pleural mesothelioma can mimic other causes of pleural thickening and effusions, requiring careful differentiation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pleural metastases from other cancers:} Secondary pleural tumours originating from primary adenocarcinomas of the lung, breast, kidney, or ovary.
    \item \textbf{Benign asbestos-related pleural diseases:} Non-malignant conditions such as pleural plaques, benign asbestos pleural effusions (BAPE), or diffuse pleural thickening.
    \item \textbf{Tuberculous pleurisy:} Pleural tuberculosis presenting with persistent pleural exudative fluid and thickening, particularly in endemic areas.
    \item \textbf{Chronic empyema:} Persistent pleural infection and inflammation leading to a thick pleural peel.
    \item \textbf{Solitary fibrous tumour of the pleura:} A rare, typically benign primary pleural neoplasm arising from mesenchymal cells.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Individuals with a known history of occupational or environmental asbestos exposure should consult a general practitioner for evaluation if they develop persistent shortness of breath, chronic cough, or chest pain.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute, severe shortness of breath or sudden respiratory distress.
    \item Sudden, sharp, or crushing chest pain.
    \item Haemoptysis (coughing up blood).
    \item Severe, radiating pain accompanied by weakness or sensory loss in the arms or chest wall (indicating nerve root compression).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Confirming a diagnosis of mesothelioma requires advanced imaging and a thoracoscopic pleural biopsy.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} Typically demonstrates a large, unilateral pleural effusion, nodular pleural thickening, or volume loss in the affected hemithorax.
    \item \textbf{Computed Tomography (CT) of the chest:} Essential for demonstrating circumferential nodular pleural thickening, pleural plaques, mediastinal pleural involvement, and contraction of the hemithorax.
    \item \textbf{Positron Emission Tomography-CT (PET-CT):} Used to identify metabolically active pleural disease, locate optimal biopsy sites, and evaluate for distant metastases.
    \item \textbf{Pleural fluid aspiration (Thoracocentesis):} Exudative fluid is obtained; however, cytological analysis is diagnostic in less than 30\% of cases due to the difficulty of distinguishing reactive mesothelial cells from malignant ones.
    \item \textbf{Video-Assisted Thoracoscopic Surgery (VATS):} The gold standard diagnostic procedure, allowing direct visualization of the pleura, drainage of pleural fluid, and the collection of multiple large tissue biopsies.
    \item \textbf{Immunohistochemistry (IHC) panel:} Critical for histopathological classification. A panel of positive mesothelial markers (calretinin, WT1, D2-40) and negative epithelial markers (CEA, TTF-1) is used to differentiate mesothelioma from adenocarcinoma.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is predominantly palliative and aimed at symptom control and survival prolongation, utilizing systemic therapy and local interventions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dual immunotherapy:} First-line combination of nivolumab and ipilimumab, which has demonstrated superior survival outcomes compared to chemotherapy, particularly in non-epithelioid subtypes.
    \item \textbf{Systemic chemotherapy:} A platinum-based doublet regimen consisting of cisplatin (or carboplatin) combined with pemetrexed, historically the standard first-line treatment.
    \item \textbf{Palliative pleural procedures:} Recurrent effusions are managed via pleurodesis (using sterile talc slurry to adhere the pleural layers) or the insertion of an indwelling pleural catheter (e.g.\ PleurX catheter) for home drainage.
    \item \textbf{Surgical resection:} Highly selective procedures like pleurectomy/decortication (P/D) or extrapleural pneumonectomy (EPP) are controversial and generally restricted to clinical trials for early-stage epithelioid disease.
    \item \textbf{Palliative radiotherapy:} Used to alleviate localized bone pain, control neuropathic pain, or prevent tumour seeding along surgical scars or chest drain tracts.
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive supportive care:} Early referral to palliative care teams for active management of dyspnea (using low-dose opioids), neuropathic pain, and psychosocial distress.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Respiratory failure:} Progressive compression of the lung parenchyma by the expanding tumor rind, leading to severe hypoxia and hypercapnia.
    \item \textbf{Severe intractable pain:} Neuropathic pain caused by tumor invasion of the intercostal nerves, chest wall muscles, or brachial plexus.
    \item \textbf{Superior vena cava obstruction:} Compression of the superior vena cava by mediastinal tumor extension, restricting blood flow from the upper body.
    \item \textbf{Pericardial constriction:} Direct invasion of the pericardial sac leading to pericardial effusion, tamponade, or constrictive physiology.
    \item \textbf{Transdiaphragmatic extension:} Invasion of the diaphragm leading to peritoneal mesothelioma, ascites, and bowel obstruction.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for malignant pleural mesothelioma remains poor, with a median survival of 9 to 18 months from diagnosis. The overall 5-year survival rate is less than 10\%. Histological subtype is the most significant prognostic factor; epithelioid mesothelioma is associated with a better response to therapy and longer survival than the highly aggressive sarcomatoid or biphasic subtypes. Most patients succumb to progressive respiratory failure, intractable chest pain, or local intrathoracic complications rather than distant metastases.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Strict asbestos avoidance:} Avoid disturbing materials containing asbestos in residential buildings constructed before 1990. Engage licensed professionals for asbestos inspection and removal.
    \item \textbf{Occupational hygiene compliance:} Adhere strictly to safety standards in industries where asbestos is present, utilizing appropriate personal protective equipment (P2 respirators), containment facilities, and air filtration.
    \item \textbf{Eliminate smoking:} Smoking does not directly increase the risk of mesothelioma, but it acts synergistically with asbestos to increase the risk of bronchogenic lung cancer by up to 50-fold.
    \item \textbf{Registration and monitoring:} Register with the National Asbestos Register if exposed to asbestos, and monitor for changes in respiratory function.
\end{itemize}
