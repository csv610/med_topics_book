\chapter{Glaucoma}
\index{Glaucoma}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Glaucoma is a group of eye conditions that damage the optic nerve, the cable of nerve fibres that carries visual information from the eye to the brain. It is most often associated with raised pressure inside the eye (intraocular pressure), which can occur when the fluid that fills the eye (aqueous humour) does not drain properly. The damage to the optic nerve causes gradual, irreversible loss of vision, usually starting with the peripheral (side) vision. Because the early stages are painless and often unnoticed, glaucoma is called the "silent thief of sight". While the damage cannot be reversed, treatment that lowers eye pressure can slow or stop further loss, so early detection through regular eye checks is vital.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Glaucoma is one of the leading causes of irreversible blindness worldwide, affecting around 80 million people globally, and that number is expected to rise. In many countries, around 300,000 people have glaucoma, but up to half do not know they have it. It is most common in people over 60, and prevalence rises steeply with age. People of African, Asian, and Hispanic descent, people with a family history of glaucoma, and those with high eye pressure, diabetes, or short-sightedness are at increased risk. Primary open-angle glaucoma, the most common type, accounts for about 90\% of cases.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Glaucoma occurs when the optic nerve is damaged, most commonly because of raised pressure within the eye.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Aqueous humour:} a clear fluid is produced in the eye and drains through a meshwork at the front of the eye; if drainage is blocked, pressure rises
    \item \textbf{Open-angle glaucoma:} the drainage channels look open but drain poorly, causing a slow, painless rise in pressure over years
    \item \textbf{Angle-closure glaucoma:} the drainage angle is blocked suddenly, causing a rapid, painful rise in pressure that is a medical emergency
    \item \textbf{Optic nerve damage:} elevated pressure compresses and damages the nerve fibres, and reduced blood flow may contribute
    \item \textbf{Normal-tension glaucoma:} optic nerve damage occurs even when eye pressure is within the normal range
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} age, family history, high eye pressure, thin corneas, and certain ethnic backgrounds
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms depend on the type of glaucoma and how advanced it is.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Open-angle glaucoma:} usually no symptoms until significant vision is lost; gradual loss of peripheral vision, often starting with subtle blind spots
    \item \textbf{Angle-closure glaucoma (emergency):} sudden severe eye pain, headache, blurred vision, seeing haloes around lights, a red eye, and nausea or vomiting
    \item \textbf{Congenital glaucoma:} a baby with a large, cloudy eye, tearing, and sensitivity to light
    \item \textbf{Tunnel vision:} in advanced disease, only central vision remains
    \item Vision loss that cannot be corrected with glasses
    \item Many people are diagnosed at a routine eye test before any symptoms are noticed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Conditions that can be confused with glaucoma or its symptoms include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cataracts, which also cause blurred vision and are common in older people
    \item Age-related macular degeneration, which affects central rather than peripheral vision
    \item Uveitis, inflammation inside the eye that can raise pressure
    \item Migraine with visual symptoms
    \item Conjunctivitis and other causes of a red eye
    \item Optic neuritis and other optic nerve disorders
    \item Corneal disease causing haloes and blurred vision
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Everyone should have a comprehensive eye examination, including measurement of eye pressure, by an optometrist or ophthalmologist. People at higher risk (over 60, family history, certain ethnic backgrounds, or diabetes) should be tested regularly, and earlier if they have risk factors.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe eye pain with blurred vision, a red eye, haloes around lights, and nausea or vomiting, which indicates acute angle-closure glaucoma and requires immediate treatment to prevent blindness
    \item Sudden loss of vision in one or both eyes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Glaucoma is diagnosed with a set of painless tests performed by an eye care professional.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tonometry:} measurement of intraocular pressure, often with a puff of air or a probe
    \item \textbf{Ophthalmoscopy:} examination of the optic nerve with a special light after dilating the pupil
    \item \textbf{Visual field testing:} mapping the peripheral vision to detect and track blind spots
    \item \textbf{OCT (optical coherence tomography):} imaging that measures the thickness of the optic nerve fibres and the retina
    \item \textbf{Gonioscopy:} inspection of the drainage angle to determine the type of glaucoma
    \item \textbf{Corneal thickness measurement:} thin corneas affect pressure readings and risk
    \item \textbf{Regular follow-up:} repeat testing tracks whether the disease is stable or progressing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Glaucoma cannot be cured, and vision already lost cannot be restored, but treatment effectively slows or halts further damage.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Eye drops:} the first-line treatment; most drops lower pressure by reducing fluid production or improving drainage. They must be used daily, often for life
    \item \textbf{Laser treatment:} laser trabeculoplasty improves drainage in open-angle glaucoma; laser iridotomy creates a drainage opening in angle-closure glaucoma
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} trabeculectomy and drainage implants create new drainage pathways for advanced or resistant glaucoma
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular eye checks and visual field tests ensure treatment is working
    \item \textbf{Acute angle-closure:} an emergency treated with pressure-lowering medicines and urgent laser treatment
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} regular exercise, avoiding smoking, and managing blood pressure and diabetes support eye health
    \item \textbf{Low vision support:} services and aids help people adapt when vision loss has occurred
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive, irreversible vision loss and eventual blindness if untreated
    \item Sudden, severe vision loss in acute angle-closure glaucoma
    \item Poor adherence to daily eye drops, which is the most common reason treatment fails
    \item Side effects of drops, including red eyes, blurred vision, and changes to eyelashes or skin
    \item Complications of surgery, including infection, bleeding, and cataract formation
    \item Falls, injuries, and reduced independence related to vision loss
    \item Psychological effects, including anxiety and depression, after diagnosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With early detection and consistent treatment, most people with glaucoma retain useful sight for life. Treatment lowers eye pressure and slows the disease substantially, though it cannot restore already lost vision. The disease requires lifelong monitoring because it can progress despite treatment, and drop adherence is the single most important factor in good outcomes. People diagnosed early and treated well can expect to maintain their daily activities and quality of life; the key is regular eye examinations, because the disease is silent in its early stages.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Have regular comprehensive eye examinations, including eye pressure measurement
    \item If you are over 60, have a family history, or are otherwise at higher risk, get tested earlier and more often
    \item Use eye drops exactly as prescribed and never run out; set reminders if needed
    \item Bring your eye drops to appointments so your doctor can check your technique and adherence
    \item Report any changes in vision, pain, or redness promptly
    \item Tell all health professionals, including pharmacists, about your glaucoma and its medicines
    \item Exercise regularly, avoid smoking, and manage blood pressure and diabetes
    \item If vision loss affects daily life, seek low vision services and home safety advice
\end{itemize}
