\chapter{Aggression in Teenagers}
\index{Aggression in Teenagers}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Aggression is behaviour intended to cause physical or emotional harm to another person, and some degree of it is common during the teenage years. It can be verbal, such as shouting, swearing, or threatening; physical, such as hitting, pushing, or damaging property; or relational, such as spreading rumours, excluding others, or damaging friendships.

Occasional angry outbursts are a normal part of adolescent development, but aggression becomes a concern when it is frequent, severe, or out of proportion to the situation. Aggression in teenagers is not a single medical diagnosis, but it can be a sign of an underlying condition such as depression, anxiety, oppositional defiant disorder, conduct disorder, or substance misuse, and it deserves proper assessment when it is persistent or harmful.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Aggressive and antisocial behaviour becomes more common in early adolescence and typically peaks in the late teenage years and early adulthood. Physical aggression is more common among boys, while relational aggression -- such as social exclusion and rumour-spreading -- is more common among girls.

A substantial minority of teenagers display aggressive behaviour at some point, although only a small proportion go on to meet the criteria for a formal conduct disorder. Rates are higher in young people exposed to family conflict, harsh or inconsistent parenting, violence, bullying, or peer groups that reward aggression, and in those with learning difficulties, mental health problems, or a history of trauma.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Teenage aggression arises from a combination of biological, psychological, and social influences rather than any single cause.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Brain development:} the prefrontal cortex, which controls impulses and foresees consequences, matures late, while emotion-driven regions develop earlier, so teenagers are prone to impulsive reactions
    \item \textbf{Hormonal changes:} the rise in testosterone and other hormones during puberty can increase irritability and reactivity, particularly in boys
    \item \textbf{Genetics and temperament:} impulsive, reactive, or sensation-seeking personality traits tend to run in families
    \item \textbf{Family factors:} conflict, harsh or unpredictable discipline, modelling of aggression by adults, and poor supervision
    \item \textbf{Peer and school factors:} bullying, peer rejection, school failure, and peer groups that encourage fighting
    \item \textbf{Social environment:} poverty, community violence, and heavy exposure to violent media and games
    \item \textbf{Mental health and substance use:} depression, ADHD, autism, post-traumatic stress, and alcohol or drug use can all lower the threshold for aggression
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Aggression can present in many ways, and the pattern often depends on the underlying cause.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Verbal aggression, such as yelling, swearing, name-calling, or threats
    \item Physical aggression, such as hitting, pushing, kicking, or damaging property
    \item Relational aggression, such as spreading rumours, excluding peers, or turning friends against one another
    \item Irritability, frequent anger, and a low tolerance for frustration
    \item Rule-breaking, truancy, and conflict with authority figures
    \item Sudden mood changes and difficulty calming down once angry
    \item Underlying features such as low mood, anxiety, withdrawal, or falling school performance
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Aggressive behaviour can be a feature of several different problems, which need to be distinguished by assessment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Oppositional defiant disorder -- a persistent pattern of defiance and hostility without serious law-breaking
    \item Conduct disorder -- repeated aggression and rule-breaking that harms others
    \item Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) -- impulsive aggression alongside inattention and hyperactivity
    \item Depression, anxiety, or post-traumatic stress disorder, which can present as irritability in teenagers
    \item Autism spectrum disorder -- aggression arising from frustration, sensory overload, or communication difficulty
    \item Substance misuse, including alcohol, cannabis, and other drugs
    \item Brain injury, epilepsy, or other neurological problems
    \item Normal adolescent moodiness and boundary-testing that does not harm others
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a GP, school counsellor, or mental health professional if aggression is frequent, escalating, or causing harm to the teenager or others, or if it is accompanied by withdrawal, low mood, or a drop in school performance. Early help is more effective than waiting until problems escalate.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Violence that poses an immediate risk of harm to the teenager or someone else
    \item Threats to seriously harm or kill others, or threats of suicide or self-harm
    \item Any injury caused by fighting or an assault
    \item A head injury, seizure, or unconsciousness, which may indicate a serious injury or alcohol poisoning
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment focuses on understanding the pattern of behaviour and its causes.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detailed history:} interviews with the teenager and with parents or carers, covering behaviour at home, at school, and with peers
    \item \textbf{School information:} reports from teachers about behaviour, attendance, and learning
    \item \textbf{Screening questionnaires:} validated tools for mood, anxiety, ADHD, and conduct problems
    \item \textbf{Physical and neurological examination:} to exclude head injury, seizures, or other medical causes
    \item \textbf{Mental health assessment:} evaluation for depression, trauma, and the risk of harm to self or others
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} occasionally used to check for substance use or medical causes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on the cause and severity and usually involves the whole family.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Parenting and family approaches:} consistent rules, calm discipline, and structured parent-training programmes
    \item \textbf{Psychological therapies:} cognitive behavioural therapy to manage anger and problem-solving, together with family therapy
    \item \textbf{Anger management:} teaching relaxation techniques, recognising triggers, and choosing alternatives to violence
    \item \textbf{School support:} working with teachers on behaviour plans and programmes to address bullying or learning needs
    \item \textbf{Treating underlying conditions:} medication and therapy for ADHD, depression, or anxiety when these are driving the aggression
    \item \textbf{Substance use intervention:} counselling and support where alcohol or drugs are involved
    \item \textbf{Safety planning:} a clear plan for de-escalation and for getting immediate help when behaviour becomes unsafe
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated or escalating aggression can have serious consequences.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Injuries to the teenager or to other people
    \item Trouble with the law, police involvement, and criminal charges
    \item School suspension, exclusion, and poor educational outcomes
    \item Damage to friendships, family relationships, and future employment
    \item Escalation into substance misuse and risk-taking behaviour
    \item Underlying depression, anxiety, or self-harm being overlooked
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the age of onset, the severity of the behaviour, and the family and social context. Many teenagers with aggressive behaviour improve substantially with consistent parenting, therapy, and school support. Early-onset and severe aggression is more likely to persist into adulthood, although many young people who are violent during adolescence do not continue to be violent adults. Relapse is common during stressful periods, and continued family involvement and follow-up improve the chances of lasting change.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Much of the harm of teenage aggression can be prevented with supportive, consistent approaches at home.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Relationships:} maintain warm, supportive relationships with clear, consistent limits
    \item \textbf{Modelling:} model calm ways of handling anger and conflict
    \item \textbf{Discipline:} encourage problem-solving rather than punishment for disagreements
    \item \textbf{Health habits:} ensure adequate sleep, physical activity, and limits on screen time and violent media
    \item \textbf{Supervision:} supervise activities and know who the teenager is spending time with
    \item \textbf{Structured activities:} encourage involvement in sport, music, or other structured activities
    \item \textbf{Substances:} limit alcohol and discuss the link between drugs and aggression
    \item \textbf{Early help:} seek help early for mood problems, bullying, or school difficulties
\end{itemize}
