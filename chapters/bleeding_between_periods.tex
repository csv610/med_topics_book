\chapter{Bleeding Between Periods}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bleeding between periods (intermenstrual bleeding) is any vaginal bleeding that occurs outside the normal menstrual period. It ranges from light spotting to heavier bleeding and may happen at a predictable point in the cycle, such as around ovulation, or entirely unpredictably. The term also encompasses bleeding after sexual intercourse (postcoital bleeding). It is a very common problem with many possible causes, from harmless hormonal fluctuations to structural, infective, and, rarely, malignant conditions, so persistent or recurrent bleeding always merits investigation.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bleeding between periods can occur at any age from the first years of menstruation to the perimenopause, and its causes vary with age. In younger women it is frequently hormonal, related to contraception, or associated with infection; in women in their 40s and older it more often reflects polyps, fibroids, endometrial pathology, or the hormonal changes that herald the menopause. Around one in ten women will experience intermenstrual bleeding at some point. Persistent or recurrent bleeding is investigated mainly to exclude cancer of the cervix or womb, the likelihood of which rises with age.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The lining of the womb and cervix is sensitive to hormonal signals, structural change, and infection, and bleeding can arise from disruption of any of these:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hormonal fluctuations:} mid-cycle ovulatory bleeding, and irregular hormone levels at the start and end of reproductive life
    \item \textbf{Contraception:} the combined pill, progestogen-only methods (pill, implant, injection), and the intrauterine system or copper coil can all cause breakthrough bleeding
    \item \textbf{Structural lesions:} cervical and endometrial polyps, and fibroids that distort the uterine lining
    \item \textbf{Infection:} chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and pelvic inflammatory disease; inflammation of the cervix from any cause
    \item \textbf{Cervical ectropion:} a zone of fragile columnar tissue on the cervix that bleeds easily, especially after intercourse
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} implantation bleeding, threatened miscarriage, or ectopic pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Malignancy:} pre-cancerous change or cancer of the cervix or womb, more likely in older women
    \item \textbf{Trauma:} recent procedures, or friction during intercourse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The presentation varies with the underlying cause, but the common threads are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Spotting or bleeding between expected periods
    \item Bleeding after sexual intercourse or after a cervical procedure
    \item Bleeding that is heavier, lighter, or more prolonged than the normal period
    \item Bleeding may be accompanied by lower abdominal pain, cramping, or a change in vaginal discharge
    \item A pattern that is new, changing, or recurrent in nature
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ovulatory bleeding or hormonal imbalance
    \item Breakthrough bleeding from contraception
    \item Cervical or endometrial polyps
    \item Uterine fibroids
    \item Sexually transmitted infections and pelvic inflammatory disease
    \item Cervical ectropion
    \item Early pregnancy, ectopic pregnancy, or miscarriage
    \item Pre-cancerous change or cancer of the cervix or womb
    \item Postcoital bleeding from friction or minor trauma
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any new, persistent, or recurrent bleeding between periods should be discussed with a doctor. Assessment is particularly important if the bleeding is heavy, repeatedly recurs, is associated with pain or an abnormal discharge, occurs after intercourse, or if there is any chance of pregnancy.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heavy vaginal bleeding that soaks through pads rapidly
    \item Severe pelvic or abdominal pain
    \item Fever with pelvic pain or abnormal discharge
    \item Bleeding with dizziness, fainting, or other signs of shock
    \item Shoulder-tip pain with vaginal bleeding and a positive pregnancy test, which suggests a ruptured ectopic pregnancy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Investigation aims to identify the source of bleeding, exclude infection and pregnancy, and reassure or treat accordingly.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} timing, amount, and pattern of bleeding; contraceptive use; sexual history; and possibility of pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test:} essential in any woman of reproductive age
    \item \textbf{Pelvic examination:} speculum inspection of the vulva, vagina, and cervix, and bimanual palpation of the uterus and adnexa
    \item \textbf{Swabs:} nucleic acid tests for chlamydia and gonorrhoea, and culture where indicated
    \item \textbf{Cervical screening:} performed if it is due, to exclude cervical pre-cancer
    \item \textbf{Pelvic ultrasound:} transvaginal scanning to detect polyps, fibroids, ovarian masses, and endometrial thickening
    \item \textbf{Hysteroscopy and endometrial biopsy:} direct inspection and sampling of the uterine lining where endometrial pathology is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment follows the diagnosis, and benign, self-limiting causes may require only reassurance once investigation is complete.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reassurance:} for physiological causes such as ovulatory bleeding once other pathology has been excluded
    \item \textbf{Contraception adjustment:} changing the method or regimen to control breakthrough bleeding
    \item \textbf{Antibiotic treatment:} for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, or pelvic inflammatory disease, with partner notification
    \item \textbf{Polypectomy:} removal of cervical or endometrial polyps
    \item \textbf{Hormonal treatment:} the combined pill, progestogens, or other hormonal therapy to regulate the cycle
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} myomectomy or other procedures for symptomatic fibroids
    \item \textbf{Cancer treatment:} for pre-cancer or cancer of the cervix or womb, coordinated by a gynaecological oncology team
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Anaemia from heavy or recurrent bleeding
    \item Delayed diagnosis of an underlying cancer if bleeding is dismissed
    \item Progression of pelvic inflammatory disease, with tubal damage and infertility, if infection goes untreated
    \item A ruptured ectopic pregnancy, which is life-threatening and requires emergency surgery
    \item Anxiety about the cause and uncertainty during investigation
    \item Reduced quality of life and interference with intimacy and daily activity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
In most cases bleeding between periods has a benign cause and settles with simple treatment, a change of contraception, or on its own. Hormonal and contraceptive-related bleeding is common, harmless, and readily managed. Because a small but important minority of cases are due to pre-cancer or cancer of the cervix or womb, persistent or recurrent bleeding should always be investigated; when malignancy is found, earlier diagnosis is associated with a substantially better outcome.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep a menstrual diary recording the timing, amount, and pattern of any bleeding
    \item Use effective contraception to prevent unplanned pregnancy, and barrier methods to reduce the risk of sexually transmitted infection
    \item Attend cervical screening when it is due
    \item Practise safe sex and have regular sexual health checks
    \item Report persistent or recurrent bleeding between periods to a doctor rather than dismissing it
    \item Discuss expected bleeding patterns when starting or changing contraception, and seek advice if troublesome breakthrough bleeding occurs
\end{itemize}
