\chapter{Vascular Dementia}
\index{Vascular Dementia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Vascular dementia is a form of dementia caused by reduced blood flow to the brain, which damages brain tissue and leads to decline in memory, thinking, and daily function. It can result from a major stroke, many small strokes, or chronic disease of the small blood vessels in the brain. Vascular dementia is the second most common cause of dementia after Alzheimer's disease, and it frequently occurs together with Alzheimer's changes. Because its major risk factors are treatable, vascular dementia is one of the most preventable forms of dementia. This chapter covers its causes, features, diagnosis, and management.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Vascular dementia accounts for around 15--20\% of dementia cases, and many more people have mixed Alzheimer's and vascular pathology. It is more common in men and in people with cardiovascular risk factors, and it typically develops after age 65, although it can occur earlier after major stroke. The risk is strongly linked to hypertension, diabetes, smoking, high cholesterol, atrial fibrillation, and previous stroke or transient ischaemic attack. As rates of stroke have declined with better prevention, the incidence of vascular dementia has also begun to fall in some countries.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stroke:} a large stroke can cause dementia directly when it damages areas critical for thinking, or multiple strokes can accumulate damage over time
    \item \textbf{Small vessel disease:} chronic damage to the tiny arteries deep in the brain causes areas of ischaemic change visible on imaging
    \item \textbf{White matter damage:} reduced blood supply damages the connections between brain regions
    \item \textbf{Hypertension:} the most important modifiable risk factor, damaging small vessels over many years
    \item \textbf{Atrial fibrillation:} increases the risk of stroke and emboli to the brain
    \item \textbf{Reduced cerebral blood flow:} heart failure, low blood pressure, and other circulatory problems contribute
    \item \textbf{Mixed dementia:} vascular changes frequently coexist with Alzheimer's disease pathology, and the two interact
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Decline in memory, but often with prominent difficulty in planning, organising, judgement, and processing speed
    \item Stepwise deterioration, with sudden drops in function following strokes
    \item Changes may be sudden after a stroke or gradual with small vessel disease
    \item Difficulty with walking, balance, and coordination; unsteadiness and falls
    \item Urinary urgency or incontinence early in the course
    \item Mood changes, apathy, and depression
    \item Problems with language and attention
    \item Apathy or slowing of thinking (bradyphrenia)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Alzheimer's disease, in which memory loss is usually the earliest and most prominent feature
    \item Lewy body dementia, with hallucinations and fluctuating cognition
    \item Frontotemporal dementia, with personality and language changes
    \item Depression, which can cause cognitive slowing
    \item Delirium, an acute confusion that can follow stroke or infection
    \item Other causes of cognitive decline, including thyroid disease and vitamin B12 deficiency
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with risk factors such as hypertension, diabetes, or previous stroke should have regular health checks and cognitive review if they notice changes in thinking. Anyone who develops sudden cognitive or neurological symptoms after a stroke or TIA should be assessed promptly. Controlling vascular risk factors can slow progression and prevent further damage.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden weakness, facial droop, difficulty speaking, or confusion (possible stroke)
    \item Sudden severe headache, dizziness, or loss of balance
    \item Seizures or loss of consciousness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cognitive assessment:} testing of memory, executive function, and processing speed
    \item \textbf{Brain imaging:} CT or MRI shows strokes, white matter changes, and other vascular damage, and helps confirm the diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} cholesterol, glucose, kidney function, thyroid, and vitamin B12
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular assessment:} blood pressure monitoring, ECG for atrial fibrillation, and heart ultrasound where indicated
    \item \textbf{Carotid imaging:} ultrasound to check for narrowing of the neck arteries in some cases
    \item \textbf{History:} from the person and family, including the timing of changes relative to strokes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stroke prevention:} the most important part of management; control of blood pressure, cholesterol, and diabetes, and anticoagulation or antiplatelet therapy as appropriate
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle change:} smoking cessation, healthy diet, regular exercise, weight management, and limited alcohol
    \item \textbf{Medication:} the same medications used in Alzheimer's disease may be tried, particularly in mixed dementia
    \item \textbf{Symptom management:} treatment of depression, and behavioural strategies for agitation or other symptoms
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and speech therapy after stroke
    \item \textbf{Support services:} dementia support services, home care, and carer support
    \item \textbf{Planning:} advance care planning and legal arrangements
    \item \textbf{Safety:} falls prevention and review of driving
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive cognitive decline and dependence
    \item Further strokes, which cause stepwise worsening
    \item Falls and fractures from balance problems
    \item Depression and apathy, which reduce quality of life
    \item Swallowing problems and pneumonia in advanced disease
    \item Carer stress and burden
    \item Complications of cardiovascular disease, including heart attack and stroke
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The course of vascular dementia is variable and depends on whether further strokes occur. Deterioration is often stepwise, with sudden declines after each event, but with good control of risk factors the condition can stabilise for long periods. Survival after diagnosis averages around 3--5 years, influenced strongly by cardiovascular health. Because vascular dementia is highly preventable, controlling blood pressure and other risk factors is the single most effective way to reduce its burden. Good care and support improve quality of life at every stage.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Control blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood glucose with regular medical review
    \item Do not smoke, and limit alcohol
    \item Exercise regularly and maintain a healthy weight
    \item Eat a heart-healthy diet with plenty of vegetables, fruit, and fish
    \item Treat atrial fibrillation to reduce stroke risk
    \item Keep mentally and socially active
    \item Attend rehabilitation and follow-up after any stroke
\end{itemize}
