\chapter{Gum Disease}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Gum disease, also called periodontal disease, is a common inflammatory condition affecting the tissues that surround and support the teeth. It ranges from gingivitis, a reversible inflammation of the gums, to periodontitis, a more serious form in which the inflammation spreads to the bone and ligaments holding the teeth, causing the gums to recede, pockets to form, and eventually tooth loss. Gum disease is caused by the bacteria in dental plaque, and its risk is increased by smoking, diabetes, and poor oral hygiene. It is largely preventable and treatable with good oral hygiene, professional cleaning, and, for advanced disease, more intensive treatment. Untreated gum disease is the leading cause of tooth loss in adults.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Gum disease is one of the most common diseases in the world. In Australia, around half of adults have some form of periodontal disease, and severe periodontitis affects around 20--30\% of adults, with rates increasing with age. It is more common and more severe in men, in smokers, and in people with diabetes. Gingivitis affects the majority of people at some stage. Severe gum disease is associated with tooth loss and has been linked to a higher risk of cardiovascular disease, diabetes complications, and adverse pregnancy outcomes, making it an important public health issue.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Gum disease develops when plaque bacteria trigger inflammation of the gums and supporting tissues.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Plaque:} a sticky film of bacteria that forms on teeth; if not removed, it hardens into tartar (calculus)
    \item \textbf{Gingivitis:} plaque toxins inflame the gums, causing redness, swelling, and bleeding; this is reversible
    \item \textbf{Periodontitis:} if inflammation persists, it spreads below the gumline, and the body's immune response damages the attachment of the gum to the tooth and the underlying bone
    \item \textbf{Periodontal pockets:} the gum separates from the tooth, forming pockets that trap more plaque
    \item \textbf{Bone loss:} progressive destruction of the bone supporting the teeth leads to loosening and loss
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} smoking, diabetes, poor oral hygiene, genetics, stress, and certain medicines
    \item \textbf{Systemic links:} inflammation in the mouth contributes to systemic inflammation, linking gum disease to other chronic conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Gum disease is often painless in its early stages, which is why it can progress unnoticed.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding gums, especially when brushing or flossing
    \item Red, swollen, or tender gums
    \item Receding gums, making teeth look longer
    \item Persistent bad breath or a bad taste in the mouth
    \item Loose or shifting teeth
    \item Pus between the teeth and gums
    \item Changes in the way teeth fit together when biting
    \item Gaps developing between teeth
    \item Pain on chewing in advanced disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of gum symptoms should be considered.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Gingivitis alone, which is limited to the gums and reversible
    \item Periodontitis, which involves bone loss
    \item Necrotising ulcerative gingivitis, an acute painful form
    \item Oral thrush and other fungal infections
    \item Herpes gingivostomatitis and other viral infections
    \item Lichen planus and other oral inflammatory conditions
    \item Medicine-related gum overgrowth
    \item Tooth-related problems, including abscesses and cracked teeth
    \item Haematological conditions causing bleeding gums
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a dentist regularly, and promptly if you notice bleeding, swollen, or receding gums, persistent bad breath, loose teeth, or pain. People with diabetes or heart conditions should have dental reviews at least twice a year.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe swelling of the face or jaw with fever, which may indicate a dental abscess spreading
    \item Difficulty breathing or swallowing from a dental infection
    \item Uncontrolled bleeding from the gums
    \item Signs of severe infection with confusion or collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Gum disease is diagnosed by a dental examination.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} inspection of the gums for redness, swelling, and bleeding
    \item \textbf{Periodontal probing:} measuring pocket depth between the gum and tooth; deeper pockets indicate periodontitis
    \item \textbf{Dental X-rays:} reveal bone loss around the teeth
    \item \textbf{Assessment of mobility:} checking for loose teeth
    \item \textbf{Review of risk factors:} smoking, diabetes, and medicines
    \item \textbf{Staging and grading:} periodontitis is classified by severity and rate of progression to guide treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment aims to control infection and inflammation, restore gum health, and save teeth.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Professional cleaning:} scaling and root planing remove plaque and tartar above and below the gumline
    \item \textbf{Improved oral hygiene:} correct brushing, flossing, and interdental cleaning are essential
    \item \textbf{Antibacterial rinses:} mouthwashes containing chlorhexidine may be used short term
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} topical or oral antibiotics for persistent infection
    \item \textbf{Surgical treatment:} flap surgery to clean deep pockets and gum grafts to cover receding roots
    \item \textbf{Laser treatment:} used in some practices to treat periodontal pockets
    \item \textbf{Smoking cessation:} stopping smoking is one of the most effective measures
    \item \textbf{Diabetes control:} good blood glucose control improves gum disease, and treating gum disease improves diabetes control
    \item \textbf{Maintenance:} regular periodontal maintenance visits every three to six months
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Tooth loss, which is the most serious consequence
    \item Abscesses and pain
    \item Recession of gums and tooth sensitivity
    \item Worsening of diabetes control
    \item Increased cardiovascular risk
    \item Adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preterm birth and low birth weight
    \item Difficulty eating, speaking, and social confidence
    \item Costs of complex dental treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Early gum disease is completely reversible. Gingivitis resolves within one to two weeks of improved hygiene and professional cleaning. Periodontitis cannot be cured, but it can be very well controlled: with treatment and maintenance, further bone loss is stopped in most people, and teeth can be kept for life. Outcomes are best when gum disease is caught early and when patients maintain excellent daily hygiene and attend regular maintenance visits. Advanced disease may still lead to tooth loss, but modern treatment preserves many teeth that would previously have been lost.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Brush twice daily for two minutes with fluoride toothpaste
    \item Clean between teeth daily with floss or interdental brushes
    \item Use a soft-bristled brush and replace it regularly
    \item Visit the dentist at least yearly, and every six months if you have gum disease risk factors
    \item Do not smoke, and manage diabetes well
    \item Eat a balanced diet and limit sugary foods and drinks
    \item Recognise that bleeding gums are not normal and seek dental advice
    \item Attend periodontal maintenance appointments as advised
    \item Tell your dentist about all medicines and health conditions
\end{itemize}
