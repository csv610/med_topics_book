\chapter{Hodgkin Lymphoma}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hodgkin lymphoma is a cancer of the lymphatic system, the network of vessels and nodes that helps fight infection. It is distinguished from other lymphomas by the presence of large, abnormal cells called Reed--Sternberg cells. Hodgkin lymphoma most commonly begins in lymph nodes in the neck, armpits, or chest, and typically spreads in a predictable way along lymph node chains. It is one of the most curable cancers: with modern chemotherapy and radiation, over 90\% of people with early-stage disease and most with advanced disease are cured.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hodgkin lymphoma affects around 700 Australians each year. It has a distinctive age pattern, with a first peak in young adults aged 15--35 and a second peak in people over 55. It is slightly more common in men. The incidence has been stable for decades, and the cause in most cases is unknown, although past infection with Epstein--Barr virus is associated with a minority of cases. Survival has improved dramatically: five-year survival exceeds 90\% overall, making it one of the most treatable adult cancers.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reed--Sternberg cells:} the malignant cells of Hodgkin lymphoma, which arise from B lymphocytes
    \item \textbf{Epstein--Barr virus:} associated with about a third to half of cases, particularly in childhood and older adults
    \item \textbf{Immune factors:} immunodeficiency and some autoimmune conditions increase risk
    \item \textbf{Family history:} a small increased risk in first-degree relatives
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} malignant cells in the lymph node attract a large inflammatory infiltrate; the nodes enlarge and the disease spreads through the lymphatic system
    \item \textbf{Subtypes:} nodular sclerosis (most common), mixed cellularity, lymphocyte-rich, lymphocyte-depleted, and nodular lymphocyte-predominant
    \item \textbf{Staging:} the extent of disease determines treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Painless enlargement of lymph nodes, most often in the neck, armpit, or groin
    \item A lump that may grow over weeks to months
    \item B symptoms: fever, drenching night sweats, and unexplained weight loss
    \item Itching of the skin
    \item Fatigue and malaise
    \item Cough, breathlessness, or chest pain if the chest nodes are involved
    \item Alcohol-induced pain in affected nodes, an uncommon but characteristic symptom
    \item Enlarged spleen or liver in advanced disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reactive lymph node enlargement from infections, including glandular fever
    \item Non-Hodgkin lymphoma and other lymphoproliferative disorders
    \item Tuberculosis and other infections causing lymphadenopathy
    \item Sarcoidosis
    \item Metastatic cancer to lymph nodes
    \item Biopsy with Reed--Sternberg cells confirms Hodgkin lymphoma
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for any lymph node that persists beyond a few weeks, enlarges, or is accompanied by fever, night sweats, or weight loss. Unexplained persistent symptoms warrant assessment.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing or severe chest pain
    \item High fever with severe illness during treatment
    \item Sudden weakness, numbness, or confusion
    \item Severe bleeding or bruising during chemotherapy
    \item Collapse or fainting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lymph node biopsy:} excision biopsy is the diagnostic test, showing Reed--Sternberg cells
    \item \textbf{Immunohistochemistry:} confirms the subtype
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, inflammatory markers, liver and kidney function
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT, PET, and sometimes MRI to stage the disease
    \item \textbf{Bone marrow biopsy:} in selected cases
    \item \textbf{PET scan:} essential for staging and assessing treatment response
    \item \textbf{Fertility and cardiac assessment:} before treatment planning
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy:} combination regimens such as ABVD, given in cycles
    \item \textbf{Radiation therapy:} involved-site radiation for early-stage disease
    \item \textbf{Combined modality:} chemotherapy plus radiation in selected patients
    \item \textbf{Response-adapted therapy:} PET scans guide treatment intensity
    \item \textbf{Advanced disease:} more intensive chemotherapy, and escalation for resistant disease
    \item \textbf{Relapsed disease:} high-dose chemotherapy with stem cell transplantation
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} management of side effects, infection prevention, and fertility preservation
    \item \textbf{Survivorship care:} long-term monitoring for late effects
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Side effects of chemotherapy: infection, anaemia, nausea, and fatigue
    \item Late effects: second cancers, heart and lung damage, and thyroid problems from treatment
    \item Infertility after some treatments, with fertility preservation options
    \item Relapse, most often within the first few years
    \item Immunosuppression and infection risk
    \item Psychological impact of diagnosis and treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Hodgkin lymphoma has one of the best prognoses of any cancer. Cure rates exceed 90\% for early-stage disease and 80--85\% for advanced disease, and most relapses are successfully treated with salvage therapy and transplantation. Long-term survival brings a focus on monitoring for late effects of treatment, which modern protocols are designed to minimise. For most people, Hodgkin lymphoma is a curable illness with a return to full, healthy life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item See a doctor promptly for persistent or growing lymph nodes
    \item Report B symptoms: fever, night sweats, and weight loss
    \item Attend all treatment appointments and complete the prescribed therapy
    \item Manage side effects with the treatment team
    \item Consider fertility preservation before treatment
    \item Attend long-term survivorship follow-up
    \item Maintain a healthy lifestyle to support recovery
    \item Seek psychological and peer support through cancer organisations
\end{itemize}
