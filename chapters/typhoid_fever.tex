\chapter{Typhoid fever}
\index{Typhoid fever}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Typhoid fever, also known historically as enteric fever, is an acute, life-threatening systemic infection caused by the bacterium \textit{Salmonella enterica} serovar Typhi (\textit{S. Typhi}). It is characterized by a prolonged, insidious fever, severe constitutional symptoms, and abdominal manifestations. Although clinically similar to paratyphoid fever, which is caused by \textit{Salmonella enterica} serovars Paratyphi A, B, and C, typhoid fever is typically more severe and accounts for the majority of enteric fever cases globally. Unlike non-typhoidal \textit{Salmonella} strains that cause self-limiting gastroenteritis and possess wide zoonotic reservoirs in animals, \textit{S. Typhi} is an obligate human pathogen. This host restriction means that humans are the sole reservoir of infection, and transmission occurs strictly through human-to-human pathways, primarily via food and water contaminated with human faeces or urine.

The disease has a profound historical significance. Some historians hypothesize that typhoid fever was the underlying cause of the Plague of Athens in 430 BCE, which killed one-third of the city's population and altered the course of the Peloponnesian War. The bacterium was first observed in spleen sections and lymph nodes of infected patients by the German pathologist Karl Joseph Eberth in 1880, and the first successful vaccine was developed by the British immunologist Almroth Edward Wright in 1896. Perhaps the most famous historical figure associated with the disease is Mary Mallon, known as ``Typhoid Mary,'' an asymptomatic chronic carrier in New York during the early 20th century. Working as a cook, she unwittingly infected dozens of people, illustrating the major public health challenge posed by asymptomatic individuals who continue to shed the bacteria.

In the modern era, typhoid fever serves as a sensitive marker of public health infrastructure. It is virtually non-existent in high-income nations where centralized water purification, effective sewage systems, and food safety standards are universally established. However, in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), particularly in rapidly expanding urban areas with inadequate water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure, typhoid remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality. The disease highlights the persistent gap in global health equity, where access to clean drinking water and sanitary waste disposal is not yet a universal reality.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The global epidemiological burden of typhoid fever remains substantial. The World Health Organization (WHO) and the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) estimate that between 9 million and 11 million cases of typhoid fever occur annually worldwide, resulting in approximately 110,000 to 161,000 deaths. Without antibiotic treatment, the case fatality rate of typhoid fever is estimated to be between 10\% and 20\%, but with modern, appropriate antimicrobial therapy and supportive care, this rate falls to less than 1\%.

Geographically, the burden is heavily concentrated in South Asia, which is considered the global epicenter of the disease. Countries such as India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh report some of the highest incidence rates, frequently exceeding 100 cases per 100,000 population per year. Other highly endemic regions include Southeast Asia, Sub-Saharan Africa, and parts of Latin America and the Caribbean. Within these endemic regions, transmission is highest in crowded urban slums, where municipal water lines are often poorly maintained and run parallel to open sewer systems, leading to cross-contamination.

The disease disproportionately affects children and young adults, with the peak incidence occurring in school-aged children (5 to 15 years old) and preschool-aged children (2 to 5 years old). Children under 2 years of age are also affected, but they often present with atypical symptoms, making diagnosis difficult. Travelers from non-endemic, high-income nations who visit endemic regions represent another important demographic. Because they lack the background immunity acquired through repeated subclinical exposures, travelers often present with severe, acute symptoms.

Seasonal fluctuations in typhoid incidence are common. In many endemic regions, cases peak during the hot, dry season when water supplies become scarce and highly concentrated, leading to a reliance on contaminated secondary sources. Conversely, in other regions, the peak occurs during the monsoon or rainy season, when heavy rainfall and flooding overwhelm sewage infrastructure, contaminating drinking water wells and municipal distribution pipes.

The most pressing epidemiological threat today is the rapid escalation of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in \textit{S. Typhi}. In the late 20th century, multidrug-resistant (MDR) strains emerged, defined by resistance to the traditional first-line antibiotics: ampicillin, chloramphenicol, and co-trimoxazole. This led to a shift toward fluoroquinolones (such as ciprofloxacin) as the preferred treatment. However, resistance to fluoroquinolones quickly developed and spread throughout Asia and Africa. In 2016, an outbreak of extensively drug-resistant (XDR) \textit{S. Typhi} occurred in the Sindh province of Pakistan. XDR strains are resistant to all first-line drugs, fluoroquinolones, and third-generation cephalosporins (such as ceftriaxone), leaving oral azithromycin and intravenous carbapenems (such as meropenem) as the only reliable therapeutic options. The global dissemination of XDR strains represents a major threat to typhoid control efforts.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The development of typhoid fever involves a complex interplay between the virulence factors of the bacterium and the host immune response. The major pathophysiological stages of the disease include the following:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Causative Pathogen}: The disease is caused by \textit{Salmonella enterica} subspecies \textit{enterica} serovar Typhi (\textit{S. Typhi}). It is a Gram-negative, rod-shaped, flagellated, facultative anaerobic bacterium. Its cell wall contains lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which acts as an endotoxin (O antigen), and it possesses flagella (H antigen). A crucial virulence factor is the Vi capsular polysaccharide antigen, which surrounds the outer membrane. The Vi antigen prevents antibody-mediated opsonization, inhibits complement activation, and shields the O antigen from recognition by host immune cells.
  \item \textbf{Transmission Mechanism}: Transmission occurs through the fecal-oral route. Ingestion of water or food contaminated with feces or urine from an acute patient or a chronic carrier is the primary route. Direct person-to-person transmission is less common but can occur in settings with poor personal hygiene.
  \item \textbf{Acid Tolerance Response}: To establish infection, \textit{S. Typhi} must survive the acidic environment of the stomach (pH 1.5 to 3.5). The bacterium activates an acid tolerance response, upregulating genes that produce acid-shock proteins to maintain internal pH homeostasis. Conditions that lower gastric acidity, such as the use of antacids, proton pump inhibitors, or chronic gastritis, lower the infectious dose required to cause disease.
  \item \textbf{Invasion of the Intestinal Mucosa}: Upon reaching the terminal ileum, \textit{S. Typhi} adheres to the mucosal epithelial cells. The bacterium targets the microfold (M) cells, specialized epithelial cells overlying the Peyer's patches (lymphoid follicles). Using a Type III Secretion System (T3SS-1) encoded by Salmonella Pathogenicity Island 1 (SPI-1), the bacterium injects effector proteins into the host cell. These proteins trigger actin rearrangement, causing ``membrane ruffling'' and internalizing the bacteria.
  \item \textbf{Intracellular Survival within Macrophages}: Once across the epithelial barrier, \textit{S. Typhi} is engulfed by subepithelial macrophages and dendritic cells. The bacterium survives within these phagocytes by utilizing a second Type III Secretion System (T3SS-2) encoded by Salmonella Pathogenicity Island 2 (SPI-2). Effector proteins modify the phagosome membrane, preventing phagosome-lysosome fusion and inhibiting the assembly of the NADPH oxidase complex, creating a protected niche known as the Salmonella-containing vacuole (SCV).
  \item \textbf{Primary Bacteraemia and Homing}: The infected macrophages migrate through the lymphatic system to the mesenteric lymph nodes. The bacteria then enter the bloodstream via the thoracic duct, causing a transient, low-grade primary bacteraemia. During this phase, which corresponds to the clinical incubation period, the bacteria are cleared from the blood by the organs of the reticuloendothelial system. The bacteria lodge in the Kupffer cells of the liver, the red pulp of the spleen, the bone marrow, and the lymph nodes, where they multiply intracellularly.
  \item \textbf{Secondary Bacteraemia and Systemic Symptoms}: Following multiplication within the reticuloendothelial system, a massive number of bacteria enter the bloodstream, initiating a sustained secondary bacteraemia. The lysis of these bacteria releases endotoxins into the blood, triggering a systemic inflammatory response. Host cells release pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-alpha, IL-1, and IL-6), which act on the hypothalamus to cause the characteristic sustained high fever, headache, and malaise.
  \item \textbf{Biliary Colonization and Re-infection}: During secondary bacteraemia, the bacteria enter the gallbladder via the biliary system or the hepatic artery. \textit{S. Typhi} is highly resistant to bile salts. It replicates in the gallbladder and forms biofilms on the surface of cholesterol gallstones. Bile laden with bacteria is then secreted into the duodenum, leading to re-infection of the intestinal tract, further invasion of Peyer's patches, and shedding of the bacteria in the stool.
  \item \textbf{Necrosis and Ulceration of Peyer's Patches}: Re-exposure of the sensitized Peyer's patches to \textit{S. Typhi} in the bile triggers a severe inflammatory response. The lymphoid tissue undergoes hyper-sensitization, hypertrophy, and hyperplasia. The swelling leads to localized ischemia, tissue necrosis, and sloughing of the mucosa. This leaves classic oval-shaped ulcers along the long axis of the ileum, which can erode into blood vessels, causing intestinal haemorrhage, or penetrate the bowel wall, causing free perforation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of typhoid fever is characteristically progressive and varies depending on the duration of illness. When untreated, the disease classically progresses through four distinct weekly stages:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Incubation Period}: This phase typically lasts 7 to 14 days, but can range from 3 to 60 days. The incubation period is shorter with a larger bacterial inoculum and longer in individuals with partial immunity.
  \item \textbf{First Week of Illness (Invasion Stage)}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Step-Ladder Fever}: The body temperature rises in a gradual, stepwise fashion over 3 to 4 days, peaking at 39$^\circ$C to 40$^\circ$C, where it remains as a continuous fever.
    \item \textbf{Constitutional Symptoms}: Patients experience a severe, constant frontal headache, generalized malaise, muscle aches (myalgia), joint pain (arthralgia), and extreme fatigue.
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal Symptoms}: Abdominal discomfort is common. Adults typically experience constipation, while children may present with mild diarrhoea. A dry, non-productive cough is also common.
    \item \textbf{Faget's Sign}: Relative bradycardia is often present, where the pulse rate is slower than expected for the height of the fever (e.g., a heart rate of 80 beats per minute with a temperature of 40$^\circ$C).
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Second Week of Illness (Plateau Stage)}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Persistent High Fever}: The fever plateaus at a high, continuous level (39.5$^\circ$C to 40.5$^\circ$C) with little diurnal variation.
    \item \textbf{Rose Spots}: A characteristic rash consisting of faint, salmon-pink, blanching macules (2 to 4 mm in diameter) appears in small crops of 5 to 15 lesions on the upper abdomen and lower chest. They appear around day 7 to 10 and fade within 3 to 4 days.
    \item \textbf{Typhoid Tongue}: The tongue becomes dry and coated with a thick, grayish-white fur, with red margins and tip.
    \item \textbf{Abdominal Distension and Organomegaly}: The abdomen becomes distended, and tenderness in the right iliac fossa is common. Palpation may reveal splenomegaly and hepatomegaly. Diarrhoea may develop, characteristically described as watery, green-brown ``pea-soup'' stools.
    \item \textbf{Toxic Appearance}: The patient appears severely ill, showing marked apathy, lethargy, and a dull, expressionless look.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Third Week of Illness (Complication Stage)}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The Typhoid State}: The patient may enter a state of semi-coma or delirium, characterized by muttering delirium, picking at bedclothes (carphologia), or grasping at imaginary objects (floccillation).
    \item \textbf{Severe Complications}: This is the period of highest risk for life-threatening complications. Intestinal necrosis and ulceration of Peyer's patches can lead to severe gastrointestinal haemorrhage or free bowel perforation in the terminal ileum, leading to acute peritonitis and septic shock.
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular Collapse}: Myocarditis, toxaemia, and septic shock can cause severe hypotension, rapid heart rate, and multi-organ failure.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Fourth Week of Illness (Resolution/Convalescent Stage)}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Defervescence}: In survivors of untreated typhoid, the temperature begins to fall in a stepwise, fluctuating manner (lysis) over several days.
    \item \textbf{Clinical Recovery}: The appetite returns, abdominal distension subsides, and mental status normalizes. However, the patient remains weak and emaciated.
    \item \textbf{Relapse and Carriage}: Relapse occurs in approximately 5\% to 10\% of patients, usually 1 to 2 weeks after defervescence. A small percentage of patients (1\% to 5\%) become chronic carriers, excreting the bacteria in their stool or urine for over a year.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the symptoms of typhoid fever are non-specific and overlap with many other febrile illnesses, a broad differential diagnosis must be considered. In endemic areas, the following conditions must be ruled out:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Malaria}: The primary differential diagnosis in malaria-endemic regions. Both present with high fever, headache, myalgia, and splenomegaly. However, malaria is characterized by paroxysmal fevers with rigors and sweats, whereas typhoid fever is continuous. Malaria does not present with rose spots or relative bradycardia. Diagnosis is confirmed by demonstrating \textit{Plasmodium} parasites on thick and thin blood films or via rapid diagnostic tests.
  \item \textbf{Dengue Fever}: Often co-endemic with typhoid. Dengue features a sudden onset of high fever, severe retro-orbital headache, intense muscle and joint pain (``breakbone fever''), and a maculopapular rash. While both can cause leukopenia, dengue is characterized by rapid, severe thrombocytopenia and hemoconcentration. It lacks the step-ladder fever progression and abdominal localization of typhoid. Dengue is diagnosed via NS1 antigen testing or serology.
  \item \textbf{Rickettsial Diseases (Typhus)}: Scrub typhus, murine typhus, and epidemic typhus present with fever, headache, rash, and splenomegaly. Scrub typhus is distinguished by the presence of a pathognomonic black eschar at the site of the chigger bite, regional lymphadenopathy, and a more rapid onset of symptoms. Diagnosis is confirmed by serology or PCR.
  \item \textbf{Leptospirosis}: A bacterial infection transmitted via water contaminated with rodent urine. It presents with fever, headache, myalgia, and abdominal pain. Leptospirosis is distinguished by marked conjunctival suffusion (redness of the eyes without discharge), severe calf tenderness, jaundice, and acute kidney injury. Microscopic agglutination testing (MAT) or PCR is diagnostic.
  \item \textbf{Brucellosis}: A chronic zoonosis contracted from unpasteurized dairy products or infected livestock. It presents with prolonged fever, night sweats, arthralgia, and hepatosplenomegaly. Unlike typhoid, brucellosis has an undulant fever pattern, a more chronic and indolent course, frequent musculoskeletal involvement, and is diagnosed via blood culture or specific serological agglutination tests.
  \item \textbf{Tuberculosis}: Miliary tuberculosis or abdominal tuberculosis can present with prolonged fever, night sweats, weight loss, and abdominal symptoms mimicking typhoid. Abdominal tuberculosis typically features ascites, palpable abdominal masses, or a more chronic course. Chest radiography, sputum acid-fast bacilli (AFB) stains, interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs), and tissue biopsies help differentiate the two.
  \item \textbf{Amoebic Liver Abscess}: Caused by \textit{Entamoeba histolytica}, presenting with fever, right upper quadrant abdominal pain, hepatomegaly, and weight loss. Unlike typhoid, it is characterized by localized liver tenderness, diaphragm elevation on chest X-ray, leukocytosis, and can be confirmed by abdominal ultrasound and positive amoebic serology.
  \item \textbf{Acute Viral Hepatitis}: Hepatitis A or E can present with prodromal fever, headache, malaise, and abdominal discomfort. The subsequent appearance of dark urine, scleral icterus (jaundice), hepatomegaly, and extremely high serum transaminases (AST/ALT greater than 1000 U/L) differentiates it from typhoid, where transaminases are only mildly elevated.
  \item \textbf{Influenza and COVID-19}: Viral respiratory pathogens that present with fever, headache, cough, and myalgia. They are distinguished from typhoid by the presence of prominent upper respiratory symptoms (rhinorrhea, sore throat, sneezing), respiratory distress, anosmia (in COVID-19), and positive nasopharyngeal swab PCR or antigen tests.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Typhoid fever requires prompt medical evaluation and treatment. Anyone traveling to or returning from a typhoid-endemic region who develops a persistent fever (38$^\circ$C or higher) accompanied by headache, abdominal pain, or a general feeling of illness must consult a doctor immediately. Early antibiotic treatment is critical to preventing progression to severe disease.

Immediate emergency medical care is required if any of the following ``red flag'' symptoms develop:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Sudden, severe, or worsening abdominal pain, which may indicate intestinal perforation.
  \item Abdominal rigidity or guarding, where the abdomen feels hard and is extremely tender to the touch.
  \item Blood in the stool, or stools that appear black, sticky, and tarry, suggesting active intestinal bleeding.
  \item Confusion, disorientation, hallucinations, extreme drowsiness, or difficulty waking up.
  \item Signs of systemic shock or cardiovascular collapse, such as rapid, shallow breathing, a racing heart rate, cold, clammy, or pale skin, extreme dizziness, or fainting when attempting to stand.
\end{itemize}

In the presence of any of these life-threatening symptoms, call emergency services immediately:
\begin{itemize}
  \item In \textbf{the affected region}, call \textbf{local emergency services}.
  \item In the \textbf{United States and Canada}, call \textbf{911}.
  \item In the \textbf{United Kingdom}, call \textbf{999}.
  \item In the \textbf{European Union} and globally on mobile networks, call \textbf{112}.
\end{itemize}
When seeking care, always inform the medical staff of any recent international travel, including dates and locations.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of typhoid fever relies on a combination of clinical evaluation, microbiological cultures, and supportive laboratory tests. Because clinical features are non-specific, laboratory confirmation is essential:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Clinical Examination}: Evaluates the pattern of fever, checks for relative bradycardia, examines the tongue for a thick white coat, palpates the abdomen for tenderness and organomegaly, and inspects the skin of the chest and abdomen for rose spots.
  \item \textbf{Microbiological Cultures (The Diagnostic Gold Standard)}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood Cultures}: Most useful during the first week of illness. Its sensitivity is highest (50\% to 80\%) early in the disease and decreases as the infection progresses or if the patient has already taken antibiotics. Large volume blood cultures (10 to 15 mL for adults) improve yield.
    \item \textbf{Bone Marrow Aspirate Culture}: The most sensitive diagnostic method (up to 95\% sensitivity). Unlike blood cultures, bone marrow cultures are unaffected by prior antibiotic therapy and remain positive even late in the disease. However, due to its invasive nature, it is reserved for complicated, diagnostic-dilemma cases.
    \item \textbf{Stool and Urine Cultures}: Useful during the second and third weeks of illness. Stool cultures are positive in 60\% to 70\% of patients during this period. They are also essential post-treatment to document clearance and identify potential chronic carriers.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Serological Tests}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Widal Test}: Measures agglutinating antibodies against the O (somatic) and H (flagellar) antigens. A four-fold rise in titre between acute and convalescent sera is highly suggestive, but a single high titre (e.g., TO greater than 1:160 or TH greater than 1:160) is of limited value due to high background antibody levels in endemic areas and cross-reactivity.
    \item \textbf{Typhidot}: A rapid test that detects IgM and IgG antibodies against a specific 50 kDa outer membrane protein of \textit{S. Typhi}. IgM indicates acute infection, while IgG indicates past exposure. It is faster than culture, though it can have variable specificity in endemic regions.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Molecular Assays (PCR)}: Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays targeting the flagellin gene (\textit{fliC}) or the Vi capsular antigen gene (\textit{viaB}) offer rapid and highly sensitive detection of \textit{S. Typhi} DNA in blood or urine. These tests are useful but are currently not widely available in resource-limited primary care settings.
  \item \textbf{Full Blood Count (FBC)}: Classic findings include mild leukopenia (white blood cell count typically 3,000 to 5,000/mm$^3$) with relative lymphocytosis, and a complete absence of eosinophils (aneosinophilia). Normocytic, normochromic anaemia and mild thrombocytopenia are common in the second and third weeks.
  \item \textbf{Biochemical Tests}: Liver function tests often show mild to moderate elevations of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), usually 2 to 3 times the upper limit of normal, along with mild hyperbilirubinemia. Inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) are elevated.
  \item \textbf{Imaging Modalities}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Abdominal Ultrasonography} can detect hepatomegaly, splenomegaly, mesenteric lymphadenopathy, gallbladder wall thickening, and the presence of free peritoneal fluid.
    \item \textbf{Chest and Abdominal Radiography (X-ray)}: If intestinal perforation is suspected, an erect chest X-ray is performed to look for free gas under the diaphragm (pneumoperitoneum), which indicates a surgical emergency.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of typhoid fever involves antimicrobial therapy, supportive care, monitoring for complications, and surgical intervention when necessary. The primary components of treatment include the following:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Antimicrobial Therapy}: Antibiotics must be initiated promptly upon clinical suspicion. The selection of agent is guided by the severity of the disease and local antimicrobial resistance profiles:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Susceptible Strains (Uncomplicated Case)}: Oral fluoroquinolones, such as ciprofloxacin (500 mg orally twice daily for 7 to 10 days) or ofloxacin (400 mg orally twice daily), are highly effective.
    \item \textbf{Multidrug-Resistant (MDR) Strains}: For infections resistant to chloramphenicol, ampicillin, and co-trimoxazole, oral azithromycin (1 g orally on day 1, followed by 500 mg daily for 5 to 7 days, or 1 g daily for 5 days) is the preferred choice for uncomplicated cases. For severe cases, intravenous third-generation cephalosporins, such as ceftriaxone (2 g IV daily for 10 to 14 days) or cefotaxime (2 g IV every 8 hours), are indicated.
    \item \textbf{Extensively Drug-Resistant (XDR) Strains}: For XDR strains (resistant to first-line agents, fluoroquinolones, and ceftriaxone), treatment requires intravenous carbapenems, such as meropenem (1 to 2 g IV every 8 hours for 10 to 14 days) or imipenem. Azithromycin remains an option for uncomplicated cases if the strain is susceptible.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Adjunctive Corticosteroid Therapy}: For severe typhoid fever characterized by delirium, stupor, coma, shock, or severe hemodynamic instability, high-dose corticosteroids are recommended. Intravenous dexamethasone (initial dose of 3 mg/kg by slow infusion over 30 minutes, followed by 1 mg/kg every 6 hours for 8 additional doses) has been shown to reduce mortality by up to 50\% in these critical cases. It must be administered concurrently with effective antibiotic therapy.
  \item \textbf{Supportive Fluid and Electrolyte Therapy}: Correcting dehydration and maintaining electrolyte balance is vital.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Oral Rehydration}: For patients with mild dehydration and no persistent vomiting, oral rehydration salts (ORS) should be administered frequently.
    \item \textbf{Intravenous Fluid Resuscitation}: For patients with severe dehydration, persistent vomiting, intestinal haemorrhage, or septic shock, intravenous fluids (such as 0.9\% normal saline or Ringer's lactate) must be administered immediately.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Nutritional Therapy}: Patients should receive a high-calorie, high-protein, but low-residue (easily digestible) diet to maintain nutrition while preventing irritation to the ulcerated Peyer's patches. Foods such as broths, custards, bananas, boiled rice, and pureed potatoes are recommended. Avoid high-fiber, spicy, or fried foods. Regular small meals are preferred over large ones.
  \item \textbf{Surgical Management of Perforation}: Intestinal perforation is an absolute surgical emergency.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item An immediate exploratory laparotomy must be performed to identify the site of perforation (usually in the terminal ileum).
    \item The surgical procedure typically involves primary closure of the perforation in two layers, or segmental resection of the ileum if multiple perforations or extensive necrosis are present.
    \item This is followed by thorough peritoneal toilet (irrigation of the abdominal cavity with warm normal saline) and post-operative administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics covering Gram-negative and anaerobic bacteria.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Eradication of Chronic Carriage}: To clear \textit{S. Typhi} carriage (excretion for more than 12 months), prolonged antibiotic therapy is required.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Regimens include oral amoxicillin (100 mg/kg/day in 3 divided doses) or oral ampicillin for 4 to 6 weeks, or oral ciprofloxacin (750 mg twice daily) for 4 weeks.
    \item In patients with concurrent cholelithiasis (gallstones), cholecystectomy is usually required alongside antibiotic therapy to successfully eradicate the carrier state, as the bacteria reside in biofilms on the stones.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Complications of typhoid fever are common, particularly in patients who do not receive timely or appropriate antibiotic therapy. These complications typically develop in the third week of illness and include the following:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal Haemorrhage}: Occurs in 2\% to 10\% of cases, typically in the third week of illness. It results from the erosion of blood vessels in the sloughing ulcers of Peyer's patches. Symptoms range from mild occult blood in the stool to massive haematochezia or melaena, leading to acute anaemia, hypotension, and shock. Management includes blood transfusion, intensive monitoring, and sometimes endoscopic or surgical intervention.
  \item \textbf{Intestinal Perforation}: The most lethal complication, occurring in 1\% to 3\% of hospitalized patients, typically during the third week. Necrosis of the Peyer's patch extends through the entire bowel wall of the terminal ileum, causing leakage of intestinal contents into the peritoneal cavity. This triggers acute bacterial peritonitis, presenting with sudden, severe abdominal pain, abdominal rigidity (board-like abdomen), guarding, rebound tenderness, fever spike or hypothermia, and rapid progression to septic shock and multi-organ failure.
  \item \textbf{Neuropsychiatric Complications (Toxic Encephalopathy)}: Occurs in up to 10\% of severe cases. It is characterized by delirium, confusion, stupor, or coma (``typhoid state''). Less common neurological manifestations include meningismus, peripheral neuropathy, cerebellar ataxia, and Guillain-Barré syndrome.
  \item \textbf{Cardiovascular Complications}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Myocarditis}: Inflammation of the heart muscle, occurring in 1\% to 5\% of patients. It can lead to ECG abnormalities (prolonged PR interval, T-wave changes), arrhythmias, cardiogenic shock, or congestive heart failure.
    \item \textbf{Septic Shock}: Caused by severe bacteraemia and the release of endotoxins, leading to refractory hypotension and poor tissue perfusion.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hepatobiliary Complications}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute Cholecystitis}: Inflammation of the gallbladder, presenting with right upper quadrant pain and tenderness.
    \item \textbf{Reactive Hepatitis}: Characterized by hepatomegaly and jaundice, with elevated transaminases.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Metastatic Focal Infections}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Osteomyelitis}: Occurs when \textit{S. Typhi} seeds the bones. It is particularly common in patients with sickle cell disease or other haemoglobinopathies, typically affecting the long bones or spine.
    \item \textbf{Splenic Rupture}: A rare but life-threatening complication resulting from severe splenomegaly and minor trauma or splenic infarction.
    \item \textbf{Meningitis}: Rare, localized infection of the meninges, primarily seen in infants.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Renal Complications}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)}: Usually pre-renal, secondary to severe dehydration, vomiting, or septic shock.
    \item \textbf{Glomerulonephritis}: Immune complex-mediated glomerulonephritis, presenting with proteinuria and hematuria.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of typhoid fever has been transformed by the introduction of antibiotics. In the pre-antibiotic era, typhoid was a highly lethal disease, with mortality rates ranging from 10\% to 20\%, and even higher in vulnerable populations. When appropriate antibiotic therapy is initiated promptly, the mortality rate drops to less than 1\%, and the vast majority of patients make a full recovery.

The recovery timeline depends on the speed of diagnosis and the effectiveness of the treatment. Following the start of effective antibiotic therapy, patients typically show clinical improvement and become afebrile within 3 to 5 days, although complete recovery and restoration of strength can take several weeks. If untreated or treated with ineffective antibiotics due to drug resistance, the illness can persist for 4 weeks or longer, with a prolonged convalescence and a high risk of relapse (5\% to 10\%) or progression to chronic carriage.

Several factors affect the prognosis of typhoid fever:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The age and baseline health of the patient, with infants, the elderly, and malnourished individuals experiencing a higher rate of complications and worse clinical outcomes.
  \item The presence of complications, where intestinal perforation or severe haemorrhage dramatically increases mortality rates (perforation mortality ranges from 10\% to 30\% even with surgical intervention).
  \item The resistance profile of the infecting strain, as infections with MDR or XDR \textit{S. Typhi} are associated with delayed administration of effective therapy, increased duration of illness, higher rates of complications, and increased treatment costs.
  \item Access to medical care, where delays in seeking care or lack of access to surgical facilities for perforation significantly worsen the clinical outcome.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventing typhoid fever requires a combination of public health measures, safe personal habits, and active immunization. The primary strategies for prevention and self-care include the following:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Water Sanitation and Infrastructure}: The primary public health prevention measure is the provision of safe, chlorinated municipal drinking water and the implementation of effective sewage treatment systems to prevent human waste from contaminating water supplies.
  \item \textbf{Food Safety Practices}: When traveling to or living in typhoid-endemic areas, individuals must adhere to the rule: ``Boil it, cook it, peel it, or forget it.''
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Drink only bottled, boiled, or chemically disinfected (chlorinated or iodinated) water. Avoid tap water and ice.
    \item Eat food that is thoroughly cooked and served steaming hot. Avoid food left at room temperature.
    \item Avoid raw fruits and vegetables unless they can be peeled by the consumer. Avoid salads and raw leafy greens.
    \item Avoid raw or undercooked meats, poultry, and seafood (especially shellfish).
    \item Purchase food and drinks from reputable establishments and avoid street vendors.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hand Hygiene}: Wash hands thoroughly with soap and running water for at least 20 seconds before preparing or eating food, after using the toilet, and after changing diapers. If soap and water are unavailable, use an alcohol-based hand sanitizer containing at least 60\% alcohol.
  \item \textbf{Active Immunization}: Vaccination is recommended for travelers to endemic areas, laboratory personnel working with \textit{S. Typhi}, and close contacts of chronic carriers. Three vaccines are available:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Typhoid Conjugate Vaccine (TCV)}: A single-dose intramuscular injection containing the Vi polysaccharide conjugated to a tetanus toxoid carrier protein. It is approved for infants and children from 6 months of age and adults up to 45 or 65 years. TCV provides superior immunogenicity, longer-lasting protection (at least 5 years), and is the preferred vaccine for routine childhood immunization in endemic countries.
    \item \textbf{Vi Capsular Polysaccharide Vaccine}: An intramuscular injection of purified Vi antigen. It is approved for individuals aged 2 years and older. It offers protection for 2 to 3 years, after which a booster dose is required.
    \item \textbf{Ty21a Oral Vaccine}: A live-attenuated vaccine administered as a series of 3 or 4 enteric-coated capsules taken on alternate days. It is approved for individuals aged 5 to 6 years and older. It requires a booster dose every 5 years.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Strict Self-Care During Convalescence}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Complete the Antibiotic Course}: It is essential to finish the entire prescribed antibiotic course exactly as directed by the doctor, even if symptoms disappear. Stopping early increases the risk of a relapse or of becoming a chronic carrier.
    \item \textbf{Rest and Hydration}: Adequate bed rest and oral hydration with clean water, broth, or oral rehydration salts (ORS) are essential during recovery.
    \item \textbf{Prevent Transmission to Others}: Recovering patients must not prepare or handle food for others until they are confirmed non-infectious. Strict personal hygiene must be maintained, and stools must be disposed of sanitarily.
    \item \textbf{Microbiological Clearance Testing}: Patients (especially food handlers, healthcare workers, and childcare providers) must undergo follow-up stool cultures to ensure they have cleared the bacteria. Typically, three consecutive negative stool cultures obtained at least 48 hours after completing antibiotic therapy are required before returning to work.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
