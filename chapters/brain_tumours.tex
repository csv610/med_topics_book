\chapter{Brain Tumours}
\index{Brain Tumours}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A brain tumour is an abnormal growth of cells within the brain or the tissues that surround and support it, including the meninges, the pituitary gland, the cranial nerves, and the pineal gland. Tumours are described as \textbf{primary} when they arise within the brain itself and \textbf{secondary} (or metastatic) when they spread to the brain from a cancer elsewhere in the body, such as the lung, breast, bowel, or skin. They may also be \textbf{benign} (slow-growing and non-cancerous) or \textbf{malignant} (cancerous and more aggressive). Because the skull is a rigid, closed container, any growing mass takes up space and raises the pressure inside the head, so the seriousness of a brain tumour depends less on whether it is technically cancerous and more on its size, location, rate of growth, and the extent to which it disrupts normal brain function. Even a benign, slow-growing tumour can be dangerous if it sits in a vital area or obstructs the flow of cerebrospinal fluid.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Primary brain tumours are relatively uncommon compared with cancers of the breast, lung, or bowel, but they cause a disproportionate amount of disability and death because of their location. They can occur at any age, and each type has its own pattern: some, such as medulloblastoma and pilocytic astrocytoma, are seen mainly in children, where brain tumours are the most common solid malignancy, while others, such as meningioma and glioblastoma, become more common with age and are diagnosed mostly in older adults. Overall, men and women are affected at broadly similar rates, although individual types favour one sex -- meningiomas are more common in women and gliomas in men. In adults, secondary (metastatic) brain tumours are actually more common than primary brain tumours, and their frequency has risen as treatment of systemic cancers has improved.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
For most brain tumours no clear cause can be identified, and they arise sporadically. Known and suspected risk factors include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ionising radiation:} exposure to radiation, including radiotherapy to the head for other conditions, increases the risk of meningiomas and other tumours, often decades later
    \item \textbf{Inherited conditions:} rare syndromes such as neurofibromatosis type 1 and 2, tuberous sclerosis, and Li--Fraumeni syndrome predispose to specific tumours
    \item \textbf{Suppressed immunity:} certain lymphomas of the brain are more common in people with weakened immune systems
    \item \textbf{Family history:} a small minority of brain tumours cluster in families, even without a named syndrome
\end{itemize}

In most cases the tumour arises from a spontaneous mutation in a single cell, which then multiplies out of control. As it grows, the tumour compresses normal brain tissue, blocks the circulation of cerebrospinal fluid (causing hydrocephalus and raised pressure), provokes surrounding oedema, and disrupts the pathways controlling movement, sensation, speech, and thought. Unlike most other cancers, brain tumours very rarely spread outside the nervous system.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on the size, location, and growth rate of the tumour and typically develop progressively:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Headache:} persistent and progressive, often worse on waking in the morning, and sometimes associated with nausea or vomiting
    \item \textbf{Seizures:} a first-ever seizure in an adult should always be investigated; seizures are the presenting feature of many brain tumours
    \item \textbf{Focal neurological signs:} weakness or clumsiness of one side of the body, numbness, or difficulty speaking
    \item \textbf{Visual problems:} blurred or double vision, or loss of part of the visual field
    \item \textbf{Cognitive and personality change:} memory problems, confusion, irritability, apathy, or withdrawal
    \item \textbf{Balance and coordination problems:} dizziness, unsteadiness, or recurrent falls
    \item \textbf{Nausea and vomiting} unrelated to eating, particularly in the morning
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Several other conditions can produce identical symptoms and must be considered:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Stroke or transient ischaemic attack
    \item Migraine and other primary headache disorders
    \item Multiple sclerosis and other demyelinating or inflammatory conditions
    \item Epilepsy not related to a tumour
    \item Brain abscess or other intracranial infection
    \item Encephalitis
    \item Chronic subdural haematoma, especially in older adults
    \item Hydrocephalus of other cause
    \item Idiopathic intracranial hypertension -- raised pressure within the skull without a tumour
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor promptly for a new or worsening headache, a first seizure, or any new neurological symptom such as weakness, numbness, or difficulty speaking, and seek urgent assessment if symptoms are progressive or interfering with daily life.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe weakness, facial droop, or difficulty speaking
    \item A seizure, especially a first seizure or one lasting more than a few minutes
    \item Sudden loss of consciousness or confusion
    \item Sudden severe headache with neck stiffness and fever (possible meningitis)
    \item Worsening vomiting or drowsiness suggesting rapidly rising pressure inside the head
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurological examination:} testing of strength, sensation, coordination, reflexes, gait, and cranial nerves
    \item \textbf{Eye examination:} looking for papilloedema, swelling of the optic disc caused by raised intracranial pressure
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT of the head for rapid initial assessment, and MRI of the brain, the standard investigation, which gives far greater anatomical detail and is used for diagnosis and treatment planning
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} a sample of tumour tissue, obtained during surgical resection or by image-guided needle biopsy, examined under the microscope to establish the type and grade
    \item \textbf{Molecular testing:} analysis of genetic markers such as IDH mutation status and MGMT promoter methylation, which guide treatment and help estimate the outlook
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture:} used only in selected situations, such as suspected leptomeningeal spread or where infection is a consideration
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is planned by a multidisciplinary team of neurosurgeons, clinical oncologists, neuropathologists, radiologists, and specialist nurses, and depends on the type, grade, and location of the tumour, as well as the person's age and general health:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} maximal safe removal of the tumour (resection) where possible, or a smaller diagnostic biopsy; sometimes the aim is to relieve pressure rather than to cure
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} targeted radiation to destroy residual tumour cells, including focused techniques such as stereotactic radiosurgery for small or inaccessible lesions
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy:} anti-cancer medicines such as temozolomide, used for many high-grade gliomas and some other tumour types
    \item \textbf{Targeted therapies:} newer medicines that act on specific genetic changes within the tumour
    \item \textbf{Corticosteroids:} dexamethasone and similar drugs reduce swelling around the tumour and relieve symptoms quickly
    \item \textbf{Antiepileptic medicines:} to control seizures where the tumour has caused them
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation and support:} physiotherapy, speech and language therapy, occupational therapy, and psychological support for recovery and quality of life
    \item \textbf{Palliative care:} expert symptom control and comfort for tumours that cannot be cured
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Raised intracranial pressure, causing worsening headache, vomiting, and drowsiness, and in extreme cases brain herniation
    \item Seizures and epilepsy
    \item Permanent neurological deficits such as weakness, speech problems, or visual loss, from the tumour or its treatment
    \item Hydrocephalus requiring drainage by a shunt or endoscopic procedure
    \item Cognitive and personality changes that affect relationships and work
    \item Recurrence of the tumour after apparently successful treatment
    \item Side effects of surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, including infection, bleeding, radiation necrosis, fatigue, and impaired immunity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook varies enormously and depends on the tumour type, grade, and location, the completeness of surgical removal, and the person's age and fitness. Some benign, slow-growing tumours can be cured by surgery or remain stable for many years, carrying an excellent long-term prognosis. High-grade malignant tumours such as glioblastoma are aggressive, and despite combined treatment the average survival is often measured in months to a couple of years. Other malignant types, such as oligodendrogliomas with favourable molecular features, carry a much better outlook. Early diagnosis generally improves outcomes, and long-term follow-up with regular imaging is needed to detect recurrence.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
There is no reliable way to prevent most brain tumours, but some measures may help:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Avoid unnecessary radiation exposure to the head, including medically unnecessary head CT scans
    \item Be aware of family history and seek genetic advice if a known inherited syndrome runs in the family
    \item Attend follow-up appointments and scheduled imaging after treatment
    \item Seek prompt medical review for any new or persistent neurological symptom
    \item Build a support network of family, friends, and health professionals to manage the emotional impact of diagnosis and treatment
    \item Follow rehabilitation and exercise advice to maintain strength, function, and quality of life
\end{itemize}
