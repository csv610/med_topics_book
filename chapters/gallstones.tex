\chapter{Gallstones}
\index{Gallstones}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Gallstones are hard, pebble-like deposits that form in the gallbladder, the small organ beneath the liver that stores bile. They develop when substances in bile, mainly cholesterol, precipitate and crystallise. Gallstones are extremely common, and the majority cause no symptoms at all, but when they block the flow of bile they cause attacks of severe abdominal pain called biliary colic, and they can lead to complications including inflammation of the gallbladder (cholecystitis), infection of the bile ducts (cholangitis), and pancreatitis. Symptomatic gallstones are usually treated by removing the gallbladder, which cures the problem. This chapter explains how gallstones form, their symptoms, and their treatment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Gallstones affect around 10--15% of adults and up to a third of people over 60. They are twice as common in women as in men, and risk rises with age, obesity, pregnancy, rapid weight loss, and a family history. Around 80% of people with gallstones never develop symptoms, and most of the rest have recurrent colic. Complications such as cholecystitis and pancreatitis affect a small proportion each year. Gallbladder surgery for gallstones is one of the most common operations in many countries, and most people recover quickly and completely.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bile:} the liver produces bile, a fluid rich in cholesterol, bile salts, and bilirubin, which is stored and concentrated in the gallbladder and released to digest fats
    \item \textbf{Cholesterol stones:} form when bile contains more cholesterol than the bile salts can keep dissolved, a state favoured by obesity, a high-fat diet, rapid weight loss, and hormonal changes
    \item \textbf{Pigment stones:} form from excess bilirubin in conditions such as chronic haemolysis and cirrhosis
    \item \textbf{Stasis:} a gallbladder that does not empty fully, as in pregnancy and prolonged fasting, allows crystals to form
    \item \textbf{Biliary colic:} a stone plugs the cystic duct, the gallbladder contracts against it, and pressure builds, causing pain
    \item \textbf{Cholecystitis:} sustained blockage inflames the gallbladder wall, often with bacterial infection
    \item \textbf{Duct obstruction:} stones that escape into the common bile duct cause jaundice, cholangitis, and pancreatitis
    \item \textbf{Natural history:} most stones stay in the gallbladder and remain silent; the complications occur when they move
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item No symptoms at all in most people
    \item Biliary colic: sudden, intense pain in the upper right abdomen or the upper centre, radiating to the back or right shoulder, after a fatty meal or at night, lasting from 15 minutes to several hours
    \item Nausea and vomiting during attacks
    \item Pain that recurs intermittently over months
    \item Cholecystitis: pain lasting more than a few hours, with fever, tenderness, and feeling unwell
    \item Jaundice, dark urine, and pale stools with a blocked bile duct
    \item Complications present with severe pain, high fever, rigors, and vomiting
    \item Indigestion and discomfort after fatty meals in some people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The pain of gallstones overlaps with many conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Peptic ulcer disease and reflux
    \item Pancreatitis
    \item Irritable bowel syndrome and functional dyspepsia
    \item Heart attack and other cardiac causes of upper pain
    \item Kidney stones
    \item Liver disease and hepatitis
    \item Ultrasound and blood tests distinguish gallstones reliably
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with recurrent upper abdominal pain after meals, or pain that is severe, should be assessed. Seek urgent care for pain lasting more than a few hours, pain with fever or jaundice, or vomiting.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain that does not settle
    \item Fever with abdominal pain, which may indicate cholecystitis or cholangitis
    \item Jaundice with shaking chills
    \item Vomiting with the pain
    \item Chest pain or breathlessness
    \item Collapse or confusion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the pattern of pain and tenderness in the right upper abdomen
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} the first-line test, showing the stones and any thickening of the gallbladder wall or dilated ducts
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} liver function, bilirubin, inflammatory markers, and pancreatic enzymes
    \item \textbf{MRCP:} magnetic resonance imaging for stones in the bile ducts
    \item \textbf{ERCP:} endoscopic imaging that can also remove duct stones
    \item \textbf{CT:} in emergencies and for planning surgery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asymptomatic stones:} no treatment is needed; they are usually monitored with reassurance
    \item \textbf{Biliary colic:} pain relief and planned gallbladder removal if attacks recur
    \item \textbf{Cholecystectomy:} removal of the gallbladder, usually laparoscopic keyhole surgery, which cures symptomatic stones
    \item \textbf{Cholecystitis:} hospital admission, antibiotics, fluids, and surgery
    \item \textbf{Duct stones:} ERCP to remove them, followed by cholecystectomy
    \item \textbf{Cholangitis and pancreatitis:} urgent hospital care with antibiotics, fluids, and drainage or surgery
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} ursodeoxycholic acid can dissolve small cholesterol stones in selected people but is slow and less effective than surgery
    \item \textbf{Diet:} a healthy, lower-fat diet and weight management help prevent new stones and reduce symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute cholecystitis with abscess, gangrene, or perforation
    \item Cholangitis, a life-threatening bile duct infection
    \item Acute pancreatitis, which can be severe
    \item Obstructive jaundice and liver injury
    \item Gallstone ileus, a rare bowel obstruction
    \item Increased risk of gallbladder cancer with very long-standing large stones, though rare
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people with gallstones have no symptoms and require no treatment, and the stones remain harmless for life. Symptomatic stones are cured by gallbladder removal, which is safe, quick, and allows a full return to normal life. Complications respond well to prompt treatment, and the outlook for treated gallstone disease is excellent. The key is accurate diagnosis and appropriate timing: silent stones are left alone, while symptomatic and complicated stones are treated surgically.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and avoid rapid weight-loss diets
    \item Eat a diet high in fibre and low in saturated fat
    \item Exercise regularly
    \item Do not skip meals for long periods, which encourages stasis
    \item Seek assessment for recurrent upper abdominal pain
    \item Seek urgent care for severe pain, fever, or jaundice
    \item Follow the post-operative diet advice after surgery
\end{itemize}
