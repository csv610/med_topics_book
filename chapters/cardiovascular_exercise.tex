\chapter{Cardiovascular Exercise}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cardiovascular exercise, also called aerobic or cardiorespiratory exercise, is any activity that repeatedly uses large muscle groups and raises the heart rate and breathing for a sustained period. It includes brisk walking, jogging, swimming, cycling, dancing, rowing, and team sports. Regular cardiovascular exercise strengthens the heart and lungs, improves blood flow and blood pressure, helps control blood sugar and weight, and reduces the risk of heart disease, stroke, and diabetes. It is one of the most powerful preventive measures in medicine, and health authorities worldwide recommend it for people of all ages, including those with established heart disease under appropriate guidance.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Physical inactivity is one of the leading risk factors for death worldwide, contributing to coronary heart disease, stroke, high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes, and some cancers. Globally, around one in four adults does not meet the recommended level of physical activity, and rates of inactivity are higher among women, older people, and those in urban or sedentary occupations. Despite this, a large proportion of adults in high-income countries also falls short of the recommended 150--300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity per week. Increasing population levels of cardiovascular exercise is a stated priority of the World Health Organization because the health and economic benefits are so substantial.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Cardiovascular exercise produces beneficial changes throughout the body, and inactivity produces the reverse:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Heart and circulation:} regular aerobic training strengthens the heart muscle, lowers resting heart rate and blood pressure, and improves the efficiency of oxygen delivery
    \item \textbf{Blood vessels:} exercise improves the flexibility and health of the artery lining, reducing arterial stiffness and the progression of atherosclerosis
    \item \textbf{Metabolism:} physical activity improves insulin sensitivity and blood glucose control, and helps regulate blood lipids and body fat
    \item \textbf{Lungs:} sustained activity improves the efficiency of oxygen uptake and carbon dioxide removal
    \item \textbf{Muscle and bone:} exercise preserves muscle mass, strength, and bone density, and supports balance and coordination
    \item \textbf{Brain and mood:} activity stimulates the release of endorphins and growth factors that improve mood, sleep, memory, and cognitive function
\end{itemize}
The body responds to repeated exercise with training adaptations, meaning fitness improves when exercise is regular, progressive, and sustained.

\section*{Clinical Features}
People who are inactive or who have poor cardiovascular fitness may notice:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Easily becoming breathless or tired with light activity
    \item A raised resting heart rate and reduced exercise tolerance
    \item Feeling of heaviness or fatigue in the legs with exertion
    \item Weight gain, particularly around the abdomen
    \item Poor sleep, low mood, or reduced energy
    \item In people with established heart disease, symptoms such as chest discomfort or breathlessness with exertion may limit exercise
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Symptoms that limit exercise, such as breathlessness, fatigue, or chest discomfort, should be distinguished from conditions other than poor fitness:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Coronary artery disease, which may present with chest pain on exertion
    \item Heart failure or valve disease causing breathlessness
    \item Chronic lung disease, such as asthma or COPD
    \item Anaemia or thyroid disease causing fatigue and breathlessness
    \item Low fitness that is normal deconditioning rather than disease
    \item Muscle or joint problems, including osteoarthritis, limiting movement
    \item Depression or anxiety reducing energy and motivation
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Before starting a new exercise programme, seek advice if you have known heart disease, diabetes, high blood pressure, or another long-term condition. Anyone with these symptoms should stop exercising and seek medical advice:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, pressure, or heaviness during or after exercise
    \item Severe breathlessness, dizziness, or fainting with exertion
    \item Palpitations or a very fast or irregular heartbeat during activity
    \item Persistent fatigue or joint pain after exercise
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain that lasts more than a few minutes or occurs at rest
    \item Collapse or loss of consciousness during or after exercise
    \item Severe breathlessness at rest
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Fitness is assessed largely through history and practical measures, with tests used where symptoms raise concern:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and activity review:} current levels of activity, symptoms with exertion, and risk factors for heart disease
    \item \textbf{Physical measures:} resting and exercise heart rate, blood pressure, height, weight, and waist circumference
    \item \textbf{Exercise stress test:} an ECG while walking on a treadmill or cycling, to check the heart's response to exertion in people with symptoms or known heart disease
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} cholesterol, blood glucose or HbA1c, and kidney and thyroid function where relevant
    \item \textbf{Cardiopulmonary exercise testing:} detailed measurement of oxygen use and fitness in specialist settings
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
A healthy exercise plan is individualised, progressive, and sustainable:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Amount:} aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity each week, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, in sessions of at least ten minutes
    \item \textbf{Intensity:} moderate activity raises the heart rate and makes you breathe faster but still allows conversation; vigorous activity makes conversation difficult
    \item \textbf{Types:} include a mix of activities that you enjoy, such as walking, cycling, swimming, or dancing, to improve adherence
    \item \textbf{Strength training:} add resistance exercises for major muscle groups at least twice a week
    \item \textbf{Progression:} increase duration or intensity gradually to avoid injury and maintain motivation
    \item \textbf{Medical populations:} people with heart disease, heart failure, or after cardiac events should exercise within a cardiac rehabilitation programme and follow specialist advice
    \item \textbf{Support:} exercise referral schemes, physiotherapists, and accredited exercise physiologists can design safe programmes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Cardiovascular exercise is safe for almost everyone, but problems can occur:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Muscle strains, sprains, and overuse injuries, especially with sudden increases in intensity
    \item Dehydration and heat-related illness when exercising in hot conditions without adequate fluids
    \item Worsening of joint pain in osteoarthritis or other musculoskeletal conditions
    \item Very rarely, a cardiac event during unaccustomed vigorous exercise, particularly in previously inactive people with unrecognised heart disease
    \item Drops in blood sugar during exercise in people taking insulin or certain diabetes medicines
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Regular cardiovascular exercise substantially reduces the risk of heart disease, stroke, diabetes, and premature death; physically active people have around a 30\% lower risk of early death than inactive people. Benefits appear quickly: blood pressure and mood improve within weeks, and fitness rises measurably within a few months. Even small increases in activity in previously inactive people produce meaningful health gains, and the benefits continue to grow with long-term adherence. For people with established heart disease, supervised exercise improves survival and quality of life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Start gently and build up gradually; for most people, brisk walking is an excellent starting point
    \item Choose activities you enjoy so they become a lasting habit
    \item Schedule exercise into your week like any other appointment
    \item Break activity into shorter sessions, such as three ten-minute walks a day, if time is limited
    \item Warm up before and cool down after vigorous sessions
    \item Stay well hydrated and avoid strenuous exercise in extreme heat
    \item If you have a long-term condition, exercise with professional guidance and report new symptoms
\end{itemize}
