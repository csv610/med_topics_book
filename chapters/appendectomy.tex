\chapter{Appendectomy}
\index{Appendectomy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Appendectomy is the surgical removal of the appendix, a small finger-shaped pouch attached to the caecum, the first part of the large bowel. It is most commonly performed as an emergency operation for acute appendicitis, and it is one of the most frequently performed abdominal operations worldwide. The procedure may also be undertaken as a planned interval operation after an appendix abscess has been treated conservatively, or, rarely, when the appendix contains a tumour.

Appendectomy can be performed either laparoscopically, through several small keyhole incisions, or as an open operation through a single incision in the right lower abdomen. In uncomplicated appendicitis the operation is safe and recovery is usually rapid. The appendix has no essential digestive function, and its removal does not cause long-term health problems.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Acute appendicitis is one of the commonest surgical emergencies, with a lifetime risk of approximately one in thirteen. It occurs most often in people aged 10 to 30 years, and slightly more often in males. Consequently, appendectomy is among the most frequently performed emergency operations in both children and adults. The condition is more common in developed countries and in people with a Western dietary pattern. When appendicitis is treated early the associated mortality is very low, but this rises substantially once the appendix perforates, particularly in young children and older adults in whom the diagnosis is often delayed.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The appendix is normally removed because it has become inflamed (appendicitis). Inflammation begins when the lumen of the appendix becomes obstructed and bacteria multiply rapidly within the closed space.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Luminal obstruction:} most often by a faecolith (a hard mass of stool), but also by enlarged lymphoid follicles, a foreign body, or rarely a parasitic worm
    \item \textbf{Bacterial invasion:} proliferation of gut flora within the obstructed appendix leads to mural inflammation and ischaemia
    \item \textbf{Raised intraluminal pressure:} continued secretion and distension compromise the blood supply and encourage necrosis
    \item \textbf{Perforation:} if untreated, the appendix may rupture, spilling infected content into the peritoneal cavity
    \item \textbf{Recurrent or chronic appendicitis:} repeated episodes of milder inflammation producing intermittent right iliac fossa pain
    \item \textbf{Appendix abscess:} a walled-off collection of pus that can develop when perforation is contained by omentum and adjacent bowel
    \item \textbf{Appendiceal tumour:} uncommon neuroendocrine or mucinous growths found incidentally on imaging or at operation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Before surgery the symptoms are those of the underlying condition, most often acute appendicitis.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain that typically begins around the navel and migrates to the right lower abdomen
    \item Nausea, vomiting, and loss of appetite
    \item Low-grade fever and a raised heart rate
    \item Localised tenderness in the right iliac fossa that worsens with movement, coughing, or percussion
    \item Guarding and rebound tenderness as the inflammation reaches the peritoneum
\end{itemize}

After surgery, the expected recovery pattern includes some pain and swelling around the wounds for a few days, fatigue, a temporarily reduced appetite, and a short pause before bowel function returns, particularly after open surgery.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
After the operation, symptoms that might indicate a complication must be separated from the normal course of recovery.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Wound infection versus normal wound inflammation and bruising
    \item Intra-abdominal abscess versus postoperative ileus
    \item Bowel obstruction from adhesions versus a normal delay in bowel function
    \item Urinary retention or urinary tract infection versus other pelvic discomfort
    \item Deep vein thrombosis versus ordinary calf soreness
    \item Chest infection or pulmonary embolism versus benign postoperative breathlessness
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
After surgery, contact the surgical team or seek urgent medical review if pain is severe or worsening, a fever develops that does not settle, the wound becomes red, hot, swollen, or oozing pus, there is heavy or persistent bleeding, the wound edges separate, you cannot pass urine or open your bowels, or you develop calf pain, swelling, chest pain, or breathlessness that may indicate a blood clot.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe abdominal pain with vomiting or a bloated abdomen
    \item Chest pain, difficulty breathing, or coughing up blood
    \item Heavy bleeding, fainting, or extreme weakness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of appendicitis is primarily clinical and is supported by blood tests and imaging. Once surgery is planned, fitness for anaesthesia is assessed.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the character and migration of pain, localised tenderness, guarding, and peritonism
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count and C-reactive protein to support inflammation, with the white cell count raised in most cases
    \item \textbf{Urine test:} to exclude urinary tract infection and, in women, a pregnancy test
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} ultrasound is commonly used first, with CT scanning when the diagnosis remains uncertain; both avoid surgery in a substantial proportion of patients with alternative diagnoses
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative assessment:} review of cardiovascular and respiratory fitness, current medicines, allergies, and fasting instructions
    \item \textbf{Anaesthetic review:} discussion of anaesthetic technique and peri-operative pain control
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Appendectomy is performed urgently once the diagnosis is made, because delay increases the risk of perforation. The choice of operative approach depends on the clinical picture and the skills of the surgeon.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Laparoscopic appendectomy:} preferred in most patients because recovery is quicker, pain is less, and scars are smaller
    \item \textbf{Open appendectomy:} used when keyhole surgery is unsafe, such as in late pregnancy or severe peritonitis, or when equipment is unavailable
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} a dose is given before surgery and continued after operation, particularly when the appendix has perforated
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} regular analgesia after surgery, stepping down from stronger options to simple analgesics
    \item \textbf{Early mobilisation:} walking soon after the operation reduces the risk of blood clots and chest complications
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics alone:} some adults with uncomplicated appendicitis can be treated with antibiotics only, although a proportion later need appendectomy for recurrent symptoms
    \item \textbf{Interval appendectomy:} performed weeks after an appendix abscess has been drained or settled with antibiotics
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} wound review and discussion of laboratory and pathology results at the planned post-operative visit
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Appendectomy is generally safe, but complications occur more often when the appendix was perforated at the time of surgery.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Surgical site (wound) infection, more common after open surgery
    \item Intra-abdominal collection or abscess requiring drainage
    \item Bleeding or a haematoma within the abdomen
    \item Injury to nearby structures, such as the bowel, bladder, or ureter
    \item Paralytic ileus, and later bowel obstruction from adhesions
    \item Incisional hernia at the wound site
    \item Anaesthetic complications, and rarely deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism
    \item Recurrent pain from a small remaining stump of the appendix, which is rare
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Outcomes are excellent. Most people return to normal activities within one to three weeks after laparoscopic surgery and a little longer after open surgery, with no lasting digestive consequences from losing the appendix. Complications are uncommon when appendicitis is treated early, and mortality is very low. If the appendix has already perforated, recovery is longer and the risk of infection-related complications is higher, but with prompt surgery, antibiotics, and appropriate drainage most patients still make a complete recovery.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Appendicitis itself cannot be reliably prevented, although a high-fibre diet is associated with a lower risk. After the operation, the following measures support a smooth recovery:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Follow the surgeon's wound-care and showering instructions
    \item Rest as needed but keep moving, taking short walks regularly
    \item Avoid heavy lifting and strenuous exercise until cleared by the surgeon
    \item Eat and drink normally as soon as you feel able, with plenty of fluids
    \item Do not stop pain medicines abruptly while discomfort persists
    \item Attend follow-up appointments and report any concerning symptoms promptly
    \item Seek medical help early if abdominal pain is severe or a fever develops
\end{itemize}
