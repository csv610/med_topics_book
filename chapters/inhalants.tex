\chapter{Inhalants}
\index{Inhalants}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Inhalants are common household and industrial products whose fumes are breathed in deliberately to produce a rapid intoxication, a practice known as volatile substance use or sniffing. They include petrol, glues, aerosols, paint thinners, solvents, and cleaning products. Inhalation produces an immediate euphoria that lasts only minutes, driving repeated use. Inhalants are highly dangerous: they can cause sudden death from heart rhythm disturbance even on first use, brain and nerve damage, and fatal accidents or suffocation, and dependence can develop. The practice is most common among adolescents and, in many countries, particularly among some Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, where it is a serious public health problem.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Inhalant use is most common in early adolescence, with use peaking around ages 12 to 15, and most young people try inhalants only briefly. In many countries, rates of use are highest among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander young people, particularly in remote communities, where petrol sniffing has been a long-standing concern. Most use is experimental, but a minority develop regular use with serious harm. Inhalant use is associated with disadvantage, disrupted families, poor school engagement, and other substance use. Data on the full extent of harm are limited because deaths and hospitalisations are under-recognised.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Inhaled solvents are rapidly absorbed from the lungs into the bloodstream and cross quickly into the brain, producing intoxication within seconds. They act on nerve cell membranes and neurotransmitter systems, producing effects similar to alcohol and anaesthetics: euphoria, disinhibition, and sedation. Because the effect is brief, users inhale repeatedly to maintain the high. The volatile chemicals are directly toxic to many organs: they sensitise the heart to rhythm disturbances that can cause sudden death; damage the brain and nerves with prolonged use; irritate and damage the lungs, liver, and kidneys; and can cause suffocation or choking. Withdrawal after heavy use causes anxiety, tremor, and cravings.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Euphoria, light-headedness, and disinhibition immediately after inhaling
    \item Slurred speech, unsteadiness, and poor coordination
    \item Headache, nausea, and dizziness
    \item Red eyes, runny nose, and a chemical smell on the breath and clothing
    \item Paint or glue stains on hands, face, and clothing
    \item Empty aerosol cans, rags, or plastic bags hidden in bedrooms or backpacks
    \item With prolonged use: weight loss, memory problems, tremor, and behavioural change
    \item Apathy, withdrawal from family, and declining school or work performance
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The signs of inhalant use may be confused with other conditions and substances.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Alcohol intoxication:} similar features of acute intoxication
    \item \textbf{Other drug use:} cannabis, stimulants, or sedatives
    \item \textbf{Head injury or seizures:} confusion and neurological signs
    \item \textbf{Mental health conditions:} mood and behavioural change in young people
    \item \textbf{Metabolic or infective illness:} causing confusion and drowsiness
    \item \textbf{Chronic solvent effects:} encephalopathy and neuropathy with long-term use
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents and carers who suspect inhalant use should seek help early from a GP, school counsellor, or drug and alcohol service, because early intervention prevents serious harm. Sudden intoxication, collapse, breathing difficulty, or altered consciousness after inhaling requires urgent medical care. Regular use warrants comprehensive assessment, as dependence and damage may be present.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Collapse, unconsciousness, or difficulty breathing after inhaling
    \item Seizures or convulsions
    \item Severe confusion, agitation, or hallucinations
    \item Chest pain or a racing, irregular heartbeat, which may herald sudden death
    \item Choking, or a plastic bag found over the face
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and interview:} with the young person and family, exploring the substances used and patterns of use
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} signs of intoxication, chemical smell, rashes, and weight change
    \item \textbf{Neurological assessment:} tests of coordination, tremor, and cognitive function
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} liver and kidney function for chronic toxicity
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} brain imaging where neurological damage is suspected
    \item \textbf{Screening for other substance use and mental health problems:} common coexisting issues
    \item \textbf{Assessment of the home environment:} safety, supervision, and access to products
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Emergency care:} oxygen and monitoring for acute intoxication, cardiac rhythm problems, and respiratory depression
    \item \textbf{Withdrawal support:} medical and psychological care for dependence, usually in a community or residential program
    \item \textbf{Counselling:} motivational interviewing and cognitive behavioural approaches
    \item \textbf{Family support:} involving parents and carers in treatment and supervision
    \item \textbf{School and community programs:} education and support to reduce use
    \item \textbf{Regulatory measures:} restricting access, including low-aromatic petrol in affected regions
    \item \textbf{Residential rehabilitation:} for young people with significant dependence
    \item \textbf{Treatment of complications:} liver, kidney, nerve, and brain damage
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden death from cardiac arrhythmia, even on first use
    \item Suffocation, choking, and fatal accidents during intoxication
    \item Brain damage, memory loss, and cognitive decline with chronic use
    \item Peripheral nerve damage and muscle wasting
    \item Liver, kidney, and lung damage
    \item Dependence and withdrawal
    \item Injury and death from risk-taking behaviour while intoxicated
    \item Long-term effects on education, family, and community
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most young people who experiment with inhalants do so briefly and stop, especially with effective education and family support. Those who use regularly need intensive intervention, as dependence and organ damage develop with continued use. Community-based programs, restricted availability of products, and youth engagement have reduced inhalant use in affected regions, including remote communities. With early intervention and strong family and community support, recovery is very achievable, though neurological damage may persist in chronic users.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Talk openly with young people about the serious risks of inhalants
    \item Limit access to solvents, aerosols, and other products, and store them securely
    \item Watch for signs of use: chemical smells, empty containers, and behavioural change
    \item Seek help early rather than waiting for harm
    \item Support school and community drug education
    \item Encourage healthy activities, engagement, and connection with family and culture
    \item Support community-led measures in affected regions, including low-aromatic fuel
    \item If someone collapses or is unwell after inhaling, call an ambulance immediately
\end{itemize}
