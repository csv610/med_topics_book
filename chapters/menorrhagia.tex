\chapter{Menorrhagia}
\index{Menorrhagia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Menorrhagia is the medical term for abnormally heavy or prolonged menstrual bleeding, defined clinically as blood loss exceeding 80 mL per cycle or menses lasting longer than 7 days. Today, menorrhagia is often integrated under the broader clinical term Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB-H, where H denotes heavy). Menorrhagia is a common, debilitating gynaecological complaint that can significantly impair a patient's physical health, emotional well-being, and social and occupational quality of life. The chronic, excessive blood loss often leads to iron deficiency and iron-deficiency anaemia. Managing menorrhagia requires identifying the underlying cause, addressing anaemia, and utilizing medical or surgical interventions to control bleeding and restore quality of life.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Menorrhagia is a highly prevalent condition, affecting approximately 20\% to 30\% of women of reproductive age globally. In many countries and other developed nations, it is one of the most frequent reasons for referral to gynaecological services. The incidence of menorrhagia peaks in the late reproductive years, particularly during perimenopause, due to anovulatory cycles and hormonal fluctuations, and during adolescence as the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis matures. Despite its prevalence, many women do not seek medical care, assuming that heavy bleeding is normal, leading to underdiagnosis and untreated iron deficiency.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The causes of menorrhagia are classified under the FIGO PALM-COEIN framework, which distinguishes structural (PALM) from non-structural (COEIN) aetiologies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Polyp (AUB-P):} Benign localised growths of endometrial stroma and glands that project into the uterine cavity, increasing surface area and local vascularity.
    \item \textbf{Adenomyosis (AUB-A):} The infiltration of endometrial tissue into the myometrium, causing myometrial hypertrophy and impaired uterine muscular contractility.
    \item \textbf{Leiomyoma (AUB-L):} Uterine fibroids, particularly submucosal or intramural variants, which increase the endometrial surface area, distort the uterine cavity, and dysregulate local angiogenic factors.
    \item \textbf{Malignancy and hyperplasia (AUB-M):} Endometrial hyperplasia or endometrial carcinoma, presenting with irregular, heavy bleeding, particularly in women with risk factors such as obesity or prolonged unopposed oestrogen.
    \item \textbf{Coagulopathy (AUB-C):} Systemic bleeding disorders, most commonly von Willebrand disease, which impair platelet plug formation or coagulation cascades.
    \item \textbf{Ovulatory dysfunction (AUB-O):} Anovulatory cycles leading to a lack of progesterone secretion, resulting in a persistently proliferative, unstable endometrium that sheds irregularly and heavily (common in PCOS, perimenopause, and adolescence).
    \item \textbf{Endometrial (AUB-E):} Primary disorders of endometrial haemostasis, including deficiencies in local vasoconstrictors (such as endothelin-1) or excessive local fibrinolysis.
    \item \textbf{Iatrogenic (AUB-I):} Bleeding induced by medical devices or drugs, such as copper intrauterine devices, anticoagulants, or selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors.
    \item \textbf{Not yet classified (AUB-N):} Rare or poorly understood conditions like arteriovenous malformations or chronic endometritis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features of menorrhagia relate to the blood loss itself and its systemic consequences:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Heavy bleeding indicators:} Soaking through one or more sanitary pads or tampons every hour for several consecutive hours, the need to use double sanitary protection, or waking up to change protection during the night.
    \item \textbf{Passage of large clots:} Expelling blood clots larger than a 50-cent coin during menstruation.
    \item \textbf{Prolonged bleeding:} Menstrual bleeding that lasts longer than 7 consecutive days.
    \item \textbf{Iron-deficiency anaemia features:} Chronic fatigue, generalised weakness, dyspnoea on exertion, dizziness, headache, cold hands and feet, and pale skin (pallor).
    \item \textbf{Dysmenorrhoea:} Painful pelvic cramping associated with the passage of large clots.
    \item \textbf{Disruption of daily activities:} Avoidance of social, physical, or work activities due to fear of flooding or staining clothing.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
In a patient presenting with heavy menstrual bleeding, other conditions causing abnormal bleeding must be excluded:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy-related bleeding:} Miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, or gestational trophoblastic disease, which must be ruled out immediately with a pregnancy test.
    \item \textbf{Cervical pathology:} Cervical polyps, severe cervicitis, or cervical cancer presenting with postcoital, intermenstrual, or heavy bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID):} Infection of the upper genital tract causing pelvic pain, dyspareunia, and intermenstrual or heavy bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Thyroid disorders:} Both hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism can disrupt menstrual cycles and cause heavy bleeding, requiring biochemical evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Renal or hepatic failure:} Systemic diseases that impair coagulation factor synthesis or hormone metabolism, presenting with heavy bleeding.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients with menorrhagia should seek medical advice to identify the cause, check for anaemia, and explore treatment options.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Haemorrhage:} Severe, continuous vaginal bleeding where the patient is soaking multiple sanitary pads per hour and feels lightheaded, dizzy, or faint.
    \item \textbf{Acute pelvic pain:} Sudden, severe lower abdominal or pelvic pain that may indicate a ruptured ectopic pregnancy or ovarian cyst torsion.
    \item \textbf{Signs of shock:} Cold, clammy skin, rapid heart rate, shallow breathing, extreme dizziness, or loss of consciousness.
    \item \textbf{Sepsis:} High fever, chills, rapid heart rate, or confusion associated with pelvic infection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A structured workup combines history, physical examination, imaging, and laboratory tests:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test (beta-hCG):} The mandatory first step to exclude pregnancy-related bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Full blood count (FBC):} To evaluate haemoglobin and haematocrit levels, confirming the presence and severity of anaemia.
    \item \textbf{Iron studies (ferritin):} To assess iron stores, as ferritin can be depleted even before frank anaemia develops.
    \item \textbf{Transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS):} The first-line imaging modality to identify structural abnormalities (polyps, adenomyosis, fibroids, endometrial thickness).
    \item \textbf{Coagulation screen:} Indicated if there is a family history of bleeding disorders, onset of menorrhagia at menarche, or bruising/epistaxis (checking PT, APTT, and von Willebrand factor).
    \item \textbf{Thyroid function tests (TSH):} To rule out primary thyroid disease.
    \item \textbf{Endometrial biopsy:} Recommended in women aged 45 years or older, or those with risk factors (e.g. obesity, family history of Lynch syndrome, persistent bleeding), to exclude endometrial hyperplasia or cancer.
    \item \textbf{Hysteroscopy:} Direct visual examination of the uterine cavity to diagnose and biopsy polyps or submucosal fibroids.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management targets reducing menstrual blood loss, treating anaemia, and addressing structural causes. Strategies include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Levonorgestrel intrauterine system (LNG-IUS):} The first-line, highly effective medical treatment that releases local progesterone, thinning the endometrium and reducing blood loss by up to 90\%.
    \item \textbf{Antifibrinolytics (tranexamic acid):} A non-hormonal oral medication taken only during menses to reduce bleeding by inhibiting clot breakdown.
    \item \textbf{Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs):} Medications like mefenamic acid or ibuprofen taken during menses to reduce prostaglandin levels, decreasing bleeding and relieving pain.
    \item \textbf{Hormonal therapies:} Combined oral contraceptive pills, progestogen-only pills, or depot injections to regulate cycles and thin the endometrial lining.
    \item \textbf{Iron replacement therapy:} Oral iron supplements (with vitamin C to aid absorption) or intravenous iron infusions for patients with confirmed iron deficiency or anaemia.
    \item \textbf{Surgical interventions:} Endometrial ablation (destruction of the endometrial lining) for women who do not wish to conceive; uterine artery embolisation or myomectomy to remove fibroids; or hysterectomy as a definitive cure when other treatments have failed.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Iron-deficiency anaemia:} Decreased red blood cell count leading to chronic fatigue, cardiovascular strain (tachycardia, cardiomegaly), and impaired cognitive function.
    \item \textbf{Impaired quality of life:} Anxiety, depression, social withdrawal, and high rates of absenteeism from work or school.
    \item \textbf{Infertility:} Underlying causes of menorrhagia (such as submucosal fibroids, polyps, or PCOS-related anovulation) can impair fertility.
    \item \textbf{Malignant progression:} Untreated endometrial hyperplasia progressing to endometrial carcinoma.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for menorrhagia is excellent. The vast majority of patients respond well to medical or surgical treatments, experiencing a significant reduction in menstrual blood loss and resolution of iron-deficiency anaemia. First-line therapies like the LNG-IUS have transformed management, avoiding the need for major surgery in most women. For those requiring surgery, techniques like endometrial ablation or hysterectomy provide definitive, high-satisfaction relief, allowing women to regain their physical energy and return to normal daily functioning.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tracking menstrual cycles:} Keep a detailed record of period duration, flow intensity, and clot passage (using menstrual tracking apps).
    \item \textbf{Dietary iron intake:} Eat a balanced diet rich in iron (red meat, poultry, beans, lentils, spinach) and avoid drinking tea or coffee with meals, as they inhibit iron absorption.
    \item \textbf{Prompt medical evaluation:} Consult a doctor early when bleeding increases, rather than waiting for severe anaemia to develop.
    \item \textbf{Adhering to treatment:} Take prescribed medications (like tranexamic acid or oral iron) precisely as directed by the clinician.
    \item \textbf{Post-procedure care:} Follow post-surgical instructions carefully to prevent pelvic infection after uterine procedures.
\end{itemize}
