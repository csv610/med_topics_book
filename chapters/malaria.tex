\chapter{Malaria}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Malaria is a life-threatening, febrile vector-borne infectious disease caused by protozoan parasites of the genus \textit{Plasmodium}. It is transmitted to humans through the bites of infected female \textit{Anopheles} mosquitoes. The disease is clinically characterised by periodic paroxysms of high fever, rigors, profuse sweating, anaemia, and splenomegaly. Five species of \textit{Plasmodium} routinely cause malaria in humans, with \textit{Plasmodium falciparum} and \textit{Plasmodium vivax} posing the greatest threat to human health. While malaria is preventable and curable, it remains one of the world's most significant public health challenges, capable of causing rapid multi-organ failure and death if diagnosis or treatment is delayed.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Malaria is endemic in over 80 countries, predominantly in sub-Saharan Africa, South-East Asia, the Eastern Mediterranean, the Western Pacific, and the Americas. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that malaria causes approximately 250 million clinical cases and over 600,000 deaths annually, with children under five years of age and pregnant women in sub-Saharan Africa bearing the majority of the disease burden. In Australia, malaria was declared eradicated in 1981. However, Australia reports between 300 and 400 imported cases of malaria annually. These cases occur in returned travellers, military personnel, and immigrants arriving from endemic regions, with \textit{Plasmodium falciparum} and \textit{Plasmodium vivax} accounting for the vast majority of these imported cases.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Malaria is caused by unicellular eukaryotic parasites that undergo a complex lifecycle requiring both a mosquito vector and a human host.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Plasmodium species:} \textit{Plasmodium falciparum} is the most virulent species, responsible for severe malaria, microvascular sequestration, and the majority of deaths. \textit{Plasmodium vivax} and \textit{Plasmodium ovale} are characterised by their ability to form dormant liver stages (hypnozoites) that cause disease relapse months or years later. \textit{Plasmodium malariae} causes chronic, low-density infections. \textit{Plasmodium knowlesi} is a zoonotic species transmitted from macaques in South-East Asia, causing rapid, severe disease.
    \item \textbf{Vector transmission:} The bite of an infected female \textit{Anopheles} mosquito injects sporozoites into the human bloodstream, which rapidly migrate to the liver.
    \item \textbf{Exo-erythrocytic (hepatic) phase:} Sporozoites invade hepatocytes, where they multiply and mature into schizonts. Upon rupture, they release thousands of merozoites into the circulation. In \textit{P. vivax} and \textit{P. ovale} infections, some parasites remain dormant as hypnozoites.
    \item \textbf{Erythrocytic (blood) phase:} Merozoites invade red blood cells (RBCs), maturing from ring stages into trophozoites and schizonts. The synchronous rupture of these RBCs releases merozoites, cellular debris, and pyrogenic toxins, triggering cyclic clinical fevers.
    \item \textbf{Microvascular sequestration:} In \textit{P. falciparum} infection, parasite-derived proteins (such as PfEMP1) are expressed on the RBC surface, causing them to adhere to endothelial cells, platelets, and other RBCs. This prevents clearance by the spleen and causes microvascular obstruction, tissue ischaemia, and organ dysfunction (cerebral malaria).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of malaria ranges from mild, non-specific symptoms to severe, life-threatening multi-organ dysfunction.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Classical malarial paroxysm:} A cyclical three-stage presentation: a cold stage (severe shivering and rigors lasting 15--60 minutes); a hot stage (high fever up to 41 degrees Celsius, headache, vomiting, and palpitations lasting 2--6 hours); and a sweating stage (profuse sweating and defervescence, leaving the patient exhausted).
    \item \textbf{General systemic symptoms:} Headache, myalgia, arthralgia, fatigue, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and mild diarrhoea, which can easily be mistaken for a general viral illness.
    \item \textbf{Physical signs:} Pallor (due to anaemia), mild scleral icterus (jaundice from haemolysis), and splenomegaly or hepatomegaly.
    \item \textbf{Severe malaria features:} Altered mental status or coma (cerebral malaria), generalised seizures, severe anaemia, respiratory distress (due to metabolic acidosis or pulmonary oedema), acute kidney injury, spontaneous bleeding, and circulatory collapse (algid malaria).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Any returned traveller presenting with a fever must be evaluated for malaria, but other infectious etiologies must also be considered.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Other tropical infections:} Dengue fever, Typhoid fever, Leptospirosis, Rickettsial infections (such as scrub typhus), and Chikungunya. These share geographic risks and present with fever, headache, and cytopenias, but are distinguished by specific serologies, cultures, or PCR.
    \item \textbf{Influenza and COVID-19:} Common respiratory viral infections that present with fever, myalgia, and headache, but are distinguished by upper respiratory symptoms and viral swabs.
    \item \textbf{Bacterial sepsis or meningitis:} Present with high fevers, hypotension, and altered consciousness, requiring blood cultures and lumbar puncture to differentiate from cerebral malaria.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any fever in a person who has travelled to a malaria-endemic area within the past 12 months is a medical emergency. The patient must seek immediate medical evaluation.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Altered consciousness, confusion, extreme drowsiness, or inability to wake up.
    \item Generalized seizures or fit-like activity.
    \item Severe difficulty breathing, rapid breathing, or shortness of breath.
    \item Inability to tolerate oral medications or fluids due to persistent vomiting.
    \item Severe yellowing of the skin or eyes (jaundice).
    \item Spontaneous bleeding from the gums, nose, or venepuncture sites, or passing blood in the urine (dark urine).
    \item Extreme weakness and inability to sit up or walk unaided.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Prompt and accurate diagnosis is critical, and must be performed immediately upon suspicion.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood film microscopy:} The gold standard diagnostic test. Thick blood films are used to screen for and detect the presence of parasites (high sensitivity). Thin blood films are used to identify the specific \textit{Plasmodium} species and calculate the percentage parasitaemia (percentage of infected RBCs). Films should be repeated every 12 to 24 hours if the first is negative and clinical suspicion remains high.
    \item \textbf{Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs):} Immunochromatographic tests that detect specific malaria antigens (such as Histidine-Rich Protein 2 for \textit{P. falciparum} and parasite Lactate Dehydrogenase for other species). RDTs provide rapid results but should always be confirmed with microscopy.
    \item \textbf{Polymerase chain reaction (PCR):} The most sensitive and specific test, useful for detecting low-level parasitaemia, identifying species in mixed infections, and monitoring drug resistance.
    \item \textbf{Supportive laboratory tests:} Full blood count (often showing thrombocytopenia and anaemia), urea, electrolytes, and creatinine (to monitor renal function), liver function tests (showing indirect hyperbilirubinaemia), blood glucose (to monitor for hypoglycaemia), and venous blood gas (to screen for metabolic acidosis).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is determined by the species of \textit{Plasmodium}, the degree of parasitaemia, and the presence of severe features.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Uncomplicated falciparum malaria:} Managed with Artemisinin-based Combination Therapy (ACT). In Australia, the preferred regimen is oral artemether-lumefantrine (Riamet), which rapidly reduces parasite biomass and clears symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Non-falciparum malaria:} Managed with oral chloroquine or an ACT. In \textit{P. vivax} and \textit{P. ovale} infections, a radical cure with primaquine or tafenoquine is required to eradicate hypnozoites in the liver and prevent relapse.
    \item \textbf{G6PD screening:} Prior to administering primaquine or tafenoquine, patients must be tested for glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency. In G6PD-deficient individuals, these drugs can precipitate severe, life-threatening acute haemolytic anaemia.
    \item \textbf{Severe malaria:} A medical emergency requiring admission to an intensive care unit. The first-line therapy is intravenous artesunate, which has replaced IV quinine due to its superior efficacy and better safety profile. Once the patient is conscious and tolerating oral intake, they complete a full course of oral ACT.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Severe malaria, particularly when caused by \textit{P. falciparum}, is associated with severe multi-system complications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cerebral malaria:} Diffuse encephalopathy characterised by coma, seizures, and neurological deficits, resulting from microvascular parasite sequestration in the brain.
    \item \textbf{Acute kidney injury (AKI):} Often called ``blackwater fever'' when presenting with massive intravascular haemolysis and haemoglobinuria, leading to tubular necrosis.
    \item \textbf{Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS):} Non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, which can develop rapidly even after the initiation of effective antimalarial treatment.
    \item \textbf{Hypoglycaemia:} Caused by parasite consumption of glucose and host glycogen depletion, and often worsened by quinine or artesunate therapy.
    \item \textbf{Severe anaemia:} Resulting from direct parasite destruction of red blood cells, splenic clearance of infected and uninfected RBCs, and bone marrow suppression.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for uncomplicated malaria is excellent if diagnosed and treated early, with patients achieving complete cure and recovery. If treatment is delayed, uncomplicated malaria can rapidly progress to severe disease within hours. Severe malaria carries a high mortality rate of 15\% to 20\% even with optimal intensive care management. In \textit{P. vivax} and \textit{P. ovale} infections, failure to administer radical liver-stage therapy leads to relapses, which can recur at intervals for months or years.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Travellers to malaria-endemic areas should adhere to the standard preventative guidelines.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Awareness of risk:} Consult a travel medicine practitioner or general practitioner at least six weeks before travel to assess the specific malaria risk of the destination.
    \item \textbf{Bite prevention:} Use insect repellents containing DEET (diethyltoluamide), picaridin, or oil of lemon eucalyptus on exposed skin. Wear long-sleeved shirts and long trousers, especially from dusk to dawn when \textit{Anopheles} mosquitoes are most active. Sleep in air-conditioned rooms or under insecticide-treated bed nets.
    \item \textbf{Chemoprophylaxis:} Take preventative antimalarial medications (such as doxycycline, atovaquone-proguanil, or mefloquine) exactly as prescribed. Ensure the medication is started before entering the endemic area, taken continuously while there, and completed for the recommended duration after returning.
    \item \textbf{Post-travel vigilance:} Seek immediate medical attention for any febrile illness within 12 months of returning from a malaria-endemic country.
\end{itemize}
