\chapter{Cardiomyopathy}
\index{Cardiomyopathy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cardiomyopathy is a disease of the heart muscle in which the heart becomes enlarged, thickened, or stiff, so that it cannot pump blood as effectively as it should. The main types are dilated, hypertrophic, restrictive, and arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy. Some types run in families, while others develop from infections, alcohol, chemotherapy, or unknown causes. Symptoms range from none at all to breathlessness, fatigue, palpitations, and a risk of dangerous heart rhythms or sudden cardiac death. Treatment aims to control symptoms, prevent complications, and reduce the risk of life-threatening arrhythmias.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cardiomyopathy affects people of all ages, including children and young adults. Dilated cardiomyopathy is the commonest type, estimated to affect about 1 in 300 to 1 in 2500 people. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is more common than was once thought, affecting roughly 1 in 500 of the population. Certain inherited forms are more common in some families and ethnic groups, and genetic testing is increasingly revealing more affected relatives. Cardiomyopathy is an important cause of heart failure in younger people and of sudden cardiac death in athletes and people under 35, which is why unexplained fainting in young people is always taken seriously.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The different types of cardiomyopathy have different causes and effects on the heart muscle:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dilated cardiomyopathy:} the pumping chambers enlarge and weaken; causes include inherited gene changes, previous heart attacks, alcohol, viral infections, and chemotherapy drugs such as anthracyclines
    \item \textbf{Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy:} the heart muscle thickens, often unevenly, making it stiff and prone to dangerous rhythms; it is usually inherited, often due to mutations in the proteins of the heart muscle
    \item \textbf{Restrictive cardiomyopathy:} the muscle becomes rigid and does not fill properly with blood; causes include amyloidosis, iron overload, and scarring, sometimes from unknown factors
    \item \textbf{Arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy:} heart muscle is progressively replaced by fat and scar tissue, mainly in the right ventricle, increasing the risk of arrhythmia
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} in all forms, the heart's ability to fill, pump, or both is impaired, which can cause fluid to build up in the lungs and body and predispose the heart to rhythm disturbances
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Many people with cardiomyopathy have few or no symptoms for a long time. When symptoms develop, they may include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Breathlessness, especially on exertion or when lying flat
    \item Fatigue and reduced exercise tolerance
    \item Swelling of the ankles, feet, or abdomen
    \item Palpitations, or a fluttering, racing, or irregular heartbeat
    \item Dizziness, light-headedness, or fainting, often with exertion
    \item Chest pain or discomfort
    \item Some people are diagnosed after a routine examination or ECG, or after a relative is found to have the condition
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of cardiomyopathy overlap with other conditions affecting the heart and elsewhere:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heart failure from coronary artery disease or valve disease
    \item High blood pressure causing a thickened heart muscle
    \item Athlete's heart, a normally enlarged and thickened heart in trained endurance athletes
    \item Myocarditis, inflammation of the heart muscle from infection
    \item Chronic lung disease or anaemia causing breathlessness
    \item Anxiety, panic attacks, or other causes of palpitations
    \item Fainting from causes other than the heart, such as low blood pressure or neurological conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with unexplained breathlessness, palpitations, or fainting should see a doctor. Because some forms of cardiomyopathy run in families, people with a close relative who is affected should be assessed even if they have no symptoms.
\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, pressure, or discomfort
    \item Fainting, collapse, or loss of consciousness
    \item Severe breathlessness at rest
    \item A fast or irregular heartbeat with dizziness or breathlessness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis combines the family history, examination, and heart tests:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} symptoms, family history of heart disease or sudden death, and examination of the heart, lungs, and for fluid retention
    \item \textbf{Electrocardiogram (ECG):} may show abnormal rhythm or electrical patterns suggesting cardiomyopathy
    \item \textbf{Echocardiogram:} ultrasound showing heart size, wall thickness, stiffness, and pumping strength; this is the key diagnostic test
    \item \textbf{Cardiac MRI:} detailed images of the heart muscle, including scarring and fat replacement
    \item \textbf{Exercise testing:} to provoke symptoms or rhythm problems and to assess risk
    \item \textbf{24-hour heart monitoring:} to capture intermittent arrhythmias
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} checking for causes such as iron overload, thyroid disease, or inflammation
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} offered to confirm inherited forms and to allow screening of family members
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is individualised, depending on the type and severity of the condition:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} drugs to relieve fluid retention, lower blood pressure, slow the heart, and protect heart muscle; some reduce the risk of dangerous rhythms
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} limit alcohol, avoid very strenuous competitive sport in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, maintain a healthy weight, and keep well hydrated
    \item \textbf{Devices:} an implanted defibrillator can be life-saving for people at high risk of dangerous rhythms; pacemakers are sometimes used
    \item \textbf{Procedures:} in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, reducing obstruction to blood flow may involve medication, alcohol septal ablation, or surgery to remove excess muscle
    \item \textbf{Treating the cause:} stopping harmful medications or alcohol, or treating conditions such as iron overload or amyloidosis
    \item \textbf{Advanced care:} in severe, progressive cases, a mechanical heart pump or heart transplantation may be considered
    \item \textbf{Family screening:} first-degree relatives of affected people are offered assessment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Cardiomyopathy can lead to serious problems:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heart failure with breathlessness, swelling, and hospital admissions
    \item Arrhythmias, including ventricular fibrillation, which can cause sudden cardiac death
    \item Blood clots forming in the weakened heart and causing stroke or pulmonary embolism
    \item Valve leakage, especially of the mitral valve, due to a distorted heart shape
    \item Fainting and falls from low blood pressure or arrhythmias
    \item In hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, sudden cardiac death during or just after exertion in untreated, high-risk people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook varies widely by type. Many people live for decades with stable symptoms and a good quality of life, especially with modern treatment. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, although it carries a risk of sudden death, has a good outlook for many affected people, and that risk can be substantially reduced with an implanted defibrillator where indicated. Dilated cardiomyopathy often responds well to heart-protecting medication, though some people progress to advanced heart failure. The outlook is generally better when the condition is detected early, treated consistently, and monitored regularly.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item If a close relative has cardiomyopathy, arrange assessment and genetic screening
    \item Take prescribed medicines exactly as directed and attend regular reviews
    \item Limit alcohol and avoid recreational stimulant drugs
    \item Follow specific exercise advice; moderate activity is usually encouraged but very strenuous sport may be restricted
    \item Report new or worsening symptoms such as breathlessness, palpitations, or fainting promptly
    \item Know the warning signs of dangerous rhythms and what to do in an emergency
    \item Carry a medical alert card or bracelet describing your condition
    \item Wear a seat belt and avoid heavy exertion if advised, to reduce triggers for fainting or arrhythmia
\end{itemize}
