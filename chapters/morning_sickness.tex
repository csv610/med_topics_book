\chapter{Morning sickness}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Morning sickness, clinically referred to as nausea and vomiting of pregnancy (NVP), is a highly common condition affecting women in the first trimester of pregnancy. Despite its colloquial name, symptoms can occur at any time of the day or night. Typically, NVP begins between the fourth and ninth week of gestation, peaks between the eleventh and thirteenth week, and resolves spontaneously by the twentieth week in the majority of pregnancies. The condition ranges in severity from mild, self-limiting nausea to a severe, life-threatening disorder known as Hyperemesis Gravidarum (HG). Hyperemesis gravidarum is characterised by intractable vomiting, severe dehydration, electrolyte imbalances, ketonuria, and a loss of more than 5\% of pre-pregnancy body weight, necessitating medical hospitalisation and intravenous therapy.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Nausea and vomiting of pregnancy is one of the most common clinical conditions associated with early pregnancy, affecting approximately 70\% to 80\% of all pregnant women, with vomiting occurring in about 50\% of cases. It represents a major cause of occupational absence and healthcare utilization during the first trimester. Hyperemesis gravidarum is less common, affecting approximately 0.5\% to 2\% of pregnancies. The risk of developing severe NVP or HG is higher in primigravida (first pregnancy), multiple gestations (twins or triplets), gestational trophoblastic disease (molar pregnancy), and in women with a family or personal history of hyperemesis in previous pregnancies.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The exact cause of morning sickness is multifactorial, involving hormonal fluctuations, gastrointestinal changes, and genetic predispositions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG):} Rapidly rising levels of hCG stimulate the chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ) in the brainstem. The peak concentration of hCG in the first trimester correlates closely with the peak severity of NVP symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Oestrogen and progesterone:} Elevated levels of these hormones relax the smooth muscle of the gastrointestinal tract, leading to delayed gastric emptying, reduced intestinal motility, and gastro-oesophageal reflux, which can trigger nausea.
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} Studies have identified specific genetic risk factors, particularly mutations or variations in the genes encoding GDF15 (growth differentiation factor 15) and IGFBP7 (insulin-like growth factor binding protein 7), which are expressed in the placenta and act on the brainstem to regulate appetite and emesis.
    \item \textbf{Nutritional deficiencies:} Low levels of pyridoxine (Vitamin B6) have been associated with increased severity of pregnancy-related nausea.
    \item \textbf{Evolutionary protection:} A biological hypothesis suggesting that NVP serves an evolutionary purpose by causing the pregnant mother to avoid potentially toxic or pathogen-carrying foods during the critical period of fetal organogenesis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Morning sickness presents with a spectrum of gastrointestinal and systemic symptoms that vary in intensity.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nausea and dry retching:} Often triggered by specific odours (e.g., cooking smells, perfumes, coffee), visual stimuli, or motion.
    \item \textbf{Vomiting:} May occur multiple times daily, but is not accompanied by abdominal pain or fever.
    \item \textbf{Ptyalism:} Excessive salivation, which can be distressing and difficult for the patient to swallow.
    \item \textbf{Dysgeusia:} An altered or metallic taste in the mouth.
    \item \textbf{Fatigue and exhaustion:} Physical tiredness, which is often exacerbated by disrupted sleep and poor nutritional intake.
    \item \textbf{Signs of dehydration (in severe cases):} Dry mouth, dark and concentrated urine, oliguria (decreased urine output), postural dizziness, tachycardia (rapid heart rate), and hypotension.
    \item \textbf{Weight loss:} In hyperemesis gravidarum, patients experience a documented loss of 5\% or more of their pre-pregnancy body weight.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
While nausea and vomiting are common in early pregnancy, clinicians must exclude other medical causes, particularly if symptoms begin after the ninth week of gestation or are accompanied by fever, pain, or headache.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gastroenteritis:} Presents with nausea and vomiting but is accompanied by diarrhoea, abdominal cramps, and sometimes fever, which are absent in NVP.
    \item \textbf{Urinary tract infection (UTI) or pyelonephritis:} Can cause severe nausea and vomiting, distinguished by dysuria, urinary frequency, loin pain, fever, and positive urine culture.
    \item \textbf{Cholecystitis or pancreatitis:} Causes acute vomiting, but is characterised by severe, constant right upper quadrant or epigastric pain radiating to the back, and elevated lipase or abnormal liver ultrasounds.
    \item \textbf{Gestational trophoblastic disease (molar pregnancy):} Characterised by abnormally high hCG levels, severe vomiting, uterine size greater than dates, and diagnostic vesicular patterns on pelvic ultrasound.
    \item \textbf{Peptic ulcer disease or GORD:} Burning epigastric pain or heartburn, often exacerbated by lying down, which may pre-date the pregnancy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most women can manage mild morning sickness with self-care measures. However, persistent vomiting requires evaluation by a general practitioner or obstetrician.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe dizziness, fainting, or loss of consciousness upon standing.
    \item Vomiting blood or material that resembles dark coffee grounds.
    \item Sudden, severe abdominal pain or chest pain.
    \item Severe confusion, disorientation, or difficulty speaking.
\end{itemize}
\textit{Note: Consult a doctor if you are unable to keep any fluids or food down for 24 hours, have not passed urine for more than 8 hours, or have lost weight.}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is clinical, but investigations are necessary to determine severity, guide management, and rule out complications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} Evaluation of gestational age, weight change, fluid intake, and severity scoring using the Pregnancy-Unique Quantification of Emesis (PUQE) index.
    \item \textbf{Urine dipstick:} Performed to check for ketones (indicating starvation ketosis and the need for hydration) and specific gravity (to assess hydration status), and to exclude a UTI.
    \item \textbf{Blood chemistry:} Urea, electrolytes, and creatinine to monitor renal function and detect hypokalaemia or hyponatraemia. Liver function tests and thyroid function tests (TSH, free T4) are performed in severe cases to rule out gestational thyrotoxicosis.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic ultrasound:} Essential to confirm pregnancy viability, establish accurate dating, and rule out multiple gestations or molar pregnancy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management follows a step-wise approach, starting with lifestyle and dietary measures and escalating to pharmacological and intravenous therapies.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary modifications:} Consume small, frequent meals (every 1 to 2 hours) rich in carbohydrates and protein, and low in fat. Eat dry crackers, plain biscuits, or toast in the morning before getting out of bed. Separate eating solids from drinking fluids (avoid drinking during meals).
    \item \textbf{Fluid management:} Hydrate with cold, clear, carbonated, or slightly sour fluids (such as ginger beer, lemonade, diluted juice, or ice chips). Oral rehydration solutions are recommended if vomiting is frequent.
    \item \textbf{Ginger and acupressure:} Ginger supplements (250 milligram capsules taken four times daily) and acupressure at the P6 (Neiguan) point on the inner wrist (using Sea-Bands) provide effective, non-pharmacological relief for mild-to-moderate symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological therapy (first-line):} Pyridoxine (Vitamin B6) supplements (10 to 25 milligrams up to four times daily), either alone or combined with doxylamine (an antihistamine, typically taken at night to minimise daytime drowsiness).
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological therapy (second-line):} Dopamine antagonists, including metoclopramide or prochlorperazine, which act on the chemoreceptor trigger zone to suppress emesis.
    \item \textbf{Ondansetron:} A potent 5-HT3 receptor antagonist, reserved for moderate-to-severe symptoms when other agents have failed, with careful counseling regarding its efficacy and safety profile in the first trimester.
    \item \textbf{Intravenous rehydration:} Indicated for hyperemesis gravidarum. Involves IV fluid replacement (normal saline or Hartmann's solution), correction of electrolyte deficiencies (potassium), and intravenous thiamine (Vitamin B1) supplementation. Glucose-containing fluids must be avoided until thiamine is administered to prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
If left untreated, severe nausea and vomiting of pregnancy can lead to serious maternal and fetal complications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Severe dehydration and electrolyte depletion:} Severe hypokalaemia and hyponatraemia, which can cause cardiac arrhythmias, muscle weakness, and neurological disturbances.
    \item \textbf{Wernicke's encephalopathy:} A rare, life-threatening neurological condition caused by thiamine (Vitamin B1) deficiency secondary to prolonged vomiting. Characterised by the triad of confusion, ataxia, and ocular abnormalities.
    \item \textbf{Mallory-Weiss tears:} Tears in the mucous membrane of the lower oesophagus or gastro-oesophageal junction, caused by forceful retching and resulting in haematemesis.
    \item \textbf{Acute kidney injury:} Prerenal AKI resulting from severe hypovolaemia and renal hypoperfusion.
    \item \textbf{Fetal growth restriction:} Severe, unmanaged hyperemesis gravidarum leading to maternal weight loss can be associated with low birth weight and intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for the vast majority of women experiencing morning sickness is excellent, with symptoms resolving completely as the pregnancy enters the second trimester (usually by 16 to 20 weeks). Mild-to-moderate NVP is not associated with adverse fetal outcomes and is epidemiologically linked to a lower rate of miscarriage. Even in cases of severe hyperemesis gravidarum, prompt medical management, hydration, and nutritional support ensure that maternal and fetal outcomes are normal.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Proactive self-care and pre-conception planning can help mitigate the severity of morning sickness.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prenatal multivitamins:} Take a prenatal multivitamin (containing folic acid) for at least one month prior to conception and during early pregnancy, as this has been shown to reduce the incidence and severity of NVP.
    \item \textbf{Adequate rest:} Ensure plenty of rest and sleep, as physical fatigue and emotional stress are known triggers that can worsen nausea.
    \item \textbf{Avoid triggers:} Identify and actively avoid sensory triggers, such as stuffy rooms, strong cooking odours, sudden movements, and flickering lights.
    \item \textbf{Oral hygiene:} Rinse the mouth with water or a bicarbonate rinse after vomiting to protect tooth enamel from gastric acid, and delay tooth brushing for at least 30 minutes.
\end{itemize}
