\chapter{Chemotherapy}
\index{Chemotherapy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Chemotherapy is the use of drugs to destroy cancer cells or slow their growth. It is one of the main pillars of cancer treatment, alongside surgery, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and hormone therapy. Chemotherapy drugs work by interfering with cell division, which makes them effective against the rapidly dividing cells that characterise cancer, but it also affects some healthy fast-dividing cells such as those of the bone marrow, the lining of the gut, and hair follicles -- the source of many of its well-known side effects. Chemotherapy may be given with curative intent, to control the disease and prolong life, to shrink a tumour before surgery or radiotherapy, or to relieve symptoms in advanced disease. Treatment is planned and delivered by specialist oncology teams.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cancer is one of the leading causes of death worldwide, and a very large proportion of people diagnosed with cancer will receive chemotherapy at some point in their care. The need for chemotherapy reflects the global burden of cancer, which continues to rise with ageing populations and increasing exposure to risk factors such as smoking, obesity, and alcohol. Chemotherapy is used across nearly all cancer types and at all ages, from childhood cancers to cancers of older adults. Advances in chemotherapy and the addition of newer treatments have contributed to steadily improving cancer survival rates in many countries. The impact of chemotherapy on populations is substantial both in terms of lives saved and in the supportive-care resources required to manage its side effects.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mechanism of action:} most chemotherapy drugs damage the DNA or the machinery of cell division in rapidly dividing cells, triggering cell death
    \item \textbf{Cell-cycle specificity:} some drugs act only on cells that are actively dividing, while others are effective throughout the cell cycle
    \item \textbf{Combination therapy:} drugs with different mechanisms are often combined to attack cancer cells in several ways and reduce the chance of resistance
    \item \textbf{Administration routes:} chemotherapy may be given intravenously, orally, into a body cavity, or directly into the fluid around the spinal cord
    \item \textbf{Schedule:} treatment is given in cycles, with rest periods that allow healthy tissues such as the bone marrow to recover
    \item \textbf{Toxicity to normal tissue:} rapidly dividing healthy cells -- in the bone marrow, mouth, gut, and hair follicles -- are the most affected, producing the characteristic side effects
    \item \textbf{Resistance:} cancer cells can develop mechanisms that resist chemotherapy, which is why combinations and multiple lines of treatment are used
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\medskip
\textbf{Side effects.} Side effects vary with the drugs used, the dose, and the individual, and may include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fatigue, which is the most common side effect and may persist between cycles
    \item Nausea and vomiting, now much better controlled with modern anti-emetic medicines
    \item Mouth sores, taste changes, and loss of appetite
    \item Hair loss (alopecia), which is temporary and reversible
    \item Low blood counts, causing anaemia, infection, and easy bruising or bleeding
    \item Diarrhoea or constipation
    \item Peripheral neuropathy -- tingling, numbness, or burning in the hands and feet with certain drugs
    \item Skin changes, including dryness, rash, and increased sensitivity to sunlight
    \item Reduced fertility, which may be temporary or permanent
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Chemotherapy itself is not a diagnosis, but its side effects may mimic or be confused with other conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infections -- fever during chemotherapy is usually caused by low white cell counts rather than the drug itself
    \item Anaemia from other causes, including bleeding and nutritional deficiency
    \item Disease progression or treatment-related effects on organs, such as cardiac, liver, or kidney dysfunction
    \item Anxiety and depression presenting as fatigue or poor concentration
    \item Other drug reactions, including those from supportive medicines
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People receiving chemotherapy should contact their oncology team promptly about any new or worsening symptoms, particularly fever, which may indicate a dangerous infection when blood counts are low.

\medskip
\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever, chills, or feeling generally unwell during or shortly after chemotherapy
    \item Difficulty breathing, chest pain, or coughing up blood
    \item Severe bleeding, unusual bruising, or blood in the urine or stool
    \item Severe dehydration from persistent vomiting or diarrhoea
    \item Confusion, fainting, or seizures
    \item Sudden weakness or difficulty speaking, which may suggest a stroke
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-treatment assessment:} the diagnosis and stage of the cancer are established before treatment begins, and the person's general fitness is assessed
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count to monitor the bone marrow, plus kidney and liver function tests to guide drug dosing
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT, MRI, or PET scans to assess tumour response during and after treatment
    \item \textbf{Tumour markers:} blood tests for certain cancers that reflect disease activity
    \item \textbf{Genetic and molecular testing:} used to select drugs most likely to be effective for the individual's tumour
    \item \textbf{Monitoring during treatment:} regular blood counts and clinical review before each cycle
    \item \textbf{Surveillance:} long-term follow-up after treatment to detect recurrence and late effects
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Treatment planning:} the oncology team selects drugs, doses, and schedules based on the cancer type, stage, and the person's health
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} anti-emetics, growth factors to boost white cells, and transfusion for anaemia and low platelets
    \item \textbf{Infection management:} prompt treatment of fever and infection, which may require hospital admission and intravenous antibiotics
    \item \textbf{Side-effect management:} mouth care, nutritional support, medicines for pain and diarrhoea, and dose adjustments when needed
    \item \textbf{Combined-modality care:} chemotherapy may be combined with surgery, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy
    \item \textbf{Adjuvant and neoadjuvant use:} chemotherapy after surgery to eliminate residual disease, or before surgery to shrink tumours
    \item \textbf{Palliative chemotherapy:} used to control symptoms and improve quality of life when cure is no longer possible
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} counselling, support groups, and open communication with the care team
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Neutropenic sepsis -- a life-threatening infection when white blood cell counts are very low
    \item Severe anaemia requiring transfusion
    \item Bleeding due to low platelets
    \item Damage to specific organs, including the heart, kidneys, liver, lungs, and nerves, depending on the drugs used
    \item Nausea, vomiting, and mouth ulcers that interfere with eating and nutrition
    \item Permanent infertility in some cases
    \item Second cancers many years later, particularly with certain drug combinations
    \item Long-term fatigue, cognitive changes, and reduced quality of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for people receiving chemotherapy depends on the type and stage of the cancer, the molecular features of the tumour, and how the person responds to and tolerates treatment. For many cancers, chemotherapy has substantially improved survival, and in some -- such as certain lymphomas, testicular cancer, and childhood leukaemias -- it is potentially curative. In advanced disease, chemotherapy can shrink tumours, control symptoms, and prolong good-quality life. Advances in supportive care mean that the serious side effects of treatment can now be managed far better than in the past. The prognosis for each person is individual and should be discussed with the treating oncologist.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend all scheduled treatment sessions and blood tests
    \item Report fever, bleeding, or any new symptom to the oncology team without delay
    \item Follow advice on hand hygiene and avoiding people with infections when blood counts are low
    \item Eat small, frequent, nutritious meals and drink plenty of fluids
    \item Rest when needed but try to stay gently active between cycles
    \item Practise careful mouth care and use recommended rinses to prevent mouth sores
    \item Protect skin from the sun and use gentle skincare products
    \item Discuss fertility preservation with the team before treatment begins if relevant
    \item Ask about counselling and support services to help with the emotional impact of treatment
\end{itemize}
