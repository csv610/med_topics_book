\chapter{Hypothyroidism}
\index{Hypothyroidism}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hypothyroidism is a condition in which the thyroid gland produces too little thyroid hormone, slowing the body's metabolism. It is the most common thyroid disorder and is usually caused by an autoimmune condition called Hashimoto's thyroiditis, in which the immune system attacks the thyroid. Symptoms develop gradually and include fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin, and depression. Hypothyroidism is easily diagnosed with a blood test and treated with daily thyroid hormone replacement (thyroxine), which restores normal health in the vast majority of people.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hypothyroidism affects around 5--10\% of adults, with a higher prevalence in women, who are affected five to ten times more often than men. The risk increases with age, and up to one in five older women has some degree of underactive thyroid. Many cases are mild and may go undetected for years. Hashimoto's thyroiditis is the most common cause, and it runs in families. With treatment, people with hypothyroidism have a normal life expectancy.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hashimoto's thyroiditis:} autoimmune destruction of the thyroid, the commonest cause
    \item \textbf{Treatment of hyperthyroidism:} radioactive iodine and surgery can cause hypothyroidism
    \item \textbf{Thyroid surgery:} removal of the thyroid
    \item \textbf{Medications:} lithium, amiodarone, and some other drugs
    \item \textbf{Iodine deficiency:} uncommon in many countries but a worldwide cause
    \item \textbf{Pituitary disease:} rare, with reduced TSH stimulation
    \item \textbf{Postpartum thyroiditis:} transient hypothyroidism after pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} low thyroid hormone slows metabolism in every organ system
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fatigue, weakness, and sluggishness
    \item Weight gain despite a normal appetite
    \item Cold intolerance and feeling cold
    \item Constipation
    \item Dry skin, hair loss, and brittle nails
    \item Depression, poor concentration, and memory problems
    \item Hoarse voice and puffiness of the face
    \item Muscle aches and joint pain
    \item Heavy or irregular periods
    \item Slow heart rate
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Depression and chronic fatigue can mimic hypothyroidism
    \item Anaemia, especially iron deficiency
    \item Weight gain from other causes
    \item Other causes of fatigue, including sleep disorders
    \item Thyroid function tests confirm the diagnosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for persistent fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, or other suggestive symptoms, and for a goitre or family history of thyroid disease.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe drowsiness, confusion, or reduced consciousness (myxoedema coma, rare)
    \item Chest pain or palpitations
    \item Very slow heart rate with dizziness
    \item Severe breathlessness
    \item Collapse or fainting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Thyroid function tests:} high TSH with low free T4 confirms hypothyroidism
    \item \textbf{Thyroid antibodies:} confirm Hashimoto's thyroiditis
    \item \textbf{Lipid profile:} cholesterol is often elevated
    \item \textbf{Full blood count:} for anaemia
    \item \textbf{Regular monitoring:} thyroid levels are checked during treatment
    \item \textbf{Subclinical hypothyroidism:} raised TSH with normal T4, managed according to risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Thyroxine:} daily levothyroxine tablets replace the missing hormone
    \item \textbf{Dosing:} started low and adjusted according to TSH levels
    \item \textbf{Timing:} taken on an empty stomach, 30--60 minutes before food
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} TSH checked 6--8 weeks after dose changes, then annually
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} doses are adjusted and monitored closely
    \item \textbf{Lifelong treatment:} most people need thyroxine for life
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} healthy diet and exercise support wellbeing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Untreated hypothyroidism: elevated cholesterol and cardiovascular risk
    \item Myxoedema coma, a rare life-threatening emergency
    \item Pregnancy complications if untreated
    \item Depression and reduced quality of life
    \item Over-treatment, causing symptoms of hyperthyroidism
    \item Interactions with iron, calcium, and some medicines
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Hypothyroidism is one of the most treatable chronic conditions. Within weeks of starting thyroxine, symptoms improve, and most people feel completely well on the correct dose. Life expectancy is normal with treatment. The main requirements are consistent daily medication and regular blood tests to keep the dose right. With good management, hypothyroidism has an excellent outlook.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take thyroxine daily at the same time, on an empty stomach
    \item Never stop treatment without medical advice
    \item Attend regular blood tests
    \item Separate thyroxine from iron, calcium, and antacids
    \item In pregnancy, inform your doctor for dose adjustment
    \item Report new symptoms of over- or under-treatment
    \item Maintain a healthy diet and weight
    \item Seek early testing if you have risk factors or family history
\end{itemize}
