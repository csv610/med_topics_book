\chapter{Cytomegalovirus (CMV)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cytomegalovirus (CMV) is a common virus belonging to the herpes family. Most people are infected with CMV at some point in their lives, usually during childhood, and in healthy people it causes no symptoms or a mild, flu-like illness. After infection, the virus stays in the body for life in a dormant state. CMV is a concern in pregnancy, because a first infection during pregnancy can be passed to the baby and cause hearing loss and developmental problems. It also causes serious disease in people with weakened immune systems, such as organ transplant recipients and people with advanced HIV.

\section*{Epidemiology}
CMV is one of the most widespread human viruses. By adulthood, a large majority of people have been infected, with higher rates in lower-income countries and among people who attended childcare. Around one in every 150--200 babies is born with congenital CMV, making it the most common infectious cause of birth defects, and it is the leading non-genetic cause of childhood hearing loss. Most babies born with CMV are healthy, but a minority develop hearing loss or developmental problems. In transplant recipients and people with HIV, CMV can reactivate and cause serious illness.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
CMV is spread through contact with infected body fluids.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} saliva, urine, breast milk, blood, and sexual fluids
    \item \textbf{Childcare:} young children shed the virus in saliva and urine for months after infection
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} a first infection during pregnancy can cross the placenta to the baby
    \item \textbf{Immune suppression:} the virus reactivates when immunity is weakened
    \item \textbf{Latency:} after primary infection, CMV remains dormant in the body for life
    \item \textbf{Effects in the fetus:} infection can damage the developing brain and inner ear
    \item \textbf{Effects in immunocompromised people:} pneumonia, hepatitis, colitis, and retinitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on the person's immune status.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{In healthy people:} usually no symptoms, or a mild illness with fever, sore throat, fatigue, and swollen glands
    \item \textbf{In pregnancy:} usually no symptoms, but the infection can affect the baby
    \item \textbf{Congenital CMV:} most babies have no symptoms; some have small size, jaundice, a rash, an enlarged liver or spleen, hearing loss, or developmental delay
    \item \textbf{In immunocompromised people:} fever, pneumonia with cough and breathlessness, colitis with diarrhoea, hepatitis, and retinitis with vision loss
    \item \textbf{Glandular fever-like illness:} fatigue and swollen glands in some adults
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of primary CMV overlap with other infections.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Glandular fever (Epstein-Barr virus)
    \item Other viral infections, including influenza and COVID-19
    \item Toxoplasmosis, which can also cause congenital infection
    \item Other causes of hepatitis and colitis in immunocompromised people
    \item HIV and other immunodeficiencies
    \item Other causes of hearing loss and developmental delay in babies
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if you have a persistent glandular fever-like illness, if you are pregnant and concerned about CMV exposure, or if you are immunocompromised and develop fever, breathing symptoms, or digestive symptoms.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing or chest pain
    \item Sudden vision loss or visual disturbance
    \item Severe abdominal pain or heavy diarrhoea with dehydration
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or seizures
    \item Any serious illness in a person with a weakened immune system
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis uses blood and other laboratory tests.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CMV antibody tests:} IgM and IgG antibodies distinguish recent from past infection
    \item \textbf{CMV PCR:} detects the virus in blood, urine, saliva, or other fluids
    \item \textbf{Newborn testing:} saliva or urine testing of babies, ideally within three weeks of birth
    \item \textbf{Hearing tests:} for babies with congenital CMV
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} in pregnancy, to look for signs of fetal infection
    \item \textbf{Imaging and endoscopy:} for pneumonia, colitis, or retinitis in immunocompromised people
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} to infectious diseases, obstetrics, or paediatrics as appropriate
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on who is affected.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Healthy people:} no treatment; symptoms settle on their own
    \item \textbf{Congenital CMV:} antiviral treatment (valganciclovir) for babies with moderate to severe symptoms, plus hearing and developmental follow-up
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} monitoring and specialist obstetric care; antiviral treatment is considered in some situations
    \item \textbf{Immunocompromised people:} antiviral medicines such as ganciclovir or valganciclovir
    \item \textbf{Transplant patients:} preventive antivirals or monitoring after transplantation
    \item \textbf{Retinitis:} specific antiviral treatment to preserve vision
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} hearing tests and developmental review for affected children
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Congenital hearing loss, the most important complication
    \item Developmental delay and learning difficulties
    \item Small size, jaundice, and organ enlargement in newborns
    \item Pneumonia, colitis, hepatitis, and retinitis in immunocompromised people
    \item Transplant rejection or graft dysfunction related to CMV
    \item Chronic fatigue in some people with primary infection
    \item Death in severely immunocompromised people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
In healthy people, CMV infection is harmless and resolves without treatment. Most babies born with congenital CMV are healthy, but a significant minority develop hearing loss, which can progress, and some have developmental problems; early diagnosis allows timely support. In immunocompromised people, CMV can be serious, but antiviral treatment is effective in most cases. Women who have had CMV before pregnancy have a very low risk of passing a damaging infection to their baby, since prior immunity protects the fetus.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pregnant women and those planning pregnancy should practise good hygiene, especially around young children: wash hands after nappy changes, kissing, and sharing food or utensils
    \item Do not share food, drinks, or eating utensils with young children while pregnant
    \item Avoid kissing young children on the lips
    \item Wash toys and surfaces that may have saliva or urine on them
    \item Practise safer sex during pregnancy if a partner may have CMV
    \item Immunocompromised people should seek early review of any new symptoms
    \item Attend hearing and developmental follow-up for babies with congenital CMV
\end{itemize}
