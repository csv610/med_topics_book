\section*{Definition and Overview}
Tubal ligation, often called "having your tubes tied", is a surgical procedure for permanent contraception in women. It involves blocking or dividing the fallopian tubes so that sperm cannot reach the egg and the egg cannot travel to the womb, thereby preventing fertilisation. It is also referred to as female sterilisation, and is one of the most effective forms of contraception available, with a failure rate of less than one percent. The procedure can be performed at several points in life: soon after childbirth, at the time of a caesarean section, or as a planned operation at any other time. Modern techniques have moved away from cutting and tying alone, and now commonly involve laparoscopic clipping, coagulation, or removal of segments of the tubes; removal of both fallopian tubes (salpingectomy) is increasingly preferred because it also reduces the risk of ovarian cancer. Tubal ligation is intended to be permanent, and reversal is difficult, unreliable, and not generally available through the public system. This chapter explains how it works, who it suits, the procedure itself, its risks, and the important decisions involved, including the alternatives.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Female sterilisation has been one of the most widely used contraceptive methods in the world for decades, used by an estimated 200 million women globally. In many countries it has declined in popularity in recent years, partly because long-acting reversible contraception such as the intrauterine device and the contraceptive implant offers comparable effectiveness without surgery, and partly because male vasectomy is a simpler, safer alternative. Nevertheless, tubal ligation remains a common procedure, and around one in ten women of reproductive age who use contraception relies on it. It is chosen most often by women in their thirties and forties, who have completed their families, although it is occasionally requested by younger women with strong convictions about not having children. The procedure is performed in both public and private hospitals, usually as day surgery. Rates are also shaped by regret and access: women who choose the method under pressure, or during another procedure such as caesarean section, are over-represented among those who later seek reversal.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Tubal ligation works by physically interrupting the pathway along which sperm and egg meet. In a normal cycle, an egg is released from the ovary and swept into the fallopian tube, where it can be fertilised by sperm that have travelled up through the womb. Ligation closes off this route, so that the egg cannot meet sperm and fertilisation cannot occur.

The mechanisms used include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clipping:} a small clip or ring is placed across each tube, blocking the lumen
    \item \textbf{Coagulation:} the tube is sealed using electrical or thermal energy
    \item \textbf{Ligation and excision:} a loop of each tube is tied and a segment removed
    \item \textbf{Salpingectomy:} removal of the whole fallopian tube, which is both permanent and protective against ovarian cancer, and is increasingly recommended at the time of the procedure
\end{itemize}

Because the ovaries are not removed, hormone production and the menstrual cycle are unaffected: periods continue, and menopause occurs at the natural age. The procedure prevents pregnancy by blocking the tubes but does not terminate a pregnancy that has already begun, and it offers no protection against sexually transmitted infections. A small but real failure rate exists because tubes can occasionally reopen or a rare ectopic route allows pregnancy, which is why the method is extremely effective rather than absolutely perfect.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Tubal ligation is an operation rather than an illness, so the relevant "clinical features" are the experiences around the procedure and recovery.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The procedure:} usually performed under general anaesthetic, most commonly as keyhole (laparoscopic) surgery, through a small cut in the belly button and one or two tiny incisions
    \item \textbf{Same-day discharge:} most women go home the same day or the following morning
    \item \textbf{Recovery:} mild wound pain, some shoulder tip pain from the gas used in laparoscopy, and light vaginal spotting are normal for a few days
    \item \textbf{Rapid return to normal:} most women resume light activities within a few days and full activities within one to two weeks
    \item \textbf{No change to periods or hormones:} the menstrual cycle, libido, and menopausal timing are unaffected
    \item \textbf{Immediate effectiveness:} the procedure works immediately, unlike some hormonal methods, though a follow-up check is usually arranged
\end{itemize}

The defining feature of tubal ligation, and the source of its greatest emotional weight, is permanence: there is no method that reliably reverses it.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Choosing tubal ligation is a decision between contraceptive methods, and the relevant "differential diagnosis" is the range of alternatives that should be discussed before any irreversible step is taken.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Long-acting reversible contraception (LARC):} the copper IUD and the levonorgestrel IUD last five to ten years and are as effective as sterilisation, reversible, and safe; the implant lasts three years. For many women these are the best option
    \item \textbf{Vasectomy:} male sterilisation is simpler, cheaper, and safer than tubal ligation, and is the method of choice for couples who have completed their family
    \item \textbf{Other hormonal methods:} the pill, injection, and vaginal ring, which are reversible
    \item \textbf{Barrier methods:} condoms and diaphragms, which also protect against sexually transmitted infections
    \item \textbf{Fertility-awareness methods:} for women who are highly motivated and have regular cycles
\end{itemize}

The decision should also consider age, relationship stability, and the strength of the desire for no further children, because regret is the main adverse outcome of the procedure.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
The decision to have a tubal ligation deserves careful medical counselling, and several situations make specialist review particularly important.

Seek medical advice if you:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Are considering sterilisation but have any doubt about future children
    \item Are under about 30, where regret rates are highest
    \item Have a medical condition that complicates surgery or anaesthesia
    \item Have a history of previous abdominal or pelvic surgery, which can make the procedure more complex
    \item Are considering the procedure at the same time as another operation or after childbirth, when the decision should be made well in advance, not on the day
\end{itemize}

After the operation, seek urgent care for fever, severe pain, heavy bleeding, or increasing redness around the wounds. \textbf{Contact your local emergency services} for sudden, severe abdominal pain, collapse, fainting, or heavy vaginal bleeding, which could indicate internal bleeding or a rare complication such as injury to the bowel or bladder.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Before tubal ligation, the assessment is designed to confirm that the procedure is appropriate and safe.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Counselling:} the most important part of the process. The permanence, effectiveness, risks, and alternatives are discussed fully, usually more than once, and often with a partner or support person. This is when the procedure is considered for suitability, not just for medical safety
    \item \textbf{Medical history and examination:} general health, previous surgery, weight, and any anaesthetic risks are assessed
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test:} performed before surgery to ensure the woman is not already pregnant
    \item \textbf{Sexual health screening:} recommended, since the procedure offers no protection against STIs and untreated infection increases surgical risk
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative tests:} blood tests and an assessment with the anaesthetist are arranged as for any general anaesthetic
    \item \textbf{Consent:} informed consent is taken, ideally at a separate visit from the day of surgery, allowing time for reflection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of tubal ligation is the surgical procedure itself, performed in one of several ways.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Laparoscopic tubal ligation:} the standard modern approach. Under general anaesthetic, a small telescope is passed through the belly button, and each fallopian tube is sealed with clips, cautery, or by removing a segment
    \item \textbf{Salpingectomy:} removal of the whole fallopian tube through the same keyhole approach; this is increasingly preferred because it is the most reliable and reduces the risk of ovarian cancer, with minimal added risk
    \item \textbf{Postpartum tubal ligation:} performed within hours or days after a vaginal birth, or during a caesarean section, taking advantage of the enlarged uterus to reach the tubes easily
    \item \textbf{Minilaparotomy:} a small open cut in the lower abdomen, sometimes used when laparoscopy is not suitable or after recent caesarean
    \item \textbf{Hysteroscopic methods:} newer no-incision techniques that block the tubes from inside the uterus, though these have largely been superseded by salpingectomy because of lower long-term reliability and cancer-protection considerations
    \item \textbf{Anaesthesia and day surgery:} most women have the operation as day surgery and go home the same day with simple analgesia
\end{itemize}

There is no routine follow-up test to confirm the tubes are blocked; the woman relies on the procedure having been performed correctly, and failure is very rare.

\section*{Complications}
Tubal ligation is a safe operation, but like any surgery it carries risks, and these should be understood before consent.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anaesthetic complications:} rare, but possible with any general anaesthetic
    \item \textbf{Bleeding or infection:} at the wound sites or within the abdomen, in a small percentage of cases
    \item \textbf{Injury to nearby organs:} very rare damage to the bowel, bladder, or blood vessels during laparoscopy, which may require further surgery
    \item \textbf{Failed sterilisation:} pregnancy occurs in under one percent of women; when it does, there is a higher than usual chance of ectopic pregnancy, which requires urgent medical attention
    \item \textbf{Ectopic pregnancy:} a pregnancy outside the womb can still occur after tubal ligation, and its symptoms of one-sided pain and bleeding should never be ignored
    \item \textbf{No protection against STIs:} condoms are still needed for safe sex
    \item \textbf{Regret:} the most common "complication" of all; it is an emotional outcome rather than a medical one, but it is real and is the strongest argument for careful counselling
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Tubal ligation is one of the most effective contraceptive methods available, with a failure rate of less than one in two hundred women over a lifetime, and with salpingectomy the rate is lower still. It requires no ongoing effort, no medication, and no maintenance, which many women find liberating. Periods, hormones, and sex drive are unaffected, and there is no impact on the natural timing of menopause. Reversal is possible in some cases but requires complex surgery, is rarely available publicly, and has no guarantee of success, so the procedure must be regarded as permanent. For women who are certain they have completed their family, or who are certain they never want children, tubal ligation offers dependable, permanent peace of mind. The procedure does not protect against sexually transmitted infections, and it has a small but important association with ectopic pregnancy if a failure occurs, so warning signs must be known.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take time over the decision: discuss it with your partner, your doctor, and, if useful, a counsellor, and consider it over several weeks rather than deciding on impulse
    \item Explore long-acting reversible contraception first, and ask a clinician to compare the methods honestly
    \item Consider vasectomy for your partner if you are in a relationship and have completed your family, as it is simpler and safer
    \item Make the decision about postpartum sterilisation before labour, never on the delivery day
    \item Stop smoking before surgery to reduce anaesthetic risk, if you smoke
    \item Plan the operation for a time when you can rest afterwards, and arrange help with young children
    \item Use condoms for several months after the procedure if you are at risk of sexually transmitted infections
    \item Know the symptoms of ectopic pregnancy and seek prompt care if they occur
\end{itemize}
