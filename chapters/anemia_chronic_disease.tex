\chapter{Anemia of Chronic Disease}
\index{Anemia of Chronic Disease}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Anemia of chronic disease, also called anemia of inflammation, is a type of anemia that develops in people who have a long-term illness or ongoing inflammation in the body. It occurs when the immune system's response to an underlying condition interferes with the body's ability to make and use red blood cells, even though iron stores are normal or even high.

It is most often seen in chronic infection, autoimmune disease, kidney disease, heart failure, and cancer. Treating the underlying condition is the mainstay of management, and iron replacement is only helpful if iron deficiency is also present.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Anemia of chronic disease is one of the most common types of anemia, especially in hospital patients and older people, and it is the second most frequent cause of anemia after iron deficiency. It is common in conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, chronic kidney disease, heart failure, and many cancers, affecting a large proportion of people with these illnesses.

Its frequency increases with age because the underlying conditions that cause it become more common in later life. Many people with an inflammatory illness will have at least a mild fall in hemoglobin even when they have no symptoms.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Underlying conditions:} chronic infection, autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, kidney disease, heart failure, and cancer
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory chemicals:} long-term inflammation releases proteins such as hepcidin and cytokines that disrupt normal iron handling and red blood cell production
    \item \textbf{Iron trapping:} the body holds iron in storage rather than releasing it to make red blood cells, so the marrow runs short even though iron stores appear adequate
    \item \textbf{Reduced red cell life:} inflammation also shortens the survival of red blood cells
    \item \textbf{Kidney contribution:} kidney disease reduces production of erythropoietin, the hormone that stimulates the bone marrow to make red blood cells
    \item \textbf{Result:} red blood cell production falls and the blood cannot carry as much oxygen, causing the symptoms of anemia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fatigue and weakness that add to the symptoms of the underlying illness
    \item Pallor, with paler skin and paler inner eyelids
    \item Shortness of breath on exertion
    \item Dizziness or light-headedness
    \item Palpitations or a fast heartbeat
    \item Reduced ability to exercise and perform daily tasks
    \item Symptoms of the underlying condition, such as joint pain, abdominal symptoms, or weight loss, are usually also present
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Iron-deficiency anemia, which needs different treatment and is often found together with anemia of chronic disease
    \item Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency
    \item Anemia from blood loss, especially hidden bleeding from the stomach or bowel
    \item Hemolytic anemia from rapid destruction of red blood cells
    \item Bone marrow disorders such as aplastic anemia or blood cancers
    \item Anemia caused by medicines, including some used to treat the underlying condition
    \item Kidney failure as the sole cause of anemia, which is itself a form of chronic disease anemia
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if you have a chronic illness and develop increasing fatigue, breathlessness, or paleness, or if you feel that your usual symptoms are getting worse. These may point to anemia or to a change in your underlying condition.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain or severe breathlessness
    \item Fainting or collapse
    \item A very fast or irregular heartbeat
    \item Any sudden severe symptom that causes concern
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full blood count:} typically shows a mild to moderate anemia, with red blood cells of roughly normal size
    \item \textbf{Iron studies:} distinguish anemia of chronic disease from iron deficiency; ferritin may be normal or high despite low iron availability
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory markers:} tests such as C-reactive protein or the erythrocyte sedimentation rate, which are usually raised with inflammation
    \item \textbf{Kidney function:} blood tests for urea and creatinine, and sometimes erythropoietin levels
    \item \textbf{Other tests:} vitamin B12, folate, and a blood film to exclude other causes of anemia
    \item \textbf{Tests for the underlying condition:} may include imaging or biopsy, depending on which illness is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Treat the underlying condition:} the most important step, since controlling inflammation, infection, or cancer usually improves the anemia
    \item \textbf{Review of medicines:} adjusting treatments that might contribute to anemia or bleeding
    \item \textbf{Iron:} only given if iron deficiency is also present, because extra iron does not help when the problem is iron trapping
    \item \textbf{Stimulating agents:} medicines such as erythropoiesis-stimulating agents may be used in selected cases, especially in kidney disease, under specialist guidance
    \item \textbf{Blood transfusion:} for moderate to severe symptoms that need faster correction
    \item \textbf{Symptom management:} pacing activities and resting when needed while the underlying disease is being treated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Worsening fatigue and reduced quality of life
    \item Impaired heart function in severe or prolonged cases
    \item Increased frailty and reduced independence, especially in older people
    \item More complications from surgery or other treatments when the blood count is low
    \item Risks of treatment, including reactions to transfusion or side effects of stimulating agents
    \item Difficulty distinguishing this anemia from other causes, which can delay correct treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The course of anemia of chronic disease follows the course of the underlying illness. When the underlying condition is controlled or improves, the anemia usually improves as well. When the condition is persistent, the anemia tends to persist, and the aim of care is to manage it so that symptoms stay mild. Fatigue may remain even with treatment, particularly if the underlying disease is advanced. In many cases, careful management keeps the anemia at a level that has little impact on everyday life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Follow your treatment plan for the underlying condition, since good control reduces inflammation and its effects
    \item Eat a balanced diet with enough protein, iron, and vitamins, and have iron status checked if symptoms develop
    \item Keep active within your limits to maintain energy and strength
    \item Rest when you need to and pace activities on days when you feel more tired
    \item Attend regular blood tests and medical reviews to monitor for anemia and its complications
    \item Tell your doctor about any new or worsening fatigue, breathlessness, or paleness
\end{itemize}
