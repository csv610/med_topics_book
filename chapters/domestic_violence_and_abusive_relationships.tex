\chapter{Domestic Violence and Abusive Relationships}
\index{Domestic Violence and Abusive Relationships}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An abusive relationship is one in which one partner uses power and control over the other through violence, threats, intimidation, or manipulation. Domestic violence includes physical, sexual, psychological, emotional, and financial abuse, and it occurs across all communities. Abusive relationships are often characterised by a cycle of tension, explosion, and reconciliation, which can make it difficult to recognise and leave. The abuse is never the victim's fault, help is available, and there are strong services and legal protections in many countries to support people affected. This chapter explains the signs of abuse, the effects, and how to get help.

\section*{Epidemiology}
One in four women and one in thirteen men have experienced intimate partner violence, and domestic violence is a leading cause of homelessness, injury, and death. Many more people experience emotional and financial abuse without physical violence. Abuse is under-reported, and victims often stay because of fear, financial dependence, shame, or concern for children. Children in abusive homes are affected even when they are not the direct target.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Power and control:} abuse is an ongoing pattern of domination rather than isolated incidents
    \item \textbf{Physical abuse:} violence causing injury or fear
    \item \textbf{Psychological abuse:} threats, intimidation, gaslighting, and humiliation
    \item \textbf{Emotional abuse:} constant criticism, blame, and undermining of self-worth
    \item \textbf{Financial abuse:} control of money, work, and resources
    \item \textbf{Social isolation:} restricting contact with family and friends
    \item \textbf{The cycle of abuse:} tension building, acute incident, and reconciliation, which repeats and escalates
    \item \textbf{Coercive control:} a pattern of behaviour that strips away autonomy and freedom
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A partner who is jealous, possessive, or controlling
    \item Fear of the partner's reactions and walking on eggshells
    \item Isolation from friends, family, and activities
    \item Constant criticism, blame, and put-downs
    \item Being monitored through phones, tracking, or surveillance
    \item Financial dependence or having money controlled
    \item Injuries, anxiety, depression, low self-esteem, and stress-related illness
    \item Difficulty making decisions without the partner
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Abusive relationships versus unhealthy but non-abusive conflict
    \item Psychological effects of abuse versus other causes of anxiety and depression
    \item Coercive control with no physical violence, which is still abuse
    \item Child abuse and elder abuse, which may coexist
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who recognises signs of abuse in their relationship should know that help is available. Health professionals, including GPs, can provide support, safety planning, and referral. Seeking help does not mean the relationship will end; it can also mean getting support to make it safe.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Immediate danger or an assault
    \item Serious injury needing urgent care
    \item Threats to life or threats with weapons
    \item Concerns for the safety of children
    \item Suicidal thoughts or severe distress
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Support lines:} 1800RESPECT (1800 737 732) and the Men's Referral Service (1300 766 491) are available 24 hours.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sensitive enquiry:} health professionals ask about safety and abuse routinely
    \item \textbf{Assessment of the pattern:} the nature, frequency, and escalation of controlling behaviours
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} immediate danger, weapons, jealousy, and threats
    \item \textbf{Safety planning:} a personalised plan for the person and children
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to specialist services, legal advice, and police where appropriate
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Safety planning:} escape routes, important documents, money, and a code word
    \item \textbf{Specialist support:} 1800RESPECT and local domestic violence services
    \item \textbf{Legal protection:} intervention orders and family law advice
    \item \textbf{Police:} reporting when safe and appropriate
    \item \textbf{Health care:} treatment of injuries and mental health support
    \item \textbf{Housing:} refuge and housing support when leaving
    \item \textbf{Financial support:} financial counselling and assistance
    \item \textbf{Children:} support for children affected by abuse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Injury, disability, and death
    \item Depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress, and suicide
    \item Physical health problems from chronic stress
    \item Homelessness and poverty
    \item Harm to children's development and safety
    \item Social isolation and loss of identity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Leaving an abusive relationship is often a gradual process, and people may return several times before leaving for good. With safety planning, support, and legal protection, most people who leave successfully rebuild safe and fulfilling lives. Recovery is possible, and many survivors report that the support of services, family, and health professionals was crucial. Help is always available, and no one has to face abuse alone.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Learn the signs of coercive control and abuse
    \item Build and maintain support networks outside the relationship
    \item Keep a bag with documents, keys, money, and medicines ready
    \item Have a plan for emergencies, including where to go
    \item Save helpline numbers and use them when needed
    \item Remember that abuse is never the victim's fault
    \item Model and teach respectful relationships to children
    \item Reach out to a GP or support service for guidance
\end{itemize}
