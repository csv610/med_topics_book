\chapter{Emergency Departments}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An emergency department, often called an ED or casualty, is the part of a hospital that provides urgent care for people with serious or life-threatening conditions, accidents, and sudden illness. Emergency departments operate 24 hours a day and are staffed by doctors and nurses trained in emergency medicine, with access to the full resources of the hospital. Because demand is unpredictable, patients are seen in order of clinical urgency rather than arrival time, through a process called triage. This chapter explains how emergency departments work, when to go, and what to expect.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Emergency departments in Australia see millions of patients each year, and demand has grown steadily. A substantial proportion of attendances are for conditions that could have been managed by a GP or after-hours clinic, which contributes to crowding and waiting times. The most common reasons for attendance include injuries, chest pain, breathing problems, abdominal pain, and infections. A minority of patients are admitted to hospital, while the majority are treated and discharged.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The emergency department is organised to manage the full spectrum of urgent illness and injury.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Triage:} a nurse assesses each patient on arrival and assigns an urgency category, from resuscitation (immediate) to non-urgent
    \item \textbf{Acute care:} doctors assess, investigate, and treat urgent conditions
    \item \textbf{Resuscitation:} seriously ill or injured patients are cared for in a resuscitation area with full monitoring
    \item \textbf{Short-stay observation:} some patients stay in the ED for a period of observation before going home
    \item \textbf{Admission:} patients who need ongoing hospital care are admitted to a ward
    \item \textbf{The pathway:} arrival, triage, assessment, investigation, treatment, and either discharge or admission
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Knowing when to go to an emergency department is important.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, difficulty breathing, and stroke symptoms are emergencies
    \item Major injuries, heavy bleeding, and burns need emergency care
    \item Severe abdominal pain, seizures, and sudden severe headache
    \item Collapse, fainting, and altered consciousness
    \item Serious infections with high fever, especially in infants
    \item Mental health emergencies, including suicidal thoughts
    \item For less urgent problems, a GP, after-hours clinic, or nurse line is usually more appropriate
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Emergency departments are not the only option for urgent care.
\begin{itemize}
    \item GP clinics, including after-hours services, for non-life-threatening illness
    \item Urgent care and community clinics
    \item Nurse phone lines, such as Healthdirect, for advice
    \item Ambulance services, for people who cannot travel safely
    \item Pharmacists, for minor illnesses and injuries
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Call 000 for life-threatening emergencies and go to an emergency department when an urgent assessment is needed. For less urgent problems, consider a GP or after-hours service, and call a nurse line for advice when unsure.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, severe breathlessness, or stroke symptoms
    \item Severe bleeding, major injuries, or burns
    \item Unconsciousness, seizures, or a severe allergic reaction
    \item Sudden severe abdominal or head pain
    \item Thoughts of suicide or risk of harm
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
On arrival, patients move through a standard process.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Triage:} assessment of urgency, with the most unwell patients seen first
    \item \textbf{Nursing assessment:} observations including temperature, pulse, blood pressure, and oxygen
    \item \textbf{Medical assessment:} history and examination by an emergency doctor
    \item \textbf{Investigations:} blood tests, ECGs, and imaging such as X-rays and CT scans as needed
    \item \textbf{Treatment:} medicines, procedures, and wound care
    \item \textbf{Disposition:} discharge with advice, observation, or admission
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Good outcomes depend on the whole system working well.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Communication:} tell staff about your symptoms, medicines, allergies, and medical history
    \item \textbf{Bring essentials:} a list of medicines, Medicare card, and a support person if possible
    \item \textbf{Be patient with triage:} sicker patients are seen first, which can mean waits for less urgent problems
    \item \textbf{Understand discharge advice:} know what symptoms should bring you back
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} arrange GP review after discharge as advised
    \item \textbf{For minor problems:} consider alternatives to the ED, which reduce waiting for everyone
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Long waiting times, especially for less urgent conditions
    \item Crowding, which can affect quality of care
    \item The risk of infection in waiting areas
    \item The stress and cost of an ED visit for conditions that could be managed elsewhere
    \item Miscommunication during transitions of care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Emergency departments provide life-saving care for seriously ill and injured people, and the vast majority of patients are managed safely and effectively. Outcomes depend on seeking care at the right time and the right place, and on clear communication with the team. Knowing the warning signs of emergencies and the alternatives for minor problems helps the whole system work better.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Call 000 for life-threatening emergencies
    \item See a GP for routine and non-urgent problems
    \item Keep a record of your medicines and allergies
    \item Follow preventive care, such as immunisations and safe practices, to avoid emergencies
    \item Know the closest emergency department and its services
    \item Seek care early for concerning symptoms rather than waiting
    \item Use nurse phone lines for advice when unsure
\end{itemize}
