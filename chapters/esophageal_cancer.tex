\chapter{Oesophageal Cancer}
\index{Oesophageal Cancer}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Oesophageal cancer is cancer that starts in the oesophagus, the muscular tube that carries food from the throat to the stomach. The two main types are squamous cell carcinoma, which arises from the lining cells, and adenocarcinoma, which develops from glandular cells and is linked to long-standing reflux. Oesophageal cancer often causes difficulty swallowing and weight loss, and it is usually diagnosed at an advanced stage, which makes treatment challenging. However, treatment with surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy cures some people, particularly when the cancer is found early.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Oesophageal cancer is the seventh most common cancer worldwide and is more common in men than women, with most cases occurring after the age of 60. In many countries, adenocarcinoma has become the more common type in men, driven by the rising rates of reflux and obesity, while squamous cell carcinoma is linked to smoking and alcohol. Survival is improving with earlier diagnosis and better treatment, but the outlook remains poorer than for many cancers.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The two types of oesophageal cancer have different causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adenocarcinoma:} arises from Barrett's oesophagus, a change in the lining caused by long-standing acid reflux; obesity and reflux are the main risk factors
    \item \textbf{Squamous cell carcinoma:} strongly linked to smoking and heavy alcohol use
    \item \textbf{Other risk factors:} older age, male sex, achalasia, and diets low in fruit and vegetables
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} chronic irritation of the lining leads to cellular changes that progress to cancer
    \item \textbf{Spread:} the cancer invades the oesophageal wall and spreads to lymph nodes and other organs
    \item \textbf{Symptoms develop late:} the oesophagus stretches, so swallowing difficulty appears when the cancer is advanced
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms of oesophageal cancer often appear gradually.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive difficulty swallowing, first with solids and then with soft foods and fluids
    \item A feeling that food is sticking in the chest
    \item Unintentional weight loss
    \item Pain or discomfort in the chest or back
    \item Heartburn and indigestion that is new or worsening
    \item Vomiting or regurgitation
    \item Hoarseness and coughing
    \item Black stools from bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions cause swallowing difficulty and chest symptoms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Benign oesophageal strictures from reflux
    \item Achalasia and other motility disorders
    \item GORD (reflux disease) without cancer
    \item Globus sensation
    \item Other chest and abdominal conditions causing pain and weight loss
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with progressive difficulty swallowing, unexplained weight loss, or persistent new heartburn should see a doctor promptly. These symptoms warrant urgent investigation.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden complete difficulty swallowing
    \item Vomiting blood or black, tarry stools
    \item Severe chest pain
    \item Difficulty breathing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is made by endoscopy and biopsy, with staging tests.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gastroscopy:} examination of the oesophagus with a camera, with biopsy of any abnormal areas
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} confirms the cancer and its type
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} stages the cancer and checks for spread
    \item \textbf{Endoscopic ultrasound:} assesses how deeply the cancer has invaded
    \item \textbf{PET scan:} detects spread to lymph nodes and other organs
    \item \textbf{Blood tests and general assessment:} for fitness for treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on the stage and the person's fitness.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} removal of part or all of the oesophagus (oesophagectomy) for early or locally advanced cancer
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy and radiotherapy:} used before, after, or instead of surgery, often in combination
    \item \textbf{Endoscopic treatment:} removal of very early cancers during endoscopy
    \item \textbf{Palliative care:} management of swallowing difficulty, pain, and nutrition, including stents to keep the oesophagus open
    \item \textbf{Nutritional support:} feeding tubes and dietitian care
    \item \textbf{Multidisciplinary care:} a team of surgeons, oncologists, and specialists
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Inability to swallow, leading to poor nutrition and weight loss
    \item Bleeding from the cancer
    \item Spread to the liver, lungs, and lymph nodes
    \item Complications of surgery, including leakage at the join and infection
    \item Effects of chemotherapy and radiotherapy
    \item Pain and reduced quality of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for oesophageal cancer depends on the stage at diagnosis. Cancers found early can be cured, with good survival after surgery or endoscopic removal, but most cases are found late, and overall survival remains modest. Treatment is improving, and a multidisciplinary approach gives the best outcomes. Palliative treatment can effectively manage symptoms and maintain quality of life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Do not smoke, and limit alcohol
    \item Maintain a healthy weight
    \item Manage reflux; people with long-standing reflux should discuss Barrett's screening with their doctor
    \item Eat a diet rich in fruit and vegetables
    \item See a doctor promptly for difficulty swallowing or weight loss
    \item Attend follow-up after treatment
\end{itemize}
