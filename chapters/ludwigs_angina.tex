\chapter{Ludwigs Angina}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Ludwigs Angina is a rapidly progressive, potentially life-threatening cellulitis that involves the submandibular, sublingual, and submental spaces bilaterally. It is a severe, deep neck space infection that characteristically spreads along fascial planes without forming a discrete abscess. The clinical significance of Ludwigs Angina lies in its potential to cause rapid airway obstruction due to the elevation and posterior displacement of the tongue. This condition constitutes a medical emergency that requires immediate airway assessment, high-dose intravenous antibiotics, and potentially urgent surgical decompression to prevent asphyxiation and death.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Although Ludwigs Angina is rare in modern clinical practice due to widespread access to dental care and antibiotics, it remains a highly lethal emergency if unrecognized. Historically, before the advent of antibiotics, the mortality rate exceeded 50\%; today, with prompt diagnosis and intervention, mortality is estimated to be less than 5\%. The condition occurs most frequently in young adults aged between 20 and 40, with a higher prevalence in males. The vast majority of cases (approximately 70\% to 90\%) originate from an odontogenic source, particularly in individuals with poor dental hygiene, untreated dental caries, or recent dental extractions.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Ludwigs Angina is primarily a polymicrobial infection that originates from the oral cavity. The typical aetiology and physiological progression include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Odontogenic source:} the infection most commonly arises from the second or third mandibular molars. The roots of these teeth extend below the attachment of the mylohyoid muscle to the mandible, allowing dental infections to breach the thin inner cortex of the bone and spread directly into the submandibular space.
    \item \textbf{Polymicrobial flora:} the infection is typically caused by a mixture of aerobic and anaerobic organisms native to the mouth, including viridans streptococci, \textit{Staphylococcus aureus}, \textit{Peptostreptococcus} species, \textit{Bacteroides} species, and \textit{Fusobacterium nucleatum}.
    \item \textbf{Fascial plane spread:} the infection spreads rapidly through the loose connective tissue of the submandibular, sublingual, and submental spaces. It respects no anatomical barriers and is characterized by gangrenous cellulitis rather than focal abscess formation.
    \item \textbf{Airway compromise mechanism:} as tissue oedema increases, the mylohyoid muscle limits downward expansion. Consequently, the swelling forces the floor of the mouth and the tongue upwards and backwards. This posterior displacement of the tongue compresses the epiglottis against the posterior pharyngeal wall, leading to complete upper airway obstruction.
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} predisposing factors include dental caries, periodontal disease, dental extractions, oral trauma, sialadenitis of the submandibular gland, mandibular fractures, and immunocompromised states such as diabetes mellitus, HIV/AIDS, or chemotherapy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms of Ludwigs Angina develop rapidly, often over a matter of hours, and include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bilateral neck swelling:} a rapidly expanding, painful swelling under the jaw (submandibular region) that feels extremely hard, tense, and non-fluctuant on palpation, often described as a "woody" induration.
    \textbf{Intraoral signs:} elevation and protrusion of the tongue, swelling and redness of the floor of the mouth, drooling due to an inability to swallow saliva (dysphagia), and trismus (severe difficulty opening the mouth).
    \item \textbf{Systemic signs of sepsis:} high fever, chills, shivering, rapid heart rate (tachycardia), rapid breathing (tachypnoea), and intense anxiety or restlessness.
    \item \textbf{Airway distress features:} a muffled "hot potato" voice, stridor (a high-pitched, noisy breathing sound indicating upper airway obstruction), cyanosis, and the patient adopting a "tripod position" (sitting upright, leaning forward, with the jaw jutting out to keep the airway open).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Clinicians must distinguish Ludwigs Angina from other acute conditions causing neck swelling or airway distress:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Peritonsillar abscess (Quinsy):} presents with unilateral throat pain, deviation of the uvula to the opposite side, and tonsillar swelling, rather than bilateral submandibular induration.
    \item \textbf{Retropharyngeal abscess:} more common in children, presenting with neck stiffness, pain on movement, and posterior pharyngeal wall bulging, best visualised on lateral neck X-ray or CT.
    \item \textbf{Submandibular sialadenitis:} localised inflammation or infection of the submandibular salivary gland, typically unilateral and associated with salivary duct stones, lacking the rapid bilateral spread and tongue elevation.
    \item \textbf{Angioedema:} an allergic reaction causing rapid swelling of the face, lips, and tongue, but typically lacks high fever, dental pain, and the hard, "woody" texture of the neck tissues.
    \item \textbf{Parapharyngeal abscess:} a deep neck space infection presenting with severe unilateral throat pain and swelling, medial displacement of the lateral pharyngeal wall, and trismus.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any individual who develops swelling under the jaw, severe dental pain, or swelling in the mouth should seek immediate medical or dental attention.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, shortness of breath, gasping, or noisy breathing (stridor)
    \item Inability to swallow saliva, leading to continuous drooling
    \item Swelling of the tongue or difficulty opening the mouth
    \item Rapidly expanding swelling under the jaw or in the front of the neck
    \item High fever accompanied by confusion, severe lethargy, or rapid heart rate
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of Ludwigs Angina is primarily clinical. Diagnostic investigations must never delay the immediate securing of a compromised airway:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical airway assessment:} immediate evaluation of the patient's breathing, vocal quality, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation.
    \item \textbf{Contrast-enhanced CT of the neck:} the gold standard imaging modality once the airway is secured. It delineates the extent of the infection, confirms bilateral involvement of the submandibular and sublingual spaces, identifies any gas pockets, and rules out other deep neck space abscesses.
    \item \textbf{Lateral neck X-ray:} can be performed rapidly at the bedside to show soft tissue swelling in the submandibular space and narrowing of the upper airway.
    \item \textbf{Microbiological culture:} Gram stain and cultures (aerobic and anaerobic) of fluid or tissue obtained during surgical decompression, which guide definitive antibiotic therapy.
    \item \textbf{Blood investigations:} full blood count (revealing marked neutrophilic leucocytosis), blood cultures (to rule out systemic bacteraemia), and inflammatory markers (CRP).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of Ludwigs Angina requires a coordinated approach involving emergency physicians, anaesthetists, ENT surgeons, and maxillofacial surgeons:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Airway management:} the absolute priority. If the airway is compromised, early airway security is established, ideally via awake fiberoptic nasal intubation. Blind oral or nasal intubation is contraindicated due to the risk of airway spasm or rupture. If intubation is impossible due to severe swelling or trismus, an emergency surgical airway (cricothyroidotomy or tracheostomy under local anaesthesia) is performed.
    \item \textbf{Intravenous antibiotic therapy:} broad-spectrum empiric antibiotics must be started immediately. Regimens include high-dose ampicillin-sulbactam, clindamycin, or a combination of ceftriaxone and metronidazole, targeting Gram-positive cocci and oral anaerobes.
    \item \textbf{Surgical decompression:} if the airway is threatened, if there is no clinical improvement within 24 to 48 hours of starting antibiotics, or if CT shows significant fluid collection, urgent surgical intervention is required. This involves bilateral external neck incisions to divide the mylohyoid muscle, release tissue tension, and place surgical drains.
    \item \textbf{Intensive care monitoring:} all patients require admission to an intensive care unit (ICU) for continuous airway monitoring, pulse oximetry, and supportive care.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
If untreated or if intervention is delayed, Ludwigs Angina leads to severe, life-threatening complications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute airway obstruction and asphyxiation (the primary cause of death)
    \item Mediastinitis, resulting from the infection tracking downwards through the danger space or carotid sheath into the chest cavity
    \item Necrotising fasciitis of the neck and chest wall
    \item Sepsis and septic shock, leading to multi-organ failure
    \item Internal jugular vein thrombosis (Lemierre's syndrome)
    \item Carotid artery rupture or erosion
    \item Aspiration pneumonia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt airway management, modern intravenous antibiotics, and timely surgical decompression when indicated, the prognosis is excellent, and the mortality rate is less than 5\%. Most patients experience complete resolution of the infection without long-term neck or oral dysfunction. However, if airway securement is delayed, or if the infection spreads to the mediastinum, the prognosis declines precipitously, and the risk of mortality and severe morbidity increases significantly.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventing Ludwigs Angina relies on maintaining good oral health and seeking timely treatment for dental infections:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain excellent oral hygiene by brushing teeth twice daily with fluoride toothpaste and flossing daily
    \item Visit a dentist regularly (at least once or twice a year) for routine examinations, cleaning, and early detection of dental decay
    \item Seek immediate dental evaluation and treatment for any toothache, gum swelling, facial pain, or suspected dental abscess
    \item Complete the full course of any antibiotics prescribed by a doctor or dentist for a mouth or dental infection, even if symptoms improve
    \item Avoid delaying dental procedures, such as root canal therapy or extractions, when recommended by a dental professional
\end{itemize}
