\chapter{Ankylosing Spondylitis}
\index{Ankylosing Spondylitis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Ankylosing spondylitis is a long-term inflammatory condition that mainly affects the spine and the sacroiliac joints, where the spine meets the pelvis. It is a form of inflammatory arthritis belonging to the family of diseases known as spondyloarthritis. The inflammation produces pain and stiffness in the back, and over time it can stimulate the formation of new bone on the spine, so that vertebrae may eventually fuse together -- the process known as ankylosis.

The condition can also affect other joints, tendons, and structures outside the spine, including the hips, shoulders, heels, and eyes. There is no cure, but modern treatment controls symptoms, slows progression, and preserves mobility and quality of life, and the outlook has improved substantially in recent decades.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Ankylosing spondylitis affects roughly 1 in 200 to 1 in 500 people in most populations, with variation between ethnic groups. It typically begins in late adolescence or early adulthood, most often between the ages of 20 and 40. It was historically thought to be much more common in men than in women, but the true sex ratio is now believed to be closer, with women more likely to be under-diagnosed because their presentation can be less typical.

The condition is strongly associated with the genetic marker HLA-B27, which is present in the large majority of affected people. A family history of ankylosing spondylitis or of related conditions, such as psoriatic arthritis or inflammatory bowel disease, increases the risk.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The exact cause is not fully understood, but the disease appears to result from a combination of genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HLA-B27:} the strongest known genetic risk factor, although most people who carry the gene never develop the condition
    \item \textbf{Other genes:} several additional genes contribute to susceptibility and disease severity
    \item \textbf{Environmental triggers:} some infections may trigger or flare the disease in susceptible individuals
    \item \textbf{Immune activation:} inflammation is driven by immune signalling molecules (cytokines) such as tumour necrosis factor (TNF) and interleukins such as IL-17
\end{itemize}

In ankylosing spondylitis, the immune system becomes abnormally active at the entheses, the sites where ligaments and tendons attach to bone. Chronic inflammation in these areas causes pain and swelling, and the body responds by laying down new bone. Over many years, this new bone can bridge between vertebrae, fusing the spine and progressively reducing flexibility.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The hallmark is inflammatory back pain, which differs in character from mechanical back pain:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain and stiffness in the lower back and buttocks, worse at night and in the early morning
    \item Stiffness that improves with activity and worsens with rest
    \item Symptoms that develop gradually over weeks or months, usually before the age of 40
    \item Back pain persisting for more than three months
    \item Progressive loss of spinal flexibility
    \item Pain in the hips, shoulders, or heels, where tendons attach to bone (enthesitis)
    \item Fatigue and a general feeling of being unwell
    \item Uveitis: inflammation of the eye causing a painful, red, light-sensitive eye
    \item Less commonly, chest tightness from reduced ribcage expansion, or bowel symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of back pain and stiffness must be considered:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mechanical back pain:} the most common cause of low back pain, from muscle strain or disc problems, and typically eased by rest
    \item \textbf{Other spondyloarthritis:} psoriatic arthritis, reactive arthritis, or arthritis associated with inflammatory bowel disease
    \item \textbf{Rheumatoid arthritis:} usually affects the hands and wrists rather than the spine
    \item \textbf{Diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH):} spinal stiffness from new bone formation without significant inflammation
    \item \textbf{Sacroiliac joint infection or sacral fracture:} rare causes of buttock and low back pain
    \item \textbf{Fibromyalgia:} widespread pain and fatigue without joint inflammation
    \item \textbf{Tuberculosis of the spine:} a rare but serious cause of back pain in some regions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if you have back pain and stiffness that is worse in the morning or at night, improves with exercise, has lasted more than three months, or began before the age of 40. Early referral to a rheumatologist improves outcomes.

\textbf{Seek urgent care for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A painful, red, or light-sensitive eye (possible uveitis)
    \item Sudden, severe back pain, especially with a fever
    \item Numbness, weakness, or loss of bladder or bowel control (possible spinal cord compression)
    \item Unintentional weight loss or other concerning systemic symptoms
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden loss of movement or feeling in the legs
    \item Loss of bladder or bowel control
    \item Sudden severe pain after a fall or minor injury, which may indicate a spinal fracture
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is based on the pattern of symptoms, examination findings, and supporting tests:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the pattern and timing of the back pain is the most important diagnostic clue
    \item \textbf{Examination:} assessment of spinal flexibility, tenderness over the sacroiliac joints, and chest expansion
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} inflammatory markers (ESR and CRP) are often, but not always, raised
    \item \textbf{HLA-B27 testing:} present in most affected people, but not diagnostic on its own
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} may show changes to the sacroiliac joints and spine, although these changes take years to develop
    \item \textbf{MRI:} can demonstrate inflammation early, before X-ray changes appear
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a rheumatologist to confirm the diagnosis and plan treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment aims to relieve pain and stiffness, maintain mobility, and prevent progression:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Exercise:} the cornerstone of treatment; regular daily exercise, including stretching, spinal mobility work, and breathing exercises
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} a tailored programme to improve posture, strength, and flexibility
    \item \textbf{NSAIDs:} non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are often the first medicines used to control pain and inflammation
    \item \textbf{Biological therapies:} TNF inhibitors and IL-17 inhibitors, given by injection or infusion, are highly effective in moderate-to-severe disease
    \item \textbf{Other disease-modifying drugs:} occasionally used, although they are less effective for spinal symptoms
    \item \textbf{Posture and lifestyle:} attention to posture, sleeping on a firm mattress, and regular standing breaks
    \item \textbf{Joint care:} management of hip, shoulder, and heel involvement, including injections where appropriate
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} rarely needed, but hip replacement, or spinal surgery in severe cases, can restore function
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spinal fusion:} progressive stiffness and loss of mobility, especially in severe or untreated disease
    \item \textbf{Spinal fractures:} the fused, brittle spine breaks more easily, even after minor trauma
    \item \textbf{Uveitis:} recurrent eye inflammation that can threaten sight if untreated
    \item \textbf{Osteoporosis:} reduced bone density, increasing the risk of fracture
    \item \textbf{Cauda equina syndrome:} rare compression of the spinal nerves causing numbness, weakness, and bladder or bowel problems
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular disease:} a higher risk of heart disease and aortic inflammation (aortitis)
    \item \textbf{Lung problems:} restricted chest expansion can reduce lung function
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The course of ankylosing spondylitis varies greatly between individuals. Many people experience intermittent flares interspersed with periods of relative quiet, and with modern treatment most remain active and employed. A significant minority develop substantial stiffness over decades, and a small number become severely disabled.

Outcomes are generally better with early diagnosis, regular exercise, and effective treatment with anti-inflammatory or biological medicines. The condition does not usually shorten life expectancy substantially, although there is a modestly increased risk of cardiovascular disease, which is why management of other risk factors such as smoking and blood pressure is important.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Exercise every day, including spinal stretches, deep breathing, and posture work
    \item Maintain good posture when sitting, standing, and sleeping, and choose a supportive mattress
    \item Take regular breaks from sitting, and stand or stretch throughout the day
    \item Take prescribed medication as directed and attend rheumatology follow-up appointments
    \item Quit smoking, since smoking worsens the disease and accelerates spinal damage
    \item Watch for symptoms of uveitis (a red, painful, light-sensitive eye) and seek treatment promptly
    \item Protect bone health with adequate calcium, vitamin D, and weight-bearing exercise
    \item Manage your weight and stay active to reduce strain on joints and the heart
\end{itemize}
