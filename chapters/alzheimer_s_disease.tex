\chapter{Alzheimer's Disease}
\index{Alzheimer's Disease}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Alzheimer's disease is the most common cause of dementia, a progressive neurodegenerative disorder in which brain cells are gradually damaged and die. It typically begins with subtle difficulty remembering recent events and, over the course of years, spreads to affect language, judgement, orientation, and the ability to carry out everyday activities. The disease is named after Alois Alzheimer, the German psychiatrist who first described its characteristic brain changes in 1906. Alzheimer's disease is not a normal part of ageing, although increasing age is its strongest risk factor. There is currently no cure, but medications, structured support, and lifestyle interventions can improve or stabilise symptoms for a time, and disease-modifying antibody therapies are now available in some countries for people with early disease.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Alzheimer's disease is strongly age-related: it is uncommon before age 65 and becomes progressively more common with each decade thereafter. Roughly one in fourteen people over the age of 65 and about one in six people over the age of 80 are affected. Worldwide, tens of millions of people live with dementia, and Alzheimer's disease accounts for approximately two-thirds of all cases. Women are affected more often than men, in part because they tend to live longer, though some studies suggest a genuine sex difference in risk. The global burden is expected to rise substantially as populations age, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Alzheimer's disease results from a combination of age-related changes, genetic susceptibility, and lifestyle and environmental factors:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Abnormal proteins:} extracellular clumps of amyloid-beta peptide form \emph{plaques}, and twisted threads of hyperphosphorylated tau protein form \emph{tangles} within neurones; both damage and eventually kill nerve cells.
    \item \textbf{Cell loss and synaptic failure:} neurones shrink and die, and connections between them are lost, beginning in the hippocampus and other memory-related regions and spreading through the cortex.
    \item \textbf{Age:} increasing age is the single most important risk factor.
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} a family history raises risk; rare inherited mutations cause early-onset disease, and the \emph{APOE4} allele of the apolipoprotein E gene substantially increases risk in the common late-onset form.
    \item \textbf{Vascular factors:} high blood pressure, diabetes, smoking, and high cholesterol increase risk, consistent with the overlap between vascular and degenerative disease.
    \item \textbf{Other factors:} previous head injury, hearing loss, depression, and low levels of education or cognitive engagement are associated with higher risk.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The earliest changes are often mistaken for normal ageing:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Forgetting recent events, appointments, or conversations while retaining distant memories.
    \item Repeatedly losing items and placing them in unusual locations.
    \item Difficulty finding words, following a conversation, or understanding new information.
    \item Getting lost in familiar places and confusion about time and dates.
    \item Trouble with planning, problem-solving, and handling money.
    \item Mood changes, apathy, irritability, anxiety, or withdrawal from social activities.
    \item In later stages: difficulty with dressing, feeding, continence, and mobility, together with behavioural change.
\end{itemize}

The pattern of gradual, progressive decline distinguishes Alzheimer's disease from the sudden onset of vascular dementia and the fluctuations seen in dementia with Lewy bodies.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of memory and thinking problems must be excluded, because several are reversible or treatable:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Vascular dementia, from stroke or small vessel disease.
    \item Dementia with Lewy bodies, with fluctuating cognition and visual hallucinations.
    \item Frontotemporal dementia, with prominent personality and language change.
    \item Mild cognitive impairment, which is less disabling and does not always progress.
    \item Depression, anxiety, or the effects of medication.
    \item Delirium, a sudden confused state often due to infection, dehydration, or metabolic upset.
    \item Thyroid disease, vitamin B12 deficiency, and alcohol-related brain damage.
    \item Normal pressure hydrocephalus, which is occasionally treatable.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if you or a family member notice persistent memory or thinking problems that interfere with daily life, because early assessment can identify treatable causes, allow time for planning, and, in suitable cases, enable access to newer treatments.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden confusion or a sudden change in behaviour, which may indicate delirium or stroke.
    \item Sudden weakness, numbness, or difficulty speaking.
    \item Fainting, a fall with head injury, or loss of consciousness.
    \item Any rapid worsening of mental state that causes concern.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
There is no single test for Alzheimer's disease; the diagnosis is made clinically, with investigations used to exclude other causes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} taken from the person and, importantly, from a family member or carer, covering onset, progression, and impact on daily life.
    \item \textbf{Cognitive testing:} screening tools such as the Mini-Mental State Examination or Montreal Cognitive Assessment, with more detailed neuropsychological testing in complex cases.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} thyroid function, vitamin B12, full blood count, and other tests for reversible causes.
    \item \textbf{Brain imaging:} CT or MRI to detect strokes, atrophy typical of Alzheimer's, or other structural causes.
    \item \textbf{Specialist assessment:} review in a memory clinic by a geriatrician, neurologist, or old-age psychiatrist.
    \item \textbf{Advanced tests:} amyloid PET imaging or cerebrospinal fluid analysis in specialist or research settings, and increasingly to select people for disease-modifying treatment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management focuses on treating symptoms, supporting the person and their carers, and planning for the future:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Medication:} cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) and memantine may improve or stabilise cognitive symptoms for a time.
    \item \textbf{Disease-modifying therapies:} amyloid-targeting antibodies such as lecanemab and donanemab are licensed in some countries for people with confirmed early disease.
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} cognitive stimulation, structured activities, and reminiscence work.
    \item \textbf{Occupational therapy:} practical help with daily activities, home safety, and orientation aids.
    \item \textbf{Carer support:} education, respite care, and carer support groups.
    \item \textbf{Managing coexisting conditions:} treating depression, hearing loss, and vascular risk factors.
    \item \textbf{Behavioural symptoms:} non-drug approaches are first-line; medication is used only when needed and closely monitored.
    \item \textbf{Planning:} advance care planning and early attention to legal and financial arrangements.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
As the disease advances, complications become increasingly common:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Falls and fractures due to impaired balance and judgement.
    \item Pneumonia and other infections, including aspiration pneumonia.
    \item Malnutrition, dehydration, and weight loss.
    \item Urinary incontinence and recurrent urinary tract infections.
    \item Wandering and getting lost.
    \item Agitation, aggression, and hallucinations.
    \item Pressure sores in people who become immobile.
    \item Carer stress, exhaustion, and burnout.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Alzheimer's disease progresses gradually, and the average time from diagnosis to death is around eight to ten years, although this varies widely from a few years to more than twenty. The rate of decline differs greatly between individuals, and most people eventually require full-time care. Good support, treatment of coexisting conditions, and early planning can improve quality of life for both the person and their family. Participation in research, including trials of new treatments, offers hope for the future.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
There is no guaranteed way to prevent Alzheimer's disease, but lifestyle measures may reduce risk and are beneficial for brain and heart health:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep the mind active with reading, puzzles, learning, and new skills.
    \item Stay socially connected and physically active.
    \item Eat a balanced diet, such as a Mediterranean-style diet.
    \item Control blood pressure, blood sugar, and cholesterol.
    \item Protect the head by wearing helmets for cycling and other sports.
    \item Avoid smoking and limit alcohol.
    \item Treat hearing loss early and maintain good sleep habits.
    \item If diagnosed, stay engaged in meaningful activities and plan ahead with family and clinicians.
\end{itemize}
