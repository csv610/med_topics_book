\chapter{Adolescent Mental Health}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Adolescence is a period of profound physical, emotional, and social change, and it is also a peak time for the first emergence of mental health difficulties. The term \textit{adolescent mental health} encompasses the full spectrum of emotional and behavioural wellbeing in young people aged approximately 10 to 19 years, from everyday stress and worry to diagnosable conditions such as anxiety disorders, depression, eating disorders, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, and psychosis.

Mental health in this age group matters not only because of the immediate suffering it causes but because many adult psychiatric disorders begin in adolescence. Early recognition, supportive family environments, and timely access to care substantially improve outcomes, whereas untreated difficulties tend to persist and accumulate over the life course. Promoting adolescent mental health therefore includes both treating established problems and building resilience, coping skills, and healthy relationships that protect against future illness.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Around 1 in 7 young people aged 10 to 19 years experiences a mental health condition, making this one of the most common health problems of adolescence. Anxiety disorders and depression are the most frequent diagnoses, and eating disorders, ADHD, conduct problems, and self-harm are also common. Worldwide, mental health conditions account for a large share of the disease burden in this age group, and suicide is one of the leading causes of death among adolescents.

Half of all lifetime mental health conditions begin by age 14, and three-quarters by the mid-20s. Several groups carry a higher burden, including young people who experience bullying, family conflict, abuse, or chronic physical illness, those with a family history of mental illness, and those who identify as LGBTQ+. Notably, many adolescents who need help do not receive it, often because of stigma, limited awareness of symptoms, long waiting times, or uncertainty about how to access services.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Mental health difficulties in adolescence arise from a complex interaction of biological, psychological, and social factors rather than any single cause.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Brain development:} the limbic system and emotional centres mature earlier than the prefrontal cortex, which governs judgement, impulse control, and planning; this mismatch contributes to intense emotions and risk-taking
    \item \textbf{Hormones:} the hormonal changes of puberty influence mood, sleep, appetite, and sensitivity to stress
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} a family history of depression, anxiety, or other mental illness increases susceptibility
    \item \textbf{Life events:} bullying, academic pressure, family conflict, parental separation, relationship break-ups, and bereavement are common triggers
    \item \textbf{Social media:} heavy use, cyberbullying, disrupted sleep from screens, and constant social comparison can worsen mood and self-esteem
    \item \textbf{Sleep:} irregular or insufficient sleep is strongly linked to anxiety, depression, and irritability
    \item \textbf{Substance use:} alcohol and other drugs can trigger, mask, or worsen mental health problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Warning signs are often subtle and appear as changes in behaviour, mood, or functioning rather than as classic complaints.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent sadness, irritability, or low mood lasting more than two weeks
    \item Loss of interest in previously enjoyed activities and withdrawal from family or friends
    \item Falling school grades, refusal to attend school, or conflict with teachers
    \item Significant changes in sleep (insomnia or sleeping excessively) or appetite
    \item Frequent physical symptoms such as headaches and abdominal pain without an obvious cause
    \item Excessive worry, panic attacks, or avoidance of social situations
    \item Self-harm, such as cutting or burning
    \item Comments about hopelessness, worthlessness, or not wanting to be alive
    \item Risky or reckless behaviour, including substance use
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Distress in adolescence has multiple possible explanations, and careful assessment is needed to distinguish between them.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Normal adolescent moodiness, which is short-lived, milder, and linked to clear events
    \item Adjustment reactions to a specific stressor, such as an exam or a break-up
    \item Physical illness, including anaemia, thyroid disorders, glandular fever, or chronic disease
    \item Substance use and its effects on mood and behaviour
    \item Eating disorders, which can present with low mood, irritability, and social withdrawal
    \item Neurodevelopmental conditions such as ADHD and autism spectrum disorder
    \item Early psychosis, which may begin with withdrawal, decline in functioning, and unusual beliefs or experiences
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
A general practitioner should be consulted when changes in mood or behaviour last more than two weeks, are severe, or interfere with school, family life, or friendships. The GP can assess the situation, offer initial support, and refer to a psychologist, psychiatrist, or specialist youth service. In many regions, free or low-cost youth-specific services such as headspace provide accessible early help.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Any immediate threat of self-harm or suicide
    \item Any statement or suggestion that the young person intends to end their life
    \item Dangerous, erratic, or threatening behaviour
    \item Signs of psychosis that place the young person or others at risk
\end{itemize}
Do not leave the young person alone while arranging urgent help. Crisis lines, including Lifeline (13 11 14) and the Kids Helpline (1800 55 1800) in Australia, offer immediate telephone support.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment is primarily conversational and collaborative, conducted with the young person, often together with parents or caregivers.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical interview:} with the young person alone and with the family, covering mood, anxiety, sleep, appetite, school performance, friendships, substance use, and risk
    \item \textbf{Standardised questionnaires:} validated tools such as the PHQ-A for depression and the GAD-7 for anxiety help gauge severity and track progress
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} a direct, non-judgmental discussion about self-harm and suicidal thoughts is essential in every assessment
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} performed to look for signs of medical illness, self-harm, or nutritional compromise
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, iron studies, thyroid function, and sometimes vitamin D or inflammatory markers to exclude physical causes of low mood and fatigue
    \item \textbf{Collateral information:} input from parents and teachers provides a fuller picture of functioning across settings
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Most young people improve with a combination of psychoeducation, psychological therapy, family support, and, in moderate to severe cases, medication.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychoeducation:} helping the young person and family understand the condition and its treatment
    \item \textbf{Psychological therapy:} cognitive behavioural therapy and interpersonal therapy are the first-line treatments for anxiety and depression in this age group
    \item \textbf{Family involvement:} family therapy or structured parenting support where conflict, family stress, or parental mental illness is contributing
    \item \textbf{School support:} collaboration with teachers to provide adjustments, reduce pressure, and maintain engagement in education
    \item \textbf{Medication:} selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors may be prescribed, usually by a specialist, for moderate to severe depression or anxiety, with careful monitoring for emerging suicidality in the early weeks
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} regular physical activity, consistent sleep routines, and agreed limits on social media and screen time
    \item \textbf{Crisis care:} community acute teams, intensive home-based support, or hospital admission when there is significant risk of harm
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Self-harm and suicide -- the most serious and urgent complications
    \item Substance misuse, sometimes adopted as a coping strategy
    \item School dropout, unemployment, and reduced long-term life opportunities
    \item Relationship breakdown, peer rejection, and social isolation
    \item Persistence or recurrence of mental illness into adulthood
    \item Physical health consequences of poor sleep, poor diet, and inactivity
    \item Stigma and its effects on self-esteem and help-seeking
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most adolescents with mental health difficulties improve substantially with appropriate support, and many recover fully. Early intervention produces the best outcomes, because untreated problems in adolescence tend to persist and recur. Relapses can occur, particularly during stressful transitions such as exams or the move to independent living, so ongoing monitoring and a reliable support network are important. Open, non-judgmental family support and strong peer relationships are among the most powerful protective factors for long-term wellbeing.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Communication:} keep conversations open, and listen without judging or rushing to solve problems
    \item \textbf{Sleep:} encourage regular bedtimes, adequate sleep, and screen-free periods before bed
    \item \textbf{Activity:} promote daily physical activity and time outdoors
    \item \textbf{Screen limits:} set sensible and consistent boundaries around social media use
    \item \textbf{Coping skills:} teach and model healthy ways of managing stress, disappointment, and conflict
    \item \textbf{Connections:} maintain strong family ties and friendships, and involve trusted adults
    \item \textbf{Early help:} see a GP early, before problems become entrenched
    \item \textbf{Vigilance:} take any talk of hopelessness or self-harm seriously, and recognise early warning signs of relapse in mood or behaviour
\end{itemize}
