\chapter{Lung cancer screening}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Lung cancer screening is a secondary prevention strategy aimed at detecting lung cancer in asymptomatic, high-risk individuals at an early, localized stage when curative treatment is most feasible and effective. The primary screening modality is low-dose computed tomography (LDCT), which utilizes specialized scanning techniques to obtain high-resolution, three-dimensional images of the thorax at a significantly lower radiation dose than standard chest CT. Screening targets individuals with a substantial history of tobacco smoking, as they bear the highest risk of developing the disease. The clinical objective is to identify small, malignant pulmonary nodules before they metastasise or cause symptoms, thereby reducing lung cancer-specific mortality.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Lung cancer remains the leading cause of oncology-related mortality worldwide, primarily because the vast majority of cases are diagnosed at an advanced, symptomatic stage when cure is no longer possible. Major international clinical trials, including the National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) and the European NELSON trial, demonstrated that screening high-risk individuals with LDCT reduces lung cancer-specific mortality by 20\% to 26\% compared to chest X-rays or no screening. In response to this robust evidence, Australia has scheduled the implementation of the National Lung Cancer Screening Program (NLCSP) to commence in July 2025. This government-funded public health initiative is projected to screen thousands of eligible Australians annually, shifting the stage distribution of diagnoses toward early-stage, treatable disease and saving lives.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The physiological and pathological basis of lung cancer screening lies in the relationship between chronic carcinogen exposure, tumour growth kinetics, and early detection.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High-risk cohort targeting:} Screening is restricted to cohorts with significant tobacco exposure, measured in pack-years, which correlates directly with somatic mutation burden and neoplastic risk in respiratory epithelial cells.
    \item \textbf{Detection of pre-invasive lesions:} LDCT is highly sensitive at detecting early malignant changes, such as atypical adenomatous hyperplasia or adenocarcinoma in situ, prior to stromal invasion.
    \item \textbf{Asymptomatic growth phase:} Early-stage lung cancers typically grow slowly over several years. During this subclinical phase, they are radiologically detectable but do not cause cough, pain, or systemic symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Linage-specific detection:} Screening is highly effective at identifying peripheral adenocarcinomas and early squamous cell carcinomas. It is less effective at reducing mortality in small cell lung cancer due to its extremely rapid doubling time and early systemic spread.
    \item \textbf{Limitation in low-risk populations:} In individuals without a history of smoking, the incidence of lung cancer is low, meaning the harms of screening (such as radiation exposure and false positives) outweigh the diagnostic benefits.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
In the context of a screening program, clinical features refer to the defined eligibility criteria and the characteristics of screen-detected pulmonary nodules.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Age criteria:} Typically targets asymptomatic adults aged 50 to 70 or 74 years, who are within the age range of peak lung cancer incidence.
    \item \textbf{Tobacco exposure threshold:} Individuals with a cumulative smoking history of 30 pack-years or more (e.g.\ smoking one pack of 20 cigarettes per day for 30 years, or two packs per day for 15 years).
    \item \textbf{Cessation timeframe:} Current smokers or former smokers who have ceased tobacco use within the past 10 to 15 years.
    \item \textbf{Strictly asymptomatic status:} Screening is only indicated for individuals without active respiratory symptoms. The presence of symptoms (such as haemoptysis or chronic cough) requires diagnostic imaging, not screening.
    \item \textbf{Nodule characteristics:} Screen-detected nodules are characterized by their size, density (solid, part-solid, or non-solid/ground-glass), margins (spiculated vs.\ smooth), and growth rate.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A major challenge of lung cancer screening is the high prevalence of non-malignant pulmonary nodules, which must be carefully distinguished from malignant lesions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infectious granulomas:} Calcified or non-calcified nodules resulting from past infections, such as tuberculosis or histoplasmosis, which are common in specific geographical areas.
    \item \textbf{Active localized infections:} Subclinical pneumonia, atypical mycobacterial infections, or lung abscesses that present radiologically as focal lung nodules.
    \item \textbf{Pulmonary hamartomas:} Common benign neoplasms composed of a mixture of cartilage, fat, and fibrous tissue, often showing characteristic "popcorn-like" calcification.
    \item \textbf{Intrapulmonary lymph nodes:} Small, benign lymph nodes located within the lung parenchyma, typically near the pleura or bronchovascular bundles.
    \item \textbf{Vascular anomalies:} Arteriovenous malformations or small pulmonary infarcts that mimic the appearance of a solid nodule on LDCT.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Individuals who meet the age and smoking history criteria should schedule a consultation with their general practitioner to discuss eligibility for the national screening program.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Haemoptysis (coughing up blood or blood-streaked sputum).
    \item Sudden, severe shortness of breath or acute difficulty breathing.
    \item Severe, sharp, or crushing chest pain.
    \item Signs of a pneumothorax (collapsed lung) following an invasive diagnostic procedure, including sudden-onset pleuritic chest pain and rapidly worsening breathlessness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The screening pathway involves initial imaging followed by structured risk assessment and diagnostic follow-up for suspicious findings.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low-dose computed tomography (LDCT):} The core screening test, performed annually or biennially, which does not require intravenous contrast media.
    \item \textbf{Structured nodule classification (e.g.\ Lung-RADS):} A standardized quality assurance tool used to classify LDCT findings from Category 1 (negative) to Category 4 (highly suspicious) based on nodule size and type.
    \item \textbf{High-resolution CT (HRCT):} Performed as a diagnostic follow-up to evaluate nodules with suspicious features or growth.
    \item \textbf{Positron Emission Tomography (PET-CT):} Indicated for nodules larger than 8 mm with intermediate-to-high risk features to assess metabolic activity (fluorodeoxyglucose avidity).
    \item \textbf{Tissue biopsy:} Performed via EBUS-guided bronchoscopy or CT-guided transthoracic needle biopsy to obtain a histological diagnosis if a nodule is classified as highly suspicious.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of screening outcomes ranges from routine rescreening to active diagnostic intervention and surgical management.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Routine surveillance:} Annual rescreening for patients with negative scans or benign-appearing stable nodules.
    \item \textbf{Short-interval follow-up imaging:} Repeat LDCT in 3 to 6 months to monitor for growth in nodules with intermediate-risk features.
    \item \textbf{Surgical resection:} Early lobectomy or sublobar resection (segmentectomy or wedge resection) for screen-detected nodules confirmed to be malignant.
    \item \textbf{Integrated smoking cessation support:} A mandatory, structured component of any lung cancer screening program, providing counseling and pharmacotherapy (e.g.\ nicotine replacement therapy, varenicline) to help active smokers quit.
    \item \textbf{Multidisciplinary team (MDT) review:} Collaborative management of complex or high-risk nodules by chest radiologists, respiratory physicians, and thoracic surgeons.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{False positive results:} Up to 20\% to 30\% of initial screens detect benign nodules that require follow-up, causing significant patient anxiety and leading to unnecessary imaging or invasive procedures.
    \item \textbf{Overdiagnosis:} The detection of slow-growing, indolent malignancies that would not have progressed or caused death during the patient's natural lifetime, leading to unnecessary treatment.
    \item \textbf{Radiation exposure:} Although LDCT uses low doses, cumulative radiation from decades of annual screening poses a small theoretical risk of inducing secondary cancers.
    \item \textbf{Biopsy-related complications:} Pneumothorax (requiring a chest tube in minor cases) or pulmonary haemorrhage resulting from transthoracic needle biopsy of what may ultimately be a benign nodule.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The implementation of structured lung cancer screening dramatically shifts the prognostic outlook for high-risk populations. While symptomatic lung cancer is typically diagnosed at stage III or IV (with 5-year survival rates below 10\%), screen-detected lung cancers are diagnosed at stage I in approximately 70\% of cases. For these patients, the 5-year survival rate exceeds 80\% to 90\% following localized treatment. Overall, adherence to an annual screening program translates to a substantial reduction in the risk of dying from lung cancer for eligible current and former smokers.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prioritize smoking cessation:} Screening should never be viewed as a substitute for quitting smoking; cessation remains the most effective way to lower lung cancer risk.
    \item \textbf{Adhere to screening schedules:} Commit to attending scheduled annual or biennial screening appointments to ensure early detection is not missed.
    \item \textbf{Engage in shared decision-making:} Discuss the benefits, limitations, and potential harms of screening with a general practitioner before commencing.
    \item \textbf{Report interim symptoms immediately:} Consult a doctor if respiratory symptoms (such as haemoptysis or new shortness of breath) develop between scheduled screen appointments.
\end{itemize}
