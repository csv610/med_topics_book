\section*{Definition and Overview}
Myalgic encephalomyelitis / chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) is a complex, chronic, disabling illness characterised by severe, persistent fatigue that is not relieved by rest and is worsened by physical or mental activity (post-exertional malaise, PEM). It involves multiple body systems, including neurological, immune, autonomic, and energy-metabolism dysfunction. The condition can profoundly affect daily function, work, and quality of life. ME/CFS is a diagnosis of exclusion made on clinical grounds using recognised criteria (e.g., Canadian Consensus Criteria, IOM/NAM 2015 criteria). There is no single diagnostic test, and the cause is not fully understood. Severity ranges from mild (reduced activity) to very severe (house/bed-bound and dependent).

\section*{Epidemiology}
ME/CFS affects people of all ages, including children and adolescents. Estimated prevalence is roughly 0.4--1\% of the population (around 0.5--1\% in Australia, affecting approximately 100,000--250,000 people). It is two to four times more common in women than men. Peak onset is in early-to-mid adulthood (20--40 years), though it can occur at any age, including after infectious mononucleosis in teenagers. Many cases follow an acute infectious illness (e.g., Epstein-Barr virus, Ross River virus, Q fever, other viral infections), but not all. The condition is often under-recognised and under-diagnosed, with significant diagnostic delay. Around 25\% of those affected are severely affected and house-bound or bed-bound.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Triggers:} many cases follow an acute viral or other infection; other triggers include major physical or emotional stress, surgery, or trauma
    \item \textbf{Genetic susceptibility:} some evidence of familial clustering and genetic predisposition
    \item \textbf{Viral hypotheses:} links with EBV, human herpesvirus, enteroviruses, and other pathogens, though no single causative agent is established
    \item \textbf{Immune dysfunction:} chronic immune activation, low-grade inflammation, altered cytokine profiles, and evidence of immune dysregulation
    \item \textbf{Energy metabolism:} impaired cellular energy production (mitochondrial dysfunction), reduced aerobic capacity, and abnormal post-exertional responses
    \item \textbf{Autonomic dysfunction:} orthostatic intolerance, neurally mediated hypotension, postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS)
    \item \textbf{Neuroendocrine changes:} hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis abnormalities, altered sleep architecture
    \item \textbf{Neurological:} cognitive dysfunction (brain fog), pain, and sensory disturbances suggesting central nervous system involvement
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Post-exertional malaise (PEM):} characteristic worsening of symptoms 12--48 hours after physical or mental exertion, lasting for days; the hallmark feature
    \item \textbf{Severe fatigue:} persistent, profound, disabling fatigue not relieved by rest, lasting at least 6 months in adults (per some criteria)
    \item \textbf{Unrefreshing sleep:} unrefreshing or disturbed sleep despite adequate opportunity
    \item \textbf{Cognitive impairment:} difficulty concentrating, poor memory, slowed thinking, "brain fog"
    \item \textbf{Orthostatic intolerance:} dizziness, light-headedness, or fainting on standing (may have POTS)
    \item \textbf{Pain:} muscle pain, joint pain (without swelling/redness), headache, sore throat (in some)
    \item \textbf{Other symptoms:} sensitivity to light, sound, or smells; swollen/tender lymph nodes; temperature intolerance; irritable bowel-type symptoms
    \item \textbf{Symptom variability:} symptoms fluctuate; relapses and remissions are common
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Other causes of chronic fatigue: hypothyroidism, anaemia, diabetes, chronic infection, autoimmune disease (e.g., SLE, Sjögren), sleep disorders (e.g., obstructive sleep apnoea, narcolepsy)
    \item Depression, anxiety, and somatic symptom disorders (though ME/CFS may coexist with mood disturbance)
    \item Chronic pain conditions (fibromyalgia often overlaps)
    \item Cardiovascular/pulmonary conditions causing exercise intolerance (e.g., heart failure, COPD)
    \item Neurological disorders (e.g., multiple sclerosis, myasthenia gravis)
    \item Medication side effects or substance use
    \item Malignancy or other chronic systemic disease presenting with fatigue
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical evaluation for persistent, disabling fatigue (especially lasting more than 6 months) or if fatigue is associated with other concerning symptoms. A GP is the first point of contact for assessment and exclusion of other causes.

\textbf{Call 000 or attend an emergency department for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, severe breathlessness, or palpitations with collapse
    \item Sudden severe weakness, neurological signs, or seizures
    \item Suicidal thoughts or severe deterioration in mental state
    \item Fainting or falls causing injury
    \item Sudden, severe, or worsening symptoms that worry you
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical criteria:} diagnosis based on recognised criteria (Canadian Consensus Criteria or IOM/NAM 2015 criteria), including PEM, fatigue, unrefreshing sleep, and cognitive/orthostatic symptoms
    \item \textbf{Exclusion of other conditions:} a thorough history and examination plus targeted investigations to rule out alternative causes
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} FBC, electrolytes, renal/liver/thyroid function, glucose, B12/folate, inflammatory markers (ESR/CRP), iron studies, coeliac/autoimmune screen, and other tests as indicated
    \item \textbf{Specialist tests:} sleep studies if sleep disorder suspected; cardiology assessment (e.g., tilt table) for orthostatic intolerance/POTS
    \item \textbf{No confirmatory biomarker:} routine tests are typically normal; diagnosis remains clinical
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to ME/CFS specialists or multidisciplinary services (where available) for confirmation and support
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pacing (core intervention):} balance activity and rest to avoid PEM; energy management with planned activity and rest periods; gradual, careful increase only when tolerated
    \item \textbf{Symptom management:} medications for pain, sleep disturbance, and orthostatic intolerance (e.g., for POTS) as individually appropriate; treat coexisting conditions (e.g., depression, anxiety)
    \item \textbf{Avoid harmful advice:} avoid graded exercise therapy (GET) protocols that push through symptoms, and avoid cognitive behavioural therapy framed as "unhelpful beliefs causing illness"; these are no longer recommended
    \item \textbf{Sleep hygiene:} regular sleep-wake routine, relaxation, and fatigue management
    \item \textbf{Nutrition and hydration:} balanced diet and adequate fluid; manage coexisting IBS-type symptoms
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} counselling or CBT to help cope with chronic illness, when used as supportive care (not as a cure for fatigue)
    \item \textbf{Multidisciplinary team:} GP, nurse, physiotherapist, occupational therapist, psychologist, and specialist input as needed
    \item \textbf{Frequent review:} regular follow-up to adjust management and monitor for coexisting conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe disability and inability to work or attend school; in very severe cases, house-bound or bed-bound
    \item Depression, anxiety, and social isolation
    \item Deconditioning and muscle weakness from inactivity
    \item Nutritional compromise and weight changes
    \item Financial and social consequences of chronic illness
    \item Misdiagnosis or inappropriate treatment (e.g., harmful exercise or psychological approaches) delaying recovery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
ME/CFS is a chronic condition with an unpredictable course. Some people improve gradually over months to years, and a minority recover substantially; others have a fluctuating or progressive course, and a significant proportion remain moderately to severely affected. Children and adolescents generally have a better prognosis than adults. Prognosis is influenced by severity at onset, coexisting conditions, and access to appropriate supportive management (especially pacing). There is no cure, and outcomes are improved by early recognition, realistic goal-setting, and multidisciplinary support rather than by any single treatment.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pacing:} learn to balance activity and rest; avoid overexertion that triggers PEM
    \item \textbf{Prioritise sleep:} maintain a consistent sleep-wake schedule and good sleep hygiene
    \item \textbf{Energy conservation:} plan tasks, delegate, and take planned rest breaks
    \item \textbf{Nutrition and hydration:} eat a balanced diet and stay hydrated
    \item \textbf{Symptom diary:} track symptoms to identify triggers and manage flares
    \item \textbf{Support networks:} seek support from family, friends, and ME/CFS patient organisations
    \item \textbf{Regular GP review:} ongoing monitoring and management of coexisting conditions
    \item \textbf{Advocacy:} access reliable information from reputable patient and health organisations
\end{itemize}
