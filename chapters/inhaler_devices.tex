\chapter{Inhaler Devices}
\index{Inhaler Devices}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Inhaler devices deliver medicine directly to the lungs as a fine mist or powder, allowing rapid action with a low dose and fewer side effects than tablets. They are the mainstay of treatment for asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). There are several types: pressurised metered-dose inhalers (puffers), which may be used with a spacer; dry powder inhalers; and soft-mist inhalers. Each device works best with a specific technique, and using it correctly is essential for the medicine to reach the airways. Poor technique is very common and is a major reason treatment fails. Pharmacists, GPs, and respiratory nurses can teach and check inhaler technique regularly.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Asthma affects around one in nine people and COPD a further significant proportion, making inhalers among the most widely used medicines in some countries. Despite their effectiveness, up to two-thirds of people use their inhaler incorrectly in at least one step of the technique. This leads to poor symptom control, more reliever use, more flare-ups, hospitalisations, and higher doses of medicines than necessary. Device choice, training, and regular technique review are therefore central to respiratory care, and national guidelines recommend matching the device to the individual and checking technique at every opportunity.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Inhalers work by depositing aerosolised medicine in the airways. For particles to reach the small airways, they must be inhaled at the right speed and timing, with the breath held afterwards. Pressurised metered-dose inhalers require coordination: the medicine is released as a spray that must be inhaled at the same moment, which is difficult for many people, especially children and older adults; a spacer device overcomes this by holding the spray so it can be inhaled over several breaths. Dry powder inhalers are breath-activated, requiring a fast, deep inhalation, while soft-mist inhalers produce a slow, fine mist. Asthma and COPD cause airway narrowing, inflammation, and mucus, which reliever and preventer medicines aim to reverse.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Common types: pressurised metered-dose inhalers, dry powder inhalers, and soft-mist inhalers
    \item Relievers (blue or grey) relax the airways quickly for symptoms
    \item Preventers (often brown, red, or orange) reduce inflammation when taken regularly
    \item Combination inhalers contain a preventer and a long-acting reliever
    \item Spacers are recommended with metered-dose inhalers, especially for children
    \item Symptoms that continue despite treatment may reflect poor technique or device problems
    \item A dry mouth or hoarse voice can follow inhaled steroid use and rinsing prevents it
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When symptoms are poorly controlled, the cause may be more than the device.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Incorrect inhaler technique:} the most common cause of poor response
    \item \textbf{Insufficient dose or frequency:} undertreatment
    \item \textbf{Non-adherence:} not taking the preventer regularly
    \item \textbf{Wrong device for the person:} poor fit with age or ability
    \item \textbf{Triggers continuing:} allergens, smoke, or occupational exposures
    \item \textbf{Worsening disease:} requiring review of the treatment plan
    \item \textbf{Other conditions:} vocal cord dysfunction, reflux, or heart disease mimicking respiratory symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone using an inhaler should have their technique checked regularly and should see a doctor if symptoms are not well controlled, if reliever use increases, or if they have an asthma action plan that is not working. Inhaler technique should be reviewed when treatment is changed, and at least yearly. People with new or worsening respiratory symptoms need assessment.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe breathlessness, inability to speak in full sentences, or blue lips
    \item A severe asthma attack not responding to the reliever
    \item Chest pain, collapse, or confusion
    \item A child who is struggling to breathe or very drowsy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Technique check:} observation of the person using the device against a step-by-step checklist
    \item \textbf{Spirometry:} breathing tests to assess airflow and response to reliever
    \item \textbf{Peak flow monitoring:} home measurement to track control
    \item \textbf{Review of adherence:} how often the preventer is actually taken
    \item \textbf{Device review:} checking that the inhaler is not empty or blocked
    \item \textbf{Assessment of symptoms:} reliever use, night waking, and activity limitation
    \item \textbf{Individualised asthma action plan:} updated with the person and their doctor
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Choice of device:} matched to age, ability, and preference, with the least steps for the individual
    \item \textbf{Metered-dose inhaler technique:} shake, breathe out, place mouthpiece, press and inhale slowly, and hold the breath
    \item \textbf{Spacer use:} with metered-dose inhalers, especially in children, with single puffs inhaled separately
    \item \textbf{Dry powder technique:} load, breathe out away from the device, inhale forcefully and deeply
    \item \textbf{Rinsing:} after inhaled steroid use to prevent oral thrush and hoarseness
    \item \textbf{Regular technique training:} by pharmacists, GPs, or respiratory nurses
    \item \textbf{Review and adjustment:} of medicines according to the written action plan
    \item \textbf{Good inhaler hygiene:} cleaning spacers and devices as recommended
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Poor symptom control and more frequent flare-ups from incorrect technique
    \item Overuse of relievers, which can mask worsening disease
    \item Higher medicine doses than necessary, increasing side effects
    \item Oral thrush and hoarseness from unrinsed inhaled steroids
    \item Missed preventer doses leading to airway damage over time
    \item Emergency presentations and hospitalisation for severe attacks
    \item Frustration and loss of confidence when treatment seems ineffective
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Correct inhaler technique transforms outcomes in asthma and COPD. With good technique and adherence, most people with asthma achieve good control and live active lives, and the risk of severe attacks falls markedly. COPD is managed rather than cured, but proper inhaler use improves symptoms, exercise capacity, and quality of life and reduces flare-ups. Since technique can be learned and checked at every pharmacy and clinic visit, most people can achieve effective delivery of their medicines.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ask a pharmacist or doctor to demonstrate your inhaler technique and check it regularly
    \item Use a spacer with your puffer, especially for children
    \item Rinse your mouth after inhaled steroid use
    \item Take preventers every day as prescribed, even when well
    \item Carry your reliever and asthma action plan at all times
    \item Know what to do in an asthma attack
    \item Check that your inhaler is not empty or expired
    \item Keep spacers clean and replace devices as advised
    \item Have regular reviews, at least yearly
\end{itemize}
