\chapter{CPR}
\index{CPR}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) is an emergency procedure performed on someone whose heart has stopped beating or who has stopped breathing. CPR combines chest compressions, which pump blood around the body, with rescue breaths, which provide oxygen. It keeps vital organs alive until emergency services arrive or a defibrillator can restart the heart. CPR is most effective when started immediately, and anyone can learn it. In many countries, the national guideline is to contact local emergency services, start chest compressions at a rate of 100--120 per minute, and use a defibrillator (AED) as soon as one is available.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cardiac arrest is a major public health issue, with tens of thousands of out-of-hospital cardiac arrests occurring in many countries each year. The chance of survival falls by around 10\% for every minute without CPR and defibrillation. Bystander CPR more than doubles the chance of survival, yet it is performed in only a minority of arrests in the community. Most out-of-hospital cardiac arrests happen at home, and the victim is often a family member. Widespread CPR training and public access defibrillators have improved survival in many communities.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Cardiac arrest is the sudden loss of effective heart function, usually from a heart rhythm problem.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ventricular fibrillation:} the heart quivers instead of pumping, the most common cause of sudden cardiac arrest
    \item \textbf{Heart attack:} blockage of a coronary artery can trigger cardiac arrest
    \item \textbf{Drowning, choking, and suffocation:} oxygen deprivation stops the heart
    \item \textbf{Severe allergic reaction or drug overdose:} can cause cardiac arrest
    \item \textbf{Electrical injury, trauma, and major bleeding:} other causes
    \item \textbf{Mechanism of CPR:} compressions manually pump blood to the brain and heart; breaths provide oxygen
    \item \textbf{Defibrillation:} an electric shock resets the heart rhythm in shockable arrests
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Recognising cardiac arrest quickly is essential, because CPR must start immediately.

\begin{itemize}
    \item The person is unresponsive
    \item Not breathing normally, or only gasping
    \item No pulse (for trained responders)
    \item Collapse, often sudden, sometimes with a brief seizure-like movement
    \item Pale, grey, or blue skin
    \item The person may have just complained of chest pain or breathlessness before collapsing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
CPR is started when a person is unresponsive and not breathing normally, and rescues should not be delayed by trying to confirm a pulse.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A faint (syncope), where the person is breathing and usually recovers quickly
    \item A seizure, where breathing usually continues
    \item Choking, where the airway is blocked and requires specific treatment
    \item Low blood sugar or drug overdose causing unresponsiveness
    \item A stroke or head injury causing unconsciousness
    \item Severe intoxication
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
CPR is performed while emergency help is on the way.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services immediately if:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A person is unresponsive and not breathing normally
    \item You witness a collapse
    \item A child is unresponsive or not breathing
    \item Someone has a serious injury, severe bleeding, or is choking and becoming unresponsive
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
CPR is an action, not a diagnostic test, but assessment guides its use.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Check for response:} shake gently and ask loudly if the person is okay
    \item \textbf{Check breathing:} look, listen, and feel for normal breathing for up to ten seconds
    \item \textbf{Call for help:} contact local emergency services and ask someone to bring a defibrillator
    \item \textbf{Start compressions:} push hard and fast in the centre of the chest, 100--120 per minute
    \item \textbf{Rescue breaths:} give two breaths after every thirty compressions, if trained and willing
    \item \textbf{Defibrillator use:} turn on the AED and follow its voice instructions
    \item \textbf{Continue:} keep going until emergency services arrive or the person shows signs of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management follows the local Resuscitation Council guidelines (DRSABCD).

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Danger:} check the scene is safe
    \item \textbf{Response:} check if the person responds
    \item \textbf{Send for help:} contact local emergency services and get a defibrillator
    \item \textbf{Airway:} open the airway by tilting the head back and lifting the chin
    \item \textbf{Breathing:} check for normal breathing; give two rescue breaths if trained
    \item \textbf{CPR:} perform chest compressions at 100--120 per minute, 30 compressions to 2 breaths
    \item \textbf{Defibrillation:} apply the AED pads and follow the prompts, continuing CPR around the shocks
    \item \textbf{Handover:} continue until paramedics take over
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rib fractures and bruising from chest compressions, which are acceptable to save a life
    \item Injury to internal organs from compressions, uncommon
    \item Aspiration of stomach contents during rescue breathing
    \item Brain injury from prolonged lack of oxygen
    \item Survival with significant disability in some cases
    \item Fatigue of the rescuer, making it important to swap with others where possible
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outcome of cardiac arrest depends on how quickly CPR starts, how soon defibrillation occurs, and the cause of the arrest. Bystander CPR and early defibrillation dramatically improve survival, with survival rates for shockable rhythms treated promptly reaching 50\% or more in some communities. CPR is not a cure; it buys time by maintaining blood flow until a defibrillator or advanced care can restore the heart rhythm. Even imperfect CPR is far better than no CPR, and starting immediately is the single most important thing a bystander can do.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Learn CPR through a certified course; refresher training every one to two years is recommended
    \item Know where the nearest defibrillator is at home, work, and public venues
    \item Contact your local emergency services before starting CPR and follow the dispatcher's instructions
    \item Practise hands-only CPR if unwilling or unable to give rescue breaths
    \item Do not delay: start compressions within seconds of recognising cardiac arrest
    \item If the person is a child or baby, adapt the technique and use smaller compressions
    \item Look after your own wellbeing after a resuscitation attempt; debriefing and support are available
\end{itemize}
