\chapter{Vaginal ring contraception}
\index{Vaginal ring contraception}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The vaginal ring (commonly sold under brand names such as NuvaRing) is a soft, flexible plastic ring about 5 centimetres in diameter that is inserted into the vagina and left in place for three weeks to provide effective contraception. The ring releases a steady, low dose of two hormones, a progestogen (etonogestrel) and an oestrogen (ethinylestradiol), which are absorbed through the vaginal lining directly into the bloodstream. It is used for one cycle at a time: the ring is worn for 21 days, removed for 7 days (the hormone-free week during which a withdrawal bleed occurs), and a new ring is then inserted.

The vaginal ring is one of the combined hormonal contraceptive methods, alongside the pill and the patch, and works in essentially the same way. It has the advantage of being user-controlled, discrete, and long-acting enough that it does not require daily attention. When used correctly and consistently, it is highly effective, with failure rates of less than 1 percent under perfect use and around 7 to 9 percent with typical use. Many women find the ease of a monthly change preferable to a daily pill.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Combined hormonal contraception is among the most widely used methods of birth control worldwide, and the vaginal ring is a popular delivery form in countries where it is available. It appeals particularly to women who find daily pill-taking inconvenient, who want predictable withdrawal bleeds, or who value a method that does not interrupt sex. The ring is suitable for a wide range of women, from young adults establishing contraception to women in their forties who do not smoke, provided they have no medical contraindications.

In many countries, the vaginal ring is available on prescription and listed on the public medicines subsidy, which improves its accessibility. Like other hormonal methods, its use is driven by individual preference, convenience, and tolerance of side effects, and many women switch between the pill, patch, and ring over their reproductive years. The ring does not protect against sexually transmitted infections (STIs), so condoms are recommended in addition when there is risk of infection, a consideration that shapes how clinicians counsel women about the method.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The vaginal ring prevents pregnancy through three combined actions, mirroring the effects of the natural hormones oestrogen and progesterone during the menstrual cycle.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Suppression of ovulation:} the progestogen and oestrogen act on the pituitary gland to suppress the release of follicle-stimulating hormone and luteinising hormone, preventing the ovary from releasing an egg in most cycles
    \item \textbf{Thickened cervical mucus:} the progestogen makes cervical mucus thick and hostile to sperm, blocking their passage through the cervix
    \item \textbf{Altered uterine lining:} the lining of the womb becomes thin and less receptive to implantation, though this is a secondary effect rather than the main mechanism
\end{itemize}

The hormones are absorbed through the thin, highly vascular lining of the vagina, giving steady blood levels without the daily peaks and troughs of the oral pill. The ring's effectiveness depends on it remaining in place for the full three weeks; if it is removed or falls out for more than a few hours, pregnancy risk rises and the user must follow the manufacturer's instructions, which generally involve restarting and using a backup method such as condoms.

\section*{Clinical Features}
As a contraceptive method, the vaginal ring has characteristic features rather than symptoms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Method of use:} the woman inserts the ring into the vagina like a tampon, where it usually sits comfortably and is not felt; it does not need to be positioned at the cervix to work
    \item \textbf{Cycle control:} withdrawal bleeding usually occurs during the hormone-free week and is typically lighter and more predictable than a natural period
    \item \textbf{Discreetness:} the ring is invisible and generally not noticed during intercourse, although some women or partners report feeling it occasionally
    \item \textbf{Comfort:} if the ring is uncomfortable, it may not be positioned correctly and can be adjusted; occasionally a larger ring fits poorly or is expelled
    \item \textbf{Side effects:} as with other combined hormonal methods, common early side effects include breast tenderness, nausea, headaches, and irregular bleeding, which usually settle within the first few cycles
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The relevant differential questions for a woman considering the ring concern suitability and method choice.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Combined oral contraceptive pill, which offers the same hormones but requires daily administration
    \item Contraceptive patch, which releases the same hormones through the skin and is changed weekly
    \item Progestogen-only methods (pill, implant, injection, or intrauterine system) for women who cannot take oestrogen
    \item Long-acting reversible contraception such as the intrauterine device or contraceptive implant, which requires less user involvement
    \item Barrier methods such as condoms or the diaphragm for women preferring a non-hormonal approach
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Before starting the ring, a consultation with a doctor, nurse, or sexual health service is needed to ensure it is safe and suitable. Discuss the ring if you have migraine with aura, high blood pressure, a personal or close-family history of blood clots, a history of breast cancer, liver disease, or if you smoke and are over 35, as these may make combined hormonal contraception unsuitable. Medical review is also appropriate if bleeding becomes heavy or prolonged, if there are signs of a possible pregnancy, or if you have difficulty keeping the ring in place.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe chest pain or breathlessness, which can indicate a blood clot in the lungs
    \item Severe pain or swelling in one leg, suggesting a deep vein thrombosis
    \item Sudden severe headache, visual loss, weakness, or speech difficulty, which can indicate a stroke
    \item Severe abdominal pain or signs of a serious complication
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Medical assessment:} blood pressure measurement, body mass index, smoking history, migraine history, and a review of personal and family risk of blood clots
    \item \textbf{Exclusion of pregnancy:} pregnancy is excluded before starting the method
    \item \textbf{STI screening:} offered when appropriate, since the ring does not protect against infection
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} a check after the first few months to review side effects, bleeding pattern, and satisfaction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\subsection*{Starting the ring}
The ring can be inserted on the first day of the menstrual period for immediate protection, or at another time in the cycle following the clinician's instructions, when backup contraception is used for the first seven days. A pregnancy test or waiting until the next period is needed if starting after a gap in contraception.

\subsection*{Practical tips for insertion}
The ring is compressed between the thumb and forefinger before insertion, then pushed gently into the vagina as far as is comfortable, in the same way as a tampon. It does not need to reach the cervix to work, and most women cannot feel it once in place. If the ring is uncomfortable, it may simply be pushed a little higher. Some women prefer to remove it briefly during intercourse, but the ring does not need to be taken out for sex and, if removed, must be reinserted within three hours to remain fully effective.

\subsection*{Ongoing use}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{21/7 schedule:} three weeks in, one week out, then a new ring
    \item \textbf{Extended or continuous use:} some women use ring back-to-back to delay or avoid withdrawal bleeds; this can be done safely with medical advice
    \item \textbf{Ring out of place:} if the ring has been out for less than 48 hours, rinse it and reinsert; if longer, use condoms for seven days and, if intercourse has occurred, consider emergency contraception
    \item \textbf{Ring lost or expelled:} insert a new ring as soon as possible and follow the same rules about backup contraception
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Stopping or switching}
The ring can be stopped at any time, and fertility returns promptly. When switching from another method, the clinician will advise on timing to maintain continuous cover.

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood clots:} a small increased risk of venous thromboembolism, as with all combined hormonal methods; the risk is higher in smokers, in women over 35, and with obesity, but remains low in healthy women
    \item \textbf{Breast cancer:} combined hormonal contraception is associated with a small increase in the relative risk of breast cancer, which declines after stopping
    \item \textbf{Vaginal irritation:} some women and partners report soreness, discharge, or discomfort
    \item \textbf{Ring-related problems:} expulsion, especially with heavy periods or tampon removal, and rarely the ring breaking
    \item \textbf{Migraine:} hormonal contraception can worsen migraines and, in the presence of aura, increases stroke risk, so the ring is avoided in this group
    \item \textbf{No STI protection:} the ring does not prevent infection, so condoms remain important for risk reduction
\end{itemize}

Blood-clot risk deserves a little more detail because it worries many women considering any combined hormonal method. The absolute risk in healthy, non-smoking women using the ring is small, estimated at roughly 5 to 7 additional cases of venous thromboembolism per 10,000 women per year, compared with about 2 per 10,000 in non-users. This is lower than the blood-clot risk during pregnancy and the weeks after childbirth. The important steps are a careful medical review before starting, so that women with risk factors such as a personal or family history of clots, migraine with aura, or smoking after age 35 are offered alternative methods, and prompt review of any leg pain, chest pain, or sudden breathlessness, which are the warning signs of a clot.

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
When used consistently, the vaginal ring is over 99 percent effective at preventing pregnancy, and its effectiveness is closely tied to adherence to the three-week schedule. Most women who continue the method find it convenient, well tolerated, and reversible, with fertility returning within a month or two of stopping. Side effects such as nausea and breast tenderness are most common in the first few cycles and generally settle, although a minority of women choose to switch to another method. The long-term health effects are the same as those of other combined hormonal contraceptives, including a small increased risk of blood clots and breast cancer balanced against the well-established protective effects against ovarian, endometrial, and bowel cancer. Regular reviews with a health professional ensure the method remains safe and appropriate over time.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Insert and remove the ring according to the schedule, using reminders or the manufacturer's app if helpful
    \item Check the ring is in place occasionally, particularly after intercourse or heavy periods
    \item Use condoms in addition when there is any risk of sexually transmitted infection
    \item Store spare rings according to the instructions and check expiry dates
    \item Do not flush the ring; dispose of it in a rubbish bin after removal
    \item Report persistent side effects, unusual bleeding, or any symptoms suggesting a blood clot promptly
    \item Seek emergency contraception advice if the ring has been out for longer than the recommended window and unprotected sex has occurred
\end{itemize}
