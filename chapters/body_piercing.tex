\chapter{Body Piercing}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Body piercing is the deliberate creation of a hole in the skin, cartilage, or other body tissue to insert jewellery. It is a form of body modification practised across many cultures and has become widely popular in Western societies, with the most common sites being the earlobe, ear cartilage, nostril, lip, tongue, navel, nipple, and eyebrow. Although piercing is generally safe when performed by an experienced practitioner using sterile equipment, it is a minor surgical procedure and carries real risks, including infection, bleeding, allergic reactions, and problems with healing. Complications are more common at certain sites -- such as the cartilage of the upper ear and the navel -- and are influenced by jewellery quality, aftercare, and the individual's general health.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Body piercing is common, particularly among adolescents and young adults, with studies reporting that around a third to a half of young people have had at least one piercing beyond the earlobe. The prevalence is higher in women than in men, and nipple and genital piercings are more common in men. Complications are frequent: prospective studies suggest that up to a fifth or more of piercings develop some problem, with infection and delayed healing among the most reported. Complication rates vary by site, being lowest for earlobes and highest for cartilage piercings, the navel, and the nipple. Complications are also more common in people who are pierced by informal or untrained practitioners, such as friends or in mall-based parlours with poor hygiene.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infective agents:} skin bacteria such as \textit{Staphylococcus aureus} and \textit{Streptococcus pyogenes} enter through the wound; oral and genital piercings carry mouth and skin flora and transmit infection more readily.
    \item \textbf{Contaminated equipment:} unsterilised needles, reused jewellery, and ungloved hands introduce organisms directly into the tissue.
    \item \textbf{Trauma:} snagging, catching, or pulling the jewellery delays healing, and pressure from tight jewellery compromises blood supply to the pierced tissue.
    \item \textbf{Jewellery materials:} nickel and other metals cause contact allergy in a substantial minority of people, producing itching, redness, and weeping.
    \item \textbf{Cartilage considerations:} cartilage has a poor blood supply, so upper-ear and nose piercings heal slowly and are prone to perichondritis, which can permanently deform the ear.
    \item \textbf{Site-specific hazards:} navel piercings are irritated by waistbands; tongue jewellery can damage teeth and gums; nipple piercings interfere with breastfeeding.
    \item \textbf{Host factors:} diabetes, immune suppression, bleeding disorders, and certain heart conditions increase the risk of complications.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The expected appearance of a recent piercing is mild redness, slight swelling, and a small amount of clear or lightly blood-stained discharge for a few days. Features of a problem include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Increasing or spreading redness, warmth, and tenderness around the site
    \item Thick yellow or green pus, or a foul odour
    \item Pain that worsens rather than settles after the first days
    \item Swelling that enlarges the tissue around the jewellery, especially in the upper ear
    \item Fever, chills, or feeling generally unwell with infection
    \item Itching, a red weeping rash, or blistering around the jewellery, suggesting a contact allergy
    \item A lump or hard scar (keloid) forming at the piercing site
    \item A jewellery piece that has embedded into the skin or migrated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal healing:} early redness and tenderness that settle within days and need no treatment.
    \item \textbf{Contact dermatitis:} an allergic reaction to nickel or other metals, producing itching and a rash rather than pus.
    \item \textbf{Localised infection:} bacterial cellulitis or an abscess at the piercing site.
    \item \textbf{Perichondritis:} infection of the ear cartilage that causes a hot, swollen, painful ear and can leave a "cauliflower" deformity if untreated.
    \item \textbf{Keloid formation:} overgrowth of scar tissue that forms a firm, often itchy lump.
    \item \textbf{Embedded jewellery:} where the skin grows over the back of the barbell or the jewellery sinks into the tissue.
    \item \textbf{Tetanus:} a rare but serious risk if immunisation is out of date and the wound is contaminated.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if there are signs of infection -- spreading redness, pus, worsening pain, or swelling -- particularly with a cartilage, navel, or nipple piercing, or if you have diabetes, a weakened immune system, or a heart valve condition. Seek advice early if the jewellery appears to be embedding into the skin or if an allergic reaction is suspected. People with congenital heart disease or valvular conditions should discuss piercing with their cardiologist beforehand, as antibiotic prophylaxis may be needed.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rapidly spreading redness, red streaks, or severe swelling
    \item Fever, chills, or feeling very unwell
    \item Difficulty breathing or marked swelling of the tongue or throat after an oral piercing
    \item Signs of septicaemia, such as confusion, faintness, or collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the site and date of piercing, the practitioner and sterilisation used, the jewellery material, and any previous problems with piercings.
    \item \textbf{Examination:} inspection of the site for redness, discharge, swelling, embedded jewellery, or signs of perichondritis.
    \item \textbf{Swabs:} a wound swab for culture and antibiotic sensitivity is helpful if infection is present or recurrent.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} rarely needed, but full blood count and inflammatory markers may be checked if infection is severe or the person is unwell.
    \item \textbf{Allergy testing:} patch testing may be arranged if contact dermatitis to nickel is suspected.
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} plain X-rays may occasionally be needed to locate a swallowed or aspirated oral jewellery fragment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Minor problems:} most piercings settle with careful cleaning twice daily using saline spray or a mild antiseptic, and avoiding unnecessary handling.
    \item \textbf{Infection:} an infected piercing usually requires removal of the jewellery (which may need to be done by a professional), cleaning, and topical or oral antibiotics; superficial infections are treated topically, deeper infections need oral antibiotics.
    \item \textbf{Perichondritis:} treated with antibiotics active against \textit{Pseudomonas} and staphylococci, and sometimes needs drainage of a collection.
    \item \textbf{Allergy:} replace the offending metal with surgical-grade titanium, 18-carat gold, or niobium, and treat the dermatitis with a topical steroid.
    \item \textbf{Embedded jewellery:} requires prompt removal, sometimes with a small incision under local anaesthetic.
    \item \textbf{Keloids:} treated with silicone gel sheeting, steroid injections, or, for troublesome cases, surgical excision combined with further treatment.
    \item \textbf{Tongue and lip jewellery:} advise against biting the jewellery, which fractures teeth; dentists can restore damaged teeth and gingival recession should be reviewed.
    \item \textbf{Removal decision:} jewellery should generally be left in place during infection unless trapping pus, because early removal can close the channel and seal in infection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Local infection progressing to cellulitis, abscess, or septicaemia
    \item Perichondritis and permanent deformity of the ear cartilage
    \item Nickel allergy and chronic contact dermatitis
    \item Bleeding, haematoma, and prolonged oozing, especially in people on anticoagulants
    \item Scarring, including keloid formation at the site
    \item Damage to teeth, gums, and dental restorations from oral jewellery
    \item Speech and swallowing interference from tongue or lip jewellery
    \item Interference with breastfeeding and mammography for nipple piercings
    \item Transmission of blood-borne viruses such as hepatitis B and C if equipment is shared
    \item Rarely, endocarditis in people with structural heart disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With competent technique, sterile equipment, and consistent aftercare, the large majority of body piercings heal uneventfully. Healing times vary by site: earlobes heal in six to eight weeks, while cartilage, navel, and nipple piercings can take several months to a year. Most complications are minor and resolve with simple measures, but delays in treating cartilage infections or embedded jewellery can lead to permanent cosmetic deformity. Once a piercing is removed, the hole usually closes partly or completely, sometimes leaving a small scar. People who choose reputable studios, use high-quality hypoallergenic jewellery, and follow aftercare instructions have the best outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Choose a registered, experienced piercer who uses a single-use, sterile needle and autoclaved equipment.
    \item Confirm the jewellery is surgical-grade titanium, surgical stainless steel, or 18-carat gold, particularly if you have sensitive skin.
    \item Clean the piercing twice daily with sterile saline or a mild antiseptic, and wash your hands first.
    \item Do not twist, spin, or remove the jewellery before it is fully healed.
    \item Avoid swimming pools, spas, and dirty water until the piercing has healed.
    \item Avoid tight clothing or friction over the site, and sleep away from the piercing where possible.
    \item See a doctor early for any signs of infection, especially with ear cartilage or navel piercings.
    \item Keep tetanus immunisation up to date, and check your hepatitis B status before any piercing.
\end{itemize}
