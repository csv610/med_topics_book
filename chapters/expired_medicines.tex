\chapter{Expired Medicines}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Expired medicines are medicines that are past their use-by or expiry date. All medicines have an expiry date because their chemical stability and effectiveness decline over time, and some become harmful after they expire. Using expired medicines is risky: they may not work when they are needed, and some, such as tetracyclines and eye drops, can be actively dangerous. The right approach is to check dates regularly, never use expired medicines, and dispose of them safely through pharmacy return programs.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Most households have medicines stored at home, and a large proportion of people keep medicines past their expiry date. Expired medicines commonly accumulate because of leftover prescriptions, discontinued treatments, and forgotten supplies. Disposing of old medicines in the bin or down the toilet is common but unsafe, and many people are unaware of the free pharmacy return programs available in Australia.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Medicines expire because their ingredients break down over time.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stability:} chemical compounds degrade with heat, light, moisture, and time
    \item \textbf{Loss of effect:} the medicine becomes weaker and may not treat the condition
    \item \textbf{Toxicity:} some breakdown products are harmful; expired tetracyclines can damage the kidneys
    \item \textbf{Contamination:} liquids, creams, and eye drops can grow bacteria or mould after expiry
    \item \textbf{Important exceptions:} some medicines, such as adrenaline autoinjectors and glyceryl trinitrate, need special attention and are replaced well before their printed date
    \item \textbf{Storage:} medicines kept in bathrooms and hot cars degrade faster
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Expired medicines create several problems.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Medicines that do not work, including antibiotics that fail to treat infection
    \item Risky medicines, including expired eye drops and tetracyclines
    \item Emergency medicines that fail when needed, such as expired adrenaline
    \item Confusion between current and old medicines
    \item Accidental use by children or others
    \item Environmental harm from improper disposal
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The decision about a medicine depends on its type and storage.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Medicines are generally safe until the printed expiry date if stored correctly
    \item Some medicines have short shelf lives after opening, such as eye drops (four weeks) and insulins
    \item Refrigerated medicines, such as vaccines and some insulins, must be kept cold
    \item When in doubt, a pharmacist can advise
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People should not use expired medicines to treat illness. If the only available medicine for a serious condition is expired, seek advice from a GP or pharmacist rather than using it.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Anaphylaxis or other emergencies, where an expired adrenaline autoinjector is used only if nothing else is available, while calling for help
    \item Suspected poisoning from taking an old medicine
    \item Severe symptoms needing urgent care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Managing medicines is about checking and organising.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Regular checks:} review medicines every six months
    \item \textbf{Reading dates:} check the expiry date on every product
    \item \textbf{Checking opened products:} note how long creams, drops, and liquids last after opening
    \item \textbf{Pharmacist advice:} ask about any medicine you are unsure of
    \item \textbf{Organising:} keep current medicines together and old ones removed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The right approach to expired medicines is simple.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Do not use them:} never take expired medicines
    \item \textbf{Return them:} Australian pharmacies accept unwanted and expired medicines through the Return Unwanted Medicines (RUM) program, free of charge
    \item \textbf{Do not flush or bin them:} disposal in water or rubbish harms the environment
    \item \textbf{Replace essentials:} renew emergency medicines such as adrenaline autoinjectors before they expire
    \item \textbf{Seek advice:} a pharmacist can advise on what to replace
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Treatment failure, including infection not cleared by expired antibiotics
    \item Poisoning from toxic breakdown products
    \item Eye infections from contaminated drops
    \item Failure of emergency medicines during a crisis
    \item Accidental overdose by children
    \item Environmental damage from improper disposal
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Preventing the harms of expired medicines is straightforward with good habits. Regular checks, safe storage, and pharmacy returns keep households safe and reduce waste. When medicines are stored correctly and used within their dates, they work as intended.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Check medicine cabinets every six months
    \item Store medicines in a cool, dry place, not the bathroom
    \item Keep medicines in their original containers
    \item Remove old medicines and return them to the pharmacy
    \item Note when opened products expire
    \item Keep emergency medicines current
    \item Ask a pharmacist when unsure
\end{itemize}
