\chapter{Melanoma}
\index{Melanoma}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Melanoma is a malignant neoplasm of melanocytes, the pigment-producing cells of the skin. It is the most aggressive form of skin cancer due to its high metastatic potential, but when detected early (in situ or thin invasive disease), it is highly curable with surgical excision alone. the affected region and New Zealand have the highest melanoma incidence rates in the world, reflecting high UV exposure and a predominantly fair-skinned population. Melanoma can arise on any skin surface, including non-sun-exposed areas (acral, mucosal, ocular), and rarely in internal organs.

\section*{Epidemiology}
some countries have the world's highest melanoma incidence: approximately 18,000 new cases and 1,400 deaths annually. Lifetime risk is 1 in 17 for men and 1 in 23 for women. Incidence increases with age but melanoma is one of the most common cancers in young adults (15--39 years). Rates have stabilised or declined in younger cohorts due to decades of SunSmart campaigns, but continue to rise in older age groups. Queensland has the highest rates nationally. Five-year survival is >95\% for Stage I, 80--90\% for Stage II, 50--70\% for Stage III, and 20--30\% for Stage IV, though survival for advanced disease has improved dramatically with immunotherapy and targeted therapy.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{UV radiation:} the primary environmental carcinogen; UVB causes direct DNA damage (cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers); UVA generates oxidative stress; intermittent intense exposure (sunburns) and childhood exposure confer highest risk
    \item \textbf{Genetic susceptibility:} fair skin (Fitzpatrick I--II), red/blonde hair, blue eyes, multiple naevi (>100), atypical naevi, family history; high-penetrance genes: \textit{CDKN2A} (p16), \textit{CDK4}, \textit{BAP1}, \textit{POT1}, \textit{TERF2IP}; low-penetrance MC1R variants (red hair colour)
    \item \textbf{Molecular pathways:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{MAPK pathway:} \textit{BRAF} V600E/K mutations in ~50\% (drives proliferation via MEK/ERK)
        \item \textbf{NRAS mutations:} ~25\%, mutually exclusive with BRAF
        \item \textbf{NF1 loss:} ~15\%, RASopathy pathway
        \item \textbf{TERT promoter mutations:} >70\%, telomerase activation, immortalisation
        \item \textbf{PTEN loss, TP53 mutations:} in advanced disease
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immunology:} high mutational burden (UV signature) generates neoantigens; PD-L1 expression; T-cell infiltration predicts response to immunotherapy
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} personal history of melanoma or NMSC, immunosuppression (transplant, HIV), PUVA therapy, large congenital melanocytic naevus
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ABCDE criteria:} Asymmetry, Border irregularity, Colour variation (multiple shades of brown, black, red, white, blue), Diameter >6 mm, Evolution (change in size, shape, colour, elevation, symptoms)
    \item \textbf{EFG criteria for nodular melanoma:} Elevated, Firm, Growing rapidly
    \item \textbf{Subtypes:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Superficial spreading (70\%): radial growth phase, irregular borders, variable colour
        \item Nodular (15--20\%): vertical growth from onset, symmetric, uniform colour, often amelanotic, worse prognosis for same thickness
        \item Lentigo maligna (10--15\%): sun-damaged skin of elderly, slow radial growth, irregular pigmentation
        \item Acral lentiginous (2--3\%): palms, soles, nail apparatus; most common in darker skin types; often advanced at diagnosis
        \item Desmoplastic: fibrous, often amelanotic, neurotropism, head/neck in elderly
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Advanced disease:} lymphadenopathy, subcutaneous metastases, organ-specific symptoms (lung, liver, brain, bone)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Benign melanocytic naevus:} symmetric, uniform colour, regular borders, stable
    \item \textbf{Dysplastic (atypical) naevus:} clinical overlap with melanoma; often biopsied; architectural disorder and cytologic atypia on histology
    \item \textbf{Seborrhoeic keratosis:} ``stuck-on'' appearance, waxy, verrucous surface, often pigmented
    \item \textbf{Pigmented basal cell carcinoma:} pearly border, telangiectasia, ulceration
    \item \textbf{Dermatofibroma:} firm, dimple sign, usually small
    \item \textbf{Blue naevus:} homogeneous steel-blue colour, usually <1 cm
    \item \textbf{Spitz naevus:} dome-shaped, pink/red, in children/young adults; histologically challenging
    \item \textbf{Merkel cell carcinoma:} rapidly growing violaceous nodule, elderly, sun-exposed
    \item \textbf{Metastatic deposit to skin:} from visceral primary; history of known cancer
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any new or changing pigmented lesion, especially if meeting ABCDE criteria. A lesion that looks different from the patient's other naevi (``ugly duckling'' sign). A lesion that bleeds, itches, or is tender. New pigmentation under a nail not due to trauma. Prompt excision biopsy is the standard; do not shave or laser suspicious lesions.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services or attend emergency for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe symptoms from metastatic disease (seizures, breathlessness, acute abdomen)
    \item Complications of systemic therapy (immune-related adverse events: colitis, hepatitis, pneumonitis, endocrinopathies)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Excision biopsy:} 2--3 mm margins, full thickness to subcutaneous fat; narrow margins to allow accurate Breslow thickness measurement; do not shave or punch biopsy if melanoma suspected
    \item \textbf{Histopathology:} Breslow thickness (mm), ulceration, mitotic rate, Clark level, microsatellites, regression, tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes, perineural/lymphovascular invasion; AJCC 8th edition staging
    \item \textbf{Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB):} recommended for T1b (0.8--1.0 mm with ulceration or $\ge$1 mm) and T2--T4; prognostic and guides adjuvant therapy
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT chest/abdomen/pelvis or PET-CT for Stage III/IV; brain MRI for Stage IIIC/IV or neurological symptoms
    \item \textbf{Molecular testing:} \textit{BRAF} V600 mutation status (PCR, IHC, or NGS) for Stage III/IV to guide targeted therapy
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} LDH (prognostic in Stage IV), CBC, LFTs, renal function, thyroid function (baseline for immunotherapy)
    \item \textbf{Dermoscopy:} aids clinical diagnosis; chaos and clues, pigment network, streaks, blue-white veil, regression structures
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary treatment (localised):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Wide local excision (WLE) margins per AJCC: in situ 0.5 cm, $\le$1 mm 1 cm, 1.01--2 mm 1--2 cm, 2.01--4 mm 2 cm, >4 mm 2 cm
        \item SLNB at time of WLE for eligible patients
        \item Adjuvant therapy for Stage IIB/IIC/III after complete resection:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Anti-PD-1 immunotherapy: pembrolizumab or nivolumab (12 months) -- improves recurrence-free survival
            \item Dabrafenib + trametinib (BRAF V600 mutant) -- 12 months
            \item Clinical trials
        \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Regional recurrence / in-transit metastases:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Surgical resection if feasible
        \item Intralesional therapy: talimogene laherparepvec (T-VEC), rose bengal
        \item Regional perfusion/infusion (melphalan) for limb disease
        \item Radiotherapy for unresectable nodal disease
        \item Systemic therapy as for Stage IV
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stage IV (metastatic):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Immunotherapy (first-line for most):} anti-PD-1 monotherapy (pembrolizumab, nivolumab) or combination ipilimumab + nivolumab (higher response but higher toxicity); continue until progression or unacceptable toxicity (max 2 years)
        \item \textbf{Targeted therapy (BRAF V600 mutant):} dabrafenib + trametinib or encorafenib + binimetinib; rapid response but acquired resistance at ~12 months; can be used first-line or after immunotherapy
        \item \textbf{Brain metastases:} stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) preferred; immunotherapy has intracranial activity; targeted therapy crosses blood-brain barrier
        \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} palliative for bone, symptomatic brain, spinal cord compression
        \item \textbf{Clinical trials:} novel combinations, neoadjuvant approaches, TIL therapy, vaccines
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} risk-stratified; Stage I 6--12 monthly for 5 years then annually; Stage II/III 3--6 monthly for 3 years then 6--12 monthly; self-skin examination, lymph node palpation, imaging per protocol; lifelong surveillance for second primaries
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Local recurrence:} inadequate margins, aggressive histology
    \item \textbf{Metastatic spread:} lymph nodes, skin/subcutis, lung, liver, brain, bone, GI tract
    \item \textbf{Lymphoedema:} after lymph node dissection (axilla, groin, neck)
    \item \textbf{Immunotherapy toxicities (irAEs):} colitis, hepatitis, pneumonitis, endocrinopathies (thyroiditis, hypophysitis, adrenal insufficiency, type 1 diabetes), dermatologic, neurologic, cardiac; graded by CTCAE; managed with corticosteroids and immunosuppression
    \item \textbf{Targeted therapy toxicities:} fever, rash, arthralgia, cardiomyopathy, ocular toxicity, cutaneous squamous cell carcinomas (paradoxical MAPK activation)
    \item \textbf{Psychosocial:} anxiety, depression, fear of recurrence, financial toxicity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Prognosis is primarily determined by Breslow thickness, ulceration, and nodal status (AJCC stage). Stage I: >95\% 5-year survival. Stage II: 80--95\%. Stage III: 50--90\% depending on nodal burden and ulceration. Stage IV: historically <10\% 5-year survival, now 30--50\% with modern systemic therapy; some patients achieve durable complete responses and potential cure. Neoadjuvant immunotherapy shows promise for improving pathological complete response rates. Long-term survivors require ongoing surveillance for late recurrence (can occur >10 years) and second primaries.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sun protection:} Slip (protective clothing), Slop (SPF 50+ broad-spectrum sunscreen, reapplied 2-hourly), Slap (broad-brim hat), Seek (shade), Slide (sunglasses); UV index $\ge$3 requires protection
    \item \textbf{Avoid solariums:} banned in many countries; classified as Group 1 carcinogen by IARC
    \item \textbf{Regular self-skin examination:} every 3 months; partner-assisted for back/scalp; photograph naevi for comparison
    \item \textbf{Professional skin checks:} annually for high-risk (personal/family history, multiple naevi, fair skin, immunosuppression); more frequently if prior melanoma
    \item \textbf{Dermoscopic surveillance:} digital dermoscopy (mole mapping) for high-risk patients
    \item \textbf{Vitamin D:} balance sun protection with adequate vitamin D; supplementation if deficient
    \item \textbf{Support:} Melanoma Institute some countries, Melanoma Patients some countries, Cancer Council
\end{itemize}
