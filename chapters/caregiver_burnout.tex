\chapter{Caregiver Burnout}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Caregiver burnout is a state of physical, emotional, and mental exhaustion that develops in response to the sustained demands of caring for another person. It most often affects people providing unpaid care to a partner, parent, child, or friend who is ill, disabled, or frail, and it can occur in any setting, including caring for someone in the same household or from a distance. Caregiving commonly involves helping with activities of daily living, administering medications, providing personal care, attending appointments, managing finances, and offering emotional support.

Burnout is distinct from ordinary tiredness. It develops gradually, often over months or years, and is characterised by feeling overwhelmed, drained, emotionally detached, and unable to cope with the caring role. Because caregivers are often the mainstay of a person's support, recognising burnout early matters: an exhausted caregiver may struggle to provide safe care and is at increased risk of both physical and mental illness. Burnout is a recognised problem rather than a personal failure, and it responds well to support and practical help.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Millions of people worldwide provide unpaid care for a relative or friend, and the numbers are rising as populations age and more people live with chronic illness and disability. Most caregivers are family members, and although women make up the majority of informal caregivers, men also take on substantial caring roles. The burden falls particularly heavily on middle-aged people caring for ageing parents while also raising children or working, a situation sometimes called the "sandwich generation", and on partners caring for a spouse.

The risk of burnout is higher among those caring for someone with dementia, a progressive neurological condition, or high-level physical disability, and among people who live with the person they care for, care alone without backup, or combine caregiving with paid employment. Studies consistently show that caregivers report higher rates of depression, anxiety, chronic stress, and physical health problems than non-caregivers of the same age.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Burnout arises when the demands of caregiving repeatedly exceed the caregiver's personal, social, and financial resources. Key contributing factors include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chronic stress:} the physical and emotional load of caring, often for years without meaningful breaks
    \item \textbf{Role strain:} juggling caregiving with paid work, parenting, and financial pressures, often with little help
    \item \textbf{Social isolation:} loss of friendships, social contact, and leisure time
    \item \textbf{Lack of support:} few or no other family members, services, or respite options sharing the load
    \item \textbf{Grief and loss:} watching a loved one decline and experiencing anticipatory grief in progressive illness
    \item \textbf{High-demand conditions:} caring for someone with behavioural disturbance, poor sleep, or complex medical needs
    \item \textbf{Perfectionism or guilt:} believing that only the caregiver can do the job well enough
\end{itemize}

Prolonged stress keeps the body's stress-response systems switched on for long periods, with sustained elevation of stress hormones such as cortisol. Over time this can disturb sleep, impair immune function, raise blood pressure, and contribute to the physical and emotional symptoms of burnout. The effect is a downward spiral in which exhaustion reduces the ability to cope, which in turn increases the sense of being overwhelmed.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Constant exhaustion, feeling drained or "running on empty", even after rest
    \item Irritability, frustration, or anger directed at the person being cared for, followed by guilt
    \item Loss of interest in activities that once brought pleasure
    \item Sleep problems, including difficulty getting to sleep, frequent waking, and unrefreshing sleep
    \item Anxiety, low mood, hopelessness, or feeling trapped in the caring role
    \item Difficulty concentrating, remembering, or making decisions
    \item Withdrawal from friends, family, and social activities
    \item Neglect of personal health, such as missed appointments, poor diet, and reduced exercise
    \item Physical symptoms including headaches, muscle tension, chest tightness, and frequent infections
    \item Increased reliance on alcohol, tobacco, or other substances to cope
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Clinical depression, which is common in caregivers and may require specific treatment
    \item Anxiety disorders, including generalised anxiety and panic disorder
    \item Normal grief or anticipatory grief related to the care recipient's illness
    \item Physical conditions causing fatigue, such as anaemia, hypothyroidism, diabetes, or chronic sleep deprivation
    \item Stress arising from other sources, such as work, financial, or relationship pressures
    \item Adverse effects of medicines, alcohol, or substance use
\end{itemize}

A primary care assessment helps distinguish burnout from other mental health conditions and physical causes of fatigue, because the treatments differ.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor or counsellor if feelings of exhaustion, low mood, or irritability are persistent, worsening, or affecting your health, relationships, or ability to provide safe care. Help is also appropriate when you feel unable to keep up with the care recipient's needs, when you are neglecting your own health, or when you are relying increasingly on alcohol or other substances.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:} any thoughts of suicide or self-harm, any thoughts of harming the person you care for, or signs that you can no longer manage the care situation safely.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical interview:} a structured discussion of the caring role, its demands, its history, and its impact on your life
    \item \textbf{Mental health screening:} validated tools such as the PHQ-9 for depression and the GAD-7 for anxiety
    \item \textbf{Caregiver burden scales:} standardised questionnaires, such as the Zarit Burden Interview, that measure the load of caregiving
    \item \textbf{Physical examination and blood tests:} to exclude physical causes of fatigue, including anaemia, thyroid dysfunction, and diabetes
    \item \textbf{Review of support networks:} exploring who else can help and what formal services are available
    \item \textbf{Assessment of the care recipient's needs:} a functional and safety review that informs what support could be put in place
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Respite care:} regular planned breaks through day programs, in-home support, or short-term residential respite
    \item \textbf{Delegation:} sharing tasks with family, friends, neighbours, or paid support workers rather than doing everything alone
    \item \textbf{Counselling and psychological therapy:} cognitive behaviour therapy and related approaches help manage stress, guilt, and low mood
    \item \textbf{Peer support groups:} connecting with other caregivers who understand the experience can reduce isolation
    \item \textbf{Carer assessment:} many regions offer a formal carer assessment and a coordinated support plan
    \item \textbf{Medication:} antidepressants or other treatment may be needed if depression or anxiety develops, prescribed and monitored by a doctor
    \item \textbf{Planning:} advance care planning and a written care plan for the care recipient can reduce uncertainty and daily decision-making
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Depression and anxiety disorders requiring treatment
    \item Increased risk of physical illness, high blood pressure, and impaired immunity
    \item Alcohol and substance misuse as a coping strategy
    \item Relationship strain with the care recipient and other family members
    \item Social isolation, loss of identity, and loss of friendships
    \item Reduced ability to provide safe, consistent care, increasing the risk of harm or an unplanned placement in residential care
    \item Thoughts of suicide or self-harm in severe cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Burnout is not a permanent state, and most caregivers recover substantially once they receive practical help, regular breaks, and emotional support. Early recognition and action lead to better outcomes, and many caregivers go on to provide loving, competent care for many years once their own needs are addressed. Some families, with support, choose to adjust the care arrangement to one that is more sustainable, such as sharing duties or introducing paid support. Caregivers who look after their own health, accept help, and maintain a network of support generally cope better and report a higher quality of life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Build a support team early -- family, friends, neighbours, and community services -- rather than waiting until you are exhausted
    \item Arrange regular respite breaks and plan them into the routine
    \item Protect time for yourself, including sleep, exercise, regular meals, and activities you enjoy
    \item Keep your own health appointments and stay up to date with screening
    \item Set realistic expectations and learn to ask for and accept help
    \item Stay connected with friends and community activities outside the caring role
    \item Join a caregiver support group or carer organisation for information and peer support
    \item Learn the warning signs of burnout and act early when they appear
\end{itemize}
