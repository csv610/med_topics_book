\chapter{Mpox (Monkeypox)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Mpox (formerly known as monkeypox) is a viral zoonotic disease caused by the Mpox virus (MPXV), an enveloped double-stranded DNA virus belonging to the \textit{Orthopoxvirus} genus of the family Poxviridae, making it closely related to the variola virus (the causative agent of smallpox). Mpox typically presents clinically with a prodromal febrile illness, prominent lymphadenopathy, and a distinctive vesicular-pustular rash that progresses through structured stages before crusting and desquamating. While historically endemic to the tropical rainforest regions of Central and West Africa, a major global outbreak initiated in 2022 established widespread human-to-human transmission internationally. Although most cases are self-limiting, Mpox can cause severe disease, complications, and death, particularly in young children, pregnant women, and severely immunocompromised individuals.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Mpox was first identified in laboratory monkeys in 1958, with the first human case recorded in 1970 in the Democratic Republic of the Congo. Historically, the disease was categorised into two main genetic clades: Clade I (formerly Central African clade), which is associated with a mortality rate of up to 10\%, and Clade II (formerly West African clade), which is less severe with a mortality rate of less than 1\%. The 2022--2023 global outbreak was driven by a sub-clade of Clade II (specifically Clade IIb), spreading rapidly via close, intimate, and sexual contact networks, predominantly but not exclusively among men who have sex with men (MSM). In Australia, hundreds of cases have been notified since the start of the 2022 outbreak. In 2024, a public health emergency was declared due to the emergence and rapid expansion of a novel, more virulent Clade Ib strain in Central Africa.

\sectionsection*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The transmission and pathogenesis of Mpox involve viral entry through epithelial barriers, replication, and subsequent systemic dissemination.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transmission pathways:} Human-to-human transmission occurs through direct contact with infectious skin lesions, scabs, or bodily fluids during intimate or sexual contact. It can also spread via respiratory droplets during prolonged face-to-face contact and through contaminated fomites (e.g. shared bedding, towels, or clothing). Zoonotic transmission occurs via bites, scratches, or direct contact with the blood and fluids of infected wild rodents or primates.
    \item \textbf{Viral entry and replication:} The virus enters the host through broken skin (even micro-abrasions), the respiratory tract, or mucous membranes (oral, anogenital, or conjunctival). It replicates locally in epithelial cells and macrophages at the site of inoculation.
    \text{\item \textbf{Lymphatic dissemination and viraemia:}} The virus migrates to regional lymph nodes, where it undergoes secondary replication. This leads to primary and secondary viraemia, resulting in systemic dissemination to the skin, spleen, bone marrow, and other organs.
    \item \textbf{Lesion development:} Viraemic spread to the dermis triggers local inflammatory cell infiltration, dermal oedema, and epidermal necrosis, manifesting as the characteristic deep-seated, umbilicated cutaneous lesions.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical course of Mpox is divided into a prodromal phase and an eruptive skin rash phase, though in recent outbreaks, these phases have often overlapped or occurred atypical.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prodromal phase (1 to 4 days):} Characterised by sudden high fever, chills, intense headache, backache, myalgia (muscle aches), severe fatigue, and lymphadenopathy.
    \item \textbf{Lymphadenopathy:} A hallmark clinical feature of Mpox (occurring in up to 90\% of cases) that distinguishes it from smallpox and chickenpox. Lymph nodes in the submandibular, cervical, axillary, or inguinal regions become swollen, firm, and tender.
    \item \textbf{Distinctive skin eruption:} Lesions appear 1 to 3 days after the fever. They evolve synchronously over 2 to 4 weeks through five distinct stages: macules (flat base), papules (raised firm lesions), vesicles (fluid-filled), pustules (pus-filled), and finally crusts (scabs) that dry and fall off.
    \item \textbf{Atypical distribution in global outbreaks:} Classic cases present with rash concentrated on the face and extremities (including palms and soles). In sexual transmission networks, lesions are often localized to the genital, perineal, and perianal areas, and may be few in number.
    \text{\item \textbf{Severe mucosal inflammation:}} Painful oral ulcers (enanthem) causing dysphagia, tonsillitis, and severe proctitis (rectal inflammation) presenting with excruciating rectal pain, bleeding, tenesmus, and constipation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Clinicians must differentiate Mpox from other common vesicular, pustular, and ulcerative infectious conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mpox vs.\ Varicella (chickenpox):} Varicella lesions are superficial, not deep-seated, appear in different stages of development simultaneously ("crops"), are concentrated on the trunk, and are not associated with prominent lymphadenopathy.
    \item \textbf{Mpox vs.\ Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV) and Syphilis:} Genital or perianal Mpox lesions can mimic HSV vesicles or primary syphilitic chancres. Direct swab testing is required, as co-infections are common.
    \item \textbf{Mpox vs.\ Hand, Foot, and Mouth Disease:} Caused by enteroviruses, presenting with small vesicles on the hands, feet, and mouth of children, lacking the severe prodrome, lymphadenopathy, and pustular evolution of Mpox.
    \item \textbf{Mpox vs.\ Molluscum contagiosum:} Molluscum lesions are small, firm, umbilicated papules that are not painful, lack a febrile prodrome, and contain a waxy core rather than fluid/pus.
    \item \textbf{Mpox vs.\ Disseminated shingles (herpes zoster):} Reactivation of varicella-zoster virus presenting in a dermatomal or disseminated distribution, typically in older or immunocompromised individuals.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Individuals should seek medical advice from a general practitioner or sexual health clinic if they develop an unexplained rash, blisters, or ulcers, particularly if accompanied by fever, swollen glands, or a history of travel or contact with a confirmed Mpox case.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe respiratory distress or persistent difficulty breathing.
    \item Inability to swallow fluids or severe dehydration due to excruciating mouth or throat pain.
    \item Severe confusion, altered consciousness, neck stiffness, or seizures (suggesting encephalitis).
    \item Severe eye pain, redness, or changes in vision, which indicates corneal infection.
    \item Sudden collapse or signs of systemic sepsis (high heart rate, cold extremities, confusion).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A definitive diagnosis of Mpox must be established using molecular laboratory techniques.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) testing:} The gold standard diagnostic investigation. Swabs are taken directly from the base of active skin lesions (exudate from vesicles or pustules, or dry scabs) and sent in viral transport media.
    \item \textbf{Lesion biopsy:} In atypical cases, histopathology reveals ballooning degeneration of keratinocytes, multinucleated giant cells, and eosinophilic intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies (Guarnieri bodies).
    \item \textbf{Serological testing:} Detecting anti-orthopoxvirus IgM (indicating recent infection or vaccination) and IgG. However, serology is limited by cross-reactivity with other orthopoxviruses (e.g. smallpox vaccine) and is not used for primary diagnosis.
    \item \textbf{Screening for sexually transmitted infections (STIs):} Comprehensive screening for HIV, syphilis, gonorrhoea, and chlamydia, as co-transmission is highly prevalent in outbreak cohorts.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management involves isolation to prevent transmission, supportive care to manage symptoms, and targeted antiviral therapy for high-risk cases.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Isolation and infection control:} Patients must isolate at home or in a hospital setting until all skin lesions have fully resolved, the scabs have fallen off, and a fresh layer of healthy skin has formed underneath.
    \item \textbf{Analgesia and symptom support:} Pain management is critical, particularly for proctitis and oral lesions. This involves paracetamol, NSAIDs, neuropathic agents, sitz baths, and oral lidocaine rinses. Opioid analgesics may be required for severe rectal pain.
    \text{\item \textbf{Antiviral therapy (Tecovirimat):}} Tecovirimat (TPOXX) is an antiviral that inhibits the orthopoxvirus VP37 envelope wrapping protein. It is reserved for patients with severe disease, lesions in critical anatomical sites (e.g. the eye, mouth, or urethra), and immunocompromised individuals.
    \item \textbf{Vaccination:} Administration of the third-generation, non-replicating smallpox/Mpox vaccine (MVA-BN, brand name JYNNEOS). It is utilised for pre-exposure prophylaxis in high-risk cohorts and post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) within 4 days of contact.
    \item \textbf{Secondary infection care:} Topical or oral antibiotics (e.g. cephalexin) if skin lesions become secondarily infected with bacteria.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While many cases resolve without intervention, Mpox can lead to serious and potentially life-threatening complications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Secondary bacterial skin infection:} Cellulitis, impetigo, or necrotising soft-tissue infections resulting from scratching or contamination of open pustules.
    \item \textbf{Ophthalmic complications (Keratitis):} Inoculation of the virus into the eye, leading to corneal ulceration, keratitis, and permanent scarring or blindness.
    \text{\item \textbf{Respiratory complications (Bronchopneumonia):}} Severe primary viral pneumonia or secondary bacterial pneumonia, particularly in children and vulnerable hosts.
    \item \textbf{Neurological disorders (Encephalitis):} Rare inflammation of the brain parenchymal tissue, presenting with altered mental state, seizures, and focal deficits.
    \item \textbf{Sepsis:} Systemic bacterial bloodstream infection arising from skin barrier breakdown or severe pneumonia.
    \item \textbf{Severe anorectal complications:} Rectal strictures, fistulas, or bowel perforation secondary to severe necrotising proctitis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals infected with Clade IIb Mpox is generally highly favourable, with the vast majority of cases resolving spontaneously within 2 to 4 weeks with supportive care. The case fatality rate for Clade IIb is less than 0.1\%. However, prognosis is significantly poorer for individuals with advanced, untreated HIV (CD4 count $<200$ cells/$\mu$L), where Mpox can present as a severe, necrotising systemic disease with high mortality. Infection with Clade I strains also carries a higher mortality rate (up to 10\% in under-resourced settings). Resolved skin lesions may leave behind permanent hypopigmentation, hyperpigmentation, or pitted scars.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventative strategies and targeted self-care are vital to control outbreaks and protect vulnerable populations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Get vaccinated:} Completing a two-dose primary course of the JYNNEOS vaccine if belonging to a recommended high-risk cohort (e.g. sexually active MSM, healthcare workers in specialized units).
    \item \textbf{Avoid physical contact with active cases:} Refraining from skin-to-skin, intimate, or sexual contact with anyone who has a visible rash, sores, or fluid-filled blisters.
    \item \textbf{Infection control in the home:} If isolating, avoiding sharing bedding, towels, clothing, or eating utensils with other household members, and washing all laundry at high temperatures.
    \item \textbf{Hand hygiene:} Washing hands frequently with soap and water or using an alcohol-based hand sanitiser.
    \item \textbf{Do not touch or scratch lesions:} Refraining from popping, scratching, or shaving over active Mpox bumps to prevent secondary bacterial infection or spreading the virus to other parts of the body.
\end{itemize}
