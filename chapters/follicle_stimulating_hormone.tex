\chapter{Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH)}
\index{Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) is a hormone made by the pituitary gland in the brain that plays a central role in reproduction. In women, it stimulates the ovaries to grow eggs each month, and in men, it stimulates the testes to produce sperm. FSH is measured by a blood test to investigate fertility problems, absent or irregular periods, and menopause, and to assess the function of the pituitary gland. FSH levels change predictably across the menstrual cycle and across life: they rise as menopause approaches and are high after menopause. This chapter explains what FSH does, when testing is used, and what the results mean.

\section*{Epidemiology}
FSH testing is common in many countries, particularly in the assessment of fertility, menopause, and menstrual disorders. Around one in six couples experiences infertility, and FSH measurement is a routine part of the fertility work-up in women. It is also used to confirm menopause, which affects every woman who lives to midlife, and to investigate primary ovarian insufficiency in younger women, which affects around 1% of women under 40. In men, FSH is measured when sperm counts are low. Most FSH testing is normal; the test's value is in identifying the specific patterns that guide diagnosis and treatment.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Production:} FSH is made and released by the pituitary gland in response to gonadotrophin-releasing hormone from the hypothalamus
    \item \textbf{In women:} FSH drives the growth of ovarian follicles, which produce oestrogen; as follicles grow, oestrogen rises and suppresses FSH
    \item \textbf{The menstrual cycle:} FSH peaks briefly at the start of the cycle and rises further just before ovulation
    \item \textbf{Menopause:} as the ovaries exhaust their eggs and oestrogen falls, the pituitary produces more FSH in a feedback response, so FSH levels are high
    \item \textbf{Primary ovarian insufficiency:} the same high-FSH pattern occurs when the ovaries fail prematurely
    \item \textbf{In men:} FSH stimulates Sertoli cells to support sperm production; low sperm counts can be accompanied by high FSH when the testes fail
    \item \textbf{Pituitary problems:} low FSH with low oestrogen or testosterone suggests failure of the pituitary or hypothalamus
    \item \textbf{Feedback control:} FSH and its partner luteinising hormone (LH) are regulated by the sex hormones they stimulate
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features are the clinical situations in which FSH is measured.
\begin{itemize}
    \item In women with irregular or absent periods, to assess ovarian function
    \item In women trying to conceive, as part of fertility assessment
    \item In women with menopausal symptoms, to confirm the menopausal transition
    \item In women under 40 with stopping periods, to diagnose premature ovarian insufficiency
    \item In men with low sperm counts or testicular problems
    \item In people with suspected pituitary disease, together with LH and sex hormone levels
    \item FSH is measured on specific days of the cycle in fertility testing, usually day 2--3
    \item A single measurement is often repeated because levels fluctuate
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Abnormal FSH levels point to specific diagnoses.
\begin{itemize}
    \item High FSH with low oestrogen: menopause or primary ovarian insufficiency in women; testicular failure in men
    \item High FSH with a low sperm count: primary testicular failure in men
    \item Low FSH with low sex hormones: pituitary or hypothalamic failure
    \item Normal FSH with symptoms: other causes of irregular periods, including polycystic ovary syndrome, thyroid disease, and stress
    \item The distinction matters because treatments differ: high FSH indicates the ovaries or testes are failing, while low FSH indicates a problem higher up
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
FSH testing is arranged by a doctor in specific clinical situations, and results should be discussed with the doctor who ordered them. Anyone with fertility concerns, absent or irregular periods, or menopausal symptoms should seek assessment.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe symptoms, including headache with visual disturbance, which may indicate a pituitary problem
    \item Heavy vaginal bleeding with faintness
    \item Any emergency symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{FSH blood test:} measured with LH, oestradiol (in women), or testosterone (in men) for full interpretation
    \item \textbf{Timing:} in premenopausal women, FSH is measured early in the cycle (day 2--3); in menopause assessment, repeated samples are sometimes taken
    \item \textbf{Full blood count and other hormones:} thyroid function, prolactin, and iron studies in the menstrual and fertility work-up
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} pelvic ultrasound for ovarian reserve and structure in fertility assessment
    \item \textbf{Semen analysis:} in male infertility
    \item \textbf{Pituitary imaging:} MRI when pituitary disease is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Menopause:} high FSH with menopausal symptoms confirms the diagnosis, and management focuses on symptoms, bone health, and cardiovascular risk
    \item \textbf{Primary ovarian insufficiency:} hormone replacement until the natural age of menopause, and discussion of fertility options including egg donation
    \item \textbf{Fertility treatment:} FSH levels inform the approach to ovulation induction and IVF
    \item \textbf{Male infertility:} high FSH with azoospermia indicates testicular failure; low FSH may respond to hormonal treatment
    \item \textbf{Pituitary failure:} hormone replacement under specialist endocrine care
    \item \textbf{PCOS and other causes:} normal FSH with irregular periods is managed according to the specific diagnosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Misinterpretation of FSH without other hormone levels
    \item Diagnostic delay when FSH is not measured in the right clinical context
    \item The psychological impact of infertility and premature menopause diagnoses
    \item Bone loss and cardiovascular risk if premature ovarian insufficiency is not treated with hormones
    \item Complications of the underlying fertility or pituitary conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
FSH testing reliably identifies the pattern of reproductive hormone function and guides diagnosis in fertility, menopause, and pituitary disease. Treatment outcomes are good when the pattern is correctly interpreted: menopausal symptoms and bone risk are managed effectively, many causes of infertility are treatable, and pituitary failure responds to hormone replacement. The test is most valuable as part of a panel of hormones and a full clinical assessment, and its correct use prevents both missed diagnoses and unnecessary treatment.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Understand the purpose and timing of the test before having it
    \item Have FSH measured with LH and sex hormones for accurate interpretation
    \item Seek assessment early for fertility concerns and irregular periods
    \item Discuss menopause symptoms and hormone options with a doctor
    \item Attend follow-up appointments to review results in context
    \item Maintain bone health with calcium, vitamin D, and weight-bearing exercise after menopause
    \item Seek psychological support for fertility and menopause concerns
\end{itemize}
