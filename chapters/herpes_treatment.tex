\chapter{Herpes Treatment}
\index{Herpes Treatment}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Herpes treatment aims to shorten outbreaks, relieve symptoms, reduce recurrences, and lower the risk of passing the virus to others. Antiviral medicines, including aciclovir, valaciclovir, and famciclovir, are the mainstay of treatment and work best when started early in an outbreak. Treatment is used in three ways: episodically for individual outbreaks, continuously to suppress frequent recurrences, and, in special situations, to prevent transmission. Herpes cannot be cured, but modern treatment makes it a well-managed, minor condition for most people.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Genital herpes affects around one in eight adults, and cold sores affect most of the population at some point. Many people never require treatment because their outbreaks are mild or infrequent, while others have frequent or severe recurrences that benefit from suppressive therapy. Antiviral medicines are safe, well tolerated, and widely used. Rates of neonatal herpes remain low, reflecting the effectiveness of obstetric care for affected women.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Virus:} herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) and type 2 (HSV-2)
    \item \textbf{Mechanism of antivirals:} they inhibit the viral enzyme needed for viral DNA replication, stopping the virus multiplying
    \item \textbf{Episodic treatment:} started at the first sign of an outbreak to shorten and reduce its severity
    \item \textbf{Suppressive treatment:} daily antivirals keep viral replication in check, preventing most outbreaks
    \item \textbf{Effect on transmission:} suppression reduces shedding and lowers transmission risk
    \item \textbf{Limitations:} antivirals do not eliminate the latent virus from nerve cells, so treatment cannot cure infection
    \item \textbf{Resistance:} uncommon, but occurs mainly in immunocompromised people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Treatment is most effective when started during the prodrome, before blisters appear
    \item First episodes are the most severe and benefit most from treatment
    \item Frequent recurrences, severe pain, or outbreaks affecting daily life indicate suppressive therapy
    \item Cold sores and genital herpes respond to the same antiviral class
    \item Eye involvement requires urgent specialist treatment
    \item Pregnancy requires specialist planning and sometimes antiviral prophylaxis near term
    \item Immunocompromised people may need higher doses and longer courses
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Genital ulcers from syphilis and other causes must be excluded by testing
    \item Mouth ulcers from other causes do not respond to antivirals
    \item Shingles requires different antiviral management and doses
    \item Bacterial skin infection may complicate herpes and needs antibiotics
    \item Recurrent symptoms without confirmed herpes should be investigated
    \item Confirmation by PCR swab before treatment avoids unnecessary antivirals
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for first-episode or severe herpes, frequent recurrences, or symptoms in pregnancy. Seek urgent care for eye symptoms, neurological symptoms, or infection in an immunocompromised person.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe headache, confusion, or seizures with herpes
    \item Eye pain, redness, or vision changes
    \item A newborn with fever or rash whose mother has active herpes
    \item Difficulty breathing, swallowing, or signs of severe infection
    \item Rapidly spreading rash with fever
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PCR swab:} confirms the diagnosis and viral type
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} pattern and severity of outbreaks guide the treatment approach
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy planning:} history of herpes in mother and partner reviewed
    \item \textbf{Immune status:} assessment in people with recurrent or severe disease
    \item \textbf{Eye assessment:} slit-lamp examination for suspected keratitis
    \item \textbf{Review:} response to treatment and need for ongoing suppression
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Episodic treatment:} 1--5 day courses of oral antivirals, started at the first sign of an outbreak
    \item \textbf{Suppressive therapy:} daily antiviral tablets for people with six or more recurrences a year, reducing outbreaks by 70--80\%
    \item \textbf{First-episode treatment:} a longer 5--10 day course for initial infections
    \item \textbf{Cold sores:} topical antivirals or short oral courses
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} suppressive antivirals from 36 weeks reduce outbreaks and the need for caesarean
    \item \textbf{Eye and brain infection:} urgent specialist care with high-dose antivirals
    \item \textbf{Pain and comfort:} analgesics, saline baths, and numbing agents
    \item \textbf{Counselling:} information about transmission, recurrence, and coping reduces distress
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Untreated severe first episodes are more painful and prolonged
    \item Frequent recurrences can cause significant psychological distress
    \item Transmission to partners continues without suppression
    \item Neonatal herpes, preventable with obstetric care
    \item Herpes keratitis can scar the cornea
    \item Antiviral side effects are uncommon but include nausea, headache, and rarely kidney effects
    \item Drug resistance in immunocompromised patients
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Treatment of herpes is highly effective. Episodic therapy shortens outbreaks by a day or two and reduces severity, while suppressive therapy controls the condition in almost everyone with frequent recurrences, improving quality of life substantially. Antivirals are safe for long-term use. Although the virus remains in the body, most people find their outbreaks become less frequent over time, and treatment ensures the condition rarely interferes with life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Start antiviral treatment at the first sign of tingling or an outbreak
    \item Discuss suppressive therapy with your doctor if recurrences are frequent
    \item Use condoms and inform partners; suppression reduces transmission risk
    \item Avoid sex during active outbreaks
    \item Keep the area clean and dry, and use saline baths for comfort
    \item Manage triggers such as stress, illness, and sun exposure
    \item Pregnant women should discuss herpes with their maternity team
    \item Take antivirals exactly as prescribed and complete courses
\end{itemize}
