\chapter{Anxiety}

\section*{Overview}
Anxiety is a mental health condition that affects the way a person thinks, feels, or behaves. Mental health conditions are common and treatable. They can be triggered by a combination of biological factors (such as genetics and brain chemistry), psychological factors (such as coping style and past trauma), and social factors (such as stress, relationships, and life events). With appropriate support, most people recover or learn to manage their condition well.

\section*{Symptoms}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent low mood, sadness, or emptiness
    \item Excessive worry, fear, panic, or irritability
    \item Loss of interest in activities once enjoyed
    \item Changes in sleep (too much or too little)
    \item Changes in appetite or weight
    \item Tiredness, low energy, or reduced motivation
    \item Difficulty concentrating, remembering, or making decisions
    \item Thoughts of self-harm or suicide
    \item Withdrawal from family, friends, and usual activities
\end{itemize}

\section*{Causes}
Mental health conditions usually arise from several factors acting together:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A family history of mental illness
    \item Long-term stress, trauma, abuse, or significant loss
    \item Physical illness, chronic pain, or substance use
    \item Imbalances in brain chemicals and hormones
    \item Social isolation or relationship breakdown
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A thorough assessment by a GP, psychologist, or psychiatrist
    \item Discussion of symptoms, thoughts, mood, and behaviour
    \item Questions about medical history, family history, and life events
    \item Physical examination and blood tests to rule out other causes
    \item Use of standard questionnaires to gauge severity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Treatment}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Psychological treatments such as cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT), interpersonal therapy, or counselling
    \item Medication such as antidepressants, anti-anxiety medicines, mood stabilisers, or antipsychotics, prescribed by a doctor
    \item Lifestyle changes including regular exercise, good sleep, and reducing alcohol and other drugs
    \item Social support from family, friends, peer groups, and community services
    \item Hospital or specialist care for severe or crisis presentations
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prevention}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Build strong relationships and social connections
    \item Manage stress with relaxation, mindfulness, or exercise
    \item Maintain a regular sleep routine
    \item Limit alcohol and avoid recreational drugs
    \item Seek help early when symptoms first appear
    \item Follow treatment plans and keep follow-up appointments
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a GP or mental health professional if symptoms persist for more than a couple of weeks or interfere with daily life. \textbf{Seek immediate help} if there are thoughts of suicide or self-harm: call emergency services on 000, contact Lifeline on 13 11 14, or go to the nearest hospital emergency department. Mental health crises are medical emergencies and can be treated.
