\chapter{Morphine}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Morphine is a potent opioid analgesic medication derived from the opium poppy (\textit{Papaver somniferum}). It is the gold standard agonist at the mu-opioid receptor and is primarily indicated for the management of severe acute pain (such as post-operative pain, myocardial infarction, and major trauma) and chronic severe pain, particularly in palliative care. Available in various formulations---including immediate-release and sustained-release oral preparations, intravenous, intramuscular, subcutaneous, and epidural injections---morphine acts directly on the central nervous system to alter the perception of and emotional response to pain. Due to its high potential for tolerance, dependence, and respiratory depression, its clinical use is strictly regulated as a Schedule 8 controlled drug in Australia.

\section*{Epidemiology}
As one of the oldest and most widely used strong opioids, morphine is key in pain management worldwide. In Australia, opioid prescribing rates have risen significantly over the past two decades, though clinical guidelines now advocate for more restricted prescribing in chronic non-cancer pain to mitigate the risk of opioid use disorder. According to Australian health statistics, morphine and other strong prescription opioids are associated with thousands of accidental poisonings and deaths annually, prompting the implementation of real-time prescription monitoring systems (such as SafeScript) across states. It remains a staple medication in hospital emergency departments, surgical units, and hospice settings.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The therapeutic effects and adverse profile of morphine are governed by its interaction with opioid receptors in the central and peripheral nervous systems.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mu-opioid receptor agonism:} Morphine binds selectively to mu-opioid receptors, which are G-protein-coupled receptors located in the brain, spinal cord, and gastrointestinal tract. This binding inhibits adenylyl cyclase, closes voltage-gated calcium channels, and opens potassium channels, hyperpolarising the neuron and reducing neurotransmitter release (such as substance P and glutamate).
    \item \textbf{Central pain modulation:} By acting on the periaqueductal grey matter and the superficial dorsal horn of the spinal cord, morphine blocks the ascending transmission of nociceptive signals and activates descending inhibitory pain pathways.
    \item \textbf{Hepatic metabolism:} Morphine undergoes extensive first-pass metabolism in the liver via glucuronidation. The primary metabolites are morphine-3-glucuronide (M3G), which possesses neuroexcitatory but no analgesic properties, and morphine-6-glucuronide (M6G), which is a highly potent analgesic agent.
    \item \textbf{Renal excretion:} The metabolites, including active M6G, are excreted primarily by the kidneys. Accumulation of these metabolites in patients with renal impairment can lead to profound toxicity, including respiratory depression and myoclonus.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of a patient receiving morphine includes both therapeutic pain relief and a characteristic profile of physiological side effects.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Analgesia and sedation:} Effective reduction in pain severity accompanied by drowsiness, mental clouding, and a sense of physical relaxation.
    \item \textbf{Respiratory changes:} Dose-dependent reduction in respiratory rate and tidal volume, which can progress to life-threatening respiratory depression in cases of overdose.
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal effects:} Constipation (due to decreased peristalsis and increased sphincter tone), nausea, vomiting, and dry mouth.
    \item \textbf{Miosis:} Pinpoint pupils resulting from stimulation of the Edinger-Westphal nucleus (oculomotor nerve).
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular and dermatological signs:} Mild hypotension, bradycardia, peripheral vasodilation, and pruritus (itching) caused by histamine release.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When managing patients who exhibit adverse signs or clinical deterioration while on morphine, clinicians must differentiate opioid-induced pathology from other clinical conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Opioid toxicity vs. sedative overdose:} Opioid toxicity (characterised by respiratory depression and miosis) must be distinguished from toxicity due to other sedatives like benzodiazepines, which typically do not cause pinpoint pupils or respond to naloxone.
    \item \textbf{Opioid-induced hyperalgesia (OIH) vs. disease progression:} OIH is a state of nociceptive sensitisation caused by chronic opioid exposure, which must be distinguished from disease progression or tolerance requiring a dose increase.
    \item \textbf{Opioid withdrawal vs. systemic infection:} Acute withdrawal features (such as sweating, tachycardia, rhinorrhoea, and abdominal cramps) can mimic early sepsis, influenza, or acute gastroenteritis.
    \item \textbf{Renal failure with metabolite accumulation vs. primary neurological disorders:} Neurological signs like myoclonus, delirium, or seizures in a patient on morphine may be due to renal impairment causing M3G accumulation, rather than primary neurological pathology.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients and carers should contact their healthcare provider if they experience unmanaged pain, severe constipation, persistent nausea, or significant drowsiness.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Extreme drowsiness, difficulty waking up, or loss of consciousness.
    \item Slow, shallow, or irregular breathing (less than 8 to 10 breaths per minute) or blue-tinged lips/fingernails.
    \item Severe, generalised itching accompanied by swelling of the face, tongue, or throat (suggestive of anaphylaxis).
    \item Sudden, severe muscle twitching (myoclonus) or seizures.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Monitoring safety and efficacy during morphine therapy requires structured diagnostic assessment and laboratory investigations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Renal function testing:} Serum urea, electrolytes, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) are essential, as renal impairment impairs metabolite clearance and increases toxicity risk.
    \item \textbf{Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis:} Indicated in acute respiratory depression to assess for hypercapnia and respiratory acidosis.
    \item \textbf{Pain assessment scale:} Continuous evaluation of pain severity using standardised visual analogue or numerical rating scales.
    \item \textbf{Sedation score and respiratory monitoring:} Frequent monitoring of the patient's respiratory rate and level of consciousness using scales like the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) or Pasero Opioid-Induced Sedation Scale (POSS).
    \item \textbf{Urine toxicology screening:} Used in chronic pain management settings to monitor compliance and screen for non-prescribed substances.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Safe administration of morphine involves tailored dosing, concurrent management of predictable side effects, and strict clinical oversight.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Individualised titration:} Dosing must be titrated to the lowest effective dose for the patient's pain, taking into account prior opioid exposure, age, and organ function.
    \item \textbf{Anticipatory side effect management:} Prescribing a stimulant laxative (e.g., senna) and stool softener (e.g., docusate) routinely from the start of therapy to prevent opioid-induced constipation, along with PRN antiemetics (e.g., metoclopramide) for nausea.
    \item \textbf{Overdose reversal:} Immediate administration of intravenous or intranasal naloxone (an opioid antagonist) in the event of life-threatening respiratory depression.
    \item \textbf{Opioid rotation:} Switching to alternative opioids (such as oxycodone or fentanyl) if the patient experiences intolerable side effects or inadequate pain control despite dose titration.
    \item \textbf{Non-pharmacological pain strategies:} Incorporating physiotherapy, psychological support (e.g., cognitive behavioural therapy), and regional anaesthetic techniques to reduce overall opioid requirements.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fatal respiratory depression:} The most critical acute complication of dose titration errors or intentional overdose.
    \item \textbf{Opioid use disorder (OUD):} Psychological and physical dependence characterised by impaired control over drug use, craving, and compulsive behaviours.
    \item \textbf{Opioid-induced bowel dysfunction:} Severe, chronic constipation that can lead to bowel obstruction, faecal impaction, or pseudo-obstruction.
    \item \textbf{Endocrine suppression:} Long-term use can suppress the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, leading to hypogonadism, sexual dysfunction, osteoporosis, and adrenal insufficiency.
    \item \textbf{Opioid-induced hyperalgesia:} Paradoxical increase in pain sensitivity with escalating doses of morphine.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for patients requiring morphine is highly dependent on the underlying cause of their pain. For acute pain and post-operative recovery, morphine provides excellent short-term pain relief, allowing early mobilisation and improving recovery times, with most patients successfully weaning off the drug within days. In palliative care, morphine is highly effective in managing cancer pain and dyspnoea, significantly enhancing quality of life during terminal phases. For chronic non-cancer pain, long-term outcomes are poorer due to the development of tolerance, dependence, and potential cognitive impairment, prompting clinical strategies that limit long-term use.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adherence and record-keeping:} Taking the medication strictly as prescribed, keeping a pain diary, and avoiding self-directed dose increases.
    \item \textbf{Safe storage and disposal:} Keeping morphine secure (preferably in a locked cabinet) to prevent accidental ingestion by children or diversion, and returning unused medication to a pharmacy.
    \item \textbf{Bowel regime adherence:} Maintaining adequate fluid intake, dietary fibre, and daily laxative use to manage opioid-induced constipation.
    \item \textbf{Avoiding central depressants:} Refraining from consuming alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other sedating medications unless specifically approved by the prescriber.
    \item \textbf{Cognitive awareness:} Avoiding driving or operating machinery when starting morphine, changing doses, or if feeling drowsy.
\end{itemize}
