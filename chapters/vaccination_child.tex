\index{Vaccination child}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Vaccination of children is the use of vaccines to protect infants, children, and adolescents against serious infectious diseases. Vaccines work by training the immune system to recognise and fight specific germs without causing the disease itself. Childhood immunisation is one of the most effective and safest public health measures ever developed, saving millions of lives worldwide each year. In many countries, the National Immunisation Program provides a schedule of recommended vaccines that begins at birth and continues through adolescence, covering diseases such as hepatitis B, diphtheria, tetanus, whooping cough (pertussis), polio, \textit{Haemophilus influenzae} type b (Hib), pneumococcal disease, rotavirus, measles, mumps, rubella, varicella (chickenpox), meningococcal disease, and human papillomavirus (HPV). The schedule is delivered through general practices, community clinics, and school-based programs, and the vaccines are provided free under the program. Routine childhood vaccination not only protects the individual child but also provides herd immunity, which helps protect vulnerable people who cannot be vaccinated, such as very young infants and those with weakened immune systems. The schedule is carefully timed so that each vaccine is given at the age when it is most needed and most effective, and the vaccines are all assessed for safety before they are added to the schedule. Serious side effects are extremely rare, and the benefits of protection far outweigh the small risks of vaccination.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Before vaccination, diseases such as measles, whooping cough, and polio caused widespread illness, disability, and death in children. Measles alone was estimated to infect millions of children globally each year, with a significant proportion developing pneumonia or brain inflammation. Polio paralysed thousands of children annually before the vaccine. In many countries, high immunisation coverage, currently around 93--95\% of five-year-olds, has eliminated polio, rubella, and measles as endemic diseases, and has dramatically reduced hospitalisations for pneumonia, whooping cough, and invasive meningococcal disease. However, these diseases have not disappeared. Outbreaks still occur when vaccination coverage falls, and imported cases are seen because the diseases remain common in other parts of the world. Whooping cough continues to cause periodic epidemics, particularly among infants too young to be fully vaccinated, which is why maternal vaccination during pregnancy and the early childhood doses are so important. Diseases do not respect national borders, and the ease of international travel means that unvaccinated children remain at risk of importing infections from countries where these diseases are still common. Maintaining high coverage is therefore a continuing, active effort rather than a completed achievement.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The diseases prevented by childhood vaccination are caused by viruses and bacteria that spread from person to person, usually through respiratory droplets, close contact, or contaminated surfaces and objects.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Measles, mumps, rubella:} highly contagious viruses spread through coughing and sneezing; measles is one of the most infectious diseases known
    \item \textbf{Whooping cough (pertussis):} a bacterium that causes severe coughing bouts and is most dangerous in young babies
    \item \textbf{Polio:} an enterovirus that can invade the nervous system and cause permanent paralysis
    \item \textbf{Diphtheria and tetanus:} bacteria that produce powerful toxins; tetanus enters through wounds
    \item \textbf{Hepatitis B:} a virus transmitted by blood and body fluids that can cause lifelong liver infection and liver cancer
    \item \textbf{Hib and pneumococcus:} bacteria that cause meningitis, pneumonia, and bloodstream infections in young children
    \item \textbf{Rotavirus:} a virus that causes severe diarrhoea and dehydration in infants
    \item \textbf{Varicella:} the chickenpox virus, which can cause serious skin infections and pneumonia
    \item \textbf{Meningococcus:} a bacterium that causes meningitis and septicaemia
    \item \textbf{HPV:} a virus spread by skin-to-skin contact that causes genital warts and later cancers of the cervix, mouth, and throat, and is now routinely vaccinated against in early adolescence
    \item \textbf{Influenza:} a seasonal respiratory virus that is particularly dangerous for children with asthma, heart conditions, and other chronic illnesses
\end{itemize}

Vaccines use weakened, inactivated, or partial components of these germs. The immune system responds by producing antibodies and memory cells, so that when the real germ is encountered, the body responds rapidly and destroys it before it can cause serious disease. This is the same way natural infection trains the immune system, but without the risk of the disease itself. Most childhood vaccines are given by injection, while the rotavirus vaccine is given as drops by mouth, and many are combined so that several protections are delivered in a single injection with fewer visits.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The relevant clinical features are those of the diseases the vaccines prevent, which in their worst forms are severe.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Measles causes fever, cough, runny nose, red watery eyes, and a blotchy rash
    \item Whooping cough causes long coughing bouts with a whooping noise, sometimes with vomiting and breath-holding in babies
    \item Polio can cause fever, neck stiffness, and floppy weakness or paralysis
    \item Diphtheria causes a grey membrane in the throat, breathing difficulty, and heart damage
    \item Meningococcal disease causes fever, a non-blanching rash, neck stiffness, and severe illness that progresses rapidly
    \item Rotavirus causes profuse watery diarrhoea, vomiting, and dehydration in babies
    \item Mumps causes painful swelling of the salivary glands
    \item Chickenpox causes an itchy blistering rash, with a risk of infected sores and pneumonia
    \item Hib and pneumococcal disease cause fever, meningitis, and pneumonia, with symptoms that can worsen quickly in a young child
\end{itemize}

A vaccinated child who is exposed to these germs is either protected completely or, at worst, experiences a much milder illness.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When a child presents with a fever and rash or other symptoms, clinicians consider many causes other than vaccine-preventable disease.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Viral illnesses such as roseola, hand, foot and mouth disease, and fifth disease
    \item Bacterial infections such as scarlet fever
    \item Allergic rashes, eczema, and heat rash
    \item Viral gastroenteritis, which can mimic rotavirus
    \item Croup, bronchiolitis, and other respiratory infections that can mimic whooping cough
    \item Vaccine reactions themselves, which are usually mild and self-limiting
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents should consult a doctor if their child develops a fever with a rash, has breathing difficulty, seems unusually drowsy or floppy, is not drinking or feeding, has a seizure, or is not responding to the normal comfort measures of an ill child.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, including rapid breathing, grunting, or flaring nostrils
    \item A non-blanching rash, which does not fade when pressed with a glass, as this may indicate meningococcal disease
    \item Seizures
    \item A child who is difficult to wake, unusually floppy, or confused
    \item Severe dehydration with a dry mouth, no wet nappies, and sunken eyes
    \item Blue or very pale lips and skin
    \item Fever in a baby under three months old
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Most vaccine-preventable infections are diagnosed clinically, with laboratory confirmation used to guide public health responses.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} a careful history of vaccination status, exposure to sick contacts, and symptom pattern, including the timing of any rash relative to the fever
    \item \textbf{Nasal or throat swabs:} polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing can confirm measles, whooping cough, and other respiratory viruses
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} for suspected meningococcal disease, blood cultures are taken urgently
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture:} cerebrospinal fluid is sampled if meningitis is suspected
    \item \textbf{Notification:} doctors must notify public health authorities of cases of measles, pertussis, and meningococcal disease so that contacts can be protected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of vaccine-preventable diseases is supportive care for the sick child, together with urgent treatment where specific therapies exist, and the main strategy is prevention.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} fluids, paracetamol or ibuprofen for fever and discomfort, and rest
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} for bacterial infections such as whooping cough and meningococcal disease
    \item \textbf{Antivirals:} rarely used, but available in some situations for severe varicella
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} for severe dehydration, breathing problems, seizures, or suspected meningitis or septicaemia
    \item \textbf{Isolation:} keeping a child with measles, chickenpox, or whooping cough away from others to prevent spread
    \item \textbf{Contact management:} antibiotics or vaccination for close contacts where appropriate
    \item \textbf{Catch-up vaccination:} children who have missed scheduled doses should be vaccinated at the earliest opportunity according to a catch-up schedule, which a doctor or nurse can arrange
    \item \textbf{Managing vaccine reactions:} a sore arm or leg, mild fever, and irritability after vaccination can be relieved with paracetamol or ibuprofen and extra comforting; these reactions usually settle within a day or two
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pneumonia and ear infections, common complications of measles and whooping cough
    \item Brain inflammation (encephalitis), a rare but serious complication of measles, mumps, and varicella
    \item Permanent paralysis from polio
    \item Meningitis and septicaemia, which can be fatal, from meningococcal and Hib infection
    \item Severe dehydration from rotavirus, especially in infants
    \item Liver damage and cancer from hepatitis B
    \item Subacute sclerosing panencephalitis, a rare fatal brain disease years after measles
    \item Cancers of the cervix, mouth, and throat from HPV acquired in adolescence
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for vaccinated children is excellent: they are protected against diseases that once caused disability and death. When a vaccine-preventable disease does occur in an unvaccinated or partially vaccinated child, the outcome depends on the disease and how quickly treatment begins. Measles in a healthy vaccinated child is usually mild, but in an unvaccinated child it can be severe, with pneumonia and brain inflammation possible. Whooping cough is most dangerous in the first months of life, and babies can die from it or from its complications. Meningococcal disease can be fatal within hours, which is why the childhood meningococcal vaccine is so important. Overall, routine vaccination is one of the most effective interventions in medicine, and fully vaccinated children have the best possible protection. Even a child who falls behind can catch up, and the protection gained is the same as if the doses had been given on time.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Follow the National Immunisation Program schedule from birth, and keep an up-to-date record of vaccinations, such as the local Immunisation Register
    \item Use the free vaccines provided through the program at GP clinics, community health services, and school-based programs
    \item Get vaccinated on time; delays leave a child unprotected during the window of risk
    \item Ensure pregnant women receive the whooping cough and influenza vaccines, which protect the newborn from birth
    \item Practise good hygiene, including handwashing and covering coughs, to reduce the spread of infection
    \item Keep children at home when they are unwell with a fever, rash, or cough to avoid infecting others
    \item Discuss any concerns about vaccination with a doctor or nurse, who can provide accurate information
    \item Catch up any missed doses as soon as possible, and take advantage of school-based vaccination programs
\end{itemize}
