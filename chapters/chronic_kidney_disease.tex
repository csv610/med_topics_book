\chapter{Chronic Kidney Disease}
\index{Chronic Kidney Disease}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a long-term condition in which the kidneys are damaged and gradually lose their ability to filter waste products and excess fluid from the blood. It is defined by evidence of kidney damage or a reduced filtering rate (glomerular filtration rate, or GFR) that has been present for three months or longer.

The kidneys perform many vital jobs: removing waste and extra fluid, balancing salts and minerals, helping to control blood pressure, producing hormones that stimulate red blood cell production, and keeping bones strong. In CKD these functions decline, often silently. The condition is graded from stage 1 (mild, with evidence of damage but normal filtration) through to stage 5 (kidney failure, sometimes called end-stage kidney disease), when dialysis or a kidney transplant may be needed.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Chronic kidney disease is common worldwide, and many people do not know they have it because early disease often causes no symptoms. Risk is higher with diabetes, high blood pressure, cardiovascular disease, a family history of kidney disease, some infections or autoimmune diseases, and exposure to kidney-harming medicines. The burden and causes vary between populations and health systems.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The most common causes of CKD are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diabetes:} prolonged high blood sugar damages the small blood vessels of the filtering units (glomeruli); this is the commonest cause worldwide
    \item \textbf{High blood pressure:} long-standing hypertension damages the kidney's blood vessels and is the second most common cause
    \item \textbf{Glomerulonephritis:} a group of inflammatory conditions that directly affect the kidney filters
    \item \textbf{Polycystic kidney disease:} an inherited condition in which fluid-filled cysts gradually replace normal kidney tissue
    \item \textbf{Recurrent kidney infections} or reflux of urine back into the kidneys (vesicoureteral reflux)
    \item \textbf{Obstruction:} blockage of urine flow, for example by an enlarged prostate, kidney stones, or tumours
    \item \textbf{Certain medications:} long-term use of some pain relievers (NSAIDs), some antibiotics, lithium, and other drugs
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune disease:} conditions such as lupus can inflame the kidneys
\end{itemize}

As kidney tissue is lost, the remaining filters work harder and become scarred. Even when the original cause is treated, this scarring can continue to progress, so the filtering capacity falls over time. Wastes such as urea and creatinine build up in the blood, fluid accumulates, and complications such as anaemia, bone disease, and high blood pressure develop.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Early CKD usually causes no symptoms at all, and the condition is often found on routine blood or urine tests. As kidney function declines, symptoms may include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Fatigue, weakness, and poor concentration
    \item Swelling (oedema) of the ankles, feet, or face from fluid retention
    \item Reduced urine output, or urine that is frothy, bloody, or dark
    \item Itchy skin
    \item Loss of appetite, nausea, or an unpleasant metallic taste in the mouth
    \item Shortness of breath
    \item Muscle cramps, especially at night
    \item Trouble sleeping and restless legs
    \item High blood pressure that becomes harder to control
    \item Anaemia, causing tiredness and breathlessness on exertion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When kidney problems are found, other conditions need to be considered:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute kidney injury -- a sudden, often reversible fall in kidney function over days
    \item Dehydration or low blood flow to the kidneys
    \item Urinary tract infection or pyelonephritis
    \item Kidney stones or obstruction of urine flow
    \item Nephrotic syndrome or nephritic syndromes
    \item Normal ageing changes, which reduce kidney filtration more slowly
    \item Medication effects on the kidneys or on test results
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with risk factors for CKD -- including diabetes, high blood pressure, cardiovascular disease, or a family history of kidney disease -- should ask a clinician whether regular kidney checks are needed. See a doctor promptly if you develop persistent swelling, blood in the urine, frothy urine, or symptoms such as unexplained fatigue, poor appetite, or nausea.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe breathlessness or chest pain
    \item Passing very little or no urine
    \item Confusion, seizures, or loss of consciousness
    \item Severe swelling associated with breathing difficulty
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Chronic kidney disease is diagnosed with simple blood and urine tests, and is usually confirmed in primary care:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{eGFR blood test:} an estimate of the kidney filtration rate calculated from creatinine in the blood; a level below 60 mL/min/1.73m$^2$ sustained for three months suggests CKD
    \item \textbf{Urine albumin-creatinine ratio (ACR):} measures protein (albumin) leaking into the urine, a marker of kidney damage
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure measurement} and regular monitoring
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} urea, creatinine, electrolytes, calcium, phosphate, and full blood count to detect anaemia
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound of the kidneys:} to check size, shape, scarring, cysts, or obstruction
    \item \textbf{Specialist tests:} kidney biopsy, CT scan, or specific blood tests may be arranged when the cause is unclear or a treatable condition is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management aims to slow the loss of kidney function, treat the underlying cause, and prevent complications:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure control:} the single most important measure -- usually with medicines such as ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers, which also protect the kidneys
    \item \textbf{Diabetes control:} good blood sugar management slows progression
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} some people benefit from an SGLT2 inhibitor, a medicine originally developed for diabetes that also protects the kidneys
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} a healthy diet, limiting salt, regular physical activity, weight management, and quitting smoking
    \item \textbf{Cholesterol-lowering therapy:} usually a statin, where indicated
    \item \textbf{Dietary advice:} from a dietitian, who may recommend limits on salt, potassium, phosphate, or protein as kidney function falls
    \item \textbf{Anaemia management:} iron supplements and sometimes other agents under medical supervision
    \item \textbf{Bone health:} monitoring calcium, phosphate, and vitamin D levels
    \item \textbf{Kidney replacement therapy:} dialysis (haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis) or kidney transplant when advanced kidney failure develops, after discussion with a kidney specialist
    \item \textbf{Regular review:} with a general practitioner and, as the condition advances, a nephrologist
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progression to kidney failure (stage 5), requiring dialysis or transplant
    \item High blood pressure and its effects, including stroke and heart attack
    \item Cardiovascular disease -- the leading cause of death in people with CKD
    \item Anaemia, causing fatigue and breathlessness
    \item Bone and mineral disorders, including bone pain and fractures
    \item Fluid overload, leading to swelling and breathing difficulty
    \item Electrolyte disturbances, including dangerously high potassium
    \item Reduced immune function and increased risk of infection
    \item Psychological effects, including depression and reduced quality of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Chronic kidney disease usually progresses slowly, over many years, and in many people it never reaches kidney failure. The rate of decline depends on the cause, how well blood pressure and diabetes are controlled, and whether complications are managed. With good treatment, many people with early CKD maintain their normal lives for decades.

People with stage 5 CKD require dialysis or a transplant to survive; a successful transplant offers the best long-term quality of life and survival. Even among people who need dialysis, many live active lives for many years. The overall outlook for people with CKD has improved substantially in recent decades as treatment has become more effective, and early detection offers the best chance of slowing progression.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Much of CKD can be prevented or slowed:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep blood pressure in a healthy range and check it regularly
    \item Manage diabetes carefully, with regular blood glucose checks
    \item Eat a balanced diet with limited salt
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and stay physically active
    \item Do not smoke and limit alcohol
    \item Follow individualized fluid advice; some people with advanced CKD, swelling, heart failure, or dialysis need a fluid limit
    \item Use over-the-counter pain relievers sparingly, and check with a doctor before long-term use
    \item Have regular kidney checks (blood and urine tests) if you have risk factors
    \item Take prescribed medicines as directed and attend follow-up appointments
\end{itemize}
