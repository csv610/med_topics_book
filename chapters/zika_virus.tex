\chapter{Zika virus}
\index{Zika virus}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Zika virus is an enveloped, single-stranded RNA virus belonging to the family Flaviviridae and the genus Flavivirus. It is an arthropod-borne virus (arbovirus) primarily transmitted to humans through the bites of infected Aedes mosquitoes, most notably \textit{Aedes aegypti}. Historically classified as a neglected tropical pathogen that caused only sporadic, mild infections in equatorial Africa and Asia, Zika virus has recently emerged as a global public health threat of significant concern. The discovery of the virus dates back to April 1947, when it was first isolated from a sentinel rhesus macaque monkey in the Zika Forest near Entebbe, Uganda. For nearly six decades following its discovery, human cases remained extremely rare, with fewer than twenty documented cases in the medical literature, presenting primarily as a self-limiting, mild febrile illness.

The epidemiological landscape of Zika virus underwent a dramatic paradigm shift in the early 21st century. The first major outbreak outside its historical range occurred on the island of Yap in Micronesia in 2007, followed by a massive epidemic in French Polynesia and other Pacific islands in 2013--2014. The most significant event, however, occurred in 2015 when the virus entered the Americas, leading to an explosive pandemic that affected millions of individuals across South and Central America, the Caribbean, and parts of North America. It was during this pandemic that clinical researchers established a causal link between maternal Zika virus infection during pregnancy and severe fetal abnormalities, most notably congenital microcephaly, as well as an increased incidence of Guillain-Barr\'e syndrome in adults.

The realization that a mosquito-borne virus could cross the placental barrier and cause severe, irreversible birth defects prompted the World Health Organization to declare the Zika virus epidemic a Public Health Emergency of International Concern in February 2016. This chapter provides a comprehensive, evidence-based review of the Zika virus, detailing its epidemiology, viral biology, clinical manifestations, diagnostic protocols, and management strategies. It serves as an authoritative reference for clinicians, medical writers, and health educators seeking to understand this multi-system pathogen.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of Zika virus has transitioned from a localized, enzootic cycle in African forests to a global, urban epidemic cycle involving billions of at-risk individuals. Following its initial isolation in Uganda in 1947, serological surveys indicated that human exposure to the virus was widespread in tropical regions of Africa and Asia, including Nigeria, India, Indonesia, Malaysia, and the Philippines. However, because the clinical presentation of Zika was indistinguishable from other circulating tropical fevers and generally mild, the virus was rarely diagnosed or reported.

The 2007 Yap Island outbreak marked the beginning of Zika's global expansion. During this outbreak, approximately 73\% of the island's population aged three and older were infected, demonstrating the high susceptibility of immunologically naive populations. A subsequent outbreak in French Polynesia in 2013--2014 infected an estimated 30,000 people. This outbreak was clinically significant because it was the first time health authorities observed a statistical rise in Guillain-Barr\'e syndrome, a rare autoimmune neurological condition, temporally linked to Zika virus infection.

The virus reached the Americas in early 2015, with Brazil reporting the first cases of local transmission. By late 2015, millions of people had contracted the virus, and Brazilian clinicians noticed an unprecedented surge in newborns with microcephaly and other congenital neurological disorders. The epidemic spread rapidly due to the wide distribution of its primary vector, \textit{Aedes aegypti}, and the lack of pre-existing immunity in the population. Over 80 countries and territories have since reported evidence of vector-borne Zika virus transmission. While the epidemic subsided by late 2017 due to the development of widespread herd immunity, sporadic outbreaks continue to occur, and the virus remains endemic in many tropical and subtropical areas.

The primary populations affected by Zika virus are residents of and travelers to tropical and subtropical regions. Socioeconomic factors heavily influence the epidemiology of the disease; individuals living in areas with poor sanitation, lack of screened windows, inadequate municipal waste management, and stagnant water are at a significantly higher risk of exposure to the vector. Pregnant women represent the demographic of highest clinical concern because vertical transmission can occur during any stage of pregnancy, with devastating consequences for the fetus.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The transmission, viral replication, and pathogenic mechanisms of the Zika virus involve complex interactions between the host, vector, and viral proteins.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Viral Structure and Classification}: Zika virus is a member of the Flaviviridae family, genus Flavivirus. It possesses a positive-sense, single-stranded RNA genome of approximately 11,000 nucleotides. The genome is surrounded by an icosahedral nucleocapsid, a lipid envelope, and two surface proteins: the envelope (E) protein and the membrane (M) protein. The E protein is the principal immunogen and plays a critical role in cellular attachment, receptor binding, and subsequent membrane fusion during viral entry.
\item \textbf{Vector-Borne Transmission}: The principal mode of transmission is the bite of an infected female mosquito of the genus Aedes, primarily \textit{Aedes aegypti}. \textit{Aedes albopictus}, known as the Asian tiger mosquito, is also a competent vector and has a broader geographic distribution, raising concerns about the potential spread of Zika to temperate regions. These mosquitoes are highly adapted to urban settings, typically breed in small collections of standing water, and are daytime feeders, with peak biting activity occurring during the early morning and late afternoon.
\item \textbf{Vertical and Intrapartum Transmission}: Unlike most other flaviviruses, Zika virus can cross the placental barrier. Vertical transmission from mother to fetus can occur during any trimester of pregnancy. The virus infects placental cells, including trophoblasts and Hofbauer cells (placental macrophages), allowing it to access the fetal bloodstream. Intrapartum transmission can also occur during childbirth if the mother is viraemic.
\item \textbf{Sexual Transmission}: Zika virus can be transmitted sexually through vaginal, anal, or oral intercourse. The virus replicates and persists in the male reproductive tract, particularly in semen, for several months after the resolution of clinical symptoms. Viral RNA has also been detected in vaginal secretions and saliva, enabling transmission from both symptomatic and asymptomatic partners.
\item \textbf{Blood-Borne and Other Routes}: Zika virus can be transmitted via blood transfusions, organ transplantation, and laboratory accidents. Although viral RNA has been detected in breast milk, transmission through breastfeeding has not been documented, and the health benefits of breastfeeding are considered to outweigh any theoretical risk of transmission.
\item \textbf{Cellular Tropism and Pathogenesis}: Following the bite of an infected mosquito, the virus replicates locally in skin cells, including dermal fibroblasts, epidermal keratinocytes, and immature dendritic cells (Langerhans cells). Viral entry is mediated by specific cell-surface receptors, such as the AXL receptor tyrosine kinase. The virus then travels through the lymphatic system to the bloodstream, establishing viraemia and disseminating to peripheral organs, including the brain, eyes, and reproductive tracts.
\item \textbf{Neurotropism and Brain Pathology}: Zika virus exhibits a high affinity for neural tissue. It specifically targets neural progenitor cells (NPCs), radial glial cells, and brain organoids. Once inside NPCs, the virus interferes with the cell cycle, induces apoptosis (programmed cell death), and impairs cell differentiation. This disrupts normal cortical development, leading to severe brain abnormalities, cerebral calcifications, and microcephaly.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of Zika virus infection ranges from asymptomatic seroconversion to acute, self-limiting febrile illness, and severe neurological or congenital syndrome.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Asymptomatic Infection}: Approximately 80\% of individuals infected with Zika virus do not develop clinical symptoms. These asymptomatic individuals can still transmit the virus sexually or through blood donation, which presents a significant challenge for public health control.
\item \textbf{Acute Illness}: For those who do develop symptoms, the incubation period is estimated to be 3 to 14 days. The illness is typically mild and self-limiting, with symptoms lasting between 2 and 7 days.
\item \textbf{Maculopapular Rash}: A hallmark feature of acute Zika infection is a generalized, pruritic (itchy) maculopapular rash. The rash typically begins on the face and spreads downwards to the trunk, arms, legs, and occasionally the palms of the hands and soles of the feet.
\item \textbf{Low-Grade Fever}: Patients often present with a mild fever, usually below 38.5 degrees Celsius (101.3 degrees Fahrenheit). This is in contrast to the high, spiking fevers typical of dengue or malaria.
\item \textbf{Non-Purulent Conjunctivitis}: Bilateral conjunctival injection (red eyes) without purulent discharge is common. It is often accompanied by eye pain, irritation, and mild photophobia.
\item \textbf{Arthralgia and Myalgia}: Joint pain and muscle aches are common, typically affecting the small joints of the hands and feet. The joint pain is generally mild to moderate, unlike the severe, debilitating joint pain associated with chikungunya.
\item \textbf{Systemic Symptoms}: Other symptoms include retro-orbital pain, headache, asthenia (weakness), and general malaise.
\item \textbf{Congenital Zika Syndrome (CZS)}: Maternal infection during pregnancy can result in a distinct pattern of severe birth defects known as congenital Zika syndrome. The features of CZS include:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item Severe microcephaly (head circumference significantly below average, reflecting incomplete brain development).
  \item Partially collapsed skull with redundant scalp skin and overlapping cranial sutures.
  \item Thin subcortical brain tissue, ventriculomegaly, and prominent intracranial calcifications.
  \item Ocular damage, including chorioretinal scarring, focal retinal pigmentary mottling, and optic nerve hypoplasia.
  \item Congenital joint contractures (arthrogryposis) and clubfoot, resulting from damage to the fetal central nervous system.
  \item Neurological deficits, including severe developmental delays, hypertonia, spasticity, and difficulty swallowing.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Given the non-specific nature of the symptoms associated with acute Zika virus infection, it is essential to distinguish it from other infectious diseases that present with fever, rash, and joint pain.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Dengue Fever}: Dengue is a primary differential diagnosis because it is transmitted by the same mosquito vectors in overlapping geographic areas. Dengue is typically characterized by a much higher fever, severe retro-orbital pain, severe myalgia and arthralgia (often referred to as ``breakbone fever''), leukopenia, and thrombocytopenia. Severe cases can progress to dengue haemorrhagic fever or dengue shock syndrome, which are life-threatening.
\item \textbf{Chikungunya}: Also transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes, chikungunya is characterized by the sudden onset of high fever and severe, debilitating, often symmetric polyarthralgia. The joint pain is typically much more intense than that of Zika, can involve significant joint swelling, and may persist for months or even years as chronic arthritis.
\item \textbf{Malaria}: Malaria must be ruled out immediately in any patient presenting with a febrile illness after traveling to a malaria-endemic region. It typically presents with high, cyclical fevers, severe chills, sweating, splenomegaly, and anemia. Unlike Zika, malaria does not usually present with a rash or conjunctivitis and can be rapidly fatal if untreated.
\item \textbf{Rubella (German Measles)}: Rubella presents with a maculopapular rash, low-grade fever, and posterior auricular or suboccipital lymphadenopathy. Like Zika, rubella infection during pregnancy can cause severe congenital anomalies (congenital rubella syndrome), including cataracts, deafness, and congenital heart defects.
\item \textbf{Measles}: Measles is characterized by high fever, cough, coryza, and prominent conjunctivitis with discharge, followed by Koplik spots on the buccal mucosa and a classic maculopapular rash that starts at the hairline and becomes confluent.
\item \textbf{Parvovirus B19}: Parvovirus B19 infection can cause a characteristic ``slapped cheek'' rash in children and acute, symmetric joint pain in adults, mimicking the clinical features of Zika. In pregnant women, it can cause fetal hydrops but does not typically cause microcephaly.
\item \textbf{Leptospirosis}: This bacterial zoonotic infection can present with fever, headache, myalgia (especially in the calves), conjunctival suffusion (redness without discharge), and rash. It is typically associated with exposure to water contaminated with animal urine.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Understanding when to consult a healthcare provider or seek emergency medical attention is critical for the appropriate management of Zika virus and its potential complications.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Routine Medical Consultation}: Any individual who develops a fever, rash, joint pain, or red eyes within two weeks of traveling to or residing in an area with active Zika virus transmission should seek medical advice. It is especially critical for pregnant women who have traveled to or live in a Zika-affected region to consult their obstetrician, regardless of whether they have experienced symptoms.
\item \textbf{Post-Exposure Consultation}: Individuals who have had unprotected sexual contact with a partner who resides in or has traveled to a Zika-endemic area should consult a doctor to discuss testing and appropriate prevention measures, such as condom use.
\item \textbf{Emergency Medical Attention}: Immediate emergency medical care is required if a patient experiences symptoms suggestive of Guillain-Barr\'e syndrome or other severe neurological complications. Warning signs that necessitate calling emergency services include:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item Rapidly progressive weakness, numbness, or tingling in the legs, feet, or hands that spreads to the arms and upper body.
  \item Difficulty breathing, shortness of breath, or inability to take a deep breath.
  \item Difficulty swallowing, speaking, or moving the facial muscles.
  \item Confusion, altered mental status, severe lethargy, or loss of consciousness.
  \item Severe, unremitting headache accompanied by neck stiffness or sensitivity to light.
  \end{itemize}
If any of these life-threatening symptoms occur, contact emergency services immediately. In many countries, call local emergency services; in the United States and Canada, call 911; in the United Kingdom, call 999; or contact your local emergency number.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Accurate diagnosis of Zika virus infection requires a combination of clinical assessment, travel history evaluation, and laboratory investigations. Because the clinical features of Zika overlap significantly with dengue and chikungunya, laboratory confirmation is essential.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Clinical and Travel Evaluation}: Healthcare providers must obtain a detailed travel history, including specific destinations and dates of travel, to assess the risk of exposure. History of sexual contact with travelers to or residents of Zika-affected areas is also crucial.
\item \textbf{Molecular Testing (RT-PCR)}: Reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) is the gold standard for diagnosing acute Zika virus infection. It detects the genetic material (RNA) of the virus in clinical specimens.
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Serum}: RT-PCR should be performed on serum within 7 days of symptom onset.
  \item \textbf{Urine}: RT-PCR is recommended on urine specimens up to 14 days after symptom onset. Urine testing is often preferred because the virus is shed in higher concentrations and for a longer period in urine than in serum.
  \item \textbf{Other Specimens}: In specific clinical scenarios, RT-PCR can be performed on amniotic fluid, cerebrospinal fluid, semen, or placental tissue.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Serological Testing (MAC-ELISA)}: For patients presenting more than 7 days after the onset of symptoms, serology is used to detect Zika-specific immunoglobulin M (IgM) antibodies using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA).
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Flavivirus Cross-Reactivity}: A major challenge with serological testing is cross-reactivity. IgM antibodies produced in response to Zika virus can cross-react with other flaviviruses, such as dengue, West Nile, yellow fever, or Japanese encephalitis. This is particularly problematic in individuals who have had prior flavivirus infections or vaccinations.
  \item \textbf{Timing}: Zika IgM antibodies typically become detectable around 4 to 7 days after symptom onset and can persist in the blood for several months, making it difficult to determine the exact timing of infection.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Plaque Reduction Neutralization Test (PRNT)}: PRNT is a highly specific confirmatory test used to measure virus-specific neutralizing antibodies. It is employed to resolve false-positive or cross-reactive ELISA results. A fourfold or greater increase in neutralizing antibody titers against Zika, compared to other flaviviruses, confirms the diagnosis. PRNT is complex and typically performed only in specialized reference laboratories.
\item \textbf{Prenatal Monitoring and Imaging}: If a pregnant woman is diagnosed with Zika virus infection or exposure, close monitoring of the fetus is required:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Serial Fetal Ultrasonography}: High-resolution obstetric ultrasounds should be performed every 3 to 4 weeks to monitor fetal growth and head circumference, and to screen for structural abnormalities such as intracranial calcifications, brain atrophy, ventriculomegaly, or abnormal posture.
  \item \textbf{Fetal Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)}: If ultrasound findings are ambiguous or suspicious, a fetal MRI can be performed in the late second or third trimester to obtain highly detailed images of the fetal brain structure.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
There is currently no specific antiviral treatment or cure for Zika virus infection. The primary goal of management is supportive care to alleviate symptoms and prevent complications.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Supportive Care for Acute Infection}:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Rest}: Adequate bed rest is recommended to support the body's immune response and promote recovery.
  \item \textbf{Hydration}: Patients should drink plenty of fluids (water, oral rehydration solutions, or clear broths) to prevent dehydration, especially if a fever is present.
  \item \textbf{Analgesics and Antipyretics}: Paracetamol (acetaminophen) is the preferred medication for managing fever, joint pain, and muscle aches.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Avoidance of NSAIDs and Aspirin}: It is critical to avoid non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen, naproxen, and aspirin until dengue fever has been ruled out by laboratory testing. If the patient has dengue, the use of NSAIDs or aspirin can significantly increase the risk of severe bleeding and haemorrhagic complications. Additionally, aspirin should not be given to children due to the risk of Reye's syndrome.
\item \textbf{Management of Guillain-Barr\'e Syndrome}: If a patient develops Guillain-Barr\'e syndrome, hospital admission is required. Patients must be monitored closely for respiratory muscle weakness. Treatment options include:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG)}: Administering healthy antibodies to block the autoantibodies causing the nerve damage.
  \item \textbf{Plasmapheresis (Plasma Exchange)}: Filtering the blood to remove the autoantibodies that are attacking the peripheral nerves.
  \item \textbf{Supportive Care}: Respiratory support (mechanical ventilation) if breathing muscles are compromised, along with physical therapy and rehabilitation.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Management of Congenital Zika Syndrome}: Infants born with congenital Zika syndrome require a highly coordinated, multidisciplinary approach to address their complex physical and developmental needs.
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Multidisciplinary Medical Team}: Care should be managed by pediatricians, pediatric neurologists, ophthalmologists, audiologists, physical therapists, speech-language pathologists, and occupational therapists.
  \item \textbf{Developmental Support}: Early intervention programs are essential to help children reach their developmental potential. This includes physical therapy to manage muscle stiffness (hypertonia) and improve motor skills.
  \item \textbf{Symptomatic Treatments}: Medications may be prescribed to manage complications such as seizures, feeding difficulties (often requiring gastrostomy tube placement), and severe irritability.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While Zika virus infection is typically a mild illness, it can lead to severe, life-altering complications, particularly in pregnant women and newborns.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Congenital Zika Syndrome}: This is the most significant and devastating complication of Zika virus infection. Maternal infection during pregnancy leads to placental infection and transmission to the fetus, resulting in severe brain damage, microcephaly, ocular defects, hearing loss, and skeletal deformities in the newborn. The risk of these complications is highest if infection occurs in the first trimester, but abnormalities can occur following infection at any stage of pregnancy.
\item \textbf{Guillain-Barr\'e Syndrome (GBS)}: Zika virus infection is associated with an increased incidence of GBS, a rare but serious neurological condition where the body's immune system mistakenly attacks its own peripheral nerves. GBS causes progressive muscle weakness, tingling, and paralysis, which can affect the chest muscles and lead to acute respiratory failure.
\item \textbf{Adverse Pregnancy Outcomes}: Zika virus infection during pregnancy increases the risk of other adverse outcomes, including spontaneous abortion (miscarriage), stillbirth, fetal growth restriction, and placental insufficiency.
\item \textbf{Neurological Conditions}: In addition to GBS, Zika virus has been associated with other central nervous system complications in adults and older children, including encephalitis (inflammation of the brain), myelitis (inflammation of the spinal cord), and acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM).
\item \textbf{Ocular Complications}: Infected adults can occasionally develop ocular complications, including uveitis (inflammation of the middle layer of the eye) or conjunctival damage. In congenital infections, severe structural eye defects can result in permanent visual impairment or blindness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals infected with Zika virus varies dramatically depending on the patient's demographic and the presence of neurological complications.

For the vast majority of healthy adults and children who contract Zika virus, the prognosis is excellent. The acute illness is mild, self-limiting, and resolves completely within 2 to 7 days. Unlike other mosquito-borne viruses like chikungunya, which can cause chronic, long-term joint pain, Zika virus rarely causes persistent symptoms or long-term complications. Once an individual has cleared the infection, they are believed to develop long-term immunity against reinfection.

For individuals who develop Guillain-Barr\'e syndrome, the prognosis is generally favorable, but recovery can be a slow and arduous process. Most patients make a full recovery, although it can take several months or even years. Approximately 20\% to 30\% of GBS patients experience persistent muscle weakness or other neurological symptoms. The mortality rate for GBS is about 3\% to 5\%, typically due to respiratory failure, cardiac arrhythmias, or secondary infections.

For infants born with congenital Zika syndrome, the prognosis is poor. The structural brain damage and microcephaly associated with CZS are irreversible, permanent conditions. These children face severe, lifelong intellectual and physical disabilities, developmental delays, and motor impairments. They require intensive, lifelong medical care and supportive therapies. The long-term survival rate is reduced, with many deaths occurring in early childhood due to complications such as aspiration pneumonia, respiratory failure, or severe seizures.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
In the absence of a vaccine or specific antiviral treatment, prevention is the primary strategy to control Zika virus transmission and protect individuals at risk.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Vector Control and Personal Protection}: Reducing exposure to mosquitoes is key to preventing infection in endemic areas.
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Insect Repellents}: Apply EPA-registered insect repellents containing active ingredients such as DEET, picaridin, IR3535, or oil of lemon eucalyptus to exposed skin and clothing. Follow product instructions carefully, especially when applying to children.
  \item \textbf{Protective Clothing}: Wear long-sleeved shirts, long trousers, socks, and hats to minimize exposed skin, particularly during peak mosquito biting hours (daytime).
  \item \textbf{Mosquito Nets}: Use insecticide-treated bed nets if sleeping in rooms that are not air-conditioned or lack window screens.
  \item \textbf{Environmental Source Reduction}: Weekly check, empty, and clean any containers around the home that can hold standing water, such as buckets, flowerpots, birdbaths, and gutters, to prevent mosquitoes from laying eggs.
  \item \textbf{Home Screening}: Install window and door screens to keep mosquitoes out of living spaces.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Prevention of Sexual Transmission}: Because the virus can persist in semen and vaginal fluids, precautions must be taken to prevent transmission to sexual partners.
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Travelers Returning from Endemic Areas}: Men who have traveled to a Zika-affected area should practice safe sex (using condoms consistently and correctly) or abstain from sex for at least 3 months after returning, regardless of whether they had symptoms. Women should follow the same precautions for at least 2 months.
  \item \textbf{During Pregnancy}: Couples with a partner who lives in or has traveled to a Zika-endemic area should use condoms or abstain from sex for the entire duration of the pregnancy to protect the fetus.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Travel Planning and Advisories}: Pregnant women should avoid traveling to any area with active Zika virus transmission. Women planning a pregnancy should consult a healthcare provider before traveling and wait the recommended period (2 months for women, 3 months for men) after returning before attempting to conceive.
\item \textbf{Vaccine Status}: Extensive research is ongoing to develop a safe and effective vaccine against the Zika virus. While several vaccine candidates have entered clinical trials, none are currently approved for public use.
\end{itemize}
