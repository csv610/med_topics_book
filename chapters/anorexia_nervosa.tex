\chapter{Anorexia Nervosa}
\index{Anorexia Nervosa}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Anorexia nervosa is a serious eating disorder in which a person deliberately restricts their food intake, becomes significantly underweight, and experiences an intense fear of gaining weight together with a distorted perception of their body size and shape. It is a mental illness, not a lifestyle choice, and it affects both how the person views themselves and how every organ system of the body functions, because prolonged starvation damages nearly every tissue.

Anorexia nervosa carries the highest death rate of any psychiatric disorder, largely through the physical consequences of malnutrition and through suicide. Despite this, it is treatable: with early recognition and evidence-based treatment, many people -- especially adolescents -- make a full recovery. Two subtypes are recognised: a restricting type, in which weight loss is achieved mainly through dieting and fasting, and a binge-eating/purging type, in which restriction is interspersed with episodes of bingeing and purging.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Anorexia nervosa affects approximately 1 in 100 to 1 in 200 people, and it is substantially more common in females, who account for the large majority of diagnosed cases. It can, however, occur in males, where it is frequently under-recognised and under-treated. The disorder most commonly begins in adolescence and young adulthood, typically between the ages of 13 and 20, although it can develop at any age.

Certain groups are at higher risk, including dancers, models, and athletes in weight-sensitive sports such as gymnastics, long-distance running, and wrestling. The condition is seen across all social backgrounds and in many countries, and it frequently coexists with depression, anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive traits.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Anorexia nervosa has no single cause; it develops from an interaction of genetic, psychological, and sociocultural factors:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} the condition runs in families, and heritability is estimated at roughly 50--60\%
    \item \textbf{Personality traits:} perfectionism, high achievement, rigidity, and low self-esteem are common predisposing features
    \item \textbf{Cultural and social pressure:} media and social expectations of thinness can act as triggers
    \item \textbf{Life events:} stress, loss, bullying, relationship problems, or the onset of puberty may precede the illness
    \item \textbf{Psychological factors:} restricting food can feel like a way of gaining control or managing painful emotions
    \item \textbf{Brain chemistry:} starvation alters brain function, and the resulting changes reinforce the restrictive behaviour, creating a self-perpetuating cycle
\end{itemize}

Physiologically, severe calorie restriction leads to weight loss, loss of menstrual periods in females, a slowed metabolism, low blood pressure, low bone density, and changes in hormones, blood counts, and heart function. These changes are the body's adaptive response to conserve energy during starvation, and they reverse only with sustained nutritional restoration.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The signs and symptoms of anorexia nervosa include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe restriction of food intake and a significantly low body weight
    \item An intense fear of gaining weight or becoming fat
    \item A distorted body image, with the person seeing themselves as overweight despite being thin
    \item Preoccupation with food, calories, weight, and dieting
    \item Denial of the seriousness of the low weight
    \item Amenorrhoea in females (loss of menstrual periods) and reduced libido
    \item Excessive exercise and, in the purging subtype, self-induced vomiting, laxatives, or diet pills
    \item Physical effects: fatigue, feeling cold, dizziness, constipation, thinning hair, and fine downy hair over the body (lanugo)
    \item Social withdrawal, irritability, and ritualised behaviours around food and eating
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of weight loss and restrictive eating must be considered:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Other eating disorders:} bulimia nervosa (bingeing with purging at a normal weight) and avoidant-restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID), in which restriction is not driven by fear of weight gain
    \item \textbf{Depression and anxiety:} can reduce appetite without the fear of weight gain that defines anorexia
    \item \textbf{Medical causes of weight loss:} thyroid disease, diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease, or malignancy
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal disorders:} conditions causing pain or nausea that lead to food avoidance
    \item \textbf{Substance use:} stimulant or other drug use that suppresses appetite
    \item \textbf{Body dysmorphic disorder:} distress about appearance without food restriction or low weight
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek help early if you or someone you care for is restricting food, losing significant weight, or becoming preoccupied with weight and body image. Early treatment gives the best chance of recovery, and a general practitioner is a good first point of contact.

\textbf{Seek urgent care if:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item There are thoughts of suicide or self-harm
    \item Weight loss is rapid or severe
    \item Dizziness, fainting, chest pain, or an irregular heart rhythm develops
    \item There is severe dehydration or an inability to keep fluids down
    \item There is evidence of purging, such as frequent vomiting or laxative misuse
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services if:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The person collapses, has a seizure, or is in immediate danger
    \item There are immediate concerns about suicide
\end{itemize}

Otherwise, contact a mental health crisis team or a support line such as a local crisis service promptly.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is made on clinical assessment rather than a single test:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical interview:} a detailed history of eating behaviour, weight, exercise, purging, and thoughts about food, weight, and body image
    \item \textbf{Weight and growth assessment:} for children and adolescents, plotting weight and height against expected growth curves
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} checking heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, and signs of malnutrition
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, electrolytes, liver function, blood sugar, kidney function, and thyroid tests to detect medical complications
    \item \textbf{ECG:} to check for heart rhythm disturbances
    \item \textbf{Bone density scan:} recommended if the illness has been prolonged or periods have been absent for a long time
    \item \textbf{Psychiatric assessment:} screening for depression, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive symptoms, and risk of suicide
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is coordinated by a multidisciplinary team and addresses both physical health and the underlying psychological illness:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nutritional rehabilitation:} a structured plan to restore weight gradually under close supervision
    \item \textbf{Family-based treatment:} the leading approach for adolescents, in which parents take an active, supported role in refeeding
    \item \textbf{Psychological therapy:} eating-disorder-focused cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT-ED) and other specialist therapies for older adolescents and adults
    \item \textbf{Medical monitoring:} regular checks of weight, heart rate, blood pressure, and blood tests, with refeeding supervised to avoid refeeding syndrome
    \item \textbf{Medication:} antidepressants or other medicines may help with associated depression, anxiety, or obsessive symptoms, although no medicine cures the disorder itself
    \item \textbf{Dietitian input:} education about nutrition, meal planning, and a healthy relationship with food
    \item \textbf{Hospital admission:} needed for very low weight, rapid weight loss, medical instability, or severe purging
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cardiac problems:} a slow heart rate, low blood pressure, and dangerous heart rhythm disturbances
    \item \textbf{Osteoporosis:} loss of bone density, raising the risk of fractures
    \item \textbf{Refeeding syndrome:} a dangerous shift in fluids and electrolytes when eating resumes too quickly
    \item \textbf{Kidney and liver damage:} from prolonged starvation and dehydration
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal problems:} constipation, bloating, and delayed stomach emptying
    \item \textbf{Hormonal effects:} absent periods, infertility, and reduced sex drive
    \item \textbf{Brain and mood effects:} poor concentration, irritability, and increased risk of depression
    \item \textbf{Suicide and self-harm:} the most serious complication of the disorder
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Anorexia nervosa is a serious illness, but recovery is possible. Approximately half of those treated make a full recovery, a further proportion improve substantially, and a minority follow a chronic course. Outcomes are better when the illness is identified early and treated in adolescence, particularly with family-based treatment. Relapse is common, so ongoing follow-up and support matter greatly.

The death rate is higher than in the general population, largely from the physical effects of starvation and from suicide. This is why early recognition, persistent treatment, and good medical and psychological monitoring are so important.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Promote a healthy, balanced relationship with food and body image within the family
    \item Discourage dieting and weight-focused talk in young people, and avoid criticising weight or shape
    \item Be alert to early warning signs such as avoiding meals, exercising excessively, or sudden weight loss
    \item Seek help early rather than waiting for the illness to become entrenched
    \item In recovery, follow the treatment plan, attend appointments, and use regular meal support
    \item Reduce exposure to social media that promotes extreme thinness or aggressive dieting
    \item If you are affected, talk to someone you trust and see a GP or mental health professional
\end{itemize}
