\section*{Definition and Overview}
Clubfoot, also called congenital talipes equinovarus, is a birth condition in which a baby's foot is twisted out of shape or position, typically turning inward and pointing downward so that the sole faces backwards. It usually affects the bones, joints, muscles, and tendons of the foot and lower leg, and it may involve one foot or both feet. Although the foot looks severely deformed, the condition is treatable, and early treatment gives excellent results in most children.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Clubfoot is one of the more common congenital orthopaedic conditions, affecting roughly 1 in 1,000 live births. It is about twice as common in boys as in girls, and the condition affects both feet in around half of all cases. Rates vary somewhat between populations and ethnic groups. Clubfoot can run in families, and the risk is increased when a parent or sibling has the condition. Many cases occur in otherwise healthy babies with no other medical problems.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The exact cause of clubfoot is not fully understood, but it appears to involve a combination of genetic factors and influences early in pregnancy.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} a family history of clubfoot increases the risk, and several genes have been linked to the condition
    \item \textbf{Positional factors:} crowding in the womb (such as with low amniotic fluid or multiple pregnancy) may contribute in some cases
    \item \textbf{Associated conditions:} clubfoot can occur with neurological or neuromuscular disorders, such as spina bifida, and with some genetic syndromes
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} the foot is held in an abnormal position because the bones are displaced and the soft tissues (tendons, ligaments, and joint capsules) on the inner and back of the foot are shortened and tight
    \item \textbf{Environmental influences:} maternal smoking during pregnancy has been linked to a slightly higher risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The typical features of clubfoot are visible at birth and may be noticed on a prenatal ultrasound.
\begin{itemize}
    \item The front of the foot is turned inward and the sole points inward and upward
    \item The foot is bent downward at the ankle (equinus), often with a high-arched, hollowed appearance to the sole
    \item The calf muscle and leg on the affected side are smaller and thinner
    \item One or both feet may be affected
    \item The foot may feel rigid or may partly straighten when pushed
    \item The baby is otherwise usually healthy and does not feel pain from the foot itself
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A similar-looking foot can occasionally be caused by other conditions, and the doctor will look for these.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Positional (postural) talipes: a flexible foot held in a similar position in the womb that corrects with gentle movement
    \item Congenital vertical talus (rocker-bottom foot): a different deformity where the foot points upward and outward
    \item Metatarsus adductus: the front of the foot is turned inward but the heel and ankle are normal
    \item Neurological conditions such as spina bifida that can cause a stiff, deformed foot
    \item Arthrogryposis and other syndromes with multiple joint contractures
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Clubfoot is usually identified at birth or on prenatal scanning, and treatment should begin in the first weeks of life, so early referral to a specialist team is important.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek assessment if your baby is born with a foot that looks turned in or down
    \item See a doctor if one leg or foot is noticeably smaller, or if movement of a foot or leg seems limited
    \item Report any worsening rigidity, skin breakdown, or pressure areas on the foot
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A blue or pale foot, which may indicate a problem with blood supply
    \item Any injury to the foot or a cast that becomes very tight, painful, or causes swelling or colour change
    \item Signs of illness such as fever together with swelling, redness, or discharge from the foot
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is made mainly by examining the foot, and little investigation is usually needed.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} the doctor assesses the position, rigidity, and flexibility of the foot and ankle
    \item \textbf{Full newborn examination:} the hips, spine, and rest of the body are checked for associated problems
    \item \textbf{Prenatal ultrasound:} clubfoot may be spotted before birth, which allows planning for treatment
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} occasionally used to assess the bones, especially in older children or if surgery is planned
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a paediatric orthopaedic clinic experienced in the Ponseti method of treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment works best when it starts early, ideally within the first one to two weeks of life.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ponseti method:} gentle weekly stretching and serial casting over about 6 weeks to gradually reposition the foot
    \item \textbf{Tenotomy:} a small procedure to release the tight Achilles tendon if the ankle remains bent down after casting
    \item \textbf{Bracing:} a foot abduction brace (boots joined by a bar) worn full time for a few months, then at night and naptime until about age 4--5 to prevent relapse
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} stretching and exercises as part of the program
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} occasionally needed for stiff or late-presenting feet, involving tendon transfers or joint releases
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular reviews through childhood to monitor for recurrence
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
With proper treatment complications are uncommon, but they can occur.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Relapse of the deformity, most often when the brace is not worn as recommended
    \item Persistent tightness, stiffness, or a residual turn of the foot
    \item A smaller calf and slight leg-length difference on the affected side
    \item A tendency to walk on the outside of the foot or to trip if deformity persists
    \item Complications of surgery, including infection, scarring, or stiffness
    \item Muscle soreness or aching in the leg after activity in later life
    \item Psychological or social impact from the appearance of the foot if treatment is delayed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for clubfoot is very good when treatment starts early and the brace routine is followed. With the Ponseti method, most children achieve a pain-free foot that looks nearly normal and functions well, allowing normal walking, running, and sport. The treated calf often remains slightly smaller than the other, and the foot may be a little less flexible. If treatment is delayed or the brace is not used, deformity can return and may require more extensive surgery.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Clubfoot cannot be reliably prevented, because its causes are largely genetic and developmental.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Avoid smoking and alcohol during pregnancy
    \item Attend routine pregnancy scans so the condition may be detected early
    \item Seek treatment within the first weeks after birth for the best outcome
    \item Follow the casting and brace schedule exactly as instructed by the treatment team
    \item Keep the skin under the brace clean and dry, and watch for pressure marks
    \item Continue physiotherapy and review visits through childhood as recommended
    \item Consider referral of other family members for assessment if a baby is born with the condition
\end{itemize}
