\chapter{Athlete's Foot}
\index{Athlete's Foot}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Athlete's foot, medically called tinea pedis, is a common fungal infection of the skin on the feet. It is caused by microscopic fungi known as dermatophytes, which live on dead skin, hair and nail tissue. The infection usually starts between the toes, where the skin is warm and moist, and can spread to the soles and sides of the feet. Despite the name, it affects many people who are not athletes; the risk is increased by sweaty feet, tight footwear, and using communal showers or changing rooms where the fungus can be picked up from the floor. It is generally a minor condition that responds well to treatment, but it is highly contagious and often recurs.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Athlete's foot is one of the most common fungal infections in the world and becomes more common with age. It affects men more often than women and is frequently seen in adolescents and adults. People who wear tight, non-breathing footwear, who sweat heavily, or who regularly use public swimming pools, gyms and changing rooms are at higher risk. The infection is easily passed from person to person, and family members often become infected through shared towels, bathmats or footwear. People with diabetes, a weakened immune system or poor circulation are more prone to severe, persistent or recurrent infection.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The fungi that cause athlete's foot grow best in warm, dark, moist environments, which is exactly what a sweaty shoe provides.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Causative fungi:} dermatophytes, most commonly \textit{Trichophyton rubrum}, which feed on keratin in the outer layer of skin
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} picked up from contaminated floors, towels, socks or shoes, often in communal wet areas
    \item \textbf{Moisture:} sweat and trapped moisture soften the skin and allow fungi to invade the outer layers
    \item \textbf{Skin damage:} small cracks or abrasions between the toes make it easier for fungi to enter
    \item \textbf{Spread:} the fungus can spread by touch to the hands, groin (tinea cruris, "jock itch") and toenails
    \item \textbf{Immune factors:} weakened immunity, diabetes or poor circulation allow more severe or persistent infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The classic form begins between the toes, but different patterns exist depending on where the infection settles.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Itching, burning or stinging, especially between the toes and on the soles
    \item Red, cracked, peeling or flaky skin, most often between the fourth and fifth toes
    \item Soft, white, soggy skin between the toes
    \item Dry, scaly skin over the soles, sometimes in a "moccasin" pattern
    \item Blisters, in some people, that are itchy and may break
    \item A musty smell and tenderness of the affected skin
    \item Spread to the toenails causing thickening, yellowing or crumbling (fungal nail infection)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Not every itchy, scaly foot is athlete's foot, and the wrong treatment delays recovery.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Eczema (dermatitis):} dry, itchy skin that can affect the feet, often also present on the hands and elsewhere
    \item \textbf{Psoriasis:} thickened, scaly patches that can mimic tinea
    \item \textbf{Contact dermatitis:} irritation or allergy to soaps, shoe materials or sock dyes
    \item \textbf{Hyperhidrosis with maceration:} excessive sweating causing soggy, sore skin between the toes
    \item \textbf{Pitted keratolysis:} a bacterial infection of sweaty soles producing small pits and a strong odour
    \item \textbf{Dry skin and calluses:} simple dryness or pressure-related thickening
    \item \textbf{Friction blisters:} rubbing from poorly fitting shoes rather than infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a GP or pharmacist for advice if the rash is spreading, very itchy or painful, if it does not improve after a couple of weeks of over-the-counter treatment, or if the toenails are also affected.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services or see a doctor urgently for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Redness, warmth, swelling or pain spreading up the foot or leg (possible cellulitis)
    \item Pus, weeping or fever, suggesting a secondary bacterial infection
    \item Dark or blackening areas of skin
    \item Symptoms in a person with diabetes or a weakened immune system
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Athlete's foot is usually diagnosed by its typical appearance, but tests may be used when the diagnosis is uncertain or treatment is failing.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} looking at the pattern and site of the rash and checking other areas such as the groin, hands and nails
    \item \textbf{Skin scraping:} scraping flakes of skin onto a slide for laboratory examination
    \item \textbf{Microscopy:} examining the scraping under a microscope for fungal elements using a potassium hydroxide preparation
    \item \textbf{Fungal culture:} growing the fungus to confirm the species, used when the diagnosis is unclear
    \item \textbf{Review of diabetes:} if infection is recurrent or severe, checking for diabetes is reasonable
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Mild athlete's foot usually responds to simple measures and over-the-counter antifungal creams. Persistent or widespread infection may need prescription treatment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Over-the-counter antifungals:} creams, sprays or powders containing terbinafine, clotrimazole or miconazole, applied for several weeks beyond apparent clearing
    \item \textbf{Keep feet dry:} wash daily, dry carefully between the toes, and use foot powder if sweaty
    \item \textbf{Change socks and footwear:} cotton or wool socks and breathable shoes, alternated day to day
    \item \textbf{Oral antifungal tablets:} for severe, persistent infection or when the nails are involved
    \item \textbf{Treat the environment:} wash towels and socks in hot water and treat shoes with antifungal spray or powder
    \item \textbf{Treat the family:} check household members for tinea and treat affected nails and skin to prevent re-infection
    \item \textbf{Pharmacist or doctor review:} if symptoms do not improve within a few weeks, reassessment is needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Athlete's foot is usually a minor nuisance, but complications can develop, particularly when the skin is cracked or immunity is lowered.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Secondary bacterial infection:} cellulitis, a spreading infection of the deeper skin, can enter through cracked skin
    \item \textbf{Spread to other body sites:} the hands, groin and toenails
    \item \textbf{Chronic fungal nail infection:} thick, discoloured nails that are difficult to treat
    \item \textbf{Severe blistering:} painful blisters on the soles that can become infected
    \item \textbf{Worse outcomes in diabetes:} foot ulcers and infections are more serious and harder to treat
    \item Recurrent infection because the fungus persists in shoes, towels or nails
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Athlete's foot responds well to treatment in most people, though it can take several weeks to clear fully and it often recurs. Relapses are common because the fungus survives in shoes, socks and bathrooms, and because some people are simply prone to it. Consistent foot care and completing the full course of treatment reduce recurrence. With good hygiene, most people can keep the infection under control, and serious complications are uncommon in otherwise healthy people.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Prevention focuses on keeping the feet dry and not spreading the fungus around.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Dry the feet thoroughly after washing, especially between the toes
    \item Wear cotton or wool socks and breathable shoes, and change socks if the feet sweat
    \item Alternate shoes so they dry out between wears
    \item Wear thongs or sandals in communal showers, pools and changing rooms
    \item Do not share towels, socks, shoes or nail clippers
    \item Wash towels and socks frequently in hot water
    \item Treat the feet promptly at the first sign of itching or peeling
    \item Check the feet regularly, particularly if diabetic or immunosuppressed
\end{itemize}
