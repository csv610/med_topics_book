\chapter{Buprenorphine}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Buprenorphine is a prescription opioid medicine used to treat moderate to severe pain and, in higher doses, to treat opioid dependence. It acts on opioid receptors in the brain and spinal cord to reduce the perception of pain and, at the doses used for dependence, to relieve withdrawal symptoms and reduce cravings.

Buprenorphine is a partial agonist at the mu-opioid receptor, which means it produces a strong but ceiling-limited effect: sufficient to control pain and suppress withdrawal without the same intensity of euphoria or the same degree of respiratory depression as full opioid agonists such as morphine or oxycodone. It is available in several formulations, including sublingual tablets and films, transdermal patches for chronic pain, and long-acting implant or injectable preparations for opioid dependence. Because of its potential for misuse, buprenorphine is a controlled medicine in most jurisdictions and must be prescribed and dispensed under strict regulatory conditions.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Opioid medicines are among the most widely prescribed drug classes worldwide, and opioid dependence is a serious public health problem associated with substantial illness and premature death. In Australia and internationally, the use of buprenorphine in opioid agonist treatment programs has grown markedly over recent decades, and it is now one of the most commonly used medicines for this purpose alongside methadone. Chronic pain affects a substantial proportion of adults, and some people with moderate to severe pain are managed with transdermal buprenorphine. Rising rates of opioid misuse and overdose in many countries have driven tighter regulation and expanded access to treatment, with buprenorphine recognised for its favourable safety profile compared with full agonists.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mechanism of action:} buprenorphine binds to mu-opioid receptors in the central nervous system, relieving pain, suppressing withdrawal, and reducing drug craving
    \item \textbf{Partial agonism:} its maximum effect is limited by a pharmacological ceiling, which reduces the risk of severe respiratory depression and overdose compared with full opioids
    \item \textbf{High receptor affinity:} buprenorphine binds very firmly to opioid receptors, which enables it to block the effects of other opioids taken concurrently
    \item \textbf{Precipitated withdrawal:} because of its tight binding and partial agonism, starting buprenorphine too soon after a full opioid can displace that opioid and cause acute withdrawal
    \item \textbf{Long duration of action:} the drug remains active for an extended period, permitting once-daily dosing or sustained-release patches
    \item \textbf{Hepatic metabolism:} buprenorphine is metabolised by the liver, so significant liver disease requires close monitoring and dose adjustment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical picture depends on the reason for treatment:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} improvement in pain intensity and function with sublingual preparations or patches
    \item \textbf{Dependence treatment:} resolution of withdrawal symptoms, reduced cravings, and reduced use of illicit opioids
    \item \textbf{Common side effects:} constipation, nausea, drowsiness, dizziness, headache, and sweating
    \item \textbf{Patch site reactions:} redness, itching, or irritation at the application site
    \item \textbf{Overdose features:} drowsiness, slowed or shallow breathing, pinpoint pupils, and reduced consciousness, more likely when combined with alcohol or other sedatives
    \item \textbf{Withdrawal on cessation:} agitation, muscle aches, sweating, and nausea, which are typically milder than withdrawal from full opioids
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When a person taking buprenorphine develops new symptoms, the following need to be considered:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Worsening pain or opioid withdrawal versus an adverse effect of the medicine
    \item Sedation from other medicines, alcohol, or intercurrent illness versus opioid side effects
    \item Respiratory depression from combined depressant drug use versus other causes of drowsiness
    \item Progression of pre-existing liver disease versus drug-related hepatic injury
    \item Opioid-induced constipation versus an unrelated gastrointestinal disorder
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Urgent medical review is needed if a person taking buprenorphine develops slow or difficult breathing, severe drowsiness or difficulty rousing, signs of a severe allergic reaction, or new jaundice such as yellowing of the skin or eyes.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Slow, shallow, or stopped breathing
    \item Collapse or loss of consciousness
    \item Severe allergic reaction with swelling of the face or lips, difficulty breathing, or a widespread rash
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Do not} drink alcohol or take other opioids, benzodiazepines, or sedatives while using buprenorphine without medical advice, as these combinations can be fatal.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} review of the indication for treatment, current pain or withdrawal symptoms, and side effects
    \item \textbf{Medication review:} checking for interacting medicines, including other opioids, benzodiazepines, and sedative drugs
    \item \textbf{Liver function tests:} performed before and during treatment because buprenorphine is metabolised in the liver
    \item \textbf{Urine drug screening:} used in opioid treatment programs to monitor adherence and detect other substance use
    \item \textbf{Electrocardiogram:} may be recommended in people at risk of abnormal heart rhythms, particularly at higher doses
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} screening for depression, concurrent substance use, and risk of misuse or diversion of the prescribed medicine
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pain management:} buprenorphine is one component of a multimodal plan that should include non-drug approaches such as physiotherapy, exercise, and psychological support
    \item \textbf{Dependence treatment:} buprenorphine is taken daily, initially under supervision, with the dose titrated; it is usually combined with counselling and psychosocial support
    \item \textbf{Adherence:} the medicine must be taken exactly as prescribed; doses should not be changed, and patches must not be cut
    \item \textbf{Avoiding interactions:} alcohol and other sedatives should be avoided, and other medicines reviewed by a pharmacist or doctor
    \item \textbf{Safe storage and disposal:} store securely out of reach of children and return unused medicine to a pharmacy
    \item \textbf{Regular review:} monitoring of pain, side effects, liver function, and, in dependence treatment, adherence and progress
    \item \textbf{Planned tapering:} stopping or reducing buprenorphine should be supervised by a clinician to minimise withdrawal and relapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Constipation, which can become severe and require active treatment
    \item Drowsiness and impaired coordination, affecting driving and operating machinery
    \item Respiratory depression and overdose, particularly when combined with alcohol or other sedatives
    \item Precipitated withdrawal if the drug is started too soon after other opioids
    \item Liver enzyme abnormalities, requiring monitoring in people with liver disease
    \item Dependence on buprenorphine itself, with withdrawal symptoms if stopped abruptly
    \item Misuse and diversion when prescribed medicines are not taken as intended
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For chronic pain, buprenorphine can provide useful relief for people who do not respond to simpler measures, but opioids are rarely a long-term solution on their own, and the benefits must be weighed against side effects and the risk of tolerance and dependence. For opioid dependence, buprenorphine treatment substantially reduces illicit opioid use, overdose, and mortality while people remain engaged in care. Many people remain on maintenance treatment for years, while others taper off successfully with support. Retention in treatment is consistently associated with far better outcomes than leaving treatment early.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take buprenorphine exactly as prescribed, at the same time each day
    \item Never combine it with alcohol, other opioids, or sedatives without medical advice
    \item Do not drive or operate machinery until you know how the medicine affects you
    \item Prevent constipation with adequate fluids, dietary fibre, and physical activity
    \item Store the medicine securely and keep naloxone available if it has been supplied for overdose emergencies
    \item Attend all appointments and be open with the treatment team about other medicine or drug use
    \item Do not stop abruptly; plan any dose changes with the prescriber
\end{itemize}
