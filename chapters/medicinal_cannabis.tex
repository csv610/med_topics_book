\chapter{Medicinal cannabis}
\index{Medicinal cannabis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Medicinal cannabis refers to the use of the cannabis plant (\textit{Cannabis sativa} or \textit{Cannabis indica}) or its constituent cannabinoids—primarily delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) and cannabidiol (CBD)—as prescribed medical therapies for specific clinical indications. Under local law, medicinal cannabis is regulated as a controlled drug (Schedule 8) or prescription-only medicine (Schedule 4) administered through regulatory schemes such as the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) Special Access Scheme (SAS) or Authorised Prescriber scheme. Pharmacologically, THC acts as a partial agonist at cannabinoid receptors (CB1 and CB2) within the endogenous cannabinoid system, producing psychoactive, analgesic, and muscle-relaxant effects. CBD, which is non-intoxicating, exhibits low direct affinity for these receptors but modulates them indirectly, alongside actions at serotonin (5-HT1A) and transient receptor potential (TRP) channels, contributing to its anticonvulsant, anti-inflammatory, and anxiolytic properties. Medicinal cannabis is indicated for conditions resistant to conventional therapies, including chronic non-cancer pain, chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting (CINV), refractory epilepsy (such as Dravet or Lennox-Gastaut syndromes), spasticity in multiple sclerosis, and palliative care symptom management.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The clinical utilisation of medicinal cannabis has increased globally and within the affected region since regulatory changes in 2016. In many countries, the TGA has approved hundreds of thousands of SAS Category B applications, with a significant upward trajectory in recent years. Chronic non-cancer pain remains the most common indication, representing approximately 60\% to 70\% of all approvals, followed by anxiety, sleep disorders, cancer-related symptoms, and neuropathic pain. Demographically, the age distribution is broad, spanning paediatric patients receiving CBD for refractory epilepsy to older adults seeking relief from chronic musculoskeletal pain and palliative symptoms. While both sexes are represented, registry data indicate a slightly higher prevalence of prescriptions among females for chronic pain and males for anxiety. Global trends mirror this expansion, with variations in access and utilisation driven by differences in national legislative frameworks and clinical guideline endorsements.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Medicinal cannabis therapy targets the endocannabinoid system (ECS), a complex cell-signalling system that plays a key role in regulating physiological processes including pain perception, immune function, sleep, mood, and neuroprotection.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Endocannabinoid system modulation:} Cannabinoid receptors CB1 and CB2 are G-protein coupled receptors. CB1 receptors are predominantly located in the central nervous system (brain, spinal cord, and peripheral nociceptors) where they modulate neurotransmitter release. CB2 receptors are primarily found in peripheral tissues, particularly immune cells, modulating inflammatory responses.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacology of delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC):} THC is the primary psychoactive component of cannabis. It acts as a partial agonist at CB1 and CB2 receptors, inhibiting adenylate cyclase and voltage-gated calcium channels while activating potassium channels. This decreases the release of neurotransmitters like glutamate and substance P, thereby reducing nociceptive signalling and producing analgesic, muscle-relaxant, and antiemetic effects.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacology of cannabidiol (CBD):} CBD is non-psychoactive and has low affinity for CB1 and CB2 receptors. It acts as a negative allosteric modulator of CB1, mitigating some of the adverse psychoactive effects of THC. CBD acts on other molecular targets, including the 5-HT1A receptor (contributing to anxiolytic effects), adenosine A2A receptors (anti-inflammatory), and transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1) channels (modulating pain and temperature sensation). It also inhibits the enzyme fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH), reducing the breakdown of the endogenous cannabinoid anandamide.
    \item \textbf{Synergistic entourage effect:} The pharmacological theory of the ``entourage effect'' suggests that the combination of cannabinoids (THC and CBD) along with minor cannabinoids and terpenes in full-spectrum products produces enhanced therapeutic benefits and fewer adverse effects compared to isolated compounds.
    \item \textbf{Receptor down-regulation and tolerance:} Chronic exposure to agonist compounds, particularly THC, can lead to the internalisation and down-regulation of CB1 receptors, resulting in pharmacological tolerance and potential withdrawal symptoms upon abrupt cessation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of patients initiating medicinal cannabis, as well as the physiological responses observed during treatment, depend on the specific formulation, ratio of cannabinoids (such as CBD-dominant, THC-dominant, or balanced), dose, and route of administration.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Analgesic response:} Patients with chronic non-cancer pain or neuropathic pain often report a reduction in pain intensity, improved pain tolerance, and a subjective shift in the emotional distress associated with pain.
    \item \textbf{Reduction in spasticity:} In patients with multiple sclerosis, medicinal cannabis can significantly decrease muscle tone, spasms, and associated stiffness, leading to improved mobility and comfort.
    \item \textbf{Anticonvulsant activity:} In paediatric patients with severe, drug-resistant epilepsy syndromes, treatment with high-purity CBD can significantly reduce the frequency and severity of convulsive and non-convulsive seizures.
    \item \textbf{Antiemetic effects:} Patients undergoing chemotherapy may experience a reduction in refractory nausea and vomiting, particularly when using THC-containing products that stimulate the appetite and suppress emetic pathways in the brainstem.
    \item \textbf{Anxiolytic and sedative effects:} Many patients experience improved sleep onset and sleep architecture, alongside decreased generalised anxiety, particularly with CBD-rich formulations.
    \item \textbf{Psychoactive side effects:} High-THC formulations can cause cognitive impairment, somnolence, dizziness, euphoria, dysphoria, depersonalisation, impaired coordination, tachycardia, and dry mouth.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Prior to prescribing medicinal cannabis, clinicians must exclude other underlying conditions that mimic the symptoms targeted for treatment, and evaluate patients for contraindications, particularly psychiatric disorders that could be exacerbated by cannabinoids.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary psychotic disorders:} Conditions such as schizophrenia or schisoaffective disorder must be differentiated from cannabis-induced psychosis, as THC can trigger or worsen psychotic symptoms in vulnerable individuals.
    \item \textbf{Uncontrolled mood or anxiety disorders:} Severe depression or bipolar disorder must be stabilised and managed using established first-line treatments before cannabis therapy, as THC can exacerbate manic episodes or panic attacks.
    \item \textbf{Substance use disorders:} Active cannabis use disorder or other severe substance dependencies must be identified and distinguished from legitimate medical needs, as cannabis use can perpetuate drug-seeking behaviours.
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular conditions:} Unstable angina, arrhythmias, or severe hypertension must be carefully evaluated; the sympathomimetic effects of THC (causing tachycardia and postural hypotension) could precipitate acute coronary events or syncope.
    \item \textbf{Alternative treatable neuropathies or pain conditions:} Underlying treatable causes of chronic pain (such as compressive myelopathy, active autoimmune inflammatory arthropathies, or malignancy) must be thoroughly investigated and targeted with specific therapies rather than relying solely on symptomatic relief with cannabinoids.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients using medicinal cannabis should consult their prescribing medical practitioner regularly to monitor therapeutic efficacy, adjust dosages, and manage mild side effects. Urgent medical review is necessary if patients experience severe side effects or mental health changes.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute chest pain, palpitations, or a rapid, irregular heart rate
    \item Severe shortness of breath or difficulty breathing
    \item Signs of a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) including swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or throat, and difficulty swallowing
    \item Acute psychiatric distress, including severe panic, paranoia, hallucinations, or suicidal thoughts
    \item Sudden loss of consciousness, severe dizziness, or falls due to low blood pressure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The decision to prescribe medicinal cannabis involves a comprehensive clinical assessment, diagnostic verification of the primary medical condition, and thorough screening for safety.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive clinical assessment:} A detailed medical history, physical examination, and psychological evaluation are performed to verify the primary diagnosis and confirm that standard, first-line treatments have failed or caused intolerable side effects.
    \item \textbf{Psychiatric screening:} Validated tools and a detailed history are used to screen for personal or family histories of psychosis, severe depression, or substance use disorders.
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular evaluation:} Baseline electrocardiogram (ECG) and blood pressure measurements are taken to identify pre-existing arrhythmias or unstable cardiovascular disease.
    \item \textbf{Liver function tests (LFTs):} Baseline and periodic hepatic monitoring is required, especially for patients prescribed high-dose cannabidiol (CBD), as it can cause elevated transaminases and interact with other hepatotoxic medications.
    \item \textbf{Urinary drug screening (UDS):} UDS may be performed to screen for illicit drug use or to monitor compliance and prevent diversion in patients receiving controlled substances.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The therapeutic use of medicinal cannabis must follow a structured, individualised approach, utilising low starting doses that are titrated gradually to achieve optimal symptom control while minimising adverse effects.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dosing and titration:} Treatment is initiated with low doses (such as 1 to 2.5 mg of THC, or 5 to 10 mg of CBD daily) and slowly titrated every few days based on clinical response and tolerability (``start low and go slow'').
    \item \textbf{Product selection and cannabinoid ratios:} Formulation choice is tailored to the clinical indication. Purified CBD is preferred for paediatric epilepsy and inflammatory conditions. Balanced THC:CBD (1:1) formulations are commonly used for neuropathic pain and multiple sclerosis spasticity, while THC-dominant products are reserved for severe pain, palliative care, or chemotherapy-induced nausea.
    \item \textbf{Route of administration:} Sublingual oils and oral capsules offer slow onset (1 to 3 hours) and prolonged duration of action (6 to 8 hours), suitable for chronic, stable symptoms. Vaporised flower provides rapid onset (minutes) and shorter duration (2 to 4 hours), useful for acute breakthroughs or severe pain.
    \item \textbf{Drug interaction monitoring:} Cannabinoids, particularly CBD, are metabolised by and inhibit cytochrome P450 enzymes (such as CYP2C19, CYP3A4, and CYP2D6). Active monitoring is required for interactions with clobazam, warfarin, valproate, and other CNS depressants.
    \item \textbf{Patient education and safety planning:} Patients must be educated about the legal implications of medicinal cannabis, including the strict prohibition on driving in most jurisdictions when taking THC-containing products, and safe storage to prevent accidental ingestion by children.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cognitive and psychomotor impairment:} Reduced attention, slower reaction times, and coordination deficits, which significantly increase the risk of motor vehicle accidents and falls in elderly patients.
    \item \textbf{Cannabis-induced psychosis:} The precipitation of acute psychotic episodes or the worsening of pre-existing schizophrenia and bipolar mania, particularly with high-THC products.
    \item \textbf{Cannabis use disorder (CUD):} The development of psychological and physiological dependence, characterised by tolerance, craving, and a withdrawal syndrome (irritability, insomnia, anxiety) upon cessation.
    \item \textbf{Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome (CHS):} A rare complication of chronic, high-dose cannabis use characterised by cyclic episodes of severe nausea, intractable vomiting, and abdominal pain, often relieved temporarily by hot bathing.
    \item \textbf{Drug-induced hepatotoxicity:} Elevated transaminase levels, particularly with high-dose CBD administration, which can lead to liver injury if not monitored.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis and clinical outcomes for patients using medicinal cannabis vary depending on the underlying pathology, the specific cannabinoid formulation used, and individual patient factors. For many patients with refractory chronic pain or spasticity, medicinal cannabis provides modest improvements in pain scores, sleep quality, and functional capacity rather than complete resolution of symptoms. In paediatric epilepsy, a substantial proportion of patients experience a reduction in monthly seizure frequency by 50\% or more, leading to a significant improvement in quality of life for the child and caregivers. In palliative care, medicinal cannabis serves as an effective adjunct to improve comfort and appetite. Long-term outcomes and safety profiles, particularly regarding cognitive function and dependency risk, are still being investigated through clinical trials and patient registries. Treatment should be re-evaluated periodically, and discontinued if clear therapeutic goals are not met within a defined period (such as 4 to 12 weeks).

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adhere to prescribed regimens:} Use the medicine exactly as directed by the prescribing doctor; do not increase doses or share the medicine with others.
    \item \textbf{Safe and secure storage:} Store all cannabis products in their original packaging, in a locked container, out of reach of children and pets to prevent accidental poisoning.
    \item \textbf{Avoid driving and operating machinery:} Do not drive or operate heavy machinery if your medicine contains THC, as it impairs coordination and reaction time, and is illegal in most jurisdictions.
    \item \textbf{Monitor for adverse effects:} Keep a daily symptom diary to track efficacy and record any side effects such as dizziness, dry mouth, or mood changes, and report these to your doctor.
    \item \textbf{Avoid abrupt discontinuation:} Do not stop using medicinal cannabis suddenly if you have been taking it for a long period; consult your doctor for a gradual tapering schedule to avoid withdrawal symptoms.
\end{itemize}
