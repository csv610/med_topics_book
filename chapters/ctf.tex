\chapter{Colorado Tick Fever (CTF)}
\index{Colorado Tick Fever (CTF)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Colorado tick fever is a viral infection transmitted by the bite of the Rocky Mountain wood tick (\textit{Dermacentor andersoni}). It is found in the western United States and Canada at altitudes above about 1,200 metres, in states such as Colorado, Wyoming, Montana, and Idaho. The illness causes fever, headache, muscle aches, and fatigue, often with a characteristic pattern of fever that resolves and then returns. Most people recover fully within two to three weeks. There is no specific treatment or vaccine, so prevention centres on avoiding and promptly removing ticks.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Colorado tick fever is the most common tick-borne viral illness in the western United States, with several hundred cases reported annually, and many more unreported. Cases occur from spring to early summer, when adult ticks are most active, and are linked to outdoor activities such as hiking, camping, and forestry work. Most cases occur in people who visit or live in mountainous areas of Colorado, Wyoming, Montana, Idaho, and Utah. The disease is not present in many countries, but travellers returning from endemic areas can bring it home.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Colorado tick fever is caused by the Colorado tick fever virus, a Coltivirus.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Virus:} the Colorado tick fever virus is transmitted by the bite of an infected \textit{Dermacentor andersoni} tick
    \item \textbf{Tick bite:} transmission occurs during feeding, usually requiring the tick to be attached for hours
    \item \textbf{Transmission to blood cells:} the virus infects red blood cell precursors in the bone marrow
    \item \textbf{Persistence in blood:} the virus can remain in red blood cells for weeks, which is why it can be transmitted by blood transfusion
    \item \textbf{Immune response:} the body's immune response clears the virus, and immunity is lifelong
    \item \textbf{No person-to-person spread:} except through blood transfusion, the infection is not contagious
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms begin about one to two weeks after the tick bite.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden fever, chills, and severe headache
    \item Muscle aches, joint pain, and fatigue
    \item Sensitivity to light and eye pain
    \item Nausea and vomiting in some people
    \item A rash in a minority of cases
    \item The classic biphasic fever: symptoms improve after a few days, then the fever returns for a further few days
    \item Low white blood cell and platelet counts on blood tests
    \item Prolonged fatigue that can last for weeks in some people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms resemble other tick-borne and viral infections.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Rocky Mountain spotted fever, a bacterial infection also transmitted by ticks
    \item Tick-borne relapsing fever
    \item Lyme disease, though rare in the CTF region
    \item Influenza and other viral infections
    \item Dengue and other viral haemorrhagic fevers in travellers
    \item Other causes of fever, headache, and low blood counts
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical advice if you develop fever within two weeks of a tick bite or of travel to a mountainous area in the western United States. Mention the tick exposure, as it guides testing.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A severe headache with neck stiffness, confusion, or drowsiness
    \item Difficulty breathing or chest pain
    \item A rapidly spreading rash
    \item Severe dehydration or an inability to keep fluids down
    \item Bleeding or bruising, which can occur with low platelets
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is confirmed with specific tests, performed in laboratories that handle the virus safely.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} tick exposure in an endemic area and the pattern of symptoms
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count showing low white cells and platelets
    \item \textbf{PCR testing:} detects viral RNA in the blood in the first two weeks of illness
    \item \textbf{Serology:} antibody tests, positive later in the illness
    \item \textbf{Virus isolation:} possible but only in specialised laboratories
    \item \textbf{Exclusion of other tick-borne diseases:} testing for rickettsial and bacterial infections
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
There is no specific antiviral treatment, and management is supportive.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fluids and rest:} the mainstay of treatment
    \item \textbf{Fever and pain relief:} paracetamol or ibuprofen
    \item \textbf{Avoid aspirin in children:} because of the risk of Reye's syndrome
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} for severe illness, dehydration, or bleeding complications
    \item \textbf{Blood products:} rarely needed for significant bleeding
    \item \textbf{Recovery support:} gradual return to activity as fatigue settles
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Prolonged fatigue lasting weeks to months
    \item Low platelet counts with bleeding or bruising
    \item Rarely, meningitis, encephalitis, or pericarditis
    \item Secondary bacterial infection
    \item Dehydration from fever and vomiting
    \item Transmission through blood transfusion from infected donors
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for Colorado tick fever is excellent. Most people recover fully within two to three weeks, although fatigue can persist for several more weeks, and a small proportion of people, especially older adults, develop more severe or prolonged illness. Death is very rare. Once recovered, immunity is lifelong. There is no vaccine, so prevention of tick bites is the key measure, and removal of attached ticks promptly reduces the risk of infection.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wear long sleeves, long pants, and closed footwear in tick habitat
    \item Use an effective insect repellent containing DEET or picaridin
    \item Check for ticks after outdoor activity and remove them promptly with fine-tipped tweezers
    \item Tuck trousers into socks and consider treating clothing with permethrin
    \item Keep grass short and create barriers between woodland and living areas
    \item Do not donate blood for several weeks after the illness, as the virus can be transmitted
    \item Seek medical review for fever after a tick bite
\end{itemize}
