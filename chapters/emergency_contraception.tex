\chapter{Emergency Contraception}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Emergency contraception is used to prevent pregnancy after unprotected sex, a contraceptive accident, or sexual assault. It works by delaying or preventing ovulation, and it must be used within a set time after sex. The main options in Australia are the emergency contraceptive pill, available over the counter or on prescription, and the copper intrauterine device (IUD), which is the most effective method and can be inserted up to five days after unprotected sex. Emergency contraception does not end an existing pregnancy and does not protect against sexually transmitted infections.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Emergency contraception is used by a significant number of women each year, often after condom failure, missed pills, or unplanned sex. Despite its availability, use of the most effective method, the copper IUD, is low, and many women use the pill, which is less effective and needs to be taken quickly. Unintended pregnancy remains common, and improving access to emergency contraception is an important public health measure.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Emergency contraception works by interfering with the process of conception.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The levonorgestrel pill:} delays ovulation, so sperm cannot meet an egg; it must be taken within 72 hours and is more effective the sooner it is taken
    \item \textbf{Ulipristal acetate pill:} also delays ovulation and works for up to 120 hours, with effectiveness maintained over the whole window
    \item \textbf{The copper IUD:} prevents fertilisation, mainly by the effect of copper on sperm and eggs, and can be inserted up to five days after unprotected sex
    \item \textbf{Timing matters:} all methods work by preventing ovulation, so they are most effective before ovulation occurs
    \item \textbf{Not an abortion:} none of the methods ends an established pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Effectiveness:} the copper IUD is over 99 per cent effective, the pills are less effective and decline in effectiveness with time
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The main considerations are the timing and the choice of method.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unprotected sex within the last five days
    \item A missed or late contraceptive pill, a broken condom, or a dislodged diaphragm
    \item Sexual assault without contraception
    \item The need to act quickly for the oral methods
    \item The option of an IUD, which is most effective and also provides ongoing contraception
    \item Follow-up: the next period should be checked, and a pregnancy test done if it is late
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Emergency contraception should be distinguished from other contraception and care.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ongoing contraception, such as the pill, implant, or IUD
    \item Abortion, which ends an established pregnancy
    \item Testing for pregnancy if the emergency contraception may have failed
    \item Screening for sexually transmitted infections, which emergency contraception does not cover
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Emergency contraception is available from pharmacies, GP clinics, and sexual health services. The copper IUD requires insertion by a trained clinician. Anyone who has had unprotected sex and wishes to avoid pregnancy should seek advice promptly, as timing is critical.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of a pregnancy complication, such as severe abdominal pain or heavy bleeding
    \item Pain, fever, or unusual discharge after IUD insertion
    \item Symptoms of an ectopic pregnancy, including shoulder tip pain and collapse
    \item After sexual assault, seek medical care and support urgently
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment before providing emergency contraception is brief but important.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the timing of unprotected sex, the cycle date, and any regular contraception
    \item \textbf{Exclusion of pregnancy:} a pregnancy test if there is a chance the woman is already pregnant from earlier sex
    \item \textbf{Assessment for the IUD:} suitability for insertion, including screening for infection
    \item \textbf{STI screening:} recommended after unprotected sex with a new partner
    \item \textbf{Follow-up planning:} advice about the next period and ongoing contraception
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management involves choosing the best method and planning follow-up.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Levonorgestrel pill:} available over the counter; one dose within 72 hours
    \item \textbf{Ulipristal acetate:} on prescription; effective up to 120 hours
    \item \textbf{Copper IUD:} the most effective option, inserted by a clinician within five days
    \item \textbf{Antiemetics:} for nausea, which the pills can cause
    \item \textbf{Ongoing contraception:} planning a reliable method for the future
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} a pregnancy test if the period is more than a week late
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Failure of the method, resulting in pregnancy
    \item Nausea and irregular bleeding after the pills
    \item Changes to the timing of the next period
    \item Discomfort during IUD insertion
    \item No protection against sexually transmitted infections
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Emergency contraception is highly effective when used correctly and promptly, and the copper IUD approaches the effectiveness of permanent methods. Most women who use it do not become pregnant, and their cycles return to normal within a month or two. The experience is also an opportunity to start a reliable ongoing method of contraception.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Act quickly: the oral methods work best the sooner they are taken
    \item Consider the copper IUD if maximum protection is wanted
    \item Use condoms to protect against sexually transmitted infections
    \item Check the date of the next period and test for pregnancy if it is late
    \item Plan a regular method of contraception for the future
    \item Seek STI screening after unprotected sex
    \item Use emergency contraception as a backup, not a regular method
\end{itemize}
