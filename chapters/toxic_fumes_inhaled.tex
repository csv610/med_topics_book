\chapter{Toxic Fumes Inhaled}
\index{Toxic Fumes Inhaled}
\medskip
\noindent\textit{Educational reference; always seek professional advice for individual care.}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Inhalation of toxic fumes means breathing smoke, gases, vapours, or chemical aerosols that injure the airways or poison the body. Examples include smoke from fires, carbon monoxide from faulty heaters or engines in enclosed spaces, chlorine or chloramine gas from mixed cleaning products, industrial solvents, ammonia, pesticides, and combustion products. Injury can range from cough and eye irritation to airway obstruction, chemical pneumonitis, delayed lung swelling, heart injury, brain injury, or death.

Carbon monoxide is especially dangerous because it is colourless and odourless. Children, pregnant people, older adults, and people with asthma, COPD, or heart disease may be more vulnerable. Move away from the exposure and seek urgent advice; do not re-enter a contaminated area to rescue someone without appropriate protection.

\section*{Types of exposure}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asphyxiants:} carbon monoxide and other substances that prevent oxygen delivery or use
    \item \textbf{Irritant gases:} chlorine, ammonia, sulphur dioxide, and nitrogen oxides that inflame the airways
    \item \textbf{Smoke and particulates:} soot and combustion products that injure airways and carry systemic toxins
    \item \textbf{Solvents and pesticides:} substances that may affect the brain, heart, liver, or kidneys
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cough, hoarseness, sore throat, wheeze, chest tightness, or breathlessness
    \item Headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, fainting, seizure, or unusual sleepiness
    \item Soot around the nose or mouth, facial burns, or a change in voice after a fire
    \item Eye watering, pain, blurred vision, or chemical burns of the skin or eyes
    \item Chest pain, palpitations, or worsening symptoms after an apparently mild exposure
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Leave the area, get to fresh air if safe, and contact a poison-information service or health professional for advice. Same-day assessment is needed for persistent cough or wheeze, chest pain, severe headache, vomiting, pregnancy, neurological symptoms, or exposure in a confined space. If several people in the same building develop headache or nausea, suspect carbon monoxide and leave immediately.

\textbf{Contact local emergency services urgently for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe breathlessness, stridor, blue lips, collapse, or reduced consciousness
    \item Confusion, seizure, or suspected significant carbon monoxide exposure
    \item Chest pain, severe palpitations, or a serious chemical burn
    \item Facial burns, soot, or voice change after a fire
    \item A large or unknown chemical release affecting several people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Clinicians ask about the substance, dose, duration, location, ventilation, and whether others were affected. Assessment may include oxygen saturation, blood gas and co-oximetry for suspected carbon monoxide, ECG and cardiac blood tests for severe exposure or chest pain, chest imaging, and observation for delayed lung injury. Standard pulse oximetry can appear normal in carbon monoxide poisoning. Specialist poison-centre advice is useful when the substance is uncertain.

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Remove the person from exposure and provide oxygen or breathing support when indicated
    \item Remove contaminated clothing and irrigate exposed skin or eyes with plenty of clean water
    \item Treat bronchospasm with inhaled bronchodilators when prescribed
    \item Consider antidotes for selected poisonings under specialist guidance
    \item Consider hyperbaric oxygen for selected severe carbon monoxide exposures according to local protocols
    \item Observe patients at risk of delayed airway or lung swelling
    \item Do not induce vomiting or mix household chemicals to neutralise an exposure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications and Outlook}
Severe exposure can cause respiratory failure, acute respiratory distress syndrome, airway obstruction, pneumonia, heart injury, or delayed neurological problems. Mild irritant exposure often improves after removal, but symptoms can worsen over several hours. Follow-up is advisable after moderate or severe exposure, especially if cough, breathlessness, chest pain, or cognitive symptoms persist.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Install and test carbon-monoxide and smoke alarms according to local safety guidance
    \item Service fuel-burning heaters and appliances regularly
    \item Never run engines, generators, or charcoal equipment in enclosed spaces
    \item Never mix bleach with acids or ammonia; follow product labels
    \item Ventilate work areas and use suitable protective equipment
    \item Keep chemicals in original containers and out of children's reach
    \item Follow local air-quality and evacuation advice during fires or industrial incidents
\end{itemize}
