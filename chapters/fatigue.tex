\chapter{Fatigue}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Fatigue is an overwhelming sense of tiredness, exhaustion, or lack of energy that is not relieved by rest and interferes with daily life. It is one of the most common reasons people see a doctor, and one of the most challenging, because it can be caused by almost anything: lifestyle, sleep problems, mental health conditions, or underlying medical illness. Fatigue is different from normal tiredness after a busy day, which resolves with rest; persistent fatigue lasting more than a month, or severe fatigue that stops normal activity, deserves assessment. This chapter explains the common causes of fatigue, how it is investigated, and what people can do about it.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Fatigue affects a large share of the population at any given time. Community surveys suggest that around 20--40\% of adults report fatigue significant enough to affect their lives, and it features in roughly 1 in 10 general practice consultations. Women report fatigue more often than men, and rates are highest in younger and middle-aged adults. Anaemia, thyroid disease, depression, and sleep disorders are among the most commonly identified underlying causes in primary care, but in many cases no single medical cause is found. Chronic fatigue syndrome, now more commonly called myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS), affects around 0.2--0.5\% of the population and is a diagnosis of careful exclusion.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The causes of fatigue span lifestyle, psychology, and physical disease, and often several factors combine.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle factors:} insufficient sleep, shift work, excessive alcohol, poor diet, and physical inactivity or overtraining
    \item \textbf{Sleep disorders:} obstructive sleep apnoea, insomnia, and restless legs syndrome prevent restorative sleep even when time in bed is adequate
    \item \textbf{Mental health:} depression, anxiety, and chronic stress are among the most common and treatable causes of fatigue
    \item \textbf{Anaemia:} low haemoglobin from iron, B12, or folate deficiency reduces oxygen delivery to tissues
    \item \textbf{Endocrine disorders:} hypothyroidism, diabetes, and adrenal insufficiency slow metabolism or impair energy use
    \item \textbf{Chronic disease:} heart, lung, kidney, and liver disease, as well as autoimmune conditions, produce fatigue through inflammation and organ dysfunction
    \item \textbf{Infections:} acute illnesses such as glandular fever and long-term conditions such as hepatitis and HIV cause fatigue directly
    \item \textbf{Medications:} many drugs, including beta-blockers, sedatives, antihistamines, and some antidepressants, cause tiredness
    \item \textbf{ME/CFS:} a complex illness triggered often by infection, with profound, post-exertional fatigue as its core feature
    \item \textbf{Cancer and its treatment:} fatigue is the most common symptom of cancer and persists in many survivors
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The history is the most important part of assessing fatigue.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Duration and pattern: how long the fatigue has lasted, whether it is constant or intermittent, and whether it is worse at particular times
    \item Severity and impact: whether it stops work, study, exercise, or social activities, and what makes it better or worse
    \item Sleep quality: snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, unrefreshing sleep, and difficulty getting or staying asleep
    \item Associated symptoms: weight change, cold intolerance, thirst, breathlessness, palpitations, pain, or low mood
    \item Psychosocial context: stress, workload, bereavement, relationship problems, and alcohol or drug use
    \item Red flags: unintentional weight loss, fevers, night sweats, bleeding, and new neurological symptoms
    \item Post-exertional malaise: a specific worsening of symptoms 12--48 hours after activity, characteristic of ME/CFS
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Fatigue has an enormous differential, and the work-up focuses on the common and the serious.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Anaemia, iron deficiency, and B12 or folate deficiency
    \item Hypothyroidism and other endocrine disorders, including diabetes
    \item Depression, anxiety, and stress-related disorders
    \item Obstructive sleep apnoea and other sleep disorders
    \item Chronic infections, including glandular fever, hepatitis, and HIV
    \item Chronic diseases of the heart, lungs, kidneys, and liver
    \item Autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis and lupus
    \item ME/CFS, which is diagnosed after other causes are excluded
    \item Medication side effects and substance use
    \item Malignancy, although rare, especially with weight loss and night sweats
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with fatigue lasting more than a few weeks, fatigue that is getting worse, or fatigue affecting work and daily life should see a doctor. Seek prompt review sooner if fatigue is accompanied by other symptoms.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe weakness, especially on one side of the body, with difficulty speaking or facial droop
    \item Chest pain, breathlessness, or palpitations with collapse
    \item Faintness or collapse with significant bleeding
    \item Confusion, or fatigue so severe the person cannot stay awake or respond normally
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Investigation is guided by the history and examination rather than performed indiscriminately.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full blood count:} detects anaemia and other blood disorders
    \item \textbf{Iron studies, B12, and folate:} for nutritional deficiency
    \item \textbf{Thyroid function tests:} for hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism
    \item \textbf{Glucose and HbA1c:} for diabetes
    \item \textbf{Kidney, liver, and inflammatory markers:} for chronic organ disease and inflammation
    \item \textbf{Infectious serology:} where exposure or symptoms suggest glandular fever, hepatitis, or HIV
    \item \textbf{Sleep studies:} for suspected obstructive sleep apnoea
    \item \textbf{Screening questionnaires:} for depression, anxiety, and sleep problems
    \item \textbf{Further specialist tests:} only when specific conditions are suspected from the history
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management targets the underlying cause where one is found and supports lifestyle improvement in all cases.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Treat the cause:} iron and B12 replacement, thyroid hormone, treatment of depression or sleep apnoea, and medication review
    \item \textbf{Sleep hygiene:} consistent bed and wake times, a cool dark bedroom, and limits on screens and caffeine before bed
    \item \textbf{Regular physical activity:} gradual, consistent exercise improves energy in most causes of fatigue, despite feeling counterintuitive
    \item \textbf{Nutrition and hydration:} regular balanced meals and adequate fluid, avoiding alcohol and excessive caffeine
    \item \textbf{Stress management:} psychological therapy, mindfulness, and pacing of work and activity
    \item \textbf{Pacing for ME/CFS:} planned activity and rest within energy limits, avoiding the boom--bust cycle; graded exercise and cognitive behavioural therapy are used carefully and individually
    \item \textbf{Work and life adjustments:} flexible hours, task prioritisation, and delegation where possible
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reduced work performance, absenteeism, and job loss
    \item Increased accident risk, especially in driving and safety-critical work
    \item Impaired relationships, social withdrawal, and reduced quality of life
    \item Progression of the underlying undiagnosed condition, such as anaemia or hypothyroidism
    \item Worsening of mental health, including depression, in chronic, unrelieved fatigue
    \item Deconditioning and worsening of symptoms when activity is avoided entirely
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the cause. Fatigue from anaemia, thyroid disease, depression, and sleep apnoea typically resolves substantially with effective treatment. Lifestyle-related fatigue responds to sustained improvements in sleep, activity, and stress. In many people with no identified medical cause, fatigue improves over months with supportive management, although a proportion continues to have symptoms. In ME/CFS, outcomes vary: some people recover, most improve to a stable level, and a minority remain severely affected; pacing and appropriate support give the best results. An honest, structured approach from a doctor who takes the fatigue seriously is associated with better outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Protect sleep: aim for 7--9 hours, keep a consistent schedule, and treat snoring or insomnia early
    \item Be active daily, building up gradually, as exercise is protective against fatigue
    \item Eat regularly and well, and drink enough water throughout the day
    \item Limit alcohol and caffeine, especially in the afternoon and evening
    \item Manage stress proactively with breaks, hobbies, and social connection
    \item Have a routine health check if fatigue has been present for more than a few weeks
    \item Do not self-prescribe stimulants, energy drinks, or supplements to push through fatigue
\end{itemize}
