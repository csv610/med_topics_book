\chapter{Female Hormone Medicines}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Female hormone medicines are treatments that contain oestrogen, progesterone or progestogens, testosterone, or other hormones that act on the female reproductive system. They are used for many purposes: contraception, management of menstrual disorders, hormone replacement therapy (HRT) at menopause, fertility treatment, and treatment of conditions such as endometriosis and polycystic ovary syndrome. These medicines are among the most widely prescribed in the world and are highly effective when matched to the individual. Because they act on the whole body, they also carry benefits and risks beyond their main purpose, and choosing among them requires understanding how they work and what they do.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hormonal medicines are used by a large proportion of women at some point in their lives. The combined oral contraceptive pill has been used by most Australian women of reproductive age, and hormonal contraception in its various forms is the most common method of preventing pregnancy. Hormone replacement therapy is used by a substantial minority of women during and after menopause, with use fluctuating over the decades in response to evolving evidence about its risks. Hormonal treatments are also central to fertility services, which assist tens of thousands of Australian women each year. Menstrual disorders such as heavy bleeding and endometriosis, which affect up to a quarter of women, are frequently managed with hormonal medicines.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Hormone medicines work by altering the natural signalling of the reproductive system.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Combined hormonal contraceptives:} oestrogen and a progestogen suppress ovulation, thicken cervical mucus, and thin the endometrium
    \item \textbf{Progestogen-only methods:} the pill, implant, injection, and hormonal IUD act mainly on cervical mucus and the endometrium, with variable ovulation suppression
    \item \textbf{Hormone replacement therapy:} low-dose oestrogen replaces the hormone lost at menopause, relieving vasomotor and genitourinary symptoms and protecting bone; a progestogen is added in women with a uterus to protect the endometrium
    \item \textbf{Progestogens for menstrual disorders:} stabilise the endometrium, reducing heavy or irregular bleeding; high doses can suppress endometriosis tissue
    \item \textbf{Gonadotrophin-releasing hormone (GnRH) analogues:} temporarily switch off ovarian hormone production, used in endometriosis, fibroids, and fertility treatment
    \item \textbf{Fertility medicines:} such as clomiphene and gonadotrophins, stimulate the ovaries to produce and release eggs
    \item \textbf{Androgens:} low-dose testosterone is used in some women for hypoactive sexual desire disorder
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The relevant features are the intended effects, side effects, and the way each medicine is taken.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Contraception: reliable pregnancy prevention, with the choice of daily pill, injection every 3 months, implant lasting 3 years, or IUD lasting years
    \item Menstrual benefits: lighter, more regular, and less painful periods on combined and progestogen methods
    \item Menopause relief: reduced hot flushes, improved sleep, and relief of vaginal dryness with HRT
    \item Common side effects: irregular bleeding, breast tenderness, headache, mood changes, and nausea in the early months
    \item Improvement of acne and premenstrual symptoms in some women on combined pills
    \item Effects on libido, which vary between women and medicines
    \item Individual tolerance: side effects often settle after the first 3 months or with a change of preparation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The main decision is which medicine, and for which purpose, but other considerations include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Medical conditions that affect safety: migraine with aura, high blood pressure, clotting history, and liver disease influence whether oestrogen-containing medicines are suitable
    \item Age and smoking: combined hormonal methods carry higher risk in smokers over 35
    \item Interactions with other medicines: some antibiotics, epilepsy medicines, and herbal products reduce effectiveness
    \item The distinction between symptoms of the condition and side effects of treatment
    \item Breastfeeding and pregnancy planning, which change the options available
    \item Menopause diagnosis before starting HRT, so that other causes of symptoms are excluded
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Women should seek medical advice when starting, changing, or stopping any hormone medicine, and promptly if side effects are troublesome or unexpected symptoms develop. Anyone with risk factors such as smoking, high blood pressure, migraine, or a family history of blood clots or breast cancer should discuss the options carefully.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe chest pain, breathlessness, coughing blood, or a painful swollen leg, which may indicate a blood clot
    \item Sudden severe headache, visual disturbance, weakness, or difficulty speaking, which may indicate stroke
    \item Severe abdominal pain, especially with an intrauterine device in place
    \item Signs of a severe allergic reaction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} menstrual pattern, symptoms, medical conditions, medicines, smoking, family history, and pregnancy plans
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure measurement:} required before prescribing oestrogen-containing medicines
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test:} to exclude pregnancy before starting
    \item \textbf{Investigations for the underlying condition:} pelvic ultrasound for bleeding or pain, hormone profiles for PCOS, and endometrial sampling where indicated
    \item \textbf{Assessment of menopause:} symptom history, with FSH testing only when the diagnosis is unclear
    \item \textbf{Thrombophilia testing:} only in selected women with a strong personal or family history of clots
    \item \textbf{Bone density testing:} for women considering long-term HRT for bone protection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Contraception:} match the method to the woman's health, lifestyle, and preferences; long-acting methods have the highest typical-use effectiveness
    \item \textbf{Menstrual disorders:} hormonal IUD for heavy bleeding, combined or progestogen pills for cycle control, and GnRH analogues for endometriosis
    \item \textbf{Menopause:} HRT is the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms; start with the lowest effective dose, review regularly, and use vaginal oestrogen for genitourinary symptoms
    \item \textbf{Fertility treatment:} ovulation induction and assisted reproduction under specialist care
    \item \textbf{Treatment of PCOS:} combined hormonal contraceptives regulate cycles and reduce androgen effects, alongside lifestyle measures
    \item \textbf{Risk minimisation:} choose progestogen-only or non-hormonal methods where oestrogen is unsafe
    \item \textbf{Regular review:} reassess the continuing need for the medicine at least annually
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Venous thromboembolism and stroke, increased with combined hormonal methods and HRT, particularly in smokers and older women
    \item Small increased risk of breast cancer with current or recent combined hormonal and menopausal hormone use
    \item Gallbladder disease with oestrogen-containing medicines
    \item Irregular or breakthrough bleeding, which is common and usually settles
    \item Mood effects and reduced libido in some women
    \item Ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome, a rare but serious complication of fertility treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Hormone medicines are among the most effective and best-studied treatments in medicine. Serious adverse events are rare in healthy, appropriately selected women, and the risks are generally small in absolute terms, especially in younger women. Menstrual disorders improve in most women, contraception is highly reliable with correct use, and menopause symptoms respond rapidly to HRT. The key to good outcomes is individualised prescribing: the right medicine, at the right dose, for the right woman, reviewed over time, with shared decision-making about risks and benefits.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Tell the doctor about all medicines, smoking, and family history of clots or breast cancer
    \item Take combined pills consistently at the same time, and know what to do after missed pills
    \item Do not smoke if using oestrogen-containing medicines
    \item Report new symptoms such as leg pain, chest pain, or severe headache promptly
    \item Attend annual reviews to reassess the continuing need and risks
    \item Store medicines correctly and check expiry dates
    \item Read the consumer medicine information leaflet provided with the medicine
\end{itemize}
