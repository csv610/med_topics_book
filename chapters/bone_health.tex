\chapter{Bone Health}
\index{Bone Health}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bone health refers to the strength, density, and structural integrity of the skeleton throughout life. Healthy bone is living tissue that constantly remodels, removing old bone and laying down new bone in response to mechanical load and hormonal signals. Bone mass increases during childhood and adolescence, peaks in the late twenties to early thirties, and then slowly declines with age, with accelerated loss after the menopause in women. Protecting bone health is a lifelong process: building strong bone early in life and minimising loss later in life substantially reduces the risk of osteoporosis, fragility fractures, and the disability that follows them. The key modifiable factors are nutrition, physical activity, vitamin D, smoking, alcohol, and the management of conditions and medicines that affect bone.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bone loss and osteoporosis are major public health problems. An estimated one in three women and one in five men over the age of 50 will sustain an osteoporotic fracture during their lifetime. Fractures of the hip, spine, and wrist are the most common and rise steeply with age. Hip fractures are particularly serious: they lead to hospitalisation, loss of independence, and increased mortality, with roughly a fifth of people dying within a year. Peak bone mass is highest in people who are physically active and well-nourished in childhood and adolescence, and it varies by sex, ethnicity, and genetics. As populations age, the number of fractures is expected to grow unless prevention is improved.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bone remodelling:} osteoclasts resorb bone and osteoblasts lay down new bone in a coupled cycle; when resorption outpaces formation, bone mass falls.
    \item \textbf{Peak bone mass:} bone density in later life depends heavily on the amount accumulated by the early thirties, which is influenced by genetics, nutrition, hormones, and physical activity during growth.
    \item \textbf{Menopause:} loss of oestrogen after the menopause removes an important brake on osteoclast activity, causing rapid bone loss in the first few years.
    \item \textbf{Nutrition:} calcium is the mineral building block of bone, and vitamin D is required for its absorption; deficiency of either impairs bone strength.
    \item \textbf{Mechanical loading:} weight-bearing and resistance exercise stimulates bone formation through strain on the skeleton, while inactivity and bed rest accelerate bone loss.
    \item \textbf{Risk factors for accelerated loss:} ageing, low body weight, smoking, excess alcohol, long-term steroids, and conditions such as thyroid disease, coeliac disease, and rheumatoid arthritis.
    \item \textbf{Consequences:} as bone mass and microarchitecture deteriorate, bone becomes porous and brittle, and increasingly minor trauma causes fracture.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Bone loss itself is silent, and good bone health produces no symptoms at all. The features that signal deteriorating bone health include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A fragility fracture -- a broken bone from a fall from standing height or less
    \item Sudden back pain and height loss from vertebral fractures
    \item A stooped posture or developing kyphosis
    \item Low body weight or a history of eating disorders, which are risk markers
    \item Loss of muscle strength, frailty, and poor balance, which increase fall risk
    \item A family history of hip fracture or osteoporosis
    \item Risk factors such as early menopause, long-term steroids, smoking, or high alcohol intake
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal age-related bone changes:} mild bone loss that is not yet at fracture-risk levels and needs only lifestyle measures.
    \item \textbf{Osteoporosis:} bone density more than 2.5 standard deviations below the young adult mean, conferring significant fracture risk.
    \item \textbf{Osteomalacia:} soft, under-mineralised bone from vitamin D deficiency, causing diffuse pain and muscle weakness.
    \item \textbf{Paget's disease:} focal, disorganised bone remodelling with characteristic radiological changes and raised alkaline phosphatase.
    \item \textbf{Secondary causes:} hyperparathyroidism, hyperthyroidism, coeliac disease, chronic kidney disease, and multiple myeloma can all present with low bone density.
    \item \textbf{Arthritis:} joint pain and stiffness that can be confused with bone pain, but which arises from joints rather than bone.
    \item \textbf{Chronic pain syndromes:} fibromyalgia and non-specific back pain can coexist with and confuse the picture of bone disease.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Discuss bone health with your GP if you have risk factors for osteoporosis, have had a fracture after a minor fall, are taking long-term steroids, have had early menopause, or have a family history of osteoporosis. Unexplained bone pain, progressive height loss, or a stooped posture should also be assessed.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe pain, deformity, or inability to bear weight after a fall (possible fracture)
    \item Sudden severe back pain with numbness, weakness, or loss of bladder or bowel control (possible spinal cord compression)
    \item Faintness, collapse, or head injury after a fall
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} age, menopausal status, fracture history, family history, diet, exercise, smoking, alcohol, and medications.
    \item \textbf{Examination:} height and weight, posture, and assessment of mobility and falls risk.
    \item \textbf{Bone density scan (DXA):} the definitive measurement of bone density at the hip and spine, reported as a T-score.
    \item \textbf{Fracture risk assessment:} tools such as FRAX combine clinical risk factors with bone density to estimate the ten-year probability of fracture.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} calcium, vitamin D, and renal function, with thyroid, coeliac, or other tests if a secondary cause is suspected.
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} plain radiographs to detect and characterise fractures, and MRI where a vertebral fracture or bone disease is suspected.
    \item \textbf{Repeat scanning:} follow-up DXA two to three years after starting treatment, or at intervals for untreated high-risk patients.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Calcium:} aim for about 1000--1200 mg daily from dairy products, leafy green vegetables, nuts, and fortified foods; supplements are used only when dietary intake is inadequate.
    \item \textbf{Vitamin D:} sun exposure through the year, with a supplement recommended in winter, for housebound people, and in those with deficiency.
    \item \textbf{Exercise:} regular weight-bearing activity such as brisk walking, running, or tennis, plus resistance and balance training at least twice a week.
    \item \textbf{Smoking and alcohol:} stopping smoking and limiting alcohol to within recommended levels slows bone loss.
    \item \textbf{Medication:} for people with osteoporosis or high fracture risk, bisphosphonates, denosumab, or anabolic agents such as teriparatide are prescribed.
    \item \textbf{Hormone-related treatment:} postmenopausal hormone therapy may be considered for younger symptomatic women after individual risk assessment.
    \item \textbf{Falls prevention:} physiotherapy, home safety reviews, vision checks, and strength and balance programs reduce fractures in older people.
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular review, repeat DXA, and assessment of adherence to treatment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Poor bone health leads to fractures, and the complications of fractures include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hip fractures, with surgery, prolonged immobility, and increased mortality
    \item Vertebral fractures causing chronic pain, height loss, and spinal deformity
    \item Wrist fractures, which impair independence during recovery
    \item Loss of mobility, muscle wasting, and fear of falling
    \item Reduced quality of life and increased reliance on care
    \item Depression and social isolation after serious fractures
    \item The side effects of osteoporosis treatment, including gastrointestinal intolerance and rare atypical fractures
    \item Missed opportunity for prevention, since many people first present after a fracture has already occurred
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Bone health is highly modifiable at every age. Children and young adults who are active and well-nourished build a higher peak bone mass that protects them for life. In mid-life and beyond, people who maintain adequate calcium and vitamin D, keep exercising, avoid smoking, and limit alcohol can slow bone loss substantially. For people already diagnosed with osteoporosis, modern treatment stabilises or improves bone density and reduces fracture risk by roughly half. Even after a first fracture, starting treatment and falls-prevention measures significantly lowers the chance of further fractures, so a diagnosis of osteoporosis is never a reason to give up: with good management, most people stay mobile and independent.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Eat a calcium-rich diet and take a vitamin D supplement in winter or if advised.
    \item Exercise regularly, combining weight-bearing activity with resistance and balance training.
    \item Keep a healthy body weight; both underweight and obesity damage bone health.
    \item Do not smoke, and keep alcohol within recommended limits.
    \item Have a bone density assessment if you have risk factors or a history of fracture.
    \item Keep your home well lit and free of trip hazards, and have your vision checked regularly.
    \item If you take steroids or other bone-depleting medicines, ask your doctor about bone protection.
    \item Ensure children and adolescents are active and eat well, since their habits build the skeleton they will keep for life.
\end{itemize}
