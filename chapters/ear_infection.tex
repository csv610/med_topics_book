\chapter{Ear Infection}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Ear infection is a general term for infection of the outer or middle ear. Otitis externa, often called swimmer's ear, is an infection of the ear canal, while otitis media is an infection of the middle ear, the air-filled space behind the eardrum. Middle ear infection is one of the most common illnesses of childhood and is usually triggered by a cold, while outer ear infection is common in swimmers and in people who insert objects into their ears. Most ear infections resolve without treatment, but they can cause significant pain and, occasionally, serious complications.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Middle ear infection is very common in children, particularly between the ages of six months and three years, and most children have at least one episode before school age. It is more common in winter, in children attending child care, and in children exposed to cigarette smoke. Outer ear infection is most common in adolescents and adults, especially swimmers, and in warm, humid climates. Both types are among the most frequent reasons for GP visits.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The two main types of ear infection have different causes and mechanisms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Middle ear infection (otitis media):} caused by viruses and bacteria, often Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and Moraxella; infection follows a cold, when the eustachian tube becomes blocked and fluid builds up behind the eardrum, becoming infected
    \item \textbf{Outer ear infection (otitis externa):} caused by bacteria, usually Pseudomonas and Staphylococcus, or fungi; it follows moisture, trauma, or irritation of the ear canal
    \item \textbf{Risk factors in children:} young age, child care attendance, bottle feeding lying down, pacifier use, and parental smoking
    \item \textbf{Risk factors in adults:} swimming, hearing aids, eczema, psoriasis, and insertion of cotton buds or other objects
    \item \textbf{Glue ear:} persistent fluid behind the eardrum after infection, which can affect hearing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms differ between the two types of infection.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ear pain, which may be worse at night and when lying down
    \item In middle ear infection: fever, irritability, pulling at the ear, and poor feeding in children; muffled hearing and a feeling of fullness in adults
    \item In outer ear infection: itching, pain on moving or tugging the ear, discharge, and tenderness of the ear canal
    \item Discharge from the ear, which may occur when the eardrum bursts in middle ear infection
    \item Hearing loss, which is usually temporary
    \item In severe cases: swelling, spreading redness, or facial weakness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Ear pain and related symptoms have other possible causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Teething pain in infants
    \item Throat infections and tonsillitis, which refer pain to the ear
    \item Dental problems, including tooth abscess
    \item Temporomandibular joint disorders
    \item Foreign body in the ear
    \item Eustachian tube dysfunction without infection
    \item Rarely, serious conditions such as mastoiditis or brain infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for ear pain, discharge, or hearing loss, particularly in a child under two, in a child with a high fever, or if symptoms persist beyond a few days.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe ear pain with swelling, redness, or tenderness behind the ear, which may indicate mastoiditis
    \item Facial weakness or drooping
    \item Stiff neck, confusion, or high fever, which may indicate spread of infection
    \item Sudden severe headache
    \item Fluid or blood draining from the ear after a head injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is usually made by examination.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Otoscopy:} examination of the ear canal and eardrum with an otoscope
    \item \textbf{Tympanometry:} assessment of middle ear pressure and fluid
    \item \textbf{Hearing assessment:} for children with recurrent or persistent infections
    \item \textbf{Swabs:} taken from the ear canal in otitis externa when treatment is not working
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} rarely needed; CT may be used if complications such as mastoiditis are suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Most ear infections are managed conservatively.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} paracetamol or ibuprofen is the mainstay of treatment
    \item \textbf{Watchful waiting:} many middle ear infections, particularly in older children, resolve without antibiotics within a few days
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} prescribed for severe infection, children under two, persistent symptoms, or those at risk of complications
    \item \textbf{Ear drops:} for otitis externa, antibiotic or combination drops are usually effective
    \item \textbf{Aural toilet:} gentle cleaning of the ear canal by a clinician for otitis externa
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} checking that the infection has resolved and hearing has returned
    \item \textbf{Grommets:} small tubes inserted into the eardrum for children with recurrent infections or persistent glue ear
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Recurrent infections and persistent fluid (glue ear), with effects on hearing and development
    \item Eardrum perforation, which usually heals but can cause scarring
    \item Mastoiditis, an infection of the bone behind the ear
    \item Cholesteatoma, an abnormal growth in the middle ear in chronic cases
    \item Rarely, spread of infection to the brain, causing meningitis or brain abscess
    \item Chronic outer ear infection, which can narrow the ear canal
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for ear infections is very good. Most middle ear infections resolve within a few days, and most outer ear infections clear within a week of treatment. Hearing usually returns to normal once the infection and fluid resolve, although some children develop persistent glue ear that requires monitoring or grommets. Serious complications are rare when infections are managed appropriately.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep immunisations up to date, including the pneumococcal vaccine
    \item Avoid exposing children to cigarette smoke
    \item Breastfeed infants where possible, and bottle-feed in an upright position
    \item Avoid inserting cotton buds or other objects into the ear
    \item Dry the ears gently after swimming and use earplugs when swimming frequently
    \item Treat colds and nasal congestion with simple measures
    \item See a doctor for persistent ear problems
\end{itemize}
