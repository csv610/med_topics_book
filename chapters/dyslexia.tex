\chapter{Dyslexia}
\index{Dyslexia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dyslexia is a specific learning difficulty that primarily affects the skills involved in accurate and fluent reading and spelling. It is characterised by difficulty with phonological processing, which is the ability to recognise and manipulate the sounds of spoken language, leading to problems decoding words. Dyslexia is not related to intelligence, vision, or educational opportunity, and it affects people across the full range of abilities. It is a lifelong condition, but with early identification, effective teaching, and appropriate support, people with dyslexia can learn to read successfully and achieve their potential.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Dyslexia is the most common specific learning difficulty, affecting an estimated 5 to 10 per cent of the population, with some studies suggesting higher figures. It is more commonly identified in boys, although research suggests it affects boys and girls at similar rates, with boys referred more often. Dyslexia frequently coexists with other difficulties, including dysgraphia, dyscalculia, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, and developmental coordination disorder.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Dyslexia is a neurodevelopmental condition with a strong genetic basis.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} dyslexia runs strongly in families, and multiple genes have been linked to the condition
    \item \textbf{Brain differences:} imaging studies show differences in the structure and function of brain regions involved in reading, particularly in the left hemisphere
    \item \textbf{Phonological processing:} the core difficulty is in breaking words into sounds and linking sounds to letters
    \item \textbf{Rapid naming:} many people with dyslexia also have difficulty rapidly naming letters, numbers, and objects
    \item \textbf{Working memory:} difficulty holding sounds and words in mind affects decoding and comprehension
    \item \textbf{Neuroplasticity:} with effective teaching, the reading networks of the brain can develop, though differently
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The signs of dyslexia change across the lifespan but share a consistent pattern.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Delayed speech and difficulty learning nursery rhymes in preschool years
    \item Difficulty learning letter names and their sounds
    \item Trouble blending sounds to read words and breaking words into sounds to spell
    \item Slow, effortful, or inaccurate reading, with frequent guessing
    \item Poor spelling that does not improve with practice
    \item Difficulty with reading comprehension because so much effort goes into decoding
    \item Avoiding reading and writing tasks
    \item Difficulty with sequences, such as the days of the week or multiplication tables
    \item Strong oral language skills and creativity alongside the reading difficulties
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Reading difficulties should be distinguished from other causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item General intellectual disability or global developmental delay
    \item Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, affecting attention during reading
    \item Hearing or vision problems
    \item Limited English proficiency or interrupted schooling
    \item Emotional and behavioural difficulties affecting learning
    \item Dysgraphia and dyscalculia, which may coexist
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents who are concerned about their child's reading should discuss this with teachers and the school learning support team, and seek assessment if difficulties persist despite good teaching. A GP can help coordinate referral to a psychologist or educational specialist for formal assessment.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden loss of reading ability or other new language difficulties in an adult, which may indicate a stroke
    \item Sudden confusion, weakness, or difficulty speaking
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Dyslexia is diagnosed through a comprehensive assessment by a psychologist or specialist teacher.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Educational history:} reports of reading development, effort, and response to teaching
    \item \textbf{Cognitive assessment:} intelligence testing to establish the pattern of strengths and weaknesses
    \item \textbf{Standardised reading tests:} measures of decoding, reading fluency, and comprehension
    \item \textbf{Phonological assessment:} tests of sound awareness and manipulation
    \item \textbf{Spelling assessment:} standardised measures of spelling accuracy
    \item \textbf{Screening for coexisting conditions:} assessment for ADHD, dysgraphia, and dyscalculia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management centres on specialised teaching and support.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Structured literacy instruction:} systematic, multisensory teaching of the sound-letter relationships, delivered explicitly and sequentially
    \item \textbf{Reading practice:} regular practice with decodable texts and gradually increasing difficulty
    \item \textbf{Assistive technology:} text-to-speech and speech-to-text software, audiobooks, and spelling tools
    \item \textbf{School accommodations:} extra time in exams, oral assessment options, and reduced copying demands
    \item \textbf{Support for writing:} as many people with dyslexia also have writing difficulties
    \item \textbf{Emotional support:} building confidence, reducing anxiety, and celebrating strengths
    \item \textbf{Adult support:} workplace adjustments and further study accommodations
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Low self-esteem, frustration, and avoidance of reading
    \item Falling behind academically across subjects that require reading
    \item Anxiety, and in some cases depression, related to school difficulties
    \item Behavioural problems arising from frustration
    \item Reduced educational and career opportunities without support
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Dyslexia is a lifelong condition, but the outlook is strongly influenced by early identification and the quality of teaching. With structured literacy instruction, the majority of children with dyslexia learn to read adequately, and many go on to achieve at the highest levels. Adults with dyslexia manage effectively with assistive technology and workplace accommodations. The condition is associated with notable strengths in areas such as problem-solving, creativity, and oral communication.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Read aloud to children from an early age and make reading enjoyable
    \item Seek assessment early if reading difficulties persist despite good teaching
    \item Advocate for structured literacy instruction at school
    \item Use audiobooks and text-to-speech technology without stigma
    \item Focus on the child's strengths and interests
    \item Allow extra time for reading and writing tasks
    \item Connect with support organisations for information and community
\end{itemize}
