\chapter{Blood Donation}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Blood donation is the voluntary giving of blood so that it can be used for transfusions in people who need it. Donated blood is processed into components -- red cells, platelets, plasma, and clotting factors -- so that a single donation can help several different patients. Donors may give whole blood, or give only plasma or platelets through a machine process called apheresis. Donation is carried out by trained staff under strict safety rules designed to protect both the donor and the recipient, and it is an essential part of modern healthcare, supplying patients with trauma, surgery, cancer, and blood disorders.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Only a small proportion of eligible adults donate blood regularly -- in many developed countries, roughly one in 25 to one in 50 people. Demand is constant because blood components have a limited shelf life: red cells last about 35--42 days and platelets only about 5--7 days. Different blood groups are needed in different proportions, with type O negative blood especially valuable because it can be given to almost anyone in an emergency. Blood services therefore run continuous public appeals to maintain stocks, particularly around holidays and after major events that strain the supply.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Blood donation is not a disease, but understanding the body's response explains the process and its effects. A standard whole-blood donation removes about 470 mL (roughly one unit), approximately 8--10\% of an adult's total blood volume. Within a day the body largely replaces the lost fluid, and the bone marrow steadily produces new red cells; full red-cell recovery takes several weeks. Because red cells contain iron, regular donors lose iron and can develop iron deficiency if their stores are not replenished. Donating platelets or plasma removes less red-cell mass but takes longer, usually 60--90 minutes per session.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Whole blood donation:} the simplest and most common form, with about 10 minutes of collection
    \item \textbf{Plasma donation:} plasma is separated by apheresis and the red cells are returned
    \item \textbf{Platelet donation:} platelets are collected by apheresis, useful for people with low platelet counts
    \item \textbf{Body response:} rapid replacement of fluid, followed more slowly by replacement of red cells and iron
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Most donors feel well during and after donation, but some experience minor effects:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Brief discomfort when the needle is inserted
    \item A small bruise or soreness at the needle site
    \item Light-headedness, dizziness, or a feeling of faintness during or shortly after donation
    \item Fatigue in the hours after donating
    \item Tiredness or pallor from iron deficiency with repeated donations
    \item Serious reactions, such as fainting with injury, nerve irritation, or local infection, are uncommon
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Feeling unwell after donation is usually a simple vasovagal reaction, but it should not be confused with other problems:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Fainting from dehydration, low blood sugar, or prolonged fasting before donation
    \item Breathlessness or chest pain that may signal an underlying heart or lung condition
    \item Prolonged bleeding or an expanding bruise suggesting an unrecognised bleeding disorder
    \item Fatigue from iron deficiency, anaemia, or another undiagnosed illness
    \item Skin infection or an allergic reaction at the needle site
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Tell donation staff immediately if you feel faint, nauseated, or unwell during or after donation. Seek medical advice if a bruise is large, painful, or enlarging, if the needle site becomes red, swollen, or oozes, or if you develop chest pain, breathlessness, or persistent dizziness.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fainting with injury, or a collapse that does not resolve quickly
    \item Chest pain or severe difficulty breathing after donation
    \item Very heavy bleeding from the needle site
    \item Signs of a severe allergic reaction, such as swelling of the face or throat
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The donation process includes a health assessment at every visit, designed to keep both donors and recipients safe:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Donor questionnaire:} questions about health, medicines, travel, and any risk factors for infection
    \item \textbf{Haemoglobin check:} a finger-prick test to ensure the donor is not anaemic
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure and pulse:} measured before donation
    \item \textbf{Donor blood testing:} each donation is screened for blood group and for infections such as hepatitis B and C, HIV, and syphilis
    \item \textbf{Iron monitoring:} some services check iron levels in regular donors
    \item \textbf{Deferral:} temporary or permanent exclusion of donors who are unwell, anaemic, or at higher risk of transmitting infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Preparation, aftercare, and spacing of donations are the keys to a safe and comfortable experience:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Preparation:} eat a normal meal and drink extra fluids before donating, and avoid alcohol beforehand
    \item \textbf{During donation:} relax, squeeze a stress ball, and tell the staff if you feel faint
    \item \textbf{After donation:} rest for a short time, eat and drink, and keep the pressure dressing on for a few hours
    \item \textbf{Same day:} avoid heavy lifting, strenuous exercise, hot showers, and long drives, and do not smoke heavily
    \item \textbf{Iron replacement:} eat iron-rich foods, and follow advice if your haemoglobin is borderline
    \item \textbf{Spacing:} leave the recommended gap between donations (often about 12--16 weeks for whole blood) to allow full recovery
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} attend any recall from the blood service, and let it know if you later develop an illness relevant to donation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bruising, soreness, or, rarely, infection at the needle site
    \item Fainting and, rarely, injury from falling
    \item Nerve irritation causing tingling or numbness in the arm
    \item Iron deficiency anaemia with frequent donations
    \item Deferral from donation if the haemoglobin is too low
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For most healthy donors, blood donation is safe and well tolerated, and any minor effects settle within a day or two. The body replaces the donated cells within weeks, and iron stores recover with a balanced diet and adequate time between donations. Regular donation can be continued for many years when the donor's health and iron levels allow. Beyond the personal experience, each donation can save or improve the lives of several recipients, making it one of the most straightforward contributions to community health.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a healthy, iron-rich diet and stay well hydrated in the days before donating
    \item Do not donate if you feel unwell, have a fever, or have been unwell recently
    \item Check your eligibility each time and answer the questionnaire honestly
    \item Wait the recommended interval between donations to protect your iron stores
    \item Tell donation staff about all medicines and any medical conditions
    \item Rest and eat after donating, and seek prompt advice if you feel unwell afterwards
\end{itemize}
