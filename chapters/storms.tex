\chapter{Storms}
\index{Storms}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A storm is a period of dangerous weather, including thunderstorms, tropical cyclones (also known as cyclones, typhoons, or hurricanes), severe windstorms, hailstorms, floods, and in some regions winter blizzards. This chapter addresses the health effects of storms and how people can protect themselves before, during, and after severe weather. Storms injure and kill people in several ways: directly through wind, flying debris, lightning, and floodwater, and indirectly through power loss, disrupted services, injury during cleanup, and the spread of infectious disease after flooding.

Climate change is making severe weather events more frequent and more intense in many parts of the world, including some countries, where heat, bushfire, and flood risk are rising. Severe storms are a public health emergency: they disrupt electricity, water, transport, and health care at exactly the time when these services are needed most. Preparedness is the single most effective protection. Understanding local warning systems, preparing the home, knowing evacuation routes, and having a plan for people who are medically vulnerable can substantially reduce the health impact of any storm.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Severe storms affect every region of some countries. Thunderstorms are common across some countries, with a high frequency in the tropical north and the eastern seaboard, and can bring lightning, flash flooding, hail, and damaging winds. Tropical cyclones affect northern the affected region most years, bringing destructive winds, storm surge, and torrential rain. The southeast experiences destructive storms associated with cold fronts and low-pressure systems, while hail and flash flooding are significant hazards in the east.

The health burden is substantial. Worldwide, floods are the most common disaster and cause more deaths than any other weather event, largely through drowning. In many countries, the majority of flood-related deaths occur when people enter floodwater in vehicles, attempting to drive through water or fording swollen rivers. Heat-related illness is also a feature of summer storms, and the combination of heat and prolonged power loss can be lethal for the elderly and the medically vulnerable. Bushfires, which often follow drought and are associated with severe fire weather rather than rain storms, have caused some of the world's worst weather disasters in recent years. Regardless of the exact hazard, the people at greatest risk are older adults, young children, people with chronic illness or disability, and those living in remote or poorly constructed housing.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\subsection*{How storms injure people}
The mechanisms of storm injury are direct and largely predictable.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Drowning:} the leading cause of storm death, occurring when people or vehicles are swept into floodwater or when flash flooding catches people in low-lying areas
    \item \textbf{Trauma:} injuries from wind-borne debris, falling trees, structural collapse, and being struck by fallen objects; serious injuries also occur during roof repairs and cleanup after the storm
    \item \textbf{Electrocution:} downed power lines and damaged wiring cause fatal and non-fatal shocks, particularly after the storm has passed
    \item \textbf{Lightning strikes:} direct strikes and ground current cause cardiac arrest, burns, and neurological injury; lightning is often under-recognised as a cause of death in storm-prone regions
    \item \textbf{Carbon monoxide poisoning:} generators and fuel-burning heaters used indoors after power loss release carbon monoxide, a colourless, odourless gas that kills silently
    \item \textbf{Hypothermia:} exposure to cold and wet conditions, especially in the elderly or in people whose homes are damaged
    \item \textbf{Heat illness:} in summer storms, particularly where power loss disables air conditioning and refrigeration
    \item \textbf{Hypovolaemic and crush injury:} entrapment in structural collapse or debris, and prolonged immersion
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Health effects of power loss and disruption}
Loss of electricity affects people who depend on powered medical equipment such as home oxygen, dialysis, or insulin refrigeration. Interrupted water and sewerage services increase the risk of gastroenteritis, and food poisoning follows the loss of refrigeration. Access to medicines may be cut when pharmacies close or roads are blocked.

\subsection*{Post-flood infectious disease}
Floodwater is contaminated with sewage, animal waste, and chemicals. Entering or drinking floodwater can cause gastroenteritis, and contaminated wounds can become infected with bacteria including \textit{Leptospira}, which causes leptospirosis. Mosquito numbers surge after floods, and standing water creates breeding sites for mosquitoes that can transmit Ross River virus and other mosquito-borne infections in many countries. Mould growth in flooded homes is a major trigger for asthma and respiratory illness in the weeks and months after the event.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The health effects of a storm are best understood in three phases.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{During the storm:} drowning, trauma from debris and collapse, lightning injury, and entrapment are the immediate threats
    \item \textbf{In the hours and days after:} electrocution during cleanup, carbon monoxide poisoning from generators, falls from roofs and ladders, heat or cold illness, and the stress and anxiety of the event itself
    \item \textbf{In the weeks and months after:} wound infections, gastroenteritis from contaminated water, mosquito-borne illness, respiratory problems from mould, worsening of chronic disease, and post-traumatic stress, anxiety, and depression
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Specific presentations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Carbon monoxide poisoning:} headache, dizziness, nausea, confusion, and drowsiness in a house where a generator or heater is running; multiple household members affected at once is a classic clue
    \item \textbf{Lightning injury:} loss of consciousness, cardiac arrest, burns, numbness, and confusion
    \item \textbf{Drowning and near-drowning:} unconsciousness, breathing difficulty, and water inhalation
    \item \textbf{Leptospirosis:} fever, headache, muscle pain, and sometimes jaundice, days after exposure to floodwater or contaminated mud
    \item \textbf{Mould-related illness:} worsening asthma, cough, and nasal symptoms after returning to a flooded home
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the environment causes multiple overlapping conditions, symptom patterns need careful thought.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Headache, nausea, and confusion: carbon monoxide poisoning versus heat illness, infection, or dehydration
    \item Chest symptoms: exposure and stress reactions versus asthma or heart problems in vulnerable people
    \item Fever: infectious gastroenteritis versus leptospirosis or other waterborne infection versus mosquito-borne disease
    \item Rash: infection versus contact with irritant floodwater or chemicals
    \item Distress, poor sleep, and agitation: normal stress reactions versus post-traumatic stress disorder, and depression
    \item Abdominal pain and diarrhoea: contaminated food and water versus stress-related symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor after a storm for a wound that is deep, dirty, or becoming red, swollen, or painful; for fever, vomiting, or diarrhoea; for worsening asthma or breathlessness; or for persistent distress, poor sleep, or anxiety that is interfering with daily life. Anyone with a chronic condition who has missed medications, lost power for medical equipment, or run out of medicines should seek review as soon as possible.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Drowning, near-drowning, or difficulty breathing
    \item Severe injury, bleeding, or a suspected fracture or head injury
    \item Collapse, confusion, or reduced consciousness, which may indicate carbon monoxide poisoning, heat illness, or lightning injury
    \item Chest pain, a major burn, or any life-threatening emergency
    \item If someone has been in floodwater and is suddenly unwell, with fever and muscle pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Emergency assessment:} the priority is airway, breathing, and circulation, treating immediate threats such as cardiac arrest from lightning or drowning
    \item \textbf{Carbon monoxide testing:} a pulse oximeter or blood test (carboxyhaemoglobin) confirms exposure when poisoning is suspected
    \item \textbf{Wound and wound-infection assessment:} cleaning, review of tetanus vaccination status, and antibiotics for infected wounds; leptospirosis is diagnosed by blood tests when suspected
    \item \textbf{Blood and stool tests:} identify infectious causes of fever and diarrhoea when symptoms persist or are severe
    \item \textbf{Respiratory assessment:} lung function and review for people with asthma or breathing problems returning to mould-affected homes
    \item \textbf{Mental health assessment:} screening for post-traumatic stress and depression in the weeks after a significant event
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\subsection*{Immediate emergency care}
Drowning and lightning victims need immediate resuscitation and emergency medical care even when they appear to recover, because complications such as lung damage and abnormal heart rhythms can develop later. Carbon monoxide poisoning requires fresh air, urgent transfer to hospital, and high-flow oxygen.

\subsection*{Preparedness before the storm}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Know the warnings:} understand the local emergency warning system and have a battery-powered or wind-up radio, and charge phones and power banks
    \item \textbf{Prepare the home:} secure or store loose outdoor items, trim overhanging branches, and consider window protection; for cyclone-prone areas, know the safe room in your home
    \item \textbf{Make a plan:} agree on evacuation routes, a meeting point, and arrangements for pets, older relatives, and neighbours with disabilities
    \item \textbf{Assemble supplies:} three days of water and non-perishable food, medicines, first-aid kit, torch, batteries, and important documents in a waterproof bag
    \item \textbf{Plan for medical needs:} know how to manage without power for equipment such as home oxygen or insulin storage, and keep a list of medicines
    \item \textbf{Review insurance} and understand the advice of local authorities about evacuation orders
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{During the storm}
Stay indoors away from windows, avoid driving through floodwater under any circumstances, and avoid using landline phones or taps during lightning. If told to evacuate, leave early and follow official instructions.

\subsection*{After the storm}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Treat downed power lines as live and stay clear; never drive over them
    \item Never run generators, petrol tools, or outdoor heaters indoors or in a garage, because of carbon monoxide
    \item Avoid wading in or drinking floodwater; assume it is contaminated
    \item Cover wounds with waterproof dressings and wash hands after exposure
    \item Use safe water for drinking and cooking, and discard food that has been in contact with floodwater or unrefrigerated during power loss
    \item Take care with roofs, ladders, and chainsaws during cleanup, where many serious injuries occur
    \item Drying a flooded home quickly, using dehumidifiers and ventilating rooms, reduces the mould that triggers asthma
    \item Use insect repellent to reduce mosquito-borne illness after flooding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Death and serious injury from drowning, trauma, and electrocution
    \item Permanent disability from head injury, crush injury, or lightning-related neurological damage
    \item Carbon monoxide poisoning, which can be fatal and affects whole families
    \item Infectious disease, including gastroenteritis, leptospirosis, and mosquito-borne infections
    \item Deterioration of chronic conditions, including asthma, diabetes, and heart disease, due to disruption of care
    \item Loss of medicines, medical records, and access to health services in the aftermath
    \item Post-traumatic stress, anxiety, and depression, which can persist for months
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people who prepare well survive severe storms without physical harm, but the aftermath can still be exhausting and stressful. Physical injuries, when they occur, have good outcomes with prompt emergency care. The greatest risks are predictable: drowning in floodwater, carbon monoxide from generators, and injuries during cleanup account for a large share of storm deaths, and all are preventable with simple behaviour change. For the broader community, the weeks after a storm bring manageable increases in infection, respiratory illness, and psychological distress, and these respond to primary care and community support. Recovery is usually complete, though people who lose their homes, who suffer severe injury, or who experience prolonged displacement are at higher risk of lasting mental health effects and deserve ongoing support.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Prepare before the season: an emergency kit, a family plan, and knowledge of local warnings
    \item Never drive, walk, or ride through floodwater; most flood deaths occur in vehicles
    \item Treat all downed power lines as live and keep children and pets away
    \item Use generators outdoors only, well away from windows and doors
    \item Install a carbon monoxide detector and a smoke alarm in the home
    \item Have a battery radio, torch, spare batteries, and a first-aid kit ready
    \item Know your health needs: keep at least a week of medicines, and a plan for powered equipment
    \item Keep tetanus vaccination up to date, especially if you may be involved in flood cleanup
    \item After flooding, protect wounds, use safe water, and use insect repellent
    \item Take care with ladders, roofs, and chainsaws during cleanup, and seek help for persistent stress or low mood
\end{itemize}
