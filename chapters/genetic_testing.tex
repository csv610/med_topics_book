\chapter{Genetic Testing}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Genetic testing is the analysis of a person's DNA to identify changes (variants) that may cause or increase the risk of disease. It is used in many settings: to diagnose a suspected genetic condition, to predict the risk of developing a condition in the future, to guide treatment choices, to assess the risk of passing a condition to children, and to screen newborn babies for treatable disorders. Genetic testing is performed on samples such as blood, saliva, or cheek swabs, and in some cases on tissue samples. Because the results can have profound implications for individuals and their families, genetic testing in Australia is usually arranged through a doctor with appropriate counselling, although some direct-to-consumer tests are also available.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Genetic testing is a rapidly growing area of health care. In Australia, testing is available for thousands of conditions, and the use of large gene panels and whole-genome sequencing is expanding. Newborn screening tests all babies for around 25 treatable conditions. Carrier screening for conditions such as cystic fibrosis and spinal muscular atrophy is increasingly offered to couples planning a pregnancy. Predictive testing is available for adult-onset conditions including Huntington disease, hereditary breast and ovarian cancer (BRCA1/BRCA2), and hereditary bowel cancer. Direct-to-consumer tests have also become popular, with millions of people worldwide having their DNA analysed for ancestry and health information.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The scientific basis of genetic testing is the relationship between variations in DNA and health outcomes.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{DNA variants:} changes in the sequence of genes that may be harmless, of uncertain significance, or disease-causing
    \item \textbf{Germline testing:} analyses DNA inherited from parents and present in all cells; results can be passed to children
    \item \textbf{Somatic testing:} analyses DNA in tumour tissue; results are confined to the cancer and guide treatment
    \item \textbf{Pharmacogenetics:} tests that predict how a person will respond to certain medicines based on their gene variants
    \item \textbf{Variant classification:} laboratories classify variants as benign, likely benign, uncertain significance, likely pathogenic, or pathogenic
    \item \textbf{Penetrance and expressivity:} not everyone with a disease-causing variant develops the condition, and severity varies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Genetic testing itself has no physical symptoms, but the context and consequences of testing are important.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Diagnostic testing in a person with features of a genetic condition
    \item Predictive testing in an unaffected person with a family history of an adult-onset condition
    \item Carrier testing to determine whether a person carries a recessive disease gene
    \item Prenatal testing during pregnancy to check the fetus for chromosomal or genetic conditions
    \item Pre-implantation genetic testing of embryos created by IVF
    \item Newborn screening for treatable conditions before symptoms appear
    \item Pharmacogenetic testing to guide medication choice and dosing
    \item Tumour testing to identify targets for cancer treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The decision to test, and the interpretation of results, must be considered alongside other possibilities.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Environmental and lifestyle causes of symptoms being investigated
    \item Non-genetic conditions that mimic the features of a suspected genetic disorder
    \item Variants of uncertain significance, which are found in most large-scale tests and may not explain symptoms
    \item Testing the wrong person or wrong tissue, leading to misleading results
    \item Contamination or laboratory error, which is rare but possible
    \item Direct-to-consumer results that use different methods and quality standards from clinical laboratories
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Speak with your doctor about genetic testing if you have symptoms of a possible genetic condition, a family history of an inherited condition, or if you are planning a family and are concerned about genetic risk. Genetic counselling before testing helps you understand what the results can and cannot tell you.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item This chapter concerns elective testing rather than emergencies; however, if you learn you carry a variant for a condition requiring urgent management, follow the advice of your genetics team promptly
    \item Any severe anxiety, distress, or crisis arising from test results should be addressed urgently with your GP, Lifeline (13 11 14), or Beyond Blue (1300 22 4636)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Genetic testing is performed by accredited laboratories, and results are interpreted by clinical geneticists and genetic counsellors.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-test counselling:} discussion of what the test involves, its limitations, implications for relatives, and the options if results are positive
    \item \textbf{Sample collection:} blood, saliva, cheek swab, or tissue biopsy depending on the test
    \item \textbf{Laboratory methods:} targeted single-gene tests, gene panels, chromosomal microarray, or whole-exome and whole-genome sequencing
    \item \textbf{Result interpretation:} variants are classified according to international guidelines, and results may take weeks to months
    \item \textbf{Post-test counselling:} explanation of the result and its implications for the individual and their family
    \item \textbf{Confirmatory testing:} findings are often confirmed in a second sample or by testing other family members
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of genetic testing focuses on informed consent, appropriate test selection, and acting on results.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Informed consent:} testing is voluntary, and decisions are made after full discussion of risks, benefits, and alternatives
    \item \textbf{Test selection:} the most appropriate test is chosen based on the clinical question and family history
    \item \textbf{Surveillance and prevention:} positive results for cancer-predisposition genes lead to enhanced screening or risk-reducing surgery
    \item \textbf{Treatment targeting:} tumour genetic testing identifies therapies matched to the cancer's genetic profile
    \item \textbf{Medication adjustment:} pharmacogenetic results guide the choice and dose of certain medicines
    \item \textbf{Family communication:} results may be relevant to close relatives, who can pursue their own counselling and testing
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} counselling is available to help individuals and families adjust to results
    \item \textbf{Data protection:} genetic information is confidential health information protected by Australian privacy law
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Psychological distress, anxiety, and depression following positive results
    \item Variants of uncertain significance that create worry without clear guidance
    \item False reassurance from negative results when a condition is not caused by the tested genes
    \item Implications for insurance and employment, although genetic discrimination is restricted by Australian law
    \item Family conflict over sharing or withholding genetic information
    \item Misinterpretation of direct-to-consumer results, leading to unnecessary worry or false security
    \item Cost, as some tests are not covered by Medicare
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Genetic testing has transformed diagnosis and prevention across medicine. It provides a definitive diagnosis for many previously unexplained conditions, enables early detection and prevention of cancers in high-risk families, and allows targeted treatments for a growing list of cancers and rare diseases. The main limitations are the uncertainty of some results and the psychological impact of learning genetic information. With skilled counselling and follow-up, most people cope well with results, and the information gained is usually of lasting value to individuals and their families.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek genetic counselling before any test so you understand what is being tested and why
    \item Provide your doctor with an accurate family history of disease in close relatives
    \item Ask what the test can and cannot tell you, and whether results could be uncertain
    \item Discuss the implications for family members before testing
    \item Consider carrier screening if you are planning a pregnancy
    \item Be cautious with direct-to-consumer tests, and verify any significant result with clinical testing
    \item Store copies of genetic reports and share them with new doctors as relevant
    \item Access support services if results cause distress or affect family relationships
\end{itemize}
