\chapter{Lupus}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), commonly referred to simply as lupus, is a chronic, multi-system autoimmune disease characterised by the production of autoantibodies directed against self-antigens, leading to widespread inflammatory tissue damage. It is a highly heterogeneous condition that can affect almost any organ system, including the skin, joints, kidneys, brain, heart, and lungs. Lupus is characterised by a fluctuating clinical course marked by periods of disease flares (exacerbations) and remissions, requiring long-term immunomodulatory therapy and multi-disciplinary clinical surveillance.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Lupus predominantly affects women of childbearing age, with a female-to-male ratio of approximately 9:1. The peak incidence occurs between the ages of 15 and 45. Globally, the prevalence of SLE varies, with estimates ranging from 20 to 150 per 100,000 people. It is significantly more common and tends to be more severe in individuals of African, Asian, Hispanic, and Indigenous Australian ancestries. In Australia, the disease is estimated to affect around 20,000 people, with Indigenous Australians experiencing higher rates of lupus nephritis and worse clinical outcomes compared to the non-Indigenous population.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The pathogenesis of lupus is multi-factorial, involving genetic predisposition, environmental triggers, and profound dysregulation of the innate and adaptive immune systems.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} Strong association with specific HLA alleles (e.g., HLA-DR2, HLA-DR3) and single-nucleotide polymorphisms in genes regulating immune responses (e.g., IRF5, STAT4).
    \item \textbf{Environmental triggers:} Ultraviolet (UV) radiation (which induces keratinocyte apoptosis), viral infections (such as Epstein-Barr virus), medications, and exposure to silica dust.
    \item \textbf{Hormonal factors:} Oestrogen promotes immune reactivity and enhances autoantibody production, explaining the strong female predominance of the disease.
    \item \textbf{Apoptotic clearance defect:} Impaired clearance of apoptotic cells leads to the accumulation of nuclear debris, presenting self-antigens to the immune system.
    \item \textbf{Autoantibody production:} Development of anti-nuclear antibodies (ANAs) and anti-double-stranded DNA (anti-dsDNA) antibodies that form immune complexes.
    \item \textbf{Immune complex deposition:} Deposition of antigen-antibody complexes in tissues (e.g., renal glomeruli, blood vessel walls) activates the complement cascade, causing severe tissue inflammation and damage.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features of lupus are highly variable and can involve multiple organ systems, often presenting sequentially rather than simultaneously.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Constitutional symptoms:} Persistent fatigue, low-grade fever, unexplained weight loss, and general malaise.
    \item \textbf{Mucocutaneous manifestations:} Malar (butterfly) rash sparing the nasolabial folds, discoid lesions (scarring plaques), photosensitivity, and painless oral or nasal ulcers.
    \item \textbf{Musculoskeletal symptoms:} Inflammatory polyarthritis affecting small joints of the hands and wrists, typically symmetrical and non-erosive.
    \item \textbf{Renal involvement (Lupus Nephritis):} Proteinuria, haematuria, cellular casts in urine, and progressive renal impairment (often asymptomatic initially).
    \item \textbf{Neuropsychiatric features:} Cognitive dysfunction, severe headaches, seizures, psychosis, mood disorders, or peripheral neuropathy.
    \item \textbf{Cardiopulmonary signs:} Pleurisy, pericarditis, pleural or pericardial effusions, Libman-Sacks endocarditis, and an increased risk of accelerated atherosclerosis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because of its diverse presentation, lupus is known as "the great imitator" and must be carefully differentiated from other autoimmune, infectious, and haematological disorders.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA):} Characterised by erosive arthritis and joint deformities, with positive rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP antibodies.
    \item \textbf{Sjögren's Syndrome:} Presents with dry eyes and dry mouth (sicca symptoms), which can overlap with lupus but features specific anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB antibody profiles.
    \item \textbf{Mixed Connective Tissue Disease (MCTD):} A distinct overlap syndrome presenting with features of SLE, systemic sclerosis, and polymyositis, with high titres of anti-U1-RNP antibodies.
    \item \textbf{Fibromyalgia:} Causes chronic widespread pain and profound fatigue, but lacks inflammatory markers, autoantibodies, and organ involvement.
    \item \textbf{Systemic vasculitis:} Conditions like granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) that present with multi-system involvement but are ANCA-associated and lack typical lupus autoantibodies.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients with lupus require lifelong specialist follow-up, and new or changing symptoms require prompt medical assessment to rule out disease flares or complications.
\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe chest pain (which may indicate pericarditis, myocardial infarction, or pleurisy).
    \item New-onset shortness of breath or difficulty breathing.
    \item Seizures, sudden weakness, numbness, or loss of vision.
    \item Severe confusion, acute psychosis, or altered mental status.
\end{itemize}
Other reasons to seek medical care include noticing new swelling in the legs or face, a sudden increase in blood pressure, a fever (which can indicate infection or a disease flare), worsening joint pain, or new skin rashes.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of lupus is established using clinical criteria (such as the EULAR/ACR 2019 criteria) supported by autoantibody testing and tissue biopsy.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anti-nuclear antibody (ANA) test:} The primary screening test, which is positive in over 95\% of lupus patients (high sensitivity, lower specificity).
    \item \textbf{Specific autoantibodies:} Testing for anti-dsDNA (highly specific and correlates with disease activity) and anti-Smith (anti-Sm) antibodies (highly specific).
    \item \textbf{Complement levels (C3, C4):} Measured to assess disease activity; low levels indicate complement consumption due to active inflammation.
    \item \textbf{Urinalysis and spot protein-to-creatinine ratio:} Essential to screen for proteinuria and haematuria, indicating renal involvement.
    \item \textbf{Renal biopsy:} The gold standard for diagnosing and classifying lupus nephritis (Class I to VI), which guides immunosuppressive therapy.
    \item \textbf{Full Blood Count (FBC):} Often reveals cytopenias, including leucopenia, lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia, or autoimmune haemolytic anaemia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of lupus aims to control symptoms, prevent organ damage, and minimise flares through a combination of pharmacological therapies and lifestyle adjustments.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antimalarials (Hydroxychloroquine):} The cornerstone of therapy for all lupus patients, proven to reduce flares, protect organs, improve survival, and manage joint and skin symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Corticosteroids (e.g., Prednisolone):} Used at the lowest effective dose to manage acute flares and organ-threatening disease; long-term use is minimised to prevent side effects.
    \item \textbf{Immunosuppressants:} Agents such as Mycophenolate mofetil, Azathioprine, or Methotrexate, used for moderate-to-severe disease and to spare steroid use.
    \item \textbf{Biologic therapies:} Monoclonal antibodies such as Belimumab or Anifrolumab, which target specific immune pathways involved in SLE pathogenesis.
    \item \textbf{Photoprotection and lifestyle measures:} Strict daily sun protection (SPF 50+ sunscreen, protective clothing), smoking cessation (smoking decreases hydroxychloroquine efficacy and increases cardiovascular risk), and regular gentle exercise.
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular risk management:} Intensive control of blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood glucose, as lupus patients have a significantly increased risk of cardiovascular disease.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lupus nephritis:} Progressive renal damage leading to chronic kidney disease or end-stage kidney failure requiring dialysis or transplantation.
    \item \textbf{Accelerated cardiovascular disease:} Early-onset myocardial infarction or stroke secondary to chronic systemic inflammation and steroid use.
    \item \textbf{Severe infections:} Deep opportunistic infections resulting from immunosuppressive therapy and underlying immune dysfunction.
    \item \textbf{Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS):} Co-occurring condition leading to arterial or venous thrombosis and recurrent pregnancy loss.
    \item \textbf{Avascular necrosis:} Bone tissue death, typically in the femoral head, associated with high-dose corticosteroid therapy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals with lupus has improved dramatically over the past few decades, with the 10-year survival rate now exceeding 90\%. However, patients with severe organ involvement (especially renal or neuropsychiatric disease) have a more guarded prognosis and face higher risks of long-term morbidity. Ongoing disease activity, treatment-related complications (such as osteoporosis and infections), and cardiovascular disease are the major drivers of late-stage mortality, making comprehensive preventative care and regular clinical monitoring essential.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Apply broad-spectrum sunscreen daily and wear a wide-brimmed hat and long sleeves when outdoors, avoiding direct sun exposure during peak UV hours.
    \item Stop smoking entirely, as nicotine triggers flares, exacerbates vascular problems, and blocks the action of antimalarial medications.
    \item Take all prescribed medications consistently, even during periods of remission, and do not abruptly stop steroids.
    \item Discuss pregnancy plans with your rheumatologist well in advance, as certain medications are teratogenic and disease should be stable for six months prior to conception.
    \item Engage in regular, low-impact exercise (such as swimming or walking) and ensure adequate rest to help manage chronic fatigue.
\end{itemize}
