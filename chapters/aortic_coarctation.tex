\chapter{Aortic Coarctation}
\index{Aortic Coarctation}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Aortic coarctation is a congenital heart condition -- present from birth -- in which there is a narrowing (coarctation) of the aorta, the main artery that carries oxygen-rich blood away from the heart. The narrowing usually occurs just beyond the origin of the left subclavian artery, near the site where the ductus arteriosus normally closes shortly after birth.

The narrowed segment forces the left ventricle to pump against increased resistance, producing high blood pressure in the arms and head and reduced blood flow to the lower body. Severity varies widely: some babies become critically unwell in the first weeks of life when the ductus closes, while others live into adulthood before the condition is discovered during an investigation of high blood pressure.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Aortic coarctation affects roughly one in 2,500 live births and is one of the more common congenital heart defects. It is more common in boys than in girls and is strongly associated with Turner syndrome, in which it occurs with greatly increased frequency. Many affected people also have a bicuspid aortic valve, and ventricular septal defect and other structural abnormalities commonly coexist.

Some cases are detected before birth on ultrasound, and many newborns are identified through routine examination or the newborn pulse and oxygen-saturation check. A significant proportion, however, remain undiagnosed until childhood or adult life, when persistent hypertension or leg symptoms lead to investigation.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Aortic coarctation is a developmental anomaly of the aorta, the exact cause of which is incompletely understood:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Developmental abnormality:} the narrowing forms as the heart and great vessels develop in early pregnancy, and it is linked to abnormal ductus arteriosus tissue extending into the aortic wall
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} most cases are sporadic, but the condition runs in families in a small proportion of cases and is strongly associated with Turner syndrome
    \item \textbf{Associated defects:} bicuspid aortic valve, ventricular septal defect, mitral valve abnormalities, and intracranial berry aneurysms are over-represented
    \item \textbf{Haemodynamic effects:} the narrowing raises the pressure the left ventricle must generate to perfuse the lower body, producing upper-body hypertension; collateral arteries develop around the chest and shoulder girdle to bypass the obstruction
    \item \textbf{Duct-dependent circulation:} in severe infantile coarctation, blood flow to the lower body depends on the ductus arteriosus, so when it closes in the first days of life, shock and heart failure can develop suddenly
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on the severity of the narrowing and the age at which it is discovered:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{In infants:} poor feeding, rapid or laboured breathing, sweating, and signs of heart failure if the narrowing is severe and duct-dependent
    \item \textbf{High blood pressure:} raised pressure in the arms, often the first clue in older children and adults, and sometimes headache or nosebleeds
    \item \textbf{Pulse difference:} weak, delayed, or absent femoral pulses compared with the arms
    \item \textbf{Leg symptoms:} cramping, coldness, or fatigue in the legs with exercise, and poor growth of the lower limbs
    \item \textbf{A heart murmur:} a systolic murmur heard over the left side of the chest or back as blood crosses the narrowing
    \item \textbf{Exertional symptoms:} breathlessness, fatigue, or chest discomfort with activity
    \item \textbf{Collateral signs:} in older, unrepaired patients, visible pulsatile arteries around the shoulder blades
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Other causes of hypertension in the young:} renal disease, renovascular disease, and endocrine disorders such as phaeochromocytoma
    \item \textbf{Other congenital heart defects:} aortic stenosis, ventricular septal defect, and other left-sided obstructions
    \item \textbf{Cardiomyopathy and other causes of infant heart failure:} myocarditis, anomalous coronary arteries, and other structural lesions
    \item \textbf{Other narrowing conditions of the aorta:} middle aortic syndrome and Takayasu arteritis
    \item \textbf{Functional differences:} blood pressure differences from measurement error or vasoconstriction
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if a child or young adult is found to have high blood pressure, weak leg pulses, or leg cramps with exercise, or if there is a heart murmur. Newborns with poor feeding, rapid breathing, or a bluish tint need urgent assessment, as do children or adults who faint or develop chest pain with exertion.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rapid or difficult breathing in an infant or child
    \item Fainting, collapse, or severe chest pain
    \item A baby who is listless, feeding very poorly, or turning blue
    \item Sudden weakness, facial droop, or difficulty speaking -- possible stroke from uncontrolled high blood pressure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure measurement:} taken in all four limbs; higher pressure in the arms than the legs suggests coarctation
    \item \textbf{Pulse examination:} weak or delayed femoral pulses, best felt simultaneously with the radial pulse
    \item \textbf{Echocardiography:} ultrasound visualising the narrowed segment, assessing gradient, heart function, and associated valve or septal defects
    \item \textbf{ECG:} to look for left ventricular hypertrophy and strain
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} may show the classic ``figure-of-three'' sign and rib notching in older untreated patients
    \item \textbf{CT or MRI:} detailed three-dimensional imaging of the narrowing, collaterals, and associated aortic changes, used to plan intervention
    \item \textbf{Cardiac catheterisation:} may be used to measure the pressure gradient across the narrowing and, in many cases, to treat it directly
    \item \textbf{Assessment for associated conditions:} screening for Turner syndrome features and for intracranial aneurysm risk where indicated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Repair of the narrowing:} surgical resection with end-to-end anastomosis, patch aortoplasty, or subclavian flap repair in infancy; balloon angioplasty with or without stenting in older children and adults
    \item \textbf{Timing:} elective repair is generally planned in infancy or early childhood, before significant hypertension and its complications develop
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure treatment:} antihypertensive medication is often needed after repair, because high blood pressure can persist
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} lifelong cardiology review, because recoarctation and late complications can arise many years after successful repair
    \item \textbf{Management of associated defects:} bicuspid aortic valve and other structural problems are monitored and treated as needed
    \item \textbf{Endocarditis prophylaxis:} good dental hygiene and antibiotic cover before certain procedures, where recommended for those with prosthetic material or valve disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent or late-onset high blood pressure, which is common even after successful repair
    \item Recoarctation: re-narrowing at the repair site, more common after infant surgery
    \item Aortic aneurysm or dissection, including at the repair site and in the ascending aorta
    \item Stroke from uncontrolled hypertension
    \item Infective endocarditis involving the aorta or an associated bicuspid valve
    \item Heart failure, particularly in untreated severe coarctation
    \item Premature coronary artery disease and hypertension-related cardiovascular disease in later life
    \item Rupture of associated intracranial aneurysms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Untreated coarctation carries a high long-term risk of heart failure, stroke, and premature death, and most untreated patients die before the age of 40. When repair is performed in childhood, the outlook is good, and most children lead normal, active lives with a near-normal life expectancy.

However, even after successful repair, high blood pressure is common and often persists or returns, and the aorta remains at risk of aneurysm and dissection. Lifelong cardiology follow-up, tight blood pressure control, and monitoring of the aorta are therefore essential. Long-term outcomes are best when the condition is detected early and repaired before significant hypertension develops.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Aortic coarctation cannot be prevented -- it is present from birth -- so early detection is the priority
    \item Attend all newborn checks, including pulse and blood pressure measurement, which help identify the condition
    \item Attend all scheduled cardiology reviews, both before and after repair
    \item Keep blood pressure well controlled with medication as prescribed
    \item Maintain a healthy diet, exercise within the limits advised by your cardiologist, and avoid smoking
    \item Practise good dental hygiene to reduce the small risk of infection of the heart and aorta
    \item Tell health professionals and dentists about the condition, as some procedures require special precautions
    \item Report new chest pain, breathlessness, or leg swelling promptly
\end{itemize}
