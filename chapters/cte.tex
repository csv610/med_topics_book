\chapter{Chronic Traumatic Encephalopathy (CTE)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE) is a progressive degenerative brain disease associated with repeated head injuries, including concussions and subconcussive blows to the head. It is most commonly found in contact sport athletes, military veterans, and others exposed to repeated head trauma. CTE causes a buildup of an abnormal protein called tau in the brain, leading to memory loss, confusion, mood changes, behavioural problems, and, in time, dementia. The diagnosis can only be confirmed after death by examining the brain, although research is seeking ways to diagnose it in life. There is no cure, and prevention focuses on reducing and managing head impacts.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The true prevalence of CTE is unknown because diagnosis requires brain examination after death. It has been identified in the brains of many former athletes in contact sports such as American football, boxing, rugby, and soccer, and in military veterans exposed to blast injuries. Risk is linked to the cumulative duration and number of head impacts rather than concussion symptoms alone, and most people with CTE have had many years of exposure. CTE appears to be underdiagnosed, and public awareness has grown markedly as research and media coverage have expanded.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
CTE develops after repeated traumatic brain injury, but the precise mechanisms are still being studied.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Repeated head impacts:} concussions and, importantly, subconcussive blows that cause no symptoms
    \item \textbf{Tau protein accumulation:} abnormal tau aggregates form in the brain, particularly around small blood vessels and in the depths of the brain's folds
    \item \textbf{Neuronal loss:} the protein buildup is accompanied by loss of brain cells and tissue shrinkage
    \item \textbf{Progressive disease:} the damage spreads over years, even after head impacts stop
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} duration and intensity of exposure, age at first exposure, and genetic factors such as the APOE gene
    \item \textbf{Distinction from other dementias:} CTE has a characteristic pattern of tau distribution
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms usually begin years or decades after the head impacts.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Memory loss and difficulty concentrating
    \item Confusion and disorientation
    \item Impulsivity, poor judgement, and aggression
    \item Depression, anxiety, and irritability
    \item Behavioural changes, including withdrawal or inappropriate behaviour
    \item Difficulty with balance, coordination, and speech
    \item Progressive decline to dementia in later stages
    \item In some people, parkinsonism with tremor and stiffness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of CTE overlap with many other conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Alzheimer's disease and other dementias
    \item Depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder
    \item Bipolar disorder and other psychiatric illness
    \item Chronic pain and substance misuse, common after head injury
    \item Post-concussion syndrome
    \item Parkinson's disease and other movement disorders
    \item Vascular dementia
    \item Other neurodegenerative diseases, such as frontotemporal dementia
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with repeated head impacts who develops memory, mood, or behavioural symptoms should seek medical assessment, ideally with a neurologist.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A head injury with loss of consciousness, confusion, vomiting, or a severe headache
    \item A seizure
    \item Sudden weakness, numbness, or difficulty speaking
    \item Suicidal thoughts or severe depression
    \item A sudden change in behaviour or consciousness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
CTE cannot currently be confirmed during life; diagnosis is made on clinical grounds after excluding other conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} detailed account of head impact exposure, symptoms, and their progression
    \item \textbf{Cognitive testing:} assessment of memory, attention, and executive function
    \item \textbf{Neurological examination:} testing of movement, balance, and reflexes
    \item \textbf{MRI of the brain:} to look for other causes and supportive changes
    \item \textbf{PET imaging:} research techniques can detect tau in some cases but are not yet standard
    \item \textbf{Psychiatric assessment:} to evaluate mood and behavioural symptoms
    \item \textbf{Blood and spinal fluid tests:} research biomarkers under development
    \item \textbf{Exclusion of other conditions:} blood tests and imaging for reversible causes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
There is no cure, and management focuses on symptoms, safety, and support.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurological care:} specialist review and monitoring over time
    \item \textbf{Symptom treatment:} medicines and therapy for mood, behaviour, and cognitive symptoms
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} counselling and therapy for depression and anxiety
    \item \textbf{Cognitive rehabilitation:} strategies to support memory and daily function
    \item \textbf{Safety planning:} support with driving, finances, and supervision as the disease progresses
    \item \textbf{Family support:} education and carer support
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} regular exercise, good nutrition, and sleep
    \item \textbf{Reducing further impacts:} avoiding further head trauma
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive cognitive decline and dementia
    \item Severe mood and behavioural problems, including aggression
    \item Depression and increased risk of suicide
    \item Falls and injuries from balance problems
    \item Loss of independence and the ability to work
    \item Substance misuse
    \item The burden on families and carers
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
CTE is a progressive disease without a cure, and it typically worsens over years, leading to dementia in advanced stages. The rate of progression varies widely between individuals. Research is active, and the goal is to develop diagnostic tests and treatments. For people with symptoms after head injury, many other conditions are treatable, so thorough assessment is essential. Prevention of head impacts in sport and military service, and prompt, proper management of concussion, remain the most important ways to reduce the risk of CTE.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reduce the number and force of head impacts in sport through rule changes and training methods
    \item Follow concussion protocols: remove from play, assess, and do not return until cleared
    \item Wear appropriate protective equipment and use correct technique
    \item Report concussion symptoms and allow full recovery before returning to sport
    \item Limit heading in children's sport and high-exposure positions
    \item Maintain a healthy lifestyle for general brain health
    \item Seek medical assessment for any persistent post-head-injury symptoms
    \item Access support services for people with cognitive or behavioural symptoms
\end{itemize}
