\chapter{Genital Herpes}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Genital herpes is a common sexually transmitted infection caused by the herpes simplex virus (HSV), usually type 2 (HSV-2) and increasingly type 1 (HSV-1). The virus causes recurrent outbreaks of painful blisters and ulcers on or around the genitals, buttocks, or anus. After the first infection, the virus remains in the body for life, hiding in nerve cells and periodically reactivating to cause new outbreaks, which may be triggered by stress, illness, fatigue, or hormonal changes. Many people who carry the virus have few or no symptoms and may not know they are infected, yet they can still transmit it to sexual partners. While genital herpes cannot be cured, antiviral medicines control outbreaks effectively and reduce the risk of transmission.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Genital herpes is one of the most common sexually transmitted infections worldwide. Around 1 in 8 people aged 14--49 in the United States have HSV-2, and prevalence is similar or higher in many other countries including Australia. HSV-1, the usual cause of cold sores, now causes a substantial proportion of genital herpes cases, particularly in young people, reflecting changes in oral sex practices. Most people acquire the infection in their teens or 20s. The majority of infected individuals are unaware of their status because symptoms are mild or absent. Recurrent outbreaks are more common with HSV-2 than HSV-1 genital infection.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Genital herpes is caused by the herpes simplex virus, which is spread by direct skin-to-skin contact during sexual activity.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} through vaginal, anal, or oral sex with an infected partner, including contact with lesions, and sometimes when no symptoms are present (viral shedding)
    \item \textbf{Primary infection:} the virus enters the skin or mucous membranes and travels up sensory nerves to the dorsal root ganglia, where it becomes latent
    \item \textbf{Reactivation:} the virus periodically travels back down the nerve to the skin, causing new lesions; triggers include stress, illness, fever, surgery, and immunosuppression
    \item \textbf{HSV-1 vs HSV-2:} HSV-2 typically causes more frequent genital recurrences, while HSV-1 genital infection recurs less often but is easily transmitted during oral sex
    \item \textbf{Neonatal transmission:} a baby can be infected during birth if the mother has active lesions, which can cause serious illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms vary widely, from completely silent infection to severe first outbreaks.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Small, painful blisters that rupture to form shallow ulcers on the genitals, vulva, penis, scrotum, anus, or buttocks
    \item Itching, burning, or tingling in the affected area before lesions appear
    \item Pain on urination and difficulty passing urine, especially in women
    \item Swollen, tender lymph nodes in the groin
    \item Flu-like symptoms including fever, headache, and muscle aches during the first outbreak
    \item Recurrent outbreaks that are shorter, milder, and less frequent than the first episode
    \item In many people, no symptoms at all despite carrying the virus
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The appearance of genital ulcers or rashes has several possible causes that should be distinguished from herpes.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Syphilis, which can cause painless genital ulcers
    \item Chancroid and lymphogranuloma venereum, bacterial STIs causing genital ulcers
    \item Genital warts (human papillomavirus), which are raised rather than ulcerative
    \item Candidiasis (thrush), which causes itching and discharge rather than blisters
    \item Contact dermatitis and allergic reactions to soaps, condoms, or lubricants
    \item Trauma from sexual activity, and genital aphthous ulcers
    \item Behçet disease, a rare inflammatory condition causing recurrent oral and genital ulcers
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor or sexual health clinic if you develop sores, blisters, or unexplained pain in the genital area, or if a sexual partner has been diagnosed with herpes. Early treatment of a first outbreak reduces its severity and duration.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty passing urine or inability to pass urine due to pain, which requires urgent care
    \item Severe headache, stiff neck, or sensitivity to light in a person with herpes, which may indicate meningitis
    \item A pregnant woman near term with active genital herpes, who needs urgent obstetric advice
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Accurate diagnosis is important both for treatment and for informing partners.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} inspection of the lesions and surrounding area
    \item \textbf{Viral swab (PCR or culture):} a swab of fluid from an unroofed blister is the most reliable test during an outbreak
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} antibody tests can detect past infection even when no lesions are present, but cannot distinguish site of infection or timing
    \item \textbf{Type-specific testing:} distinguishes HSV-1 from HSV-2, which informs prognosis for recurrences
    \item \textbf{Screening for other STIs:} because herpes often coexists with other infections, testing for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, syphilis, and HIV is recommended
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment aims to control symptoms, speed healing, and reduce transmission. It does not remove the virus from the body.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antiviral medicines:} aciclovir, valaciclovir, or famciclovir shorten the duration of a first outbreak and heal lesions faster
    \item \textbf{Episodic treatment:} starting antivirals at the first sign of a recurrence reduces the severity and length of the episode
    \item \textbf{Suppressive therapy:} daily antiviral medicine markedly reduces the frequency of recurrences and the risk of transmitting the virus to partners
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple analgesics, saltwater baths, and topical anaesthetics soothe discomfort
    \item \textbf{Urinary comfort:} drinking plenty of water and passing urine in a bath or shower can reduce pain
    \item \textbf{Partner notification:} informing sexual partners allows them to be tested and counselled
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy care:} women with a history of herpes should discuss management with their obstetric team; suppressive therapy in late pregnancy and caesarean delivery in selected cases reduce the risk to the baby
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Recurrent painful outbreaks that interfere with physical and emotional wellbeing
    \item Psychological distress, stigma, anxiety, and effects on intimate relationships
    \item Urinary retention when pain prevents passing urine
    \item Bacterial superinfection of ulcers
    \item Neonatal herpes, which can cause severe illness or death in newborns
    \item Increased susceptibility to and transmission of HIV
    \item Rarely, herpes meningitis or encephalitis in immunocompromised people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
There is no cure for genital herpes, but the condition is very manageable. First outbreaks are usually the worst; subsequent recurrences are typically milder and less frequent, and for many people outbreaks become infrequent over time. Suppressive antiviral therapy can almost eliminate outbreaks and greatly reduce transmission risk. People with herpes can have healthy sexual relationships and normal lives. Disclosure to partners, while often daunting, is well tolerated by most and is an important part of responsible sexual health.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use condoms consistently, which reduce but do not eliminate the risk of transmission
    \item Avoid sexual contact during outbreaks, when the virus is most infectious
    \item Consider suppressive antiviral therapy if you have frequent outbreaks or an HSV-2-positive partner
    \item Discuss your status openly with partners, and consider testing when starting a new relationship
    \item Manage triggers such as stress, fatigue, and illness to reduce recurrences
    \item Practise good hygiene, including handwashing after touching lesions
    \item If pregnant or planning pregnancy, tell your doctor about your herpes history
    \item Seek support from sexual health services if stigma or anxiety is affecting you
\end{itemize}
