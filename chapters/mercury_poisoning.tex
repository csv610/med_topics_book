\chapter{Mercury Poisoning}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Mercury poisoning (also known as hydrargyria or mercurialism) is a toxicity caused by exposure to mercury, a heavy metal that exists in three chemical forms: elemental (metallic), inorganic, and organic (most commonly methylmercury). Mercury is a potent neurotoxin that affects the central and peripheral nervous systems, kidneys, lungs, and skin. Exposure occurs primarily through inhaling mercury vapours during industrial processes or consuming seafood contaminated with methylmercury.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Mercury poisoning can affect individuals through occupational exposure (e.g., small-scale gold mining, chemical manufacturing, dental work using older amalgam, and waste management) or dietary habits (heavy consumption of large, predatory fish). While acute mass poisoning events (such as the historic Minamata disease in Japan) are rare today, chronic low-level exposure remains a significant global and public health concern. In Australia, food safety regulations (like those of Food Standards Australia New Zealand) restrict fish intake guidelines for pregnant women and young children to limit methylmercury exposure.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The toxicity of mercury depends on its chemical form, dose, and route of exposure:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Organic mercury (Methylmercury):} Absorbed efficiently from the gastrointestinal tract, it easily crosses the blood-brain and placental barriers. It binds to sulfhydryl groups in proteins, causing oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, and neuronal death, particularly in the cerebral cortex and cerebellum.
    \item \textbf{Elemental mercury vapour:} Inhaled vapours are rapidly absorbed through the lungs and enter the brain, causing severe neurotoxicity. Ingested liquid metallic mercury is poorly absorbed by a healthy gastrointestinal tract and is generally low risk.
    \item \textbf{Inorganic mercury salts:} Primarily absorbed through ingestion or skin contact, they are highly nephrotoxic, causing acute tubular necrosis and renal failure because they accumulate in the kidneys.
    \item \textbf{Environmental bioaccumulation:} Inorganic mercury in water bodies is methylated by microorganisms and bioaccumulates up the aquatic food chain, reaching high concentrations in long-lived predatory fish (e.g., shark, swordfish, and billfish).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation varies significantly based on the form of mercury and duration of exposure:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurological symptoms:} Tremor, erethism (characterised by excessive shyness, irritability, anxiety, and emotional lability), memory loss, and insomnia.
    \item \textbf{Sensory disturbances:} Paresthesia (tingling or numbness) around the mouth and in the extremities, constricted visual fields (tunnel vision), and hearing impairment.
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal and renal signs:} Metallic taste, gingivostomatitis (swollen, bleeding gums), severe abdominal pain, vomiting, and decreased urine output due to kidney damage.
    \item \textbf{Respiratory distress:} Inhalation of metallic mercury vapour causes acute chemical pneumonitis, cough, chest pain, and dyspnoea, which can progress to respiratory failure.
    \item \textbf{Acrodynia (Pink disease):} A hypersensitivity reaction to mercury seen in children, presenting with pink, painful, swollen hands and feet, peeling skin, excessive sweating, and irritability.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Mercury poisoning must be distinguished from other neurodegenerative, psychiatric, and toxicological conditions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Other heavy metal toxicities:} Lead poisoning (presents with abdominal colic, anaemia, and peripheral neuropathy) or arsenic poisoning.
    \item \textbf{Parkinson's disease or essential tremor:} Neurological conditions causing tremors, distinguished by clinical history and lack of heavy metal exposure.
    \item \textbf{Multiple sclerosis:} An autoimmune neurological disease causing sensory and visual disturbances.
    \item \textbf{Primary psychiatric disorders:} Anxiety, depression, or behavioural changes occurring without heavy metal exposure.
    \item \textbf{Kawasaki disease:} Can mimic acrodynia in children due to skin peeling and fever.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Individuals with suspected occupational exposure, heavy consumption of high-mercury fish, or symptoms of heavy metal toxicity should consult a GP or occupational health specialist. Immediate emergency medical care is necessary for acute inhalation of mercury vapour or ingestion of inorganic mercury salts.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe difficulty breathing, coughing, or chest pain after exposure to mercury vapour (e.g., in an industrial setting)
    \item Ingestion of inorganic mercury salts or chemical products containing mercury
    \item Seizures, sudden confusion, or loss of consciousness
    \item Severe, uncontrollable vomiting or blood in the vomit or stool
    \item Any acute, life-threatening symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Accurate diagnosis requires clinical history, physical findings, and laboratory confirmation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood mercury levels:} The most reliable test for acute elemental exposure or organic methylmercury exposure (reflects recent dietary intake).
    \item \textbf{24-hour urine mercury test:} The gold standard for assessing chronic exposure to elemental or inorganic mercury, as organic mercury is not excreted significantly in urine.
    \item \textbf{Renal and hepatic function tests:} Blood tests to evaluate kidney function (urea, creatinine) and liver enzymes.
    \item \textbf{Neuropsychological testing and electromyography:} To document the extent of neurological impairment and peripheral nerve damage.
    \item \textbf{Hair analysis:} Can be used to track historical exposure to methylmercury over time, though it is prone to external contamination.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The primary goal is stopping further exposure, followed by medical elimination therapies:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Source elimination:} Removing the patient from the contaminated environment or stopping the consumption of high-mercury seafood.
    \item \textbf{Chelation therapy:} Using chemical binders like succimer (DMSA), dimercaprol (BAL), or penicillamine to bind mercury in the blood and tissues, allowing it to be excreted in urine.
    \item \textbf{Respiratory support:} Providing oxygen, bronchodilators, and mechanical ventilation for chemical pneumonitis caused by vapour inhalation.
    \item \textbf{Renal support:} Intravenous fluid hydration and haemodialysis in cases of acute kidney injury.
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal decontamination:} Gastric lavage if inorganic salts were recently ingested (activated charcoal is poorly effective for heavy metals).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Permanent neurological damage, including cerebellar ataxia, dysarthria (slurred speech), and cognitive deficits
    \item Chronic kidney disease or permanent renal failure requiring long-term dialysis
    \item Teratogenic effects, including microcephaly, cerebral palsy, and severe intellectual disability in infants exposed in utero
    \item Cardiovascular complications, such as hypertension and increased risk of myocardial infarction due to oxidative stress
    \item Chronic psychological distress and social impairment due to cognitive and personality changes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis depends on the chemical form of mercury, the severity and duration of exposure, and the promptness of treatment. Mild cases of organic or inorganic poisoning often resolve fully once the exposure stops. However, chronic or severe poisoning (especially methylmercury exposure in utero or severe inhalation of metallic vapours) can cause permanent, irreversible neurological and developmental damage. Chelation therapy is highly effective at reducing the body burden of mercury but cannot reverse established structural brain damage.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Follow national dietary guidelines regarding fish consumption, especially during pregnancy, breastfeeding, or childhood (limit intake of shark, broadbill, swordfish, and marlin)
    \item Never use a vacuum cleaner to clean up a mercury spill from a broken thermometer, as this vaporises the mercury and increases inhalation risk
    \item Use appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE) in occupational settings where mercury is used, and ensure adequate workplace ventilation
    \item Dispose of mercury-containing items (fluorescent globes, older thermometers, and batteries) safely at designated hazardous waste facilities
    \item Advocate for the phase-out of mercury-containing medical and industrial equipment in line with global environmental treaties
\end{itemize}
