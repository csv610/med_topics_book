\chapter{Dissociative Identity Disorder}
\index{Dissociative Identity Disorder}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dissociative identity disorder (DID), previously called multiple personality disorder, is a complex mental health condition in which a person experiences two or more distinct personality states, or "alters", that take control of their behaviour at different times. It is a dissociative disorder, meaning it involves disruptions in memory, identity, and consciousness. DID develops as a coping mechanism, most often after severe and repeated childhood trauma. It is a real, recognised condition, and people with DID can recover with specialised, long-term therapy. This chapter explains the features, causes, and treatment of DID.

\section*{Epidemiology}
DID is more common than once believed, affecting around 1--3\% of the population in community studies, although it is often unrecognised and misdiagnosed. It is more common in women and is strongly associated with severe childhood abuse and neglect. People with DID often have long histories of contact with mental health services and multiple previous diagnoses before DID is identified. It frequently coexists with post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, and other conditions.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Severe childhood trauma:} repeated abuse, neglect, or other overwhelming experiences are the main risk factor
    \item \textbf{Dissociation as coping:} the child compartmentalises traumatic experiences and parts of identity to survive
    \item \textbf{Alters:} distinct personality states develop, each with its own memories, feelings, and behaviours
    \item \textbf{Amnesia:} memory gaps occur between alters, and the person may not remember periods or actions
    \item \textbf{Attachment disruption:} early relationships that should provide safety instead cause fear, shaping the fragmentation
    \item \textbf{Neurobiological factors:} dissociation involves altered brain processes related to memory and identity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Two or more distinct identity states that take control of behaviour
    \item Gaps in memory for everyday events, personal information, or traumatic events
    \item Amnesia that is more severe than ordinary forgetfulness
    \item Different alters may have different ages, genders, voices, and abilities
    \item Switching between alters, sometimes with visible changes in demeanour
    \item Intrusive symptoms of trauma, such as flashbacks
    \item Depression, anxiety, self-harm, and suicidal thoughts are common
    \item Voices or internal conversations that the person recognises as their own different parts
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Post-traumatic stress disorder, which involves dissociation but not distinct identity states
    \item Borderline personality disorder, which has emotional instability but not true alters
    \item Schizophrenia and psychosis, which involve different symptoms
    \item Bipolar disorder and depression
    \item Other dissociative disorders, such as dissociative amnesia and depersonalisation disorder
    \item Feigned illness, which is rare and requires specialist assessment
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with symptoms of dissociation, memory gaps, or distress related to trauma should seek help from a GP or mental health service. A careful assessment by a psychiatrist or psychologist with experience in dissociation is important for accurate diagnosis.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Thoughts of self-harm or suicide
    \item Severe distress, agitation, or unsafe behaviour
    \item Any crisis in which the person cannot keep themselves safe
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Support lines:} a local crisis service and Suicide Call Back Service (1300 659 467) are available 24 hours.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive clinical interview:} detailed history of identity, memory, and trauma, often over several sessions
    \item \textbf{Structured diagnostic interviews:} tools designed for dissociative disorders
    \item \textbf{Screening questionnaires:} such as the Dissociative Experiences Scale
    \item \textbf{Collateral information:} accounts from family and friends
    \item \textbf{Assessment of safety:} suicide risk and self-harm
    \item \textbf{Medical review:} to exclude physical causes of memory and identity disturbance
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Specialised psychotherapy:} long-term, trauma-informed therapy is the main treatment
    \item \textbf{Stabilisation:} building safety, coping skills, and emotional regulation early in therapy
    \item \textbf{Trauma processing:} working through traumatic memories at a pace the person can manage
    \item \textbf{Integration:} gradually reducing the barriers between alters and developing a unified sense of self
    \item \textbf{Medication:} treats co-occurring depression, anxiety, and sleep problems, but no medicine treats DID itself
    \item \textbf{Safety planning:} strategies to reduce self-harm and manage crises
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} education for the person and family, and support groups
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Self-harm and suicidal behaviour
    \item Re-traumatisation and flashbacks
    \item Misdiagnosis and years of ineffective treatment
    \item Relationship and occupational difficulties
    \item Substance use as a coping strategy
    \item Depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With specialised, long-term treatment, many people with DID improve substantially, gaining control over switching, reducing amnesia, and building a more integrated identity. Recovery is a gradual process that can take years, but meaningful improvement is common. Early accurate diagnosis and a skilled, consistent therapist are the most important factors in good outcomes. Many people with DID go on to live stable and productive lives.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek assessment from a professional experienced with dissociation
    \item Attend therapy consistently and communicate openly
    \item Learn grounding and safety skills for distressing moments
    \item Build a crisis plan with trusted contacts
    \item Prioritise sleep, nutrition, and physical health
    \item Avoid alcohol and drugs, which worsen dissociation
    \item Educate close family and friends with professional guidance
    \item Be patient with progress, which is gradual
\end{itemize}
