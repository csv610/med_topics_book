\chapter{Von willebrand disease}
\index{Von willebrand disease}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Von Willebrand disease (VWD) is the most common inherited bleeding disorder, characterized by a quantitative deficiency or qualitative dysfunction of von Willebrand factor (VWF), a complex, multimeric plasma glycoprotein. Named after the Finnish physician Erik Adolf von Willebrand, who first described the condition in 1926 in families from the Åland Islands, VWD is a heterogeneous disorder that disrupts the normal haemostatic cascade. Under physiological conditions, haemostasis is a highly regulated process designed to prevent blood loss following vascular injury. VWF plays a dual, critical role in this process: it serves as the essential molecular bridge facilitating the adhesion of platelets to the exposed subendothelial collagen at sites of vascular damage, and it acts as a carrier protein that stabilizes coagulation Factor VIII (FVIII) in the circulation, protecting it from rapid proteolytic degradation.

When VWF is deficient or dysfunctional, primary haemostasis (the formation of a platelet plug) is impaired, and secondary haemostasis (the coagulation cascade) may also be compromised due to low levels of FVIII. This dual defect leads to a characteristic bleeding phenotype dominated by mucocutaneous haemorrhage, such as recurrent epistaxis, easy bruising, menorrhagia, and prolonged bleeding after minor cuts, dental procedures, or surgeries. While many individuals with VWD exhibit mild symptoms and may remain undiagnosed for much of their lives, others, particularly those with severe quantitative deficiencies, suffer from debilitating and potentially life-threatening bleeding episodes. Understanding the nuances of VWD, from its molecular genetics to its clinical management, is of paramount importance for healthcare providers, as timely diagnosis and targeted therapeutic interventions can significantly mitigate bleeding risk, prevent severe complications, and improve the overall quality of life for affected individuals.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of von Willebrand disease is characterized by a significant discrepancy between its estimated prevalence in the general population and the number of individuals who seek medical care for clinically significant symptoms. Epidemiological studies using laboratory screening criteria suggest that VWD may affect up to 1\% of the global population, making it by far the most prevalent inherited bleeding disorder. However, the prevalence of symptomatic VWD that is clinically recognized and diagnosed is estimated to be much lower, ranging from approximately 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 1,000 (0.01\% to 0.1\%) of the population. This discordance is primarily attributed to the variable penetrance and expressivity of the genetic mutations associated with the disease, as well as the subjective nature of mild bleeding symptoms.

VWD is an autosomal disorder, meaning it is inherited on non-sex chromosomes, and therefore affects males and females in equal genetic proportions. However, females are diagnosed far more frequently than males. This clinical gender disparity is due to the unique physiological challenges faced by females, specifically menstruation, childbirth, and gynaecological procedures. Heavy menstrual bleeding (menorrhagia) is the most common presenting symptom in post-pubertal females, prompting medical evaluation that often leads to a diagnosis. In contrast, males with mild forms of the disease may go undiagnosed until they undergo major surgery or experience significant trauma. The disease is identified across all ethnic groups and geographic regions, with no specific racial predilections, although access to specialized diagnostic facilities and coagulation specialists heavily influences the reported regional prevalence rates.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The primary cause of inherited von Willebrand disease is a mutation in the \textit{VWF} gene, which is located on the short arm of chromosome 12 (12p13.3). This large gene, spanning 178 kilobases and containing 52 exons, encodes a pre-pro-VWF protein that undergoes extensive post-translational modifications, including dimerization in the endoplasmic reticulum and subsequent multimerization in the Golgi apparatus. The mature VWF protein is stored in the Weibel-Palade bodies of endothelial cells and the alpha-granules of platelets, from where it is secreted into the plasma. The pathophysiology of VWD is direct, stemming from how these mutations affect the synthesis, processing, secretion, or clearance of VWF, or how they disrupt its binding interactions with platelets, collagen, or Factor VIII.

The main pathophysiological mechanisms and classifications of VWD include the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Type 1 Von Willebrand Disease:} This is the most common form, accounting for approximately 70\% to 80\% of all cases. It is inherited in an autosomal dominant fashion with variable penetrance. The underlying pathophysiology is a mild-to-moderate quantitative deficiency of structurally normal VWF. This occurs due to mutations that decrease the synthesis of VWF, impair its intracellular transport and secretion, or accelerate its clearance from the circulation. Because VWF levels are reduced, the levels of circulating Factor VIII are often proportionally decreased, but they generally remain high enough to prevent spontaneous joint bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Type 2 Von Willebrand Disease:} This class represents qualitative defects of VWF, accounting for 15\% to 20\% of cases, and is further subdivided into four distinct phenotypes based on the specific functional impairment:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Subtype 2A:} Characterized by a decrease in VWF-dependent platelet adhesion because of a selective deficiency of high-molecular-weight (HMW) VWF multimers. This is caused by mutations that either prevent the assembly of HMW multimers or render them highly susceptible to premature proteolysis by the plasma metalloproteinase ADAMTS13.
        \item \textbf{Subtype 2B:} Characterized by gain-of-function mutations that abnormally increase the affinity of VWF for the glycoprotein Ib (GPIb) receptor on platelets. This leads to spontaneous binding of HMW multimers to circulating platelets, resulting in rapid clearance of both the HMW multimers and the platelets, which often causes transient or persistent thrombocytopenia (low platelet count).
        \item \textbf{Subtype 2M:} Characterized by qualitative defects where VWF-dependent platelet adhesion is decreased despite a normal distribution of HMW multimers. The mutations in this subtype directly impair the binding site of VWF to the platelet GPIb receptor or to subendothelial collagen.
        \item \textbf{Subtype 2N (Normandy):} Characterized by autosomal recessive mutations that disrupt the binding site of VWF for Factor VIII. Consequently, VWF cannot protect Factor VIII from rapid clearance, leading to markedly reduced circulating Factor VIII levels (often 5\% to 15\% of normal). This subtype clinically mimics mild-to-moderate Haemophilia A but is inherited autosomally rather than in an X-linked pattern.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Type 3 Von Willebrand Disease:} This is a rare, severe form of the disease, occurring in approximately 1 in 1 million individuals. It is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern, typically resulting from homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations (such as nonsense mutations, frameshifts, or large deletions) that lead to a near-total or complete absence of VWF in plasma and cellular compartments. Because VWF is virtually non-existent, Factor VIII levels are extremely low (typically less than 1\% to 5\% of normal), leading to severe primary haemostatic failure as well as secondary haemostatic defects similar to severe Haemophilia A.
    \item \textbf{Acquired Von Willebrand Syndrome (AVWS):} A rare, non-inherited bleeding disorder that mimics the clinical and laboratory features of inherited VWD. It develops later in life and is typically associated with underlying lymphoproliferative, myeloproliferative, cardiovascular (e.g., aortic stenosis), or autoimmune disorders. The pathophysiology involves the rapid clearance of VWF from the circulation due to autoantibodies, absorption onto tumor cells, or high shear stress destroying HMW multimers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of von Willebrand disease is highly variable, ranging from virtually asymptomatic states to severe, life-threatening haemorrhagic events. The severity of symptoms is broadly correlated with the type of VWD and the absolute levels of functional VWF and Factor VIII in the blood.

The clinical hallmark of VWD is mucocutaneous bleeding, which differs from the deep-tissue and joint bleeding characteristic of classic haemophilias (except in Type 3 VWD). The most common clinical features include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Epistaxis:} Recurrent, prolonged, and spontaneous nosebleeds are common, often starting in childhood. These episodes frequently last longer than 10 minutes and may require medical intervention or nasal packing.
    \item \textbf{Easy Bruising:} Patients often report frequent, large ecchymoses (bruises) occurring after minimal or unrecognized trauma. These bruises are often raised (hematomas) and occur in unusual locations, such as the trunk or upper arms.
    \item \textbf{Menorrhagia:} Heavy, prolonged menstrual bleeding is a major clinical feature in females of reproductive age. It is defined by periods lasting longer than 7 days, the passage of large blood clots, the need to change sanitary products more frequently than every two hours, or bleeding that leads to iron deficiency anaemia.
    \item \textbf{Prolonged Post-surgical Bleeding:} Excess bleeding following surgical procedures, minor lacerations, or dental extractions is a classic sign. Bleeding after dental procedures may persist for hours or resume several days after the initial event (delayed bleeding).
    \item \textbf{Postpartum Haemorrhage:} Women with VWD are at a significantly increased risk of both immediate and delayed (secondary) postpartum haemorrhage, as VWF levels, which rise during pregnancy, fall rapidly back to baseline after delivery.
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal Bleeding:} Less common but potentially severe, gastrointestinal haemorrhage can occur, particularly in older patients or those with Type 2 VWD, often associated with angiodysplasia (vascular malformations in the gut).
    \item \textbf{Hemarthrosis and Muscle Bleeding:} Bleeding into joint cavities (hemarthrosis) and deep muscles is rare in Type 1 and Type 2 VWD. However, it is a frequent and painful manifestation in patients with Type 3 VWD due to their severely low Factor VIII levels, leading to progressive joint damage (arthropathy) if untreated.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Due to the non-specific nature of mild bleeding symptoms, it is essential to distinguish von Willebrand disease from other inherited and acquired bleeding disorders. A comprehensive differential diagnosis includes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Haemophilia A (Factor VIII Deficiency):} An X-linked recessive disorder that primarily affects males. While Type 2N VWD features low Factor VIII levels, it is inherited autosomally, affecting males and females equally. Differentiation is achieved through genetic testing and specialized FVIII-VWF binding assays.
    \item \textbf{Haemophilia B (Factor IX Deficiency):} An X-linked recessive disorder characterized by a deficiency of Factor IX, presenting with deep tissue bleeding and hemarthrosis, distinguishable via specific clotting factor assays.
    \item \textbf{Bernard-Soulier Syndrome:} A rare, autosomal recessive platelet function disorder caused by a deficiency or dysfunction of the platelet glycoprotein Ib-IX-V complex (the receptor for VWF). It presents with macrothrombocytopenia (large platelets and low platelet count) and severe bleeding, but VWF levels and activity are normal.
    \item \textbf{Glanzmann Thrombasthenia:} An autosomal recessive disorder characterized by the absence or dysfunction of the platelet glycoprotein IIb/IIIa receptor, which prevents platelet aggregation. Platelet count and VWF levels are normal, but platelet aggregation studies show absent aggregation to all agonists except ristocetin.
    \item \textbf{Platelet Storage Pool Deficiencies:} Inherited defects in platelet granules (alpha or dense granules) that impair the release of clotting factors and ADP during platelet activation. These disorders present with mild mucocutaneous bleeding and normal VWF assays, but abnormal platelet electron microscopy or aggregation studies.
    \item \textbf{Connective Tissue Disorders:} Conditions such as Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome or Osteogenesis Imperfecta can present with easy bruising and mucosal bleeding due to capillary fragility and skin hyperextensibility, rather than a primary coagulation defect.
    \item \textbf{Acquired Coagulopathies:} Liver disease, chronic kidney disease (uremic platelet dysfunction), Vitamin K deficiency, and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) must be ruled out, particularly in patients with a late-onset bleeding history.
    \item \textbf{Medication-Induced Bleeding:} Bleeding secondary to the use of antiplatelet agents (aspirin, clopidogrel), non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), or anticoagulants (warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants) can mimic or exacerbate VWD.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most cases of von Willebrand disease can be managed effectively through outpatient care and planned therapeutic regimens. However, patients and caregivers must be educated on recognizing clinical scenarios that require prompt medical evaluation or emergency intervention.

Medical attention from a primary care physician or hematologist should be sought if:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding symptoms, such as epistaxis or bruising, increase in frequency, duration, or severity.
    \item Menstrual bleeding becomes unmanageable, requiring multiple sanitary pads or tampons per hour, or causes symptoms of anaemia (extreme fatigue, dizziness, pale skin).
    \item Minor cuts or lacerations continue to ooze blood despite the continuous application of direct pressure for more than 15 to 20 minutes.
    \item A surgical or dental procedure is planned, to allow for the formulation of a prophylactic haemostatic plan.
\end{itemize}

Immediate emergency services (such as calling \textbf{local emergency services} in many countries, \textbf{911} in the United States, or \textbf{999} in the United Kingdom) must be accessed if the patient experiences any of the following life-threatening symptoms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, uncontrollable bleeding that cannot be stopped with direct pressure.
    \item Signs of internal bleeding, such as coughing up blood, vomiting material that resembles coffee grounds, or passing black, tarry stools.
    \item Unexplained, sudden, and severe swelling, pain, or warmth in a joint (indicating hemarthrosis).
    \item Head trauma or any significant blow to the head, even in the absence of visible external bleeding, due to the high risk of intracranial haemorrhage.
    \item Symptoms suggestive of severe blood loss, such as confusion, rapid heart rate, shortness of breath, severe dizziness, or loss of consciousness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnostic pathway for von Willebrand disease is notoriously complex, requiring a combination of detailed clinical assessment and highly specialized laboratory investigations. Because VWF levels can fluctuate significantly in response to stress, exercise, inflammation, pregnancy, and hormonal changes, multiple blood draws over several weeks may be necessary to confirm a diagnosis.

The diagnostic process typically progresses through the following stages:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical Bleeding History:} A structured assessment using a validated Bleeding Assessment Tool (BAT), such as the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH) BAT, is performed. This tool generates a quantitative bleeding score based on the presence and severity of historical bleeding symptoms. A high score increases the pre-test probability of VWD.
    \item \textbf{Initial Coagulation Screen:} Baseline blood tests are performed to rule out other conditions. These include a Complete Blood Count (CBC) to evaluate platelet count (which is typically normal in VWD, except in Subtype 2B), Prothrombin Time (PT), and Activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (aPTT). The aPTT may be normal or prolonged, depending on the severity of the associated Factor VIII deficiency.
    \item \textbf{Primary VWD Diagnostic Panel:} If VWD is suspected, a triad of specific assays is performed:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{VWF Antigen Assay (VWF:Ag):} Measures the total amount of VWF protein in the plasma using immunological methods (ELISA or latex immunoassay).
        \item \textbf{VWF Activity Assays:} Evaluate the functional capacity of VWF. Historically, the Ristocetin Cofactor Assay (VWF:RCo) was the gold standard, measuring VWF's ability to agglutinate platelets in the presence of the antibiotic ristocetin. Newer, more sensitive assays measure VWF binding to recombinant glycoprotein Ib without ristocetin (VWF:GPIbM) or with ristocetin (VWF:GPIbR).
        \item \textbf{Factor VIII Coagulant Activity (FVIII:C):} Measures the enzymatic activity of circulating Factor VIII, which is bound to and stabilized by VWF.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Interpretation of Primary Results:} A diagnostic threshold of less than 30 IU/dL (or 30\%) of VWF:Ag and/or VWF activity is generally diagnostic of VWD. Patients with VWF levels between 30\% and 50\% who have significant bleeding symptoms are often classified as having ``low VWF'' rather than classic VWD, representing a clinical grey zone.
    \item \textbf{Secondary and Classification Assays:} Once a deficiency is established, further testing is needed to determine the specific subtype:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{VWF Multimer Analysis:} Uses gel electrophoresis to visualize the structural distribution of VWF multimers. A loss of large and intermediate multimers indicates Type 2A or 2B VWD, while a normal multimer pattern suggests Type 1.
        \item \textbf{Ristocetin-Induced Platelet Aggregation (RIPA):} Evaluates platelet aggregation in response to low concentrations of ristocetin. Hyper-responsiveness (aggregation at low doses) is characteristic of Type 2B VWD.
        \item \textbf{VWF-Factor VIII Binding Assay (VWF:FVIIIB):} Evaluates the capacity of the patient's VWF to bind exogenous Factor VIII, confirming or ruling out Type 2N VWD.
        \item \textbf{Genetic Testing:} Sequence analysis of the \textit{VWF} gene on chromosome 12 can confirm the diagnosis, especially for distinguishing Type 2 subtypes (e.g., 2B vs. pseudo-VWD) and for prenatal counseling in severe Type 3 VWD.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of von Willebrand disease is tailored to the individual patient, depending on the disease subtype, the severity of the bleeding phenotype, and the clinical scenario (e.g., routine prophylaxis, acute bleeding, or surgical planning). The therapeutic goal is to restore normal primary and secondary haemostasis by increasing circulating levels of functional VWF and Factor VIII, or by inhibiting fibrinolysis.

Treatment modalities include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Desmopressin (DDAVP):} A synthetic analogue of the antidiuretic hormone vasopressin. DDAVP stimulates the release of endogenous VWF and Factor VIII from endothelial storage sites (Weibel-Palade bodies) into the plasma. It is typically administered intravenously or via a concentrated nasal spray. DDAVP is highly effective in most patients with Type 1 VWD and some with Type 2 (excluding 2B, where it may trigger platelet aggregation and thrombocytopenia, and 2N/3, where there are no stores to release). A ``DDAVP trial'' must be performed prior to therapeutic use to assess the patient's biological response and safety.
    \item \textbf{Clotting Factor Concentrates:} Used when DDAVP is ineffective, contraindicated, or when prolonged treatment is required (such as major surgery).
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Plasma-derived VWF/FVIII Concentrates:} Virus-inactivated concentrates derived from human plasma that contain both VWF and Factor VIII (e.g., Humate-P, Alphanate). These are the standard of care for major bleeding or surgery in Type 2 and Type 3 VWD.
        \item \textbf{Recombinant VWF (rVWF):} A highly purified, recombinant VWF product (e.g., Vonvendi) that contains no Factor VIII. It allows for targeted VWF replacement without the risk of accumulation of Factor VIII, which can increase thromboembolic risk during prolonged therapy. If immediate haemostasis is required, rVWF can be co-administered with recombinant Factor VIII.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antifibrinolytic Agents:} Drugs that inhibit the activation of plasminogen to plasmin, thereby stabilizing clots. Tranexamic acid (TXA) and epsilon-aminocaproic acid (EACA) are widely used as oral or intravenous therapies. They are particularly useful for managing mucosal bleeding, such as menorrhagia, epistaxis, and dental procedures, either alone or as adjunctive therapy with DDAVP or factor concentrates.
    \item \textbf{Hormonal Therapies:} For females with menorrhagia, combined oral contraceptive pills, oral progesterone, or the levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine device (IUD) are highly effective. These therapies suppress endometrial proliferation and increase baseline VWF levels, reducing menstrual blood loss.
    \item \textbf{Local Hemostatic Measures:} Topical agents such as fibrin glues, thrombin-soaked gelloy, or collagen sponges can be applied directly to accessible bleeding sites, particularly in the mouth or nasal passages.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Although many individuals with von Willebrand disease lead active lives with minimal interruption, the condition carries a risk of significant clinical complications, particularly if left untreated or during haemostatic challenges.

Potential complications associated with VWD and its treatments include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Severe Iron Deficiency Anaemia:} Chronic, heavy menstrual bleeding in females and recurrent epistaxis or gastrointestinal bleeding in both sexes can deplete the body's iron stores, leading to debilitating anaemia characterized by fatigue, weakness, dyspnea, and cognitive impairment.
    \item \textbf{Haemophilic Arthropathy:} In patients with Type 3 VWD, recurrent, untreated hemarthroses (joint bleeds) lead to chronic synovial inflammation (synovitis), cartilage degradation, and bone erosion, resulting in chronic pain, joint deformities, and permanent loss of mobility.
    \item \textbf{Life-Threatening Haemorrhage:} Severe bleeding can occur post-trauma or during surgical procedures. Internal bleeding, particularly intracranial haemorrhage following minor head trauma or massive gastrointestinal bleeding, can be fatal.
    \item \textbf{Alloantibody (Inhibitor) Formation:} A rare but serious complication in patients with Type 3 VWD. Because their immune systems have never encountered VWF, the administration of exogenous VWF concentrates can trigger the production of neutralizing antibodies (inhibitors). This renders subsequent factor replacement therapy ineffective and can cause severe anaphylactic reactions.
    \item \textbf{Thrombosis:} Overtreatment with plasma-derived VWF/FVIII concentrates can result in supraphysiological levels of VWF and Factor VIII. This creates a prothrombotic state, increasing the risk of deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction, or stroke, especially in patients with pre-existing cardiovascular risk factors.
    \item \textbf{DDAVP-Induced Hyponatremia:} Because DDAVP acts as an antidiuretic, repeated doses can lead to water retention and severe hyponatremia (low blood sodium). This can manifest as headache, nausea, confusion, seizures, and in severe cases, coma, necessitating strict fluid restriction during DDAVP administration.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals with von Willebrand disease is generally excellent. The vast majority of patients have Type 1 VWD, which is typically mild and does not reduce life expectancy. With appropriate education, lifestyle modifications, and modern therapeutic options, individuals with mild-to-moderate VWD can expect to lead normal, active, and productive lives.

For those with more severe forms, such as Type 3 or severe Type 2 variants, the prognosis has dramatically improved over the past few decades. The development of highly purified, virus-inactivated plasma-derived concentrates and recombinant VWF has revolutionized care, allowing for effective management of acute bleeding and successful major surgeries. In patients with severe recurrent bleeding, particularly joint bleeding, long-term prophylactic factor replacement therapy can prevent the development of debilitating arthropathy.

The primary factors influencing long-term outcomes are the timeliness of diagnosis, access to comprehensive haemophilia treatment centres (HTCs), and patient compliance with treatment plans. Early diagnosis prevents unnecessary or harmful surgical interventions and allows for proactive management during high-risk events like childbirth or trauma. While VWD is a lifelong genetic condition, it is not progressive; the underlying severity of the genetic mutation remains constant throughout an individual's life, although bleeding tendencies may change with age or physiological shifts.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
While inherited von Willebrand disease cannot be prevented, comprehensive self-care strategies and proactive measures can significantly minimize the frequency of bleeding episodes, prevent complications, and ensure safety during daily activities.

Essential self-care recommendations for individuals diagnosed with VWD include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Avoid Platelet-Inhibiting Medications:} Patients must strictly avoid aspirin, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen, naproxen, and diclofenac, as well as over-the-counter cold remedies containing these compounds. These medications impair platelet function and worsen bleeding tendencies. Acetaminophen (paracetamol) is the preferred alternative for pain relief.
    \item \textbf{Maintain Excellent Oral Hygiene:} Regular brushing with a soft-bristled toothbrush, daily flossing, and biannual dental checkups are critical. Good oral hygiene prevents periodontal disease, which is a frequent source of mucosal bleeding, and reduces the need for invasive dental procedures.
    \item \textbf{Participate in Safe Physical Activities:} Physical exercise is highly recommended to maintain cardiovascular health, joint flexibility, and muscle strength. However, contact sports with a high risk of head injury or joint trauma (such as rugby, American football, boxing, and ice hockey) should be avoided. Safe alternatives include swimming, cycling (with a helmet), walking, and golf.
    \item \textbf{Wear Medical Identification:} Affected individuals, especially those with moderate-to-severe VWD (Type 2 and Type 3), should wear a medical alert bracelet or carry an emergency medical card detailing their specific VWD subtype, severity, and recommended treatment protocols.
    \item \textbf{Establish a Bleeding Management Plan:} Work with a hematologist to create a comprehensive plan for managing bleeding at home, school, or work. Keep a supply of prescribed medications, such as nasal DDAVP or oral tranexamic acid, readily available.
    \item \textbf{Pre-procedural Notification:} Always inform all healthcare providers, including dentists, surgeons, and obstetricians, about the diagnosis of VWD well in advance of any scheduled procedure, to allow for coordination with a coagulation specialist.
\end{itemize}
