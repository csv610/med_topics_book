\chapter{Macular degeneration}
\index{Macular degeneration}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Macular degeneration, commonly referred to as age-related macular degeneration (AMD), is a progressive, chronic neurodegenerative disease of the central retina that represents the leading cause of irreversible visual impairment and blindness in older populations. The disease selectively damages the macula—the highly specialised central region of the retina responsible for sharp, straight-ahead, high-acuity vision and colour perception. AMD is clinically classified into two main forms: "dry" (non-exudative), characterised by geographic atrophy and cellular accumulation of waste products (drusen), and "wet" (exudative), characterised by pathologic choroidal neovascularisation. While AMD does not typically lead to complete darkness (as peripheral vision remains intact), it severely compromises a patient's independence by affecting activities such as reading, driving, and facial recognition.

\section*{Epidemiology}
AMD is a major global public health concern, with an estimated global prevalence of nearly 200 million people, a figure projected to rise substantially due to ageing populations. In many countries, AMD is the leading cause of blindness in people aged over 50, affecting approximately 1 in 7 people in this age bracket, and rising to nearly 1 in 3 in those aged over 80. The disease is slightly more prevalent in females than males, partly due to longer female life expectancy. Major risk factors include advancing age, European ancestry, a family history of the disease, and cigarette smoking, which increases the risk of developing AMD by three- to four-fold.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The primary site of pathology in AMD is the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), Bruch's membrane, and the underlying choriocapillaris, which are crucial for maintaining photoreceptor viability.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic susceptibility:} Strongly linked to polymorphisms in the complement system genes (specifically Complement Factor H, CFH, and ARMS2/HTRA1), pointing to an underlying dysregulation of the alternative complement pathway.
    \item \textbf{Drusen accumulation:} Chronic metabolic dysfunction leads to the accumulation of lipid- and protein-rich extracellular deposits (drusen) between the RPE and Bruch's membrane, which impairs nutrient transport to the outer retina.
    \item \textbf{Oxidative stress and ageing:} Accumulation of lipofuscin within RPE cells, driven by lifetime exposure to light and oxidative stress, leads to progressive RPE cell damage and death (geographic atrophy, characteristic of dry AMD).
    \item \textbf{Choroidal neovascularisation (Wet AMD):} Hypoxia and inflammatory signals upregulate Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF), stimulating the growth of abnormal, fragile new blood vessels from the choroid through Bruch's membrane into the sub-retinal space.
    \item \textbf{Exudation and scarring:} These abnormal vessels leak fluid, lipids, and blood, causing sub-retinal swelling, haemorrhage, and ultimately fibrovascular scarring (disciform scar) that permanently destroys central vision.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms of macular degeneration develop gradually in the dry form, but can present with sudden, severe vision loss in the wet form.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Metamorphopsia:} Distortion of visual images where straight lines (such as door frames, window panes, or sentences on a page) appear bent, wavy, or crooked, which is a hallmark feature of wet AMD.
    \item \textbf{Central scotoma:} The development of a blurry or blank spot in the centre of the visual field, which progressively darkens and expands, making it difficult to see details directly in front of the viewer.
    \item \textbf{Decreased visual acuity:} Progressive blurring of near and distance vision, which is not fully correctable with glasses.
    \item \textbf{Impaired dark adaptation:} Difficulty adapting to low-light levels, such as entering a dark room or driving at night.
    \item \textbf{Altered colour perception:} Colours appear less vibrant, washed out, or difficult to distinguish.
    \item \textbf{Contrast sensitivity loss:} Increased difficulty distinguishing between objects and their background, making reading in dim light or walking on uneven surfaces challenging.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
AMD must be distinguished from other macular and retinal diseases that present with central vision loss or metamorphopsia.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diabetic macular oedema (DMO):} Central retinal swelling in patients with diabetic retinopathy, characterised by microaneurysms and hard exudates rather than drusen.
    \item \textbf{Epiretinal membrane (Macular pucker):} A thin fibrotic sheet over the macular surface causing distortion, diagnosed by optical coherence tomography (OCT) showing traction on the inner retina.
    \item \textbf{Central serous chorioretinopathy (CSCR):} A localised serous detachment of the neurosensory retina, typically affecting younger males and associated with stress or corticosteroid use.
    \item \textbf{Macular hole:} A full-thickness defect in the central fovea, presenting with rapid central vision loss and a characteristic ring-like defect on OCT.
    \item \textbf{Toxic maculopathy:} Retinal toxicity from drugs such as hydroxychloroquine, which causes bilateral central scotomas and a "bull's eye" maculopathy pattern.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Regular eye examinations are key for early detection of dry AMD, but any sudden change in central vision requires emergency ophthalmological assessment to rule out active wet AMD.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe, painful loss of vision, which may indicate acute angle-closure glaucoma or retrobulbar haemorrhage.
    \item Vision loss associated with acute neurological symptoms, such as sudden weakness, numbness, or difficulty speaking (indicative of a stroke).
\end{itemize}
Other prompt reasons to see an ophthalmologist include sudden waviness or distortion of straight lines, a new blank spot in your central vision, or rapid deterioration of reading ability.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of AMD is established through comprehensive clinical eye examination and advanced non-invasive retinal imaging.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fundus biomicroscopy:} Slit-lamp examination of the retina using specialised lenses to identify drusen, RPE pigmentary alterations, geographic atrophy, or sub-retinal fluid and haemorrhage.
    \item \textbf{Amsler grid test:} A simple grid tool used to detect metamorphopsia and scotomas, serving as a valuable self-monitoring test for patients at home.
    \item \textbf{Optical coherence tomography (OCT):} A non-invasive, high-resolution cross-sectional imaging technique that is the gold standard for diagnosing and monitoring AMD, showing sub-retinal fluid, pigment epithelial detachments (PEDs), and macular thickness.
    \item \textbf{Fluorescein angiography (FA):} Intravenous injection of sodium fluorescein dye followed by rapid retinal photography, used to locate and characterise the pattern of leaking choroidal neovascularisation in wet AMD.
    \item \textbf{Optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA):} A newer, non-invasive imaging method that visualises the retinal and choroidal microvasculature without the need for dye injection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
While there is currently no cure for AMD, management strategies differ significantly between the dry and wet forms, aiming to slow progression or suppress abnormal vessel growth.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anti-VEGF therapy:} The gold standard treatment for wet AMD, involving regular intravitreal injections of VEGF inhibitors (such as ranibizumab, aflibercept, or faricimab) to suppress choroidal neovascularisation, reduce macular oedema, and stabilise or improve vision.
    \item \textbf{Nutritional supplementation (AREDS2 formula):} Daily high-dose antioxidant vitamins and minerals (vitamins C and E, zinc, copper, lutein, and zeaxanthin) recommended for patients with intermediate or advanced dry AMD in one eye, shown to reduce the risk of progression by approximately 25\%.
    \item \textbf{Complement inhibitors:} Emerging intravitreal therapies (such as pegcetacoplan or avacincaptad pegol) approved for slowing the progression of geographic atrophy in advanced dry AMD.
    \item \textbf{Low-vision rehabilitation:} Utilisation of optical aids (magnifiers, telescopic lenses, high-contrast reading materials) and occupational therapy to help patients maximise their remaining peripheral vision and maintain independence.
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle modifications:} Complete smoking cessation, maintaining a diet rich in leafy green vegetables and oily fish, and protecting eyes from ultraviolet light.
    \item \textbf{Monitoring and home self-testing:} Daily use of the Amsler grid to detect early conversion of dry AMD to the wet form.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Severe, permanent central blindness:} Complete loss of detailed central vision, preventing reading, driving, or recognising faces.
    \item \textbf{Charles Bonnet syndrome:} Visual hallucinations (often of complex patterns, people, or animals) experienced by patients with severe vision loss, arising from sensory deprivation of the visual cortex.
    \item \textbf{Intravitreal injection complications:} Rare but serious risks of anti-VEGF therapy, including endophthalmitis (intraocular infection), retinal detachment, and vitreous haemorrhage.
    \item \textbf{Depression and social isolation:} High rates of psychological distress due to loss of independence, inability to engage in hobbies, and fear of complete blindness.
    \item \textbf{Increased risk of falls:} Loss of contrast sensitivity and depth perception leads to an elevated risk of accidental slips, trips, and fractures, particularly in elderly patients.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for macular degeneration has been revolutionised by anti-VEGF therapy, which prevents severe vision loss in over 90\% of wet AMD patients and leads to visual improvement in up to 30\% of cases when initiated early. However, treatment requires ongoing, often lifelong injections. The prognosis for dry AMD is slower and more variable; while geographic atrophy progresses slowly over years, it remains a major source of visual impairment due to the lack of treatments that can reverse existing tissue loss.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Quit smoking:} Eliminating tobacco use is the single most important modifiable risk factor to prevent AMD development and progression.
    \item \textbf{Eat an eye-healthy diet:} Consume plenty of dark leafy greens (spinach, kale), colourful vegetables, and foods rich in omega-3 fatty acids (salmon, tuna, walnuts).
    \item \textbf{Protect eyes from UV light:} Wear sunglasses that block 100\% of UVA and UVB rays when outdoors.
    \item \textbf{Manage cardiovascular health:} Maintain healthy blood pressure and cholesterol levels, as cardiovascular disease is linked to an increased risk of AMD.
    \item \textbf{Use the Amsler grid daily:} Test each eye individually to detect early signs of distortion, and report any changes immediately.
    \item \textbf{Schedule regular eye exams:} Undergo comprehensive dilated eye examinations annually if you are over 50 or have a family history of AMD.
\end{itemize}
