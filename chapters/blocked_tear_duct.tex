\chapter{Blocked Tear Duct}
\index{Blocked Tear Duct}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A blocked tear duct is a blockage of the nasolacrimal system -- the small channels that drain tears from the surface of the eye into the nose. When the duct is blocked, tears cannot drain away and spill over the lower lid, producing a constantly watering eye. The condition is very common in newborn babies, in whom it usually clears spontaneously, and also occurs in adults, in whom it is more often persistent and may require treatment. It is distinct from overproduction of tears and from a dry, irritable eye, although these can coexist.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Congenital blockage of the tear duct affects up to one in ten newborn babies, and most cases resolve on their own within the first year of life. In adults, a blocked tear duct is most common after middle age and is slightly more frequent in women. It may develop after infection, facial injury, or nasal surgery, and is more likely in people with chronic sinus disease, nasal polyps, or facial fractures. Blockage becomes increasingly common with advancing age as the drainage channels narrow.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Tears normally drain through tiny openings (puncta) at the inner corner of the eyelids into a channel that runs beside the nose (the nasolacrimal duct) and empties into the nose. A blockage can occur at any point along this pathway:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{In newborns:} the lower end of the duct fails to open fully at birth, usually because a membrane remains; most such ducts open over time
    \item \textbf{Age-related narrowing:} the duct narrows as the surrounding tissues thicken with age
    \item \textbf{Infection:} chronic conjunctivitis, sinusitis, or infection of the tear sac itself (dacryocystitis) can scar the duct
    \item \textbf{Injury:} facial or nasal fractures can damage the drainage channels
    \item \textbf{Nasal disease:} nasal polyps, previous sinus or nasal surgery, and rarely nasal tumours
    \item \textbf{Tumours:} a growth in the nose, sinus, or eyelid may rarely compress the duct
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
A blocked tear duct typically produces a distinctive set of symptoms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Tears pooling and running down the cheek, especially in wind or cold weather
    \item Persistent watering of one or both eyes
    \item A crust of dried tears on the lashes or lower lid
    \item Blurred vision from the film of tears over the eye
    \item Redness and swelling at the inner corner of the eye, sometimes with a tender lump
    \item A sticky or mucopurulent discharge, particularly when the tear sac is infected
    \item In newborns, a wet eye that has been present since birth or soon after
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A watering eye can have several causes that should be distinguished from a blocked duct:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Overproduction of tears due to a dry eye or ocular irritation (reflex tearing)
    \item Entropion or ectropion, where the lid turns in or out so the drainage openings no longer lie against the eye
    \item Allergic or infective conjunctivitis
    \item A foreign body or an ingrowing eyelash
    \item Glaucoma, uveitis, or other eye disease causing reflex tearing
    \item A tumour or swelling of the tear gland, tear sac, or eyelid
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Mild, intermittent watering can be discussed at a routine appointment, but prompt care is warranted for the following:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The inner corner of the eye becomes red, swollen, and painful
    \item There is a persistent discharge, or the eye becomes red and sticky
    \item There is a fever with facial pain or swelling
    \item Vision changes, or the eye becomes painful or sensitive to light
    \item A lump develops near the inner corner of the eye
    \item Watering is constant and interferes with reading, driving, or daily life
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:} sudden, severe facial swelling with fever, sudden loss of vision, or difficulty breathing or swallowing.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis is usually straightforward, and special tests are reserved for persistent or unclear cases:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} assessment of the lids, eye, puncta, and the inner corner of the orbit
    \item \textbf{Tear drainage test:} dye or fluorescein is placed in the eye and its rate of drainage observed
    \item \textbf{Probing and irrigation:} gently flushing the duct confirms the blockage and its level
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} in selected cases, a dye study (dacryocystography) or CT of the drainage system
    \item \textbf{Swabs:} of discharge when infection is suspected
    \item \textbf{Ophthalmology referral:} for persistent, recurrent, or complicated blockage
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on the age of the patient and the cause of the blockage:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{In newborns:} gentle massage of the tear sac several times a day; most ducts open on their own by 9--12 months of age
    \item \textbf{Lid hygiene:} cleaning the eye with warm water and a clean cloth
    \item \textbf{Treating infection:} antibiotics for an infected tear sac, with warm compresses for dacryocystitis
    \item \textbf{Probing:} a specialist passes a fine probe to open the duct, often under general anaesthesia in children
    \item \textbf{Stenting:} a small tube may be placed to keep the duct open while it heals
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR), a small procedure that creates a new drainage pathway into the nose
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review to confirm the eye has stopped watering and that no lump or infection has developed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dacryocystitis: infection of the tear sac, with redness, pain, swelling, and discharge
    \item Recurrent conjunctivitis and persistently sticky eyes
    \item Facial cellulitis in severe infections, which can be serious if untreated
    \item Constant watering that interferes with reading, driving, and social life
    \item Scarring or re-blockage after probing or surgery
    \item Uncommon surgical complications, including bleeding and infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
In most newborns the duct opens spontaneously within the first year, and probing resolves most of the remainder. In adults the blockage usually persists without treatment, but surgery to create a new drainage pathway is successful in the majority of cases and reliably stops the watering. Complications of treatment are uncommon, and recurrence after successful surgery is low. Where a tumour is the cause, the outlook depends on the type of growth and is best when it is detected and treated early.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep the eye and lids clean, especially when there is a discharge
    \item In babies, perform gentle massage over the inner corner of the eye as advised by the health visitor or doctor
    \item Treat eye infections promptly
    \item Protect the face during contact sport and avoid facial injuries where possible
    \item Manage allergies and sinus problems that can affect the drainage channels
    \item See an eye specialist early if watering is persistent, as outcomes are best with early treatment
\end{itemize}
