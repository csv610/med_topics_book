\chapter{Low FODMAP diets}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A low FODMAP diet is a structured, evidence-based dietary therapy designed to manage symptoms of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and other functional gastrointestinal disorders. FODMAP is an acronym for Fermentable Oligosaccharides, Disaccharides, Monosaccharides, and Polyols. These are short-chain carbohydrates and sugar alcohols that are poorly absorbed in the small intestine, leading to increased osmotic water delivery to the gut and rapid bacterial fermentation in the large intestine. The diet is not a permanent, restrictive lifestyle but rather a three-phase diagnostic tool: elimination (restriction), systematic reintroduction (testing), and long-term personalisation (maintenance). Because of its complexity and potential to alter nutritional intake, the diet is best conducted under the guidance of a qualified dietitian.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Irritable bowel syndrome is a common disorder affecting approximately 10\% to 15\% of the global population. In Australia, it is estimated to affect up to 1 in 5 individuals at some point in their lives. The low FODMAP diet, pioneered by researchers at Monash University in Melbourne, Australia, is now recommended globally as a first-line dietary intervention for IBS. Clinical trials demonstrate that approximately 70\% to 80\% of patients with IBS experience significant relief from abdominal pain, bloating, wind, and altered bowel habits when following the diet. The therapy is most frequently prescribed to adults aged 20 to 50 and is more commonly recommended to females, reflecting the demographic distribution of IBS presentations in primary and tertiary care.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The symptoms of IBS are triggered by the physical effects of FODMAPs as they pass through the digestive tract. The pathophysiology involves a combination of mechanical distension and individual gut hypersensitivity.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Osmotic activity:} Small, unabsorbed carbohydrate molecules draw water into the lumen of the small and large intestines, increasing fluid volume and leading to loose stools, diarrhoea, and abdominal distension.
    \item \textbf{Colonic fermentation:} Undigested carbohydrates travel to the colon, where they are rapidly fermented by resident microflora. This anaerobic process produces gases, including hydrogen, carbon dioxide, and methane, resulting in flatulence and bloating.
    \item \textbf{Visceral hypersensitivity:} While gas and water production occur in all individuals, patients with IBS possess a hypersensitive enteric nervous system. Normal stretching of the bowel wall is perceived as intense pain, cramping, and discomfort.
    \item \textbf{FODMAP categories:} Oligosaccharides (fructans in wheat, rye, onions, and garlic, and galacto-oligosaccharides in legumes), disaccharides (lactose in milk and dairy), monosaccharides (excess fructose in honey, mangoes, and apples), and polyols (sorbitol and mannitol in stone fruits and artificial sweeteners).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
FODMAP sensitivity manifests as a range of chronic or recurrent gastrointestinal symptoms. The clinical progression of the diet itself follows three distinct phases.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal symptoms:} Recurrent abdominal pain or cramping (often relieved by defaecation), bloating, abdominal distension, excessive flatulence, and altered bowel habits (diarrhoea, constipation, or alternating patterns).
    \item \textbf{Phase 1 (Elimination):} Strict restriction of all high-FODMAP foods for a period of 2 to 6 weeks. The goal is to determine if symptoms resolve or improve significantly, confirming FODMAP sensitivity.
    \item \textbf{Phase 2 (Reintroduction):} Systematic testing of specific FODMAP groups (e.g., testing fructose using honey) over 3-day periods while maintaining a baseline low-FODMAP diet, to identify specific food triggers and tolerance levels.
    \item \textbf{Phase 3 (Personalisation):} Reintroducing tolerated foods and FODMAP groups back into the daily diet, establishing a varied, nutritionally complete, and sustainable long-term eating plan.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before initiating a low FODMAP diet, it is essential to exclude organic gastrointestinal diseases that present with similar symptoms, as treating these conditions requires different clinical pathways.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coeliac disease:} An autoimmune reaction to gluten causing small bowel damage. It must be ruled out via serology and duodenal biopsy while the patient is on a gluten-containing diet, prior to starting a low FODMAP diet (which is naturally low in gluten).
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD):} Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis. Unlike IBS, IBD involves mucosal inflammation and ulceration, presenting with raised faecal calprotectin, rectal bleeding, and systemic symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Colorectal cancer:} Malignancy presenting with "red flag" symptoms such as unexplained weight loss, iron deficiency anaemia, persistent blood in the stool, or new-onset bowel changes in individuals over 50.
    \item \textbf{Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO):} An abnormal increase in bacterial population in the small intestine, which causes bloating and gas and may be investigated with breath tests.
    \item \textbf{Gynaecological conditions:} Endometriosis, ovarian cysts, or ovarian cancer, which can cause lower abdominal pain and bloating in female patients.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients experiencing chronic digestive symptoms should consult a general practitioner for an accurate diagnosis before starting any restrictive diet. 

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, sudden, or rapidly worsening abdominal pain.
    \item Vomiting blood, or passing large amounts of blood or black, tarry stools.
    \item Abdominal pain accompanied by chest tightness, severe shortness of breath, or collapse.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of IBS is clinical (based on the Rome IV criteria), but investigations are necessary to exclude other pathologies before starting the diet.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coeliac serology:} Measuring tissue transglutaminase (tTG) IgA antibodies to rule out coeliac disease.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory markers:} Blood tests for C-reactive protein (CRP) and ESR to check for systemic inflammation.
    \item \textbf{Faecal calprotectin:} A stool test to rule out active gut inflammation, separating IBS from IBD.
    \item \textbf{Haematology and biochemistry:} Full blood count to check for anaemia and iron studies to exclude malabsorption.
    \item \textbf{Endoscopy:} Gastroscopy or colonoscopy, indicated if the patient has alarm symptoms, family history of bowel cancer, or abnormal screening tests.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management involves a structured, multi-disciplinary approach centered around the three-phase dietary protocol and lifestyle modifications.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietitian consultation:} Working with a dietitian to ensure nutritional adequacy, prevent unnecessary restriction, interpret reintroduction results, and maintain food variety.
    \textbf{Three-phase protocol implementation:} Restricting high-FODMAP foods, reintroducing them systematically, and personalising the long-term diet.
    \item \textbf{FODMAP-friendly substitutions:} Replacing high-FODMAP foods with appropriate alternatives (e.g., using garlic-infused olive oil instead of garlic cloves, and choosing firm tofu instead of silken tofu).
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological therapies:} Short-term use of antispasmodics for pain, laxatives for constipation, or antidiarrhoeals (e.g., loperamide) to manage bowel habits.
    \item \textbf{Gut-directed hypnotherapy:} Evidence-based psychological therapy that target visceral hypersensitivity and has been shown to be as effective as the low FODMAP diet for many IBS sufferers.
    \item \textbf{Stress reduction:} Incorporating mindfulness, exercise, and cognitive behavioural therapy to manage the gut-brain axis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
If not managed correctly, a low FODMAP diet can lead to several nutritional, physiological, and psychological complications.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nutritional deficiencies:} Inadequate intake of calcium, iron, folate, B vitamins, and dietary fibre, particularly if the strict elimination phase is maintained indefinitely.
    \item \textbf{Microbiome alterations:} A reduction in beneficial gut bacteria, particularly bifidobacteria, due to the low intake of prebiotic fibres (fructans and GOS) which serve as fuel for these microbes.
    \item \textbf{Disordered eating:} The development of orthorexia or food anxiety, as patients may become fearful of reintroducing foods or feel overwhelmed by dietary restrictions.
    \item \textbf{Social and lifestyle impact:} Difficulty eating out, travelling, or preparing meals for families, leading to social isolation and reduced quality of life.
    \item \textbf{Treatment failure:} Lack of symptom improvement, which can occur if the diet is implemented incorrectly or if the patient's symptoms are driven by factors other than food intolerance.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for achieving symptom control using the low FODMAP diet is highly favourable, with 70\% to 80\% of patients reporting significant clinical improvement. Most patients are able to identify a small number of specific FODMAP groups that trigger their symptoms, allowing them to reintroduce all other foods. Long-term outcomes are best when patients transition from the strict elimination phase to a modified, personalised diet, which preserves gut microbial diversity and minimises lifestyle disruption while maintaining symptom control.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Self-care during the diet involves utilizing reliable tools and maintaining a structured approach to food tracking.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Use the Monash App:} Download the official Monash University FODMAP app to access up-to-date food ratings, portion guides, and recipe suggestions.
    \item \textbf{Keep a food and symptom diary:} Record all meals, portion sizes, bowel movements, and symptoms daily to identify patterns and track progress.
    \item \textbf{Limit the elimination phase:} Do not remain in the strict Phase 1 elimination phase for longer than 6 weeks to protect gut health and prevent nutritional deficits.
    \item \textbf{Understand water-solubility:} Note that FODMAPs are water-soluble; they leach into water during cooking (e.g., in soups or stews) but do not leach into oils, making garlic-infused oil a safe way to add flavour.
    \item \textbf{Read ingredient labels:} Check packaged foods carefully for hidden high-FODMAP ingredients such as onion powder, garlic powder, honey, inulin, and fruit juice concentrates.
    \item \textbf{Prepare for eating out:} Research menus in advance, choose simple dishes (like grilled meats with salad), and communicate dietary requirements clearly to restaurant staff.
\end{itemize}
