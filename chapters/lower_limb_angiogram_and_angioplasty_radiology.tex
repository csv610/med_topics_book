\chapter{Lower limb angiogram and angioplasty (radiology)}
\index{Lower limb angiogram and angioplasty (radiology)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Lower limb angiogram and angioplasty are minimally invasive interventional radiological procedures used to diagnose and treat peripheral arterial disease (PAD). An angiogram, or arteriogram, utilizes a catheter inserted into the arterial system, through which iodinated contrast medium is injected under real-time X-ray imaging (fluoroscopy). This allows the interventional radiologist to map the arterial anatomy of the legs and pinpoint areas of stenosis or occlusion. An angioplasty (balloon angioplasty) is the therapeutic extension of this procedure. It involves advancing a balloon-tipped catheter to the site of narrowing, inflating the balloon to dilate the lumen, and often deploying a metallic stent to keep the vessel patent. These procedures restore vital blood flow to the distal limb, alleviating pain and preventing tissue loss.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Peripheral arterial disease is a major global health issue, affecting over 200 million people worldwide. In many countries, PAD affects approximately 10--15\% of the population aged over 70 years. Lower limb diagnostic angiograms and therapeutic angioplasties are routinely performed in public and private hospitals. The frequency of these procedures has risen due to an ageing population, high rates of diabetes, and the shift towards minimally invasive endovascular techniques over traditional open vascular bypass surgery.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Lower limb arterial narrowing is primarily driven by systemic vascular diseases that restrict perfusion to the extremities. Aetiological factors and their physiological mechanisms include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Atherosclerosis:} The predominant cause, where chronic endothelial injury leads to the accumulation of lipid-rich plaques, inflammatory cells, calcium, and fibrous tissue within the intima of lower limb arteries (e.g.\ femoral, popliteal, or tibial arteries).
    \item \textbf{Thrombosis or embolism:} Acute occlusion of an artery by a blood clot formed in situ over a ruptured plaque, or an embolus originating from a proximal source (such as the heart in atrial fibrillation).
    \item \textbf{Diabetic vasculopathy:} Accelerated atherosclerosis combined with microvascular changes and calcification of the arterial media (Mönckeberg's arteriosclerosis), common in patients with long-standing diabetes.
    \item \textbf{Vascular trauma:} Direct trauma or iatrogenic injury causing arterial tearing, dissection, or spasm, necessitating urgent radiological intervention.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Patients requiring these radiological interventions present with symptoms of chronic or acute limb ischaemia. Typical clinical signs and symptoms include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intermittent claudication:} Aching, cramping, or squeezing pain in the calves, thighs, or buttocks that is predictably brought on by walking a specific distance and rapidly relieved by standing still.
    \item \textbf{Ischaemic rest pain:} Constant, burning pain in the foot or toes that typically worsens when lying flat in bed and is partially relieved by hanging the leg over the edge of the bed (allowing gravity to assist perfusion).
    \item \textbf{Critical limb ischaemia:} Non-healing arterial ulcers, gangrene (dry or wet), and necrosis of the toes or foot due to profound arterial insufficiency.
    \item \textbf{Physical examination signs:} Cool extremities, shiny skin, loss of hair on the lower legs, thickened toenails, and weak or completely absent peripheral pulses (femoral, popliteal, dorsalis pedis, and posterior tibial).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Lower limb pain and sensory changes must be differentiated from non-arterial causes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurogenic claudication:} Leg pain caused by lumbar spinal stenosis; it is exacerbated by standing and walking but is relieved by bending forward (flexing the lumbar spine) rather than simply stopping, and is not associated with diminished pulses.
    \item \textbf{Deep vein thrombosis (DVT):} Acute, unilateral calf pain and swelling caused by venous outflow obstruction, rather than arterial inflow restriction.
    \item \textbf{Osteoarthritis of the hip or knee:} Musculoskeletal pain localized to joints that is aggravated by weight-bearing and movement, rather than distance walked, and does not cause ischaemic skin changes.
    \item \textbf{Diabetic peripheral neuropathy:} Symmetrical, burning pain or numbness in a 'glove-and-stocking' distribution that is constant and not specifically related to exertion or posture.
    \item \textbf{Venous insufficiency ulcers:} Ulcers located typically around the medial malleolus, associated with varicose veins, hyperpigmentation, and warm, oedematous legs, contrasting with cold, pale arterial ulcers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients with known peripheral arterial disease should monitor for signs of acute vascular occlusion or complications at the radiological access site.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden onset of severe leg pain, coldness, numbness, tingling, and a pale or mottled blue appearance of the foot or leg (the '6 Ps' of acute limb ischaemia: pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia).
    \item Active, heavy bleeding, or a rapidly expanding, painful lump (haematoma/pseudoaneurysm) at the groin puncture site following the procedure.
    \item Symptoms of a severe allergic reaction to the contrast dye, such as difficulty breathing, swelling of the face, lips, or throat, or widespread hives.
    \item Sudden weakness or paralysis in the leg or foot.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A step-wise diagnostic workup is performed prior to undertaking invasive angiography and angioplasty:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ankle-Brachial Index (ABI):} A simple, non-invasive test measuring the ratio of systolic blood pressure in the ankle to the arm; an ABI of less than 0.9 confirms peripheral arterial disease, and less than 0.4 indicates critical limb ischaemia.
    \item \textbf{Duplex arterial ultrasound:} High-frequency ultrasound used to visualise the arterial walls, locate plaque stenosis, and measure blood flow velocities.
    \item \textbf{CT Angiography (CTA) or MR Angiography (MRA):} Non-invasive, detailed three-dimensional vascular imaging used to plan the endovascular intervention by identifying the length and calcification of stenotic segments.
    \item \textbf{Renal function testing (eGFR and creatinine):} Essential prior to the angiogram, as the iodinated contrast dye is excreted by the kidneys and can cause contrast-induced nephropathy.
    \item \textbf{Coagulation profile:} Blood tests to ensure normal clotting before arterial puncture, minimising the risk of post-procedure haemorrhage.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The procedure is carried out in a specialised interventional radiology suite under local anaesthesia and conscious sedation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Angiography process:} Accessing the arterial system (usually the common femoral artery), placing an vascular sheath, threading a catheter over a guidewire, injecting iodinated contrast, and taking digital subtraction angiography (DSA) images.
    \item \textbf{Balloon angioplasty:} Advancing a balloon catheter across the stenosis, inflating the balloon to compress the atherosclerotic plaque and stretch the arterial wall, thereby restoring the lumen.
    \item \textbf{Stent deployment:} Inserting a self-expanding or balloon-expandable metallic mesh tube (stent) across the lesion if there is significant elastic recoil or dissection after balloon inflation.
    \item \textbf{Groin haemostasis:} Securing the arterial access site after sheath removal using manual compression or an active vascular closure device (e.g.\ Angio-Seal or StarClose).
    \item \textbf{Antiplatelet therapy:} Initiating daily aspirin and/or clopidogrel post-procedure to prevent thrombus formation on the newly dilated vessel wall or stent.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While endovascular interventions have lower morbidity than open surgery, specific radiological and vascular risks exist:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Puncture site haematoma:} Bleeding around the arterial access site, which can occasionally lead to a pseudoaneurysm or arteriovenous fistula.
    \item \textbf{Contrast-induced nephropathy:} A transient decline in renal function caused by contrast medium toxicity, particularly in patients with pre-existing renal impairment or diabetes.
    \item \textbf{Arterial dissection or rupture:} A tear in the inner lining of the artery or a complete perforation of the vessel wall by wires or balloons, requiring emergency stenting or open surgical repair.
    \item \textbf{Distal embolisation:} Detachment of plaque fragments or clot during manipulation, which travels downstream and occludes smaller distal vessels (the 'blue toe syndrome').
    \item \textbf{Allergic contrast reactions:} Hypersensitivity to iodinated contrast, ranging from mild pruritus to life-threatening anaphylactic shock.
    \item \textbf{In-stent restenosis:} Gradual re-narrowing of the treated segment due to smooth muscle cell proliferation (neointimal hyperplasia) inside the stent.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The immediate technical success rate for lower limb angioplasty is high (exceeding 90\% for focal, short stenoses). Restoring blood flow leads to rapid relief of claudication and provides the perfusion necessary to heal ischaemic foot ulcers. Long-term vessel patency depends on the patient's risk profile, the length and location of the treated segment (iliac arteries have higher patency than tibial arteries), and compliance with smoking cessation and antiplatelet medications. Restenosis may occur, requiring surveillance and potential repeat endovascular or surgical interventions.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Patients must implement self-care measures to protect the puncture site immediately post-procedure and manage their systemic vascular disease long-term:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Post-procedure puncture site care:} Avoid lifting objects heavier than 5 kg, climbing excessive stairs, or bending at the groin for the first 48 hours to prevent bleeding. Keep the site clean and dry.
    \item \textbf{Absolute smoking cessation:} The most critical lifestyle modification; smoking accelerates atherosclerosis and dramatically reduces the lifespan of angioplasties and stents.
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular risk control:} Strictly adhere to antiplatelet drugs and statins, and maintain tight control of blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood glucose.
    \item \textbf{Inspect feet daily:} Carefully examine the feet for any minor cuts, red areas, or blisters, and wear comfortable, protective footwear to prevent pressure sores.
    \item \textbf{Regular exercise:} Engage in a supervised or independent walking programme (once the access site has healed) to promote the growth of collateral blood vessels.
\end{itemize}
