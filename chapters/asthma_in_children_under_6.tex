\chapter{Asthma in Children Under 6}
\index{Asthma in Children Under 6}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Asthma in children under 6 years of age is a chronic condition of the airways characterised by recurrent episodes of wheeze, cough and breathlessness in the preschool years. Because the airways of young children are small and continue to develop, many babies and toddlers who wheeze do not go on to have true asthma. The umbrella term "preschool wheeze" is commonly used, and it is subdivided into episodic viral wheeze (symptoms occurring only during colds) and multi-trigger wheeze (symptoms also provoked by exercise, allergens, cold air or other triggers). Diagnosing asthma at this age is more difficult than in older children because formal lung function testing is usually not possible, so clinicians rely on the pattern of symptoms over time, examination findings and the response to a carefully monitored trial of treatment. Early recognition matters because prompt, well-organised care reduces the number of severe episodes and improves quality of life for the child and the family.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Wheeze is extremely common in early childhood, affecting roughly one in three children before their third birthday. Most preschool children who wheeze have episodic viral wheeze, which is closely linked to common colds and typically improves as the child grows. Asthma is more common in boys under 6 and in children with eczema, food allergy or a family history of asthma and allergic disease. Children exposed to tobacco smoke, those born prematurely and those who attend early childcare may wheeze more frequently. Importantly, many children who wheeze in the preschool years have no symptoms at all by primary school age, which reflects the developing nature of both the airways and the immune system.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The small airways of a young child are easily narrowed by inflammation, mucosal swelling, bronchoconstriction and mucus production. During a viral cold the airways can become overly reactive even in children without asthma, which is why viral wheeze is so common.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral infection:} the most common trigger, particularly respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), rhinovirus and other cold viruses
    \item \textbf{Small airway calibre:} infants and toddlers have naturally narrow airways that obstruct easily when inflamed
    \item \textbf{Atopy:} an allergic tendency, with eczema or food allergy, increases the chance of multi-trigger wheeze and persistent asthma
    \item \textbf{Environmental factors:} tobacco smoke, indoor mould, damp and air pollution can worsen symptoms and provoke episodes
    \item \textbf{Genetic predisposition:} a family history of asthma or allergy is a strong predictor of persistent symptoms
    \item \textbf{Impaired airway development:} prematurity and low birth weight are linked to smaller airways and more wheezing illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms in young children are usually noticed and described by parents and carers rather than reported by the child.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A whistling sound on breathing out (wheeze), often during or just after a cold
    \item A persistent or recurring cough, worse at night, on laughing or when active
    \item Rapid or noisy breathing, sometimes with the chest being pulled in (recession)
    \item Difficulty feeding or reduced interest in feeding because of breathlessness
    \item Poor sleep and tiredness during and between episodes
    \item In multi-trigger wheeze, symptoms also appear with exercise, allergens, cold air or smoke exposure
\end{itemize}
Between episodes the child is often completely well, which is characteristic of viral wheeze.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because many conditions cause wheeze and cough in young children, careful assessment is required before labelling a child with asthma.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral wheeze / bronchiolitis:} wheeze with a viral illness, very common under 2 years of age
    \item \textbf{Pneumonia:} fever, cough and fast breathing from infection of the lungs
    \item \textbf{Foreign body aspiration:} sudden choking and wheeze after a child puts a small object in the mouth
    \item \textbf{Gastro-oesophageal reflux:} acid reflux causing cough, choking and occasionally wheeze
    \item \textbf{Croup:} a barking cough with noisy breathing on inspiration (stridor)
    \item \textbf{Congenital abnormalities:} structural airway, heart or lung problems that usually present from early infancy
    \item \textbf{Cystic fibrosis:} persistent cough, poor growth and frequent chest infections with other features
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a GP if a young child has recurring wheeze or cough, symptoms that wake the child at night, difficulty feeding, or symptoms that are not responding to the reliever treatment.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, with the chest sucking in or the nostrils flaring
    \item A baby or toddler who is too breathless to feed or drink
    \item A child who is very drowsy, limp or difficult to wake
    \item Lips or face turning blue
    \item Symptoms that are not improving after the reliever is given
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
There is no single test for asthma in children under 6, so the diagnosis is based on the pattern of symptoms over time, examination and response to treatment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detailed history:} episodes of wheeze and cough, their triggers and timing, the child's feeding and sleep, and the family history of allergy and asthma
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} listening to the chest for wheeze and air entry, checking for eczema and other allergic signs, and assessing growth
    \item \textbf{Trial of treatment:} a trial of a reliever and, in some cases, a preventer, with careful review of the response
    \item \textbf{Exclusion of other causes:} chest X-ray or other imaging if the picture is unusual or persistent
    \item \textbf{Allergy testing:} skin prick or blood tests where food or inhalant allergy is suspected
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} symptoms, nights disturbed and reliever use are tracked over weeks to months to confirm the pattern
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management focuses on treating episodes when they occur and reducing the frequency and severity of flare-ups. A written action plan is helpful even for young children and their carers.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reliever (e.g.\ salbutamol via spacer and mask):} given during episodes to open the airways
    \item \textbf{Preventer inhaler:} a trial of daily inhaled corticosteroid may be offered for multi-trigger wheeze and is stopped if it does not help
    \item \textbf{Correct technique:} using a spacer with a well-fitted mask makes treatment far more effective in small children
    \item \textbf{Treat the intercurrent illness:} paracetamol for fever, fluids and rest as needed; antibiotics are not helpful for viral wheeze
    \item \textbf{Remove avoidable triggers:} especially tobacco smoke in the home and car
    \item \textbf{Regular medical review:} during and after each significant episode, to adjust the action plan
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} for severe episodes needing oxygen, nebulised bronchodilators or closer monitoring
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Most preschool wheeze is mild, but complications can occur, particularly with severe episodes or ongoing symptoms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe episodes requiring emergency department attendance or hospital admission
    \item Dehydration or poor weight gain during prolonged episodes with reduced feeding
    \item Disrupted sleep for the child and the whole family
    \item Repeated courses of oral corticosteroids, which can affect growth and bone density if used very often
    \item Ongoing symptoms that limit play, sport and normal childhood activity
    \item A higher chance in some children of continuing asthma in later childhood
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for preschool wheeze is generally good. Around half of children who wheeze before school age have no further symptoms by the time they start school. Episodic viral wheeze in particular tends to resolve as the airways grow and the immune system matures. Children with multi-trigger wheeze, strong allergic sensitisation, eczema or a family history of asthma are more likely to have asthma in later childhood. With good support, appropriate treatment and a clear action plan, nearly all young children with wheeze lead full, active lives.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Asthma and preschool wheeze cannot always be prevented, but parents and carers can reduce the number and severity of episodes.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep the child away from tobacco smoke, both before and after birth
    \item Breastfeed where possible, which may reduce early wheezing illness
    \item Reduce the spread of colds with good hand and cough hygiene
    \item Control dust mites, mould and pet dander if they appear to trigger symptoms
    \item Follow the doctor's plan for reliever and preventer use, using a spacer and mask correctly
    \item Keep the child's vaccinations up to date
    \item Ask about what to do if symptoms flare at night or with a cold, and rehearse the action plan
\end{itemize}
