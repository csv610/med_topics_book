\chapter{West nile virus}
\index{West nile virus}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
West Nile virus (WNV) is a globally significant, vector-borne zoonotic pathogen belonging to the genus \textit{Flavivirus} within the family \textit{Flaviviridae}. Principally maintained in an enzootic cycle between birds and mosquitoes, the virus can be transmitted to humans and other mammals, who serve as incidental, or ``dead-end,'' hosts. First isolated in 1937 from the blood of a febrile woman in the West Nile district of Uganda, WNV was historically associated with sporadic, relatively mild outbreaks in Africa, the Middle East, and parts of West Asia. However, over the past three decades, the epidemiological landscape of the virus has shifted dramatically. WNV has emerged as a major global public health concern, characterized by widespread geographic expansion and an increased frequency of severe neurological disease outbreaks.

The virus made its dramatic and unexpected entry into the Western Hemisphere in 1999 during an outbreak in New York City. This event marked the beginning of a rapid colonization of the North American continent, where it quickly became the leading cause of epidemic meningoencephalitis. Today, WNV is recognized as the most prevalent cause of arboviral disease in the United States and is endemic across much of Europe, Africa, the Middle East, and parts of Asia.

While approximately 80\% of human WNV infections remain completely asymptomatic, the remaining 20\% manifest as a mild, self-limiting febrile illness known as West Nile fever. However, in less than 1\% of infected individuals, the virus penetrates the central nervous system, leading to West Nile neuroinvasive disease (WNND). This severe manifestation includes meningitis, encephalitis, and an acute flaccid paralysis syndrome resembling poliomyelitis. WNND carries significant morbidity and mortality, particularly in elderly and immunocompromised populations, making the study of WNV pathophysiology, surveillance, and prevention a high priority for international public health organizations.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of West Nile virus is highly complex, governed by the interactions between viral lineages, vector mosquito populations, avian reservoir hosts, and environmental factors such as temperature, precipitation, and land use. The virus exhibits a wide geographic distribution, spanning Africa, Europe, the Middle East, North America, Central America, South America, West Asia, and the affected region (where a subtype known as the Kunjin virus is endemic).

In temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere, WNV transmission is highly seasonal, peaking during the hot summer and early autumn months. This seasonality aligns with the population dynamics of \textit{Culex} mosquitoes, which proliferate as temperatures rise. Warmer ambient temperatures accelerate both the replication of the virus within the vector (reducing the extrinsic incubation period) and the biting rate of the mosquitoes, thereby escalating transmission intensity. In tropical and subtropical regions, transmission can occur year-round, often correlating with rainfall patterns that create stagnant water breeding sites.

Epidemiological surveillance data demonstrates that the incidence of WNV fluctuates from year to year, showing cyclic patterns of outbreak and quiescence. In the United States, major epidemics occurred in 2002, 2003, and 2012, with the 2012 outbreak resulting in over 5,600 cases and 286 deaths. In Europe, significant increases in WNV transmission have been documented in recent years, particularly in the Mediterranean basin and southeastern Europe, driven by warming climates and changes in avian migration.

While WNV can infect individuals of any age, the demographic risk profile for severe disease is highly skewed. The risk of developing West Nile neuroinvasive disease, as well as the risk of death, increases progressively with age. Individuals over the age of 60 are at the highest risk for severe encephalitis and death, as senescence of the immune system reduces the host's ability to restrict viral replication. Other vulnerable populations include recipients of solid organ transplants, individuals undergoing active chemotherapy, patients with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV/AIDS), and those with underlying chronic comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, arterial hypertension, and chronic kidney disease. Males are slightly more frequently diagnosed with symptomatic WNV, which may reflect occupational or recreational outdoor exposures that increase mosquito contact.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral Classification and Structure}: West Nile virus is an enveloped, spherical particle approximately 50 nanometres in diameter. It possesses a single-stranded, positive-sense ribonucleic acid (RNA) genome of approximately 11,000 nucleotides. The genome encodes three structural proteins (nucleocapsid [C], membrane [M], and envelope [E]) and seven non-structural proteins (NS1, NS2A, NS2B, NS3, NS4A, NS4B, and NS5) that facilitate viral replication and host immune evasion. The envelope glycoprotein E is critical for cell receptor binding, membrane fusion, and host cell entry, and it serves as the primary target for neutralizing antibodies.
    \item \textbf{Vector Dynamics}: The primary vectors for WNV transmission are mosquitoes of the genus \textit{Culex}. The specific vector species vary by region; in North America, \textit{Culex pipiens} (predominant in northern urban areas), \textit{Culex quinquefasciatus} (predominant in southern regions), and \textit{Culex tarsalis} (predominant in western rural and agricultural areas) are the principal vectors. In Europe, Africa, and the Middle East, \textit{Culex pipiens}, \textit{Culex modestus}, and \textit{Culex univittatus} play major roles. These mosquitoes feed on infected birds, acquire the virus, and subsequently transmit it to other hosts during subsequent blood meals.
    \item \textbf{Avian Reservoirs}: Birds are the primary amplifying hosts of WNV. Members of the family Corvidae (such as crows, blue jays, and magpies) are highly susceptible, often developing high-level viraemia that is sufficient to infect feeding mosquitoes, though they frequently succumb to the infection. Other bird families, including Passeridae (house sparrows) and Turdidae (American robins), act as critical reservoirs, maintaining high viral loads without immediate mortality, thereby sustaining the transmission cycle.
    \item \textbf{Pathogenesis of Infection}: The pathophysiological cascade begins when an infected female \textit{Culex} mosquito injects WNV-containing saliva into the host's skin during a blood meal. The saliva contains immunomodulatory proteins that facilitate viral infection. The virus initially infects dermal dendritic cells (specifically Langerhans cells) and keratinocytes. These infected cells migrate to regional lymph nodes, where primary viral replication occurs. This leads to systemic dissemination via the bloodstream (primary viraemia) to peripheral organs, including the spleen, liver, and kidneys.
    \item \textbf{Mechanisms of Neuroinvasion}: In a small subset of patients, the virus crosses the blood-brain barrier (BBB) to enter the central nervous system. Several mechanisms of neuroinvasion have been proposed, including:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Direct hematogenous passage through compromised brain capillary endothelial cells, mediated by high-level viraemia.
        \item Disruption of the BBB via the systemic release of inflammatory cytokines, particularly tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-alpha) and interleukins, which increase vascular permeability.
        \item Retrograde axonal transport from infected peripheral nerves into the spinal cord and brain, particularly through olfactory or motor neurons.
        \item The ``Trojan horse'' mechanism, where the virus is carried across the BBB inside migrating, infected inflammatory monocytes and macrophages.
    \end{itemize}
    Once inside the central nervous system, WNV preferentially infects neurons, particularly in the brainstem, basal ganglia, and the anterior horn cells of the spinal cord. Neuronal damage occurs through both direct viral-induced cytopathic effects (leading to cell lysis and apoptosis) and indirect injury from the host's inflammatory response, which is characterized by microglial activation, astrogliosis, and the recruitment of cytotoxic T lymphocytes.
    \item \textbf{Non-Vector Transmission Pathways}: Although the vast majority of cases are mosquito-borne, WNV can be transmitted through other routes. These include blood transfusions, organ transplantations, transplacental transmission from mother to foetus, exposure in laboratory settings, and potentially through breast milk. Because WNV viraemia is often asymptomatic, screening of blood donations using nucleic acid testing (NAT) is mandatory in endemic countries to prevent transfusion-transmitted infections.
    \item \textbf{Host Genetic Susceptibility}: A significant genetic risk factor for symptomatic and severe WNV infection is a functional deletion in the chemokine receptor 5 gene, known as the CCR5-delta 32 mutation. Individuals homozygous for this deletion lack functional CCR5 receptors, which are crucial for directing protective T cells into the central nervous system to clear the viral infection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of West Nile virus infection ranges from completely silent to rapidly fatal. The incubation period typically spans 2 to 14 days, though it can extend up to 21 days in severely immunocompromised individuals.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asymptomatic Infection}: Approximately 80\% of individuals infected with WNV show no clinical signs of illness. These individuals develop protective antibodies without ever realizing they have contracted the virus.
    \item \textbf{West Nile Fever (WNF)}: Approximately 20\% of infected individuals develop a systemic febrile syndrome. WNF is characterized by:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Abrupt onset of high fever, often accompanied by a severe, throbbing headache and ocular pain.
        \item Marked fatigue, malaise, and profound generalized weakness that can persist for several weeks.
        \item Myalgia (muscle pain) and arthralgia (joint pain), particularly affecting the back and limbs.
        \item Gastrointestinal symptoms, including anorexia, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, and abdominal pain.
        \item A non-pruritic, maculopapular or morbilliform rash, predominantly involving the trunk, chest, and upper extremities, which typically appears during the first few days of fever and lasts for less than a week.
        \item Generalized lymphadenopathy (swollen lymph nodes).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{West Nile Neuroinvasive Disease (WNND)}: Occurring in less than 1\% of cases, WNND is a life-threatening manifestation that presents in three main clinical forms, which can overlap:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{West Nile Meningitis}: Clinically indistinguishable from other viral meningitides, presenting with fever, severe headache, nuchal rigidity (stiff neck), photophobia (sensitivity to light), and phonophobia (sensitivity to sound). Unlike encephalitis, cognitive function and mental status remain intact.
        \item \textbf{West Nile Encephalitis}: A severe parenchymal brain infection. Symptoms include altered mental status, confusion, disorientation, lethargy, stupor, or coma. Patients frequently exhibit extrapyramidal signs, such as tremors (particularly posturing or action tremors of the upper extremities), myoclonus (sudden muscle jerks), rigidity, and parkinsonian features (bradykinesia and masked facies). Seizures can also occur.
        \item \textbf{West Nile Poliomyelitis / Acute Flaccid Paralysis (AFP)}: A syndrome characterized by the sudden, rapid onset of asymmetric limb weakness or paralysis. This occurs due to the virus selectively destroying the anterior horn cells of the spinal cord. The weakness is typically flaccid, associated with hyporeflexia or areflexia, and is not accompanied by sensory loss. Involvement of the phrenic nerve can lead to diaphragmatic paralysis and acute respiratory failure, requiring immediate mechanical ventilation. AFP can occur in the absence of meningitis or encephalitis.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Due to its diverse clinical presentations, WNV infection can mimic numerous infectious and non-infectious conditions. A comprehensive differential diagnosis must be evaluated:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Other Viral Encephalitides and Meningitides}: Other arboviruses belonging to the \textit{Flaviviridae}, \textit{Togaviridae}, or \textit{Bunyaviridae} families can cause identical clinical syndromes. These include St. Louis encephalitis, Eastern equine encephalitis, Western equine encephalitis, Powassan virus, La Crosse virus, Dengue virus, and Zika virus. Additionally, non-arboviral pathogens such as Enteroviruses (coxsackieviruses and echoviruses), Herpes Simplex Virus 1 and 2 (HSV-1, HSV-2), Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV), and Cytomegalovirus (CMV) must be considered.
    \item \textbf{Bacterial Meningitis}: Acute bacterial meningitis, caused by pathogens like \textit{Streptococcus pneumoniae}, \textit{Neisseria meningitidis}, or \textit{Listeria monocytogenes}, typically presents with a more rapid onset, higher fever, and more severe meningeal signs. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis is critical to differentiate the two, as bacterial meningitis requires immediate antibiotic therapy.
    \item \textbf{Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS)}: When WNV presents with acute flaccid paralysis, it can be mistaken for GBS. However, WNV-associated paralysis is usually asymmetric, lacks the ascending pattern typical of GBS, does not feature prominent sensory loss, and is accompanied by fever and CSF pleocytosis (whereas GBS characteristically shows albuminocytologic dissociation—elevated protein without an increase in cells).
    \item \textbf{Poliomyelitis}: Caused by the poliovirus, which is now rare due to global vaccination efforts. The clinical presentation of WNV-induced AFP is virtually identical to classical poliomyelitis, necessitating serological and molecular testing to differentiate the two.
    \item \textbf{Stroke and Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA)}: In older patients presenting with sudden focal neurological deficits, altered mental status, or weakness without a clear infectious prodrome, acute ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke must be ruled out via urgent neuroimaging.
    \item \textbf{Systemic Febrile Illnesses}: In early stages or mild cases, WNV fever must be differentiated from malaria, typhoid fever, influenza, COVID-19, leptospirosis, and rickettsial infections (such as Rocky Mountain spotted fever).
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most cases of West Nile virus are mild and resolve without medical intervention. However, it is essential to monitor for signs of progression to severe neurological disease.

Individuals should consult a healthcare provider if they develop a persistent high fever, severe headache, generalized muscle aches, or a widespread skin rash, especially if they reside in or have recently travelled to an area with active mosquito transmission.

Immediate emergency medical attention must be sought if any of the following ``red flag'' symptoms occur, as they indicate potential central nervous system involvement:
\begin{itemize}
    \item High fever accompanied by a stiff neck or severe photophobia.
    \item Altered mental status, including confusion, disorientation, hallucinations, extreme lethargy, or difficulty waking up.
    \item Sudden weakness, numbness, or paralysis in any limb or face.
    \item Severe tremors, muscle jerks, or seizures.
    \item Difficulty breathing, shortness of breath, or swallowing difficulties.
\end{itemize}
For any of these life-threatening symptoms, immediately contact emergency services. Call \textbf{local emergency services} in many countries, \textbf{911} in the United States, \textbf{999} in the United Kingdom, or \textbf{112} in the European Union to secure rapid paramedic dispatch and transport to the nearest emergency department.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Confirming a diagnosis of West Nile virus requires a combination of clinical suspicion, history of potential exposure (such as travel to endemic areas or mosquito bites), and specialized laboratory investigations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Serological Testing}: The primary and most reliable method for diagnosing WNV is the detection of WNV-specific immunoglobulin M (IgM) antibodies in serum or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) using an \textbf{IgM Antibody Capture Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (MAC-ELISA)}.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Serum IgM}: Typically detectable within 3 to 8 days after the onset of clinical symptoms and can persist for up to 30 to 90 days (and occasionally longer, which can complicate the diagnosis of recent infections in subsequent seasons).
        \item \textbf{CSF IgM}: Detection in the CSF is highly indicative of central nervous system invasion because IgM antibodies are large molecules that do not readily cross the intact blood-brain barrier.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cross-Reactivity and Confirmation}: A major challenge in WNV serology is cross-reactivity with other flaviviruses (e.g., Dengue, Zika, Japanese encephalitis, or yellow fever viruses) or prior vaccinations. To confirm a positive MAC-ELISA test, a \textbf{Plaque Reduction Neutralization Test (PRNT)} is performed. The PRNT is highly specific and measures virus-specific neutralizing antibodies, serving as the gold standard for confirmation.
    \item \textbf{Molecular Diagnostics}: \textbf{Reverse Transcriptase-Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR)} assays can detect WNV RNA in blood, CSF, or tissues. While highly specific, RT-PCR has limited sensitivity in immunocompetent patients because the viraemic phase is short-lived and usually resolves by the time the patient seeks medical care. However, RT-PCR is highly valuable in immunocompromised patients who may fail to mount an antibody response, and for screening blood and organ donations.
    \item \textbf{Lumbar Puncture and CSF Analysis}: In cases of suspected neuroinvasive disease, a lumbar puncture is performed to evaluate the cerebrospinal fluid. Typical findings include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Moderate pleocytosis (usually ranging from 10 to 500 white blood cells per microlitre), with a predominance of lymphocytes, though neutrophils may predominate in the early hours of infection.
        \item Elevated protein concentration, typically between 50 and 150 mg/dL.
        \item Normal glucose concentration (distinguishing it from bacterial or fungal meningitis).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neuroimaging}: Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) of the brain and spinal cord is preferred over Computed Tomography (CT) scans. While the MRI is normal in many WNND cases, abnormal findings may include T2-weighted and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) hyperintensities in the basal ganglia, thalamus, brainstem, or the anterior horns of the spinal cord. Leptomeningeal enhancement may also be observed.
    \item \textbf{Electroencephalography (EEG)}: In patients with encephalitis, an EEG may show diffuse slowing, focal temporal slowing, or epileptiform discharges, reflecting cortical dysfunction.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Currently, there is no specific antiviral therapy or vaccine approved for human use against West Nile virus. Management is fundamentally supportive and tailored to the severity of the clinical presentation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Supportive Care for Mild Infection}: Patients with West Nile fever can be managed in the outpatient setting. Recommendations include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Rest and adequate hydration to support the immune system and prevent dehydration.
        \item Pharmacological management of fever, headache, and myalgia using an appropriate analgesic such as acetaminophen/paracetamol. Avoid aspirin in children and adolescents. Avoid ibuprofen and other NSAIDs when dengue or another bleeding risk has not been excluded, and follow local clinical advice.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inpatient Supportive Care for Neuroinvasive Disease}: Patients presenting with signs of meningitis, encephalitis, or acute flaccid paralysis require hospitalization. Treatment focuses on:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Intravenous fluids to maintain hemodynamic stability and electrolyte balance, particularly in patients experiencing severe nausea, vomiting, or altered consciousness.
        \item Airway management and mechanical ventilation in patients with severe encephalitis who cannot protect their airway, or in patients with acute flaccid paralysis involving the respiratory muscles.
        \item Monitoring and management of elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) through head elevation, hyperosmolar therapy (such as mannitol or hypertonic saline), and adequate ventilation.
        \item Administration of anticonvulsants to control seizures.
        \item Prevention of secondary complications, including deep vein thrombosis (DVT) prophylaxis, pressure ulcer prevention, and treatment of secondary bacterial infections (such as nosocomial pneumonia or urinary tract infections).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Investigational and Experimental Therapies}: Various therapies have been explored in clinical trials or used on an compassionate-use basis, though none have demonstrated clear efficacy in large-scale studies:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG)}: Specifically, WNV-high-titer IVIG derived from donors in endemic regions has been studied for its potential to provide passive immunity, particularly in immunocompromised patients.
        \item \textbf{Antiviral Agents}: Ribavirin and interferon-alpha have demonstrated in vitro activity against WNV, but clinical studies have failed to show a definitive clinical benefit.
        \item \textbf{Monoclonal Antibodies}: Humanized monoclonal antibodies targeting the WNV envelope glycoprotein are under development and evaluation in animal models and early-phase clinical trials.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
West Nile virus infection, particularly the neuroinvasive form, can result in severe and long-lasting complications that impact multiple organ systems:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Persistent Neurological Deficits}: Many survivors of West Nile encephalitis and poliomyelitis suffer from permanent neurological sequelae. These include persistent cognitive impairment (difficulties with memory, attention, and executive function), cranial nerve palsies, tremors, ataxia (lack of voluntary coordination of muscle movements), and parkinsonism.
    \item \textbf{Chronic Muscle Weakness and Fatigue}: A significant proportion of patients, even those with West Nile fever, experience a prolonged post-viral fatigue syndrome that can last for months or years. In those who developed acute flaccid paralysis, muscle weakness and atrophy are frequently permanent, leading to long-term mobility impairment and the need for assistive devices.
    \item \textbf{Respiratory Complications}: Patients with diaphragmatic or intercostal muscle paralysis may suffer from chronic respiratory insufficiency, necessitating long-term nocturnal non-invasive ventilation or permanent tracheostomy and mechanical ventilation. They are also at high risk for recurrent aspiration pneumonia.
    \item \textbf{Psychiatric Sequelae}: Survivors frequently report neuropsychiatric complications, including clinical depression, generalized anxiety, sleep disturbances, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) related to prolonged hospitalizations and ICU stays.
    \item \textbf{Renal Persistence}: Accumulating evidence suggests that WNV can persist in the kidneys of some patients for years post-infection. This persistent low-grade infection has been linked to the development of chronic kidney disease and progressive renal function decline.
    \item \textbf{Deconditioning and Contractures}: Prolonged immobility due to severe encephalitis or paralysis can lead to joint contractures, muscle wasting, deep vein thrombosis, and decubitus ulcers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of West Nile virus infection depends heavily on the clinical syndrome, the patient's age, and the presence of underlying health conditions.

For the approximately 80\% of patients who remain asymptomatic, the prognosis is excellent, with no known long-term consequences.

Among patients who develop West Nile fever, the prognosis is also generally favorable. Most individuals recover fully within a few days to a couple of weeks. However, a significant subgroup experiences profound fatigue, malaise, and headaches that persist for weeks or even months, slowly improving over time.

For West Nile neuroinvasive disease (WNND), the prognosis is guarded:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mortality}: The overall mortality rate for patients hospitalized with WNND is approximately 10\%. The risk of death is significantly higher in patients with West Nile encephalitis and acute flaccid paralysis compared to those with isolated West Nile meningitis. Mortality rises steeply with age, reaching 15\% to 20\% in elderly individuals, and is even higher in immunocompromised patients.
    \item \textbf{Morbidity}: Over 50\% of WNND survivors experience long-term physical, cognitive, and functional deficits. Recovery is often slow and incomplete. While patients with meningitis typically recover fully within a few months, those with encephalitis often have persistent cognitive and motor deficits.
    \item \textbf{Recovery from Paralysis}: The prognosis for recovery from WNV-associated acute flaccid paralysis is particularly poor. Very few patients regain full motor function, and many are left with permanent, asymmetric limb weakness or require long-term ventilatory support.
\end{itemize}
Factors associated with a poorer prognosis include advanced age (especially >65 years), severe altered mental status at presentation, the need for mechanical ventilation, immunosuppression, and pre-existing cardiovascular or neurological disease.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
In the absence of a licensed vaccine or specific antiviral treatment for humans, prevention is the primary strategy to reduce the burden of West Nile virus. Prevention focuses on avoiding mosquito bites, controlling mosquito populations, and implementing community surveillance.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Personal Protection Against Mosquito Bites}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Insect Repellents}: Apply EPA-registered insect repellents containing active ingredients shown to be effective, such as DEET, picaridin, IR3535, oil of lemon eucalyptus (OLE), or para-menthane-diol (PMD). Repellents should be applied to exposed skin and clothing according to label instructions.
        \item \textbf{Protective Clothing}: Wear long-sleeved shirts, long pants, socks, and hats when outdoors, especially during times of high mosquito activity. Clothing can also be treated with permethrin, an insecticide that repels and kills mosquitoes.
        \item \textbf{Timing Outdoor Activities}: Minimize outdoor activities during peak mosquito feeding times. The principal \textit{Culex} vectors are most active during the hours around dawn and dusk.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Environmental Control and Source Reduction}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Eliminate Standing Water}: Mosquitoes lay their eggs in stagnant water. Homeowners should regularly empty, clean, turn over, cover, or discard items that hold water, such as flower pots, birdbaths, pet water bowls, old tires, buckets, and clogged rain gutters.
        \item \textbf{Structural Barriers}: Install or repair window and door screens to prevent mosquitoes from entering residential structures. Ensure doors and windows are kept closed or screened.
        \item \textbf{Swimming Pools}: Maintain swimming pools with proper chlorination and filtration, and cover pools when not in use to prevent them from becoming breeding sites.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Community Surveillance and Control}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Public health departments conduct routine monitoring of mosquito populations, testing captured mosquitoes for the presence of WNV.
        \item Reporting dead birds (especially crows, blue jays, and birds of prey) to local health authorities can provide an early warning of viral activity in the area.
        \item Community-wide vector control measures include larviciding (applying chemicals to water to kill mosquito larvae) and adulticiding (spraying insecticides via trucks or aircraft to reduce adult mosquito populations) during outbreaks.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Self-Care and Monitoring}: Individuals who have been exposed to mosquitoes and develop mild symptoms of West Nile fever should rest, drink plenty of fluids, and monitor their temperature. They should seek immediate medical attention if they notice any worsening of symptoms or signs of neurological involvement, such as confusion, severe headache, neck stiffness, or muscle weakness.
\end{itemize}
