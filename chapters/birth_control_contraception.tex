\chapter{Contraception}
\index{Contraception}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Contraception, commonly called birth control, is the deliberate use of methods to prevent pregnancy. Options range from hormonal methods, including the pill, implant, injection, patch, ring, and hormonal intrauterine device, to non-hormonal methods such as condoms, the copper intrauterine device, and diaphragms, natural fertility-awareness methods, and permanent sterilisation. Emergency contraception is available after unprotected sex.

There is no single best method. The most suitable choice is one that is safe, effective, acceptable to the individual, and used correctly and consistently, and it may change at different stages of life.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Contraception is used by most people during their reproductive years, although patterns of use vary widely across the world.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Most common methods:} the oral contraceptive pill and condoms are among the most widely used methods worldwide
    \item \textbf{Long-acting reversible contraception:} the implant and intrauterine devices are recommended by many authorities as a first choice because of their very low failure rates
    \item \textbf{Unplanned pregnancy:} still common, frequently linked to inconsistent use, method failure, or limited access to services
    \item \textbf{Young users:} condoms are the most common first method among adolescents, often combined with hormonal methods for added protection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Contraceptive methods prevent pregnancy through distinct mechanisms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Combined hormonal methods:} the pill, patch, and ring use oestrogen plus progestogen to prevent ovulation and thicken cervical mucus
    \item \textbf{Progestogen-only methods:} the mini-pill, implant, injection, and hormonal intrauterine device thicken mucus, thin the uterine lining, and often suppress ovulation
    \item \textbf{Copper intrauterine device:} copper ions impair sperm movement and egg survival
    \item \textbf{Barrier methods:} condoms, diaphragms, and cervical caps block sperm from reaching the egg
    \item \textbf{Fertility-awareness methods:} track the fertile window and avoid unprotected sex during it
    \item \textbf{Sterilisation:} vasectomy or tubal occlusion physically prevents sperm and egg from meeting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The effects and side effects of contraception differ by method.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Combined hormonal methods commonly produce lighter, more regular, and less painful periods
    \item Progestogen-only methods often cause irregular bleeding, which usually settles over the first few months
    \item Hormonal intrauterine devices markedly reduce menstrual bleeding, and many users eventually have no period at all
    \item Copper intrauterine devices can increase bleeding and cramping during periods
    \item Condoms and other barrier methods cause no hormonal changes and are used only at the time of sex
    \item Possible hormonal side effects include breast tenderness, headaches, mood changes, and nausea
    \item Fertility-awareness methods require daily monitoring of cycle signs and do not suit everyone
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before a method is prescribed, a clinician checks for conditions that affect the choice.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Existing or possible pregnancy
    \item Unexplained vaginal bleeding, which must be investigated before starting any method
    \item High blood pressure, migraine with aura, or a past blood clot, which limit the use of combined hormonal methods
    \item Liver disease and hormone-sensitive cancers, which may preclude hormonal contraception
    \item Pelvic infection, which must be treated before intrauterine device insertion
    \item Interactions with certain medicines, including some epilepsy treatments and some antibiotics
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek advice from a general practitioner, nurse, or family planning clinic to choose a method, to change methods, or if you have missed pills, had a late injection, or experienced a broken condom and may need emergency contraception. See a clinician if a method causes troublesome side effects or if you suspect you are pregnant while using contraception.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe lower abdominal pain with a positive pregnancy test, which may indicate an ectopic pregnancy
    \item Sudden breathlessness, chest pain, or leg swelling soon after starting combined hormonal contraception
    \item Heavy bleeding, fainting, or severe pain after intrauterine device insertion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment before prescribing contraception is quick and practical.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Medical history:} age, smoking, weight, blood pressure, migraine history, menstrual history, and personal or family history of blood clots or breast cancer
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test:} performed before starting any method
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure measurement:} required before combined hormonal methods
    \item \textbf{Sexual health screening:} recommended before intrauterine device insertion and where risk is high
    \item \textbf{Pelvic examination:} sometimes needed before fitting a diaphragm or intrauterine device
    \item \textbf{Review of medicines:} to check for interactions that reduce contraceptive effectiveness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Combined pill, patch, or ring:} taken or applied on a regular schedule and reversible at any time
    \item \textbf{Progestogen-only pill (mini-pill):} taken daily without oestrogen
    \item \textbf{Implant:} a small rod placed under the skin of the upper arm, effective for several years
    \item \textbf{Injection:} progestogen given every few months
    \item \textbf{Intrauterine devices:} copper or hormonal devices fitted by a clinician, effective for 5 to 10 years
    \item \textbf{Barrier methods:} condoms, diaphragms, and cervical caps used at the time of sex
    \item \textbf{Fertility awareness:} taught by trained educators and requiring daily diligence
    \item \textbf{Sterilisation:} a permanent option for people who are certain they want no more children
    \item \textbf{Emergency contraception:} a hormonal pill or copper intrauterine device used within five days of unprotected sex
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Combined hormonal methods slightly increase the risk of venous blood clots and stroke, particularly in smokers over 35
    \item Hormonal methods can raise blood pressure in some users
    \item Intrauterine devices may cause pain and cramping and, rarely, perforation of the uterus during insertion
    \item Copper intrauterine devices can cause heavier periods and contribute to anaemia
    \item Progestogen-only methods commonly cause irregular bleeding, a frequent reason for stopping
    \item Incorrect use of barrier methods and missed pills are the main causes of method failure
    \item Sterilisation is difficult or impossible to reverse
    \item Most methods give no protection against sexually transmitted infections
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With perfect use, all the main methods are highly effective, with failure rates under 1 per cent. In everyday use, effectiveness depends heavily on consistency: long-acting methods such as the implant and intrauterine devices have failure rates close to their perfect-use rates, while the pill and condoms fail more often because of missed doses or incorrect use.

Fertility returns quickly after most methods are stopped, although the injection can delay the return of ovulation for several months. Overall, choosing a method with which a person is comfortable and can use reliably is the strongest predictor of avoiding an unplanned pregnancy.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Choose a method you can use consistently; if daily pills are difficult, consider long-acting contraception
    \item Use condoms in addition to other methods to prevent sexually transmitted infections
    \item Take the pill at a regular time and know what to do if you miss one
    \item Set reminders for injection and implant due dates
    \item Avoid combined hormonal contraception if you smoke and are over 35, unless advised otherwise
    \item Act quickly to obtain emergency contraception after unprotected sex
    \item Report side effects to your clinician and change methods if one is not suiting you
    \item Keep up with blood pressure checks and routine sexual health screening
\end{itemize}
