\chapter{Dental Care}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dental care, also called oral health care, refers to the practices and professional services that keep the teeth, gums, and mouth healthy. Good dental care prevents the two most common oral diseases: tooth decay (caries) and gum disease, both of which are largely preventable. It involves daily self-care, a healthy diet, fluoride use, and regular visits to the dentist. Oral health is closely linked to general health, and good dental care protects against pain, infection, tooth loss, and even systemic conditions. This chapter provides practical guidance on maintaining good dental health at every age.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Oral diseases are the most widespread non-communicable conditions worldwide, affecting billions of people. In Australia, around one in three children has experienced tooth decay by school age, and a majority of adults have had fillings. Gum disease affects about one in three adults, and it becomes more common with age. Most Australians visit a dentist at least once a year, but cost, distance, and anxiety prevent some people from receiving regular care. Poor oral health is more common in rural, remote, and lower-income communities and among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Plaque:} a sticky film of bacteria that constantly forms on teeth; if not removed, it causes decay and gum inflammation
    \item \textbf{Sugars:} bacteria feed on sugars from food and drink, producing acid that dissolves tooth enamel
    \item \textbf{Acid attacks:} repeated acid exposure demineralises enamel, and without fluoride and repair, cavities form
    \item \textbf{Gum disease:} plaque below the gum line causes gingivitis, which can progress to periodontitis with bone loss
    \item \textbf{Fluoride:} strengthens enamel and promotes repair of early decay
    \item \textbf{Saliva:} washes away food and neutralises acid; reduced saliva increases decay risk
    \item \textbf{Protective factors:} regular brushing, flossing, healthy diet, and professional care prevent disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Healthy gums are firm, pink, and do not bleed when brushed
    \item Signs of gum disease: bleeding on brushing, red or swollen gums, bad breath, and receding gums
    \item Signs of decay: toothache, sensitivity to sweet or cold food, visible holes, and brown or white spots
    \item Early gum disease is often painless, so people may not realise they have it
    \item Loose teeth and abscesses occur in advanced disease
    \item Regular check-ups detect problems before they cause symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Tooth decay versus staining or fluorosis, which can look similar
    \item Gingivitis versus periodontitis, distinguished by bone loss and pocket depth
    \item Toothache from decay versus pulpitis, sinus pain, or jaw joint pain
    \item Oral infections, including abscesses and ulcers
    \item Non-dental causes of mouth pain, including neuralgia and sinusitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Everyone should have a dental check-up at least once a year, or more often if recommended. See a dentist promptly for toothache, bleeding gums, sensitivity, broken teeth, or any mouth problem. Children should see a dentist when their first teeth appear, or by age one.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Facial swelling, difficulty breathing or swallowing, or swelling that spreads (possible severe dental infection)
    \item Bleeding that does not stop after dental treatment or injury
    \item A tooth knocked out with a serious facial injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dental examination:} inspection of teeth, gums, and soft tissues
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} detect decay between teeth, bone loss, and other hidden problems
    \item \textbf{Periodontal probing:} measures gum pocket depth to assess gum disease
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} review of diet, hygiene habits, fluoride exposure, and medical history
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to specialists such as periodontists, endodontists, or oral surgeons as needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Preventive care:} professional cleaning, fluoride applications, and fissure sealants for children
    \item \textbf{Treatment of decay:} fillings, crowns, root canal treatment, or extraction depending on severity
    \item \textbf{Treatment of gum disease:} scaling and root planing, improved home care, and periodontal maintenance
    \item \textbf{Restorations:} fillings, crowns, bridges, and implants to restore function and appearance
    \item \textbf{Management of dental anxiety:} gentle techniques, sedation options, and cognitive approaches
    \item \textbf{Public dental services:} subsidised care is available for eligible people, including children and concession card holders
    \item \textbf{Special populations:} care for people with disability, older adults, and those with medical conditions affecting oral health
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain, infection, and abscesses from untreated decay
    \item Tooth loss and its effects on eating, speech, and appearance
    \item Advanced gum disease, which can loosen teeth and is linked to systemic conditions such as diabetes and heart disease
    \item Malnutrition in older adults with poor dentition
    \item Low self-esteem and social effects of poor oral appearance
    \item Cost and complexity of treating advanced disease compared with prevention
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With consistent preventive care, most people can keep their natural teeth for life. Decay and gum disease are both preventable and, when caught early, easily treatable. Regular professional care combined with good home habits keeps the mouth healthy, prevents expensive and painful treatment, and supports general health. The outlook for oral health is excellent for those who adopt good habits early and maintain them.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Brush teeth twice daily with fluoride toothpaste, including last thing at night
    \item Floss or clean between teeth daily
    \item Limit sugary foods and drinks, and avoid them between meals
    \item Drink tap water, which is fluoridated in most of Australia
    \item Visit the dentist regularly for check-ups and cleaning
    \item Replace your toothbrush every three months or after illness
    \item Do not smoke, and limit alcohol
    \item Protect teeth with a mouthguard for contact sport
\end{itemize}
