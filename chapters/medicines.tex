\chapter{Medicines}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Medicines (also referred to as pharmaceuticals, pharmacotherapies, or drugs) are chemical substances or biological products designed to prevent, diagnose, treat, manage, or alleviate the symptoms of diseases and physical or mental conditions. Medicines work by interacting with specific biological targets within the body, such as receptors, enzymes, or ion channels, to modify physiological processes. In Australia, medicines are regulated and classified under the Poisons Standard into schedules, which include prescription-only medicines (Schedule 4), pharmacist-only medicines (Schedule 3), pharmacy-only medicines (Schedule 2), and unscheduled over-the-counter (OTC) medicines. Safe and effective medicine use relies on the principles of the Quality Use of Medicines (QUM), which promotes selecting the right medicine, using it safely, and achieving the best clinical outcomes.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The utilisation of medicines is extremely high in modern society. According to the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW), more than 80\% of Australians use at least one medicine in any given week, with over-the-counter analgesics, cardiovascular medications, and respiratory drugs being the most common. Polypharmacy, defined as the concurrent use of five or more medicines, affects approximately 30\% to 40\% of older Australians, significantly increasing the risk of adverse drug events. Medication-related problems are a major public health issue in Australia, causing approximately 250,000 hospital admissions annually, costing the healthcare system an estimated 1.4 billion dollars each year, with up to half of these admissions deemed preventable.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The therapeutic effects and risks associated with medicines are determined by their pharmacokinetics (what the body does to the drug) and pharmacodynamics (what the drug does to the body). Pathological and individual factors that alter these processes include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pharmacokinetics (ADME):} The processes of absorption (uptake into circulation), distribution (movement through tissues), metabolism (primarily hepatic breakdown via cytochrome P450 enzymes), and excretion (primarily renal clearance).
    \item \textbf{Renal and hepatic impairment:} Reduced kidney function (low eGFR) or liver disease (cirrhosis) impairs drug clearance, leading to accumulation and toxicity unless doses are adjusted.
    \item \textbf{Age-related physiological changes:} Neonates and older adults have altered body composition, reduced gastric acidity, and lower metabolic capacity, making them highly sensitive to medication effects.
    \item \textbf{Genetic variations (pharmacogenomics):} Genetic polymorphisms in metabolising enzymes (such as CYP2D6 or CYP2C19) or immune receptors that lead to therapeutic failure or severe adverse reactions.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Medicines present a wide range of clinical features, including their intended therapeutic effects and potential adverse drug reactions (ADRs):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Therapeutic efficacy indicators:} The resolution of clinical symptoms (e.g., reduction in pain, control of blood pressure, clearance of infection) and improvement in physiological markers.
    \item \textbf{Mild adverse drug reactions:} Common, self-limiting side effects such as mild nausea, headache, dry mouth, drowsiness, or transient gastrointestinal upset.
    \item \textbf{Hypersensitivity reactions:} Immune-mediated reactions presenting as urticaria (hives), pruritus, maculopapular rashes, or drug-induced fever.
    \item \textbf{Organ-specific toxicities:} Features of hepatic injury (jaundice, dark urine), renal impairment (oliguria, fluid retention), or myelosuppression (pallor, bruising, recurrent infections).
    \item \textbf{Withdrawal syndromes:} Physiological symptoms (e.g., rebound hypertension, anxiety, tremors) that occur upon abrupt cessation of certain medicines, such as beta-blockers or benzodiazepines.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When evaluating a patient presenting with new symptoms, it is critical to determine whether the clinical presentation is due to a new medical condition or is drug-induced:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Drug-induced cough vs. respiratory infection:} Distinguishing the dry, persistent cough caused by angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors from viral or bacterial bronchitis.
    \item \textbf{Drug-induced liver injury (DILI) vs. viral hepatitis:} Differentiating elevated liver transaminases caused by hepatotoxic medicines (e.g., high-dose paracetamol, methotrexate) from viral hepatitis or biliary obstruction.
    \item \textbf{Medication-induced cognitive impairment vs. dementia:} Distinguishing acute confusion or cognitive decline caused by anticholinergic medications, sedatives, or polypharmacy from progressive neurodegenerative dementia.
    \item \textbf{Drug-induced lupus vs. systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE):} Differentiating autoimmune symptoms caused by specific medicines (e.g., hydralazine, procainamide) from idiopathic SLE, which typically resolves after discontinuing the offending drug.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should consult their GP or pharmacist if they experience unexpected side effects, suspect their medicine is not working, or before starting any new over-the-counter or herbal remedies.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anaphylaxis:} Swelling of the tongue or throat, severe difficulty breathing, wheezing, a rapid drop in blood pressure, dizziness, or loss of consciousness.
    \item \textbf{Severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SCARS):} Widespread painful skin rashes with blistering, peeling, or mucosal involvement (indicating Stevens-Johnson syndrome or toxic epidermal necrolysis).
    \item \textbf{Acute overdose or poisoning:} Accidental or intentional ingestion of toxic amounts of medicine, presenting with respiratory depression, seizures, or coma.
    \item \textbf{Sudden severe chest pain or palpitations:} Which may indicate drug-induced cardiac arrhythmias or acute coronary syndrome.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosing medication efficacy, toxicity, or compliance requires systematic assessment and targeted investigations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM):} Measuring serum concentrations of medicines with a narrow therapeutic index (e.g., digoxin, lithium, phenytoin, gentamicin, vancomycin) to ensure safe and effective dosing.
    \item \textbf{Renal and hepatic function panels:} Baseline and serial monitoring of serum creatinine, eGFR, and liver transaminases (ALT, AST, ALP, bilirubin) for patients on long-term or high-risk therapies.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacogenomic screening:} Genetic testing to identify high-risk alleles before prescribing specific drugs, such as HLA-B*5701 testing prior to abacavir administration to prevent hypersensitivity.
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive medication review (CMR):} A structured, multi-disciplinary review of all prescribed, OTC, and complementary medicines (e.g., Home Medicines Review or HMR in Australia) to identify drug interactions and duplication.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Safe medication management involves structured prescribing, patient education, adherence aids, and interventions to address toxicity:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Quality Use of Medicines principles:} Ensuring the medicine is selected objectively based on clinical evidence, used only when necessary, and administered at the correct dose and duration.
    \item \textbf{Deprescribing:} The planned, supervised process of reducing or stopping medicines that may no longer be beneficial or may be causing harm, particularly in older patients with polypharmacy.
    \item \textbf{Adherence strategies:} Utilising dose administration aids (DAAs) such as Webster-paks, establishing consistent medication schedules, and using digital reminder applications.
    \item \textbf{Treatment of drug toxicity:} Administering specific antidotes (e.g., acetylcysteine for paracetamol overdose, naloxone for opioid overdose, flumazenil for benzodiazepine overdose) alongside active supportive care.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Improper use, drug interactions, or physiological susceptibility can lead to significant medication-related complications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adverse drug events (ADEs):} Harm resulting from medication use, including medication errors, adverse drug reactions, allergic reactions, and overdoses.
    \item \textbf{Clinically significant drug interactions:} Alterations in drug pharmacokinetics or pharmacodynamics caused by co-administered drugs, food (e.g., grapefruit juice), or herbal supplements (e.g., St John's wort).
    \item \textbf{Medication errors:} Preventable events that occur during prescribing, transcribing, dispensing, administering, or monitoring, which can lead to patient harm.
    \item \textbf{Tolerance and physical dependence:} Progressive reduction in drug effect over time (tolerance) and development of withdrawal symptoms upon cessation, common with opioids, benzodiazepines, and stimulants.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for patients managed with medicines is generally excellent when pharmacotherapy is evidence-based, monitored, and adhered to correctly. Most chronic conditions (such as hypertension, asthma, and diabetes) can be controlled effectively for decades, preventing long-term complications. However, patient outcomes are heavily compromised by non-adherence (which affects approximately 50\% of patients with chronic diseases) and adverse drug events. Mild adverse reactions typically resolve completely upon dose adjustment or discontinuation of the offending medicine, while severe toxicities or anaphylaxis require immediate hospitalisation but carry a good recovery rate if treated promptly.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Patients can take active steps to ensure their own safety and optimise the benefits of their medicines:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Keep an updated medicine list:} Maintain a comprehensive, written or digital record of all current medicines (including strengths, doses, indications, and allergies) and share it with all healthcare providers.
    \item \textbf{Understand your medicines:} Read the Consumer Medicine Information (CMI) leaflets, and ask your doctor or pharmacist about the purpose, proper administration, and common side effects of any new medicine.
    \item \textbf{Safe storage practices:} Store medicines in their original containers in a cool, dry place (avoiding bathroom cabinets due to humidity), and keep them out of reach of children.
    \item \textbf{Safe disposal of unused medicines:} Return expired, unused, or unwanted medicines to a local pharmacy for safe, free disposal through the Return Unwanted Medicines (RUM) project.
\end{itemize}
