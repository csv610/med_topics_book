\chapter{Headache Treatment}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Headache treatment aims to relieve the pain of headaches when they occur and to reduce the frequency and severity of recurrent headaches. Treatment depends on the type of headache: tension-type headaches usually respond to simple pain relief and lifestyle measures, migraines respond best to medicines taken early in the attack together with preventive treatment for frequent attacks, and cluster headaches need specialised therapy. A key principle is to avoid overusing pain-relief medicines, which can themselves cause medication-overuse headache. Most headaches are manageable, and the small number caused by serious conditions are identified by their red-flag features and treated urgently.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Headaches are among the most common reasons for using medicines. Around half of adults take pain relief for a headache at least monthly, and migraine affects about 1 in 7 Australians. Medication-overuse headache affects up to 1 in 50 people and is caused by taking simple pain relievers on more than 15 days per month, or triptans on more than 10 days. Many people with frequent headaches have never received a proper diagnosis or a preventive treatment plan, which is a major reason treatment fails.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Treatment targets the mechanisms that cause different headache types.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tension-type headache:} muscle tension and tenderness in the scalp, neck, and shoulders; treated with simple analgesics and stress, posture, and sleep management
    \item \textbf{Migraine:} a neurological event involving the trigeminal system and brain chemical changes; triptans and anti-inflammatory medicines act on the mechanisms of the attack
    \item \textbf{Preventive treatment:} daily medicines reduce the sensitivity of the brain that triggers migraines
    \item \textbf{Cluster headache:} severe attacks driven by the hypothalamus; treated with oxygen and specific acute medicines
    \item \textbf{Medication-overuse headache:} frequent analgesics make pain pathways oversensitive; stopping the medicines reverses this
    \item \textbf{Secondary headaches:} treatment of the underlying cause, such as sinusitis, high blood pressure, or infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Treatment is selected according to the type and frequency of headaches.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Infrequent tension-type headaches that respond to simple pain relief
    \item Migraine attacks with or without aura that interfere with daily life
    \item Frequent migraines, typically four or more per month, indicating a need for prevention
    \item Cluster headaches causing severe, one-sided attacks in bouts
    \item Headaches requiring pain relief on more than two to three days per week, which risks medication overuse
    \item Headaches associated with sinusitis, neck problems, or other identifiable causes
    \item Red-flag headaches requiring urgent investigation rather than routine treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before treating a headache, the type and any serious cause must be identified.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Tension-type headache versus migraine versus cluster headache
    \item Medication-overuse headache, which complicates and mimics other types
    \item Sinusitis, dental problems, and temporomandibular joint disorders
    \item Giant cell arteritis in older people, with jaw pain and visual symptoms
    \item Raised blood pressure and pre-eclampsia in pregnancy
    \item Meningitis, subarachnoid haemorrhage, and stroke, which are emergencies
    \item Brain tumours, which cause progressive headaches with neurological signs
    \item Glaucoma and other eye conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for frequent, severe, or disabling headaches; for new headaches after age 50; for headaches that have changed in character; or when treatment is not working.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A sudden, severe "thunderclap" headache
    \item Headache with fever, stiff neck, and sensitivity to light
    \item Headache with weakness, numbness, facial drooping, or difficulty speaking
    \item Headache after a head injury with confusion, vomiting, or drowsiness
    \item The worst headache of your life, or headache with seizures or collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Most headaches are diagnosed clinically, and treatment is tailored accordingly.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the pattern, location, severity, triggers, and response to previous treatment
    \item \textbf{Headache diary:} tracking attacks identifies triggers and assesses treatment response
    \item \textbf{Examination:} blood pressure, neurological examination, and assessment of the neck and sinuses
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT or MRI only when red flags or atypical features are present
    \item \textbf{Review of medicines:} identifying overuse of pain relief
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} to a neurologist for complex or treatment-resistant headache
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment combines acute medicines, preventive strategies, and lifestyle measures.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tension-type headache:} paracetamol, ibuprofen, or aspirin taken early, plus rest, hydration, and posture and stress management
    \item \textbf{Migraine:} triptans (such as sumatriptan) plus an anti-inflammatory medicine, taken at the first sign of an attack; anti-nausea medicines and rest in a dark room help
    \item \textbf{Preventive medicines:} daily medicines such as beta-blockers, candesartan, amitriptyline, or newer CGRP-blocking agents for frequent migraines
    \item \textbf{Cluster headache:} high-flow oxygen and injectable or nasal triptans for attacks, with preventive medicines during bouts
    \item \textbf{Stopping medication overuse:} reducing pain relief under medical supervision, which resolves the headache over a few weeks
    \item \textbf{Non-drug therapies:} cognitive behavioural therapy, biofeedback, relaxation, and physiotherapy for neck-related headache
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} regular sleep, meals, hydration, and exercise, and identifying and avoiding triggers
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review to assess response and adjust treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Medication-overuse headache from frequent use of pain relievers
    \item Reduced quality of life and disability from poorly controlled migraine
    \item Chronic daily headache
    \item Side effects of medicines, including triptans, which are avoided in people with certain heart conditions
    \item Side effects of preventive medicines
    \item Missed diagnosis of serious underlying conditions
    \item Dependence on pain relief
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most headaches respond well to treatment. Acute medicines stop most migraine attacks when taken early, and preventive medicines reduce attack frequency by half or more in a large proportion of people. Medication-overuse headache resolves within weeks of stopping the overused medicines. The key to good outcomes is an accurate diagnosis and a treatment plan tailored to the headache type, supported by consistent lifestyle measures and regular review. Most people with headache achieve good control and a normal quality of life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep a headache diary to identify triggers and patterns
    \item Maintain regular sleep, meals, and hydration
    \item Limit caffeine and alcohol
    \item Exercise regularly and manage stress
    \item Take acute medicines early, but limit their use to avoid medication-overuse headache
    \item See a doctor for frequent or severe headaches and for preventive treatment if needed
    \item Do not return to a medicine that is not working without reviewing the dose and timing
    \item Seek urgent care for red-flag symptoms
    \item Follow up with your doctor to fine-tune treatment
\end{itemize}
