\chapter{Hepatitis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hepatitis is inflammation of the liver. It can be caused by viral infections, alcohol, medications, autoimmune disease, fatty liver, and other factors. The most common causes worldwide are the hepatitis viruses A, B, and C, which differ in how they are transmitted, whether they cause acute or chronic infection, and how they are treated. Acute hepatitis can resolve completely, while chronic hepatitis may progress to cirrhosis, liver failure, and liver cancer. Many forms of hepatitis are preventable through vaccination, safe practices, and lifestyle measures.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Viral hepatitis affects hundreds of millions of people worldwide and causes around 1.1 million deaths each year. In Australia, hepatitis B affects around 200,000 people and hepatitis C around 100,000, with rates falling thanks to vaccination, screening, and highly effective treatments. Hepatitis A is now uncommon because of vaccination and improved sanitation, while alcohol-related and fatty liver disease are increasing. The World Health Organization has set targets to eliminate viral hepatitis as a public health threat by 2030, and Australia is on track to meet several of these goals.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hepatitis A:} a food- and water-borne virus causing acute infection, usually self-limiting
    \item \textbf{Hepatitis B:} spread by blood and body fluids, including mother-to-child transmission, sex, and needle sharing; causes both acute and chronic infection
    \item \textbf{Hepatitis C:} spread mainly through blood, historically through unsafe injecting; now curable
    \item \textbf{Hepatitis D:} occurs only with hepatitis B and makes it more severe
    \item \textbf{Hepatitis E:} water-borne in developing countries, with special risks in pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Alcohol:} toxic to liver cells, causing alcoholic hepatitis and cirrhosis
    \item \textbf{Medications and toxins:} paracetamol overdose and other drugs damage the liver
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune hepatitis:} the immune system attacks the liver
    \item \textbf{Fatty liver:} metabolic liver disease linked to obesity and diabetes
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} liver cell injury triggers inflammation; persistent injury leads to fibrosis, cirrhosis, and cancer
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Many people with viral hepatitis have no symptoms, especially early on
    \item Acute hepatitis: fatigue, nausea, poor appetite, abdominal pain, dark urine, pale stools, and jaundice
    \item Flu-like symptoms may precede jaundice
    \item Chronic hepatitis is often silent for years, with fatigue as the main symptom
    \item Advanced disease: weight loss, abdominal swelling, bruising, bleeding, and confusion
    \item Signs of cirrhosis and liver failure appear late
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The different hepatitis viruses are distinguished by blood tests
    \item Alcohol-related and fatty liver disease are common non-viral causes
    \item Medication-induced liver injury must be considered
    \item Autoimmune hepatitis, haemochromatosis, and Wilson disease are diagnosed by specific tests
    \item Gallbladder disease and other causes of jaundice
    \item Conditions causing fatigue and nausea are common and must be excluded
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with jaundice, dark urine, unexplained fatigue, or risk factors for hepatitis should be tested. Testing is recommended for pregnant women, people born in high-prevalence countries, men who have sex with men, people who inject drugs, and people with abnormal liver tests.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or personality change with liver disease
    \item Vomiting blood or passing black, tarry stools
    \item Severe abdominal pain or swelling
    \item Sudden severe jaundice with bleeding or easy bruising
    \item Severe paracetamol overdose
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} liver function tests and specific viral serology (antigens, antibodies, and viral RNA or DNA)
    \item \textbf{Hepatitis A:} IgM and IgG antibodies
    \item \textbf{Hepatitis B:} surface antigen, antibodies, and viral DNA to determine infection status and stage
    \item \textbf{Hepatitis C:} antibody screening followed by RNA testing to confirm active infection
    \item \textbf{Liver ultrasound:} assesses liver structure, fibrosis, and tumours
    \item \textbf{Liver stiffness measurement:} non-invasive assessment of fibrosis
    \item \textbf{Liver biopsy:} rarely needed but confirms the degree of damage in complex cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hepatitis A and E:} supportive care; most people recover fully without treatment
    \item \textbf{Acute hepatitis B:} supportive care, with antiviral therapy for severe cases
    \item \textbf{Chronic hepatitis B:} antiviral tablets that suppress the virus and prevent liver damage, taken long term
    \item \textbf{Hepatitis C:} short courses of direct-acting antiviral tablets cure over 95\% of people
    \item \textbf{Alcohol-related hepatitis:} abstinence, nutrition, and corticosteroids in severe cases
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune hepatitis:} immunosuppressive medication
    \item \textbf{Fatty liver:} weight loss, exercise, and metabolic control
    \item \textbf{Liver cancer surveillance:} regular scans and blood tests in people with cirrhosis
    \item \textbf{Transplantation:} for end-stage liver failure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cirrhosis: scarring that impairs liver function
    \item Liver failure with jaundice, fluid retention, bleeding, and encephalopathy
    \item Liver cancer (hepatocellular carcinoma)
    \item Fulminant hepatitis in rare acute cases
    \item Bleeding from oesophageal varices
    \item Chronic infection can be passed to others
    \item Reduced quality of life from fatigue and other symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for hepatitis has transformed in recent decades. Acute hepatitis A and E almost always resolve completely. Hepatitis C is now curable in more than 95\% of people with short oral treatment. Hepatitis B cannot be cured but is well controlled with antiviral therapy, which prevents cirrhosis and liver cancer in most people. Alcohol-related and fatty liver disease improve with abstinence and lifestyle change. Early detection and treatment prevent the most serious complications, which is why testing at-risk people is so important.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vaccination: hepatitis A and B vaccines are safe and effective and are part of the childhood schedule
    \item Practise good hand hygiene and food safety for hepatitis A
    \item Use condoms and avoid sharing needles, razors, and toothbrushes
    \item Limit alcohol and maintain a healthy weight for liver health
    \item Take paracetamol only as directed
    \item Pregnant women should be tested so babies can be protected
    \item People with chronic hepatitis should attend regular specialist follow-up
    \item Get tested if you have ever injected drugs, had blood transfusions before 1992, or have other risk factors
\end{itemize}
