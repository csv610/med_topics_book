\chapter{E. coli Infection}
\index{E. coli Infection}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Escherichia coli, usually called E. coli, is a type of bacterium that lives naturally in the intestines of humans and animals. Most strains are harmless and are part of the normal gut flora, but some strains cause illness. Disease-causing strains can cause gastroenteritis with diarrhoea, urinary tract infections, and serious complications such as kidney failure. One particularly dangerous strain, E. coli O157, produces a toxin that can cause severe bloody diarrhoea and a life-threatening condition called haemolytic uraemic syndrome. This chapter explains how E. coli infections occur, their symptoms, and how they are treated and prevented.

\section*{Epidemiology}
E. coli is the most common cause of urinary tract infections and a leading cause of bacterial gastroenteritis worldwide. The O157 strain causes outbreaks associated with contaminated food, particularly undercooked minced beef, unpasteurised milk, and contaminated salad vegetables. In many countries, most cases of E. coli gastroenteritis are sporadic, with outbreaks occurring from time to time. Young children, older people, and people with weakened immune systems are at greatest risk of severe illness.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The different disease-causing strains of E. coli harm the body in different ways.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gastroenteritis:} strains such as enterotoxigenic and enteropathogenic E. coli cause diarrhoea by releasing toxins or damaging the intestinal lining
    \item \textbf{E. coli O157 (STEC):} produces Shiga toxin, which damages the intestinal lining, causing bloody diarrhoea, and can enter the bloodstream and injure the kidneys
    \item \textbf{Urinary tract infections:} uropathogenic strains attach to the bladder wall and cause infection
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} faecal-oral spread through contaminated food, water, hands, and surfaces; animal contact is a source in farm settings
    \item \textbf{Incubation:} symptoms typically begin one to eight days after exposure, depending on the strain
    \item \textbf{Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS):} a complication of STEC infection in which damaged red cells, low platelets, and kidney injury occur together
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on the site and strain of infection.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Watery diarrhoea, which may progress to severe, bloody diarrhoea with STEC infection
    \item Abdominal cramping and pain
    \item Nausea and vomiting
    \item Fever, which is more common with some strains
    \item Urinary symptoms with a bladder infection: burning on urination, frequency, and urgency
    \item Signs of HUS: reduced urination, swelling, lethargy, and pallor, appearing about a week into the illness
    \item Dehydration, with dry mouth, reduced urine, and weakness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of diarrhoea and urinary symptoms must be considered.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Other bacterial infections, including Salmonella, Shigella, and Campylobacter
    \item Viral gastroenteritis, including rotavirus and norovirus
    \item Parasitic infections such as giardiasis
    \item Inflammatory bowel disease, which can cause bloody diarrhoea
    \item Other causes of urinary tract infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with persistent or severe diarrhoea, bloody stools, fever, or signs of dehydration should see a doctor. People with E. coli O157 should seek urgent review, because the risk of HUS requires monitoring.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of severe dehydration: confusion, collapse, no urine for many hours, or inability to keep fluids down
    \item Bloody diarrhoea with severe weakness or faintness
    \item Reduced urine output, swelling, or lethargy after a diarrhoeal illness, which may indicate HUS
    \item Seizures or fits
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Laboratory testing confirms the diagnosis and identifies the strain.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stool culture:} identifies E. coli and distinguishes the strain, including O157
    \item \textbf{Urine testing:} confirms urinary tract infection and its antibiotic sensitivities
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count and kidney function, particularly when HUS is suspected
    \item \textbf{Hydration assessment:} clinical evaluation of the degree of dehydration
    \item \textbf{Public health investigation:} notification of cases to health authorities to detect outbreaks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management depends on the type and severity of infection.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rehydration:} oral rehydration solution for gastroenteritis; intravenous fluids for severe dehydration
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics for urinary infection:} prescribed according to culture results
    \item \textbf{Caution with antibiotics in STEC:} antibiotics are generally avoided in E. coli O157 infection because they may increase the risk of HUS
    \item \textbf{Avoiding anti-diarrhoeal medicines:} these are not recommended in invasive infections
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} children with STEC infection may need monitoring for the development of HUS
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} rest, adequate fluids, and small meals as tolerated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dehydration and electrolyte disturbance, particularly in children
    \item Haemolytic uraemic syndrome, with acute kidney injury and a need for dialysis in severe cases
    \item Chronic kidney problems after HUS
    \item Spread of infection within households and communities
    \item Complications of urinary tract infection, including kidney infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most E. coli infections resolve within a week with supportive care. Urinary tract infections respond well to antibiotics. E. coli O157 is more serious: about one in ten infected people develops HUS, and while most recover, a small proportion are left with permanent kidney damage. Prompt medical care and careful monitoring give the best outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cook minced meat thoroughly and wash salad vegetables carefully
    \item Drink pasteurised milk and juices
    \item Wash hands thoroughly after the toilet, after handling raw meat, and after contact with animals
    \item Keep raw meat separate from other foods and clean utensils and surfaces
    \item Practise good food storage and refrigeration
    \item Avoid preparing food for others while you have diarrhoea
    \item Drink plenty of fluids during any diarrhoeal illness
\end{itemize}
