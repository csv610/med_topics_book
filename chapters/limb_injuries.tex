\chapter{Limb injuries}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Limb injuries encompass a broad spectrum of traumatic and overuse conditions affecting the upper and lower extremities, including the bones, joints, ligaments, tendons, muscles, nerves, and blood vessels. These injuries range from minor soft tissue contusions, strains, and sprains to severe open fractures, joint dislocations, and limb-threatening neurovascular lacerations. Clinically, limb injuries are categorised by the anatomical structure involved (skeletal vs. soft tissue) and the mechanism of injury (acute trauma vs. repetitive strain). Prompt and accurate assessment is vital, as delayed or inadequate management of severe extremity trauma can lead to permanent functional impairment, chronic pain, joint instability, or catastrophic complications such as compartment syndrome or limb loss.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Limb injuries are exceptionally common across all age groups, presenting a major public health and economic burden globally. In Australia, musculoskeletal trauma represents one of the leading causes of emergency department presentations and hospital admissions. The epidemiology varies significantly by age and lifestyle: young adults and athletes are predisposed to sports-related ligamentous sprains (e.g. ACL tears), muscle strains, and joint dislocations, whereas older adults, particularly those with underlying osteopenia or osteoporosis, frequently present with fractures (e.g. neck of femur or distal radius fractures) resulting from low-energy falls. Paediatric populations commonly experience greenstick or growth plate (Salter-Harris) fractures due to their developing skeleton. Occupational hazards, motor vehicle accidents, and domestic falls are the leading environmental contributors to extremity injuries nationwide.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The development of limb injuries is driven by mechanical forces exceeding the structural limits of extremity tissues, compounded by biological and environmental risk factors.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Direct high-energy trauma:} Motor vehicle collisions, falls from heights, or heavy machinery accidents that cause high-impact skeletal fractures, severe crush injuries, or traumatic amputations.
    \item \textbf{Indirect rotational forces:} Sudden twisting movements or rapid deceleration during athletic activities, causing joint dislocations or ligamentous ruptures (e.g. sprained ankles or knee ligament tears).
    \item \textbf{Repetitive microtrauma:} Overuse and chronic mechanical load exceeding tissue recovery capacity, leading to stress fractures, tendinopathies (e.g. Achilles tendinopathy), or bursitis.
    \item \textbf{Ageing and bone demineralisation:} Osteoporosis and osteopenia that reduce the threshold force required to cause a fracture, making simple mechanical falls highly hazardous.
    \item \textbf{Neurovascular compromise:} High-impact forces or bone fragments lacerating or compressing major peripheral nerves (e.g. radial nerve in humeral shaft fractures) or arteries, leading to distal ischaemia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The signs and symptoms of a limb injury vary according to the severity and the specific anatomical structures compromised.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pain and tenderness:} Localised pain that is typically acute, sharp, and exacerbated by movement, weight-bearing, or palpation.
    \item \textbf{Swelling and ecchymosis:} Rapid accumulation of fluid or blood in the surrounding soft tissues, appearing as visible swelling and bruising.
    \item \textbf{Deformity:} Visual asymmetry, shortening, or abnormal angulation of the extremity, indicating a displaced fracture or joint dislocation.
    \item \textbf{Loss of function:} Inability to bear weight on the lower limb or perform normal active range of motion in the upper limb.
    \item \textbf{Neurovascular deficits:} Numbness, tingling, cool skin temperature, pallor, or absent distal pulses, signalling compromised nerve conduction or arterial blood flow.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Differentiating the exact nature of the injury is critical to direct appropriate orthopaedic, surgical, or conservative management.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fracture vs. severe sprain:} Distinguishing a disruption in bone continuity from a complete ligamentous tear, which often present with identical swelling and inability to bear weight (necessitating radiographic assessment).
    \item \textbf{Joint dislocation vs. subluxation:} Identifying complete articular separation requiring urgent reduction versus partial displacement that may self-reduce.
    \item \textbf{Compartment syndrome vs. normal postoperative pain:} Differentiating the ischaemic, excruciating pain of elevated myofascial compartment pressure (unresponsive to opioids) from expected post-injury or post-surgical pain.
    \item \textbf{Cellulitis vs. acute joint inflammation:} Distinguishing a spreading superficial bacterial infection from traumatic synovitis or gouty arthritis in a swollen limb.
    \item \textbf{Deep vein thrombosis vs. muscle strain:} Differentiating a venous blood clot in the calf from a gastrocnemius muscle tear, both presenting with localised calf pain and swelling.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Minor soft tissue injuries may be managed initially at home, but medical evaluation is advised if pain is severe, weight-bearing is impossible, or symptoms fail to improve. Urgent emergency care is required for suspected fractures, dislocations, or signs of neurovascular compromise.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Open fracture:} A fracture where the bone has broken through the skin or there is a deep wound exposing bone.
    \item \textbf{Suspected compartment syndrome:} Severe, escalating limb pain out of proportion to the injury, accompanied by a tense, swollen feeling, numbness, or pale, cold skin.
    \item \textbf{Signs of vascular compromise:} A cold, pale, or blue limb, or loss of sensation and movement distal to the injury site.
    \item \textbf{Traumatic amputation:} Complete or partial separation of a limb or digit from the body.
    \item \textbf{High-energy multi-trauma:} Extremity injuries associated with head, neck, chest, or abdominal trauma.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnostic workup aims to identify structural damage, evaluate neurovascular integrity, and guide definitive intervention.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Plain radiography (X-ray):} The primary imaging modality, taking at least two perpendicular views (anteroposterior and lateral) to confirm fractures or dislocations.
    \item \textbf{Computed tomography (CT) scan:} Used for complex intra-articular fractures (e.g. tibial plateau or calcaneus) to evaluate three-dimensional bone fragmentation and assist surgical planning.
    \item \textbf{Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):} The modality of choice for assessing soft tissue details, including ligamentous tears, meniscal injuries, tendon ruptures, and occult stress fractures.
    \item \textbf{Duplex ultrasound:} Performed when deep vein thrombosis or acute arterial injury is suspected in a swollen or ischaemic extremity.
    \item \textbf{Bedside compartment pressure measurement:} Needle manometry to measure intracompartmental pressure if compartment syndrome is clinically suspected but uncertain.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is tailored to the severity and type of injury, spanning immediate first aid, conservative therapy, and surgical reconstruction.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PRICE protocol:} Rest, Ice (for 15--20 minutes every 2--4 hours), Compression (using a supportive bandage), and Elevation (above the level of the heart) to control swelling.
    \item \textbf{Immobilisation:} Application of splints, casts, or orthopaedic braces to stabilise fractures or injured joints, reduce pain, and facilitate healing.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological management:} Use of analgesics including paracetamol, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) for pain and inflammation, and short-term oral opioids for severe pain.
    \item \textbf{Closed or open reduction:} Realigning displaced fractures or dislocated joints, either manually under sedation (closed) or surgically (open).
    \item \textbf{Surgical stabilisation:} Internal fixation using plates, screws, intramedullary nails, or external fixators to secure unstable fractures.
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation and physiotherapy:} Early mobilisation protocols, muscle-strengthening exercises, and gait retraining to restore range of motion and prevent joint stiffness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute compartment syndrome:} A surgical emergency where tissue pressure within a closed fascial space rises, restricting blood flow and causing tissue necrosis.
    \item \textbf{Osteomyelitis or soft tissue infection:} Bacterial infection of the bone or surrounding tissue, particularly common after open fractures.
    \item \textbf{Non-union or mal-union:} Failure of a fracture to heal (non-union) or healing in an anatomically incorrect alignment (mal-union).
    \item \textbf{Post-traumatic osteoarthritis:} Accelerated wear and tear of joint cartilage following intra-articular fractures or chronic joint instability.
    \item \textbf{Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS):} A chronic pain condition characterised by severe, burning pain, swelling, and vasomotor changes in the affected limb.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of limb injuries is highly variable, depending on the severity of the trauma, the specific structures involved, patient age, comorbidities, and the promptness of treatment. Minor soft tissue sprains and simple, non-displaced fractures generally carry an excellent prognosis, with most patients returning to baseline function within 6 to 12 weeks. High-energy injuries, open fractures, and injuries with neurovascular involvement require extensive rehabilitation and may result in permanent functional limitations, stiffness, or chronic pain. Consistent adherence to physiotherapy and gradual return to weight-bearing are major determinants of successful recovery and optimal long-term functional outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Engage in regular strengthening:} Incorporating resistance training and balance exercises to support joint stability and reduce fall risks.
    \item \textbf{Use appropriate safety gear:} Wearing protective equipment, such as helmets, shin guards, and wrist guards during sports or high-risk activities.
    \item \textbf{Wear supportive footwear:} Choosing shoes appropriate for the specific activity to prevent slips, trips, and ankle sprains.
    \item \textbf{Maintain adequate bone health:} Consuming a diet rich in calcium and vitamin D, and engaging in weight-bearing exercises to maintain bone density.
    \item \textbf{Apply ergonomics at work:} Adjusting work environments and using proper lifting techniques to minimise repetitive strain injuries.
\end{itemize}
