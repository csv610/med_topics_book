\chapter{Adeno-tonsillectomy in Children}
\index{Adeno-tonsillectomy in Children}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An adeno-tonsillectomy is an operation to remove both the tonsils and the adenoids in a child. It is usually performed to treat recurrent or severe tonsil infections or to relieve blockage of the airway caused by enlarged tonsils and adenoids, such as in obstructive sleep apnoea.

The operation is performed under a general anaesthetic, generally through the mouth, and most children go home the same day or the following day. Recovery takes one to three weeks, and the procedure is one of the safest and most successful operations performed in childhood.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Adeno-tonsillectomy is one of the most common operations in childhood. It is most often performed between the ages of about 3 and 7 years, when the tonsils and adenoids are largest relative to the size of the airway.

In recent decades the main reason for surgery has shifted from recurrent infection towards obstructive sleep apnoea, which can affect behaviour, growth, and learning. Boys are affected by obstructive sleep apnoea somewhat more often than girls, and surgery rates also reflect the availability of specialists and sleep studies.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The tonsils and adenoids are immune tissue that normally help to fight infection, but they can cause problems when they become repeatedly infected or enlarged. Frequent infections leave scar tissue that makes it harder to clear infection, so episodes of tonsillitis tend to recur.

Large tonsils and adenoids can also block the throat and the back of the nose, narrowing the airway during sleep. This can cause snoring, pauses in breathing (apnoea), poor-quality sleep, and, in some children, long-term effects on growth, behaviour, and concentration. Removing the tissue relieves the obstruction and removes the reservoir for repeated infection.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Before surgery, children commonly have:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Recurrent infection:} sore throats or tonsillitis with fever, repeated over the year
    \item \textbf{Snoring:} loud, persistent snoring with restless sleep
    \item \textbf{Breathing pauses:} witnessed pauses in breathing during sleep (obstructive sleep apnoea)
    \item \textbf{Mouth breathing:} especially at night, with a dry mouth in the morning
    \item \textbf{Swallowing problems:} difficulty swallowing or a muffled voice
    \item \textbf{Ear problems:} recurrent ear infections or glue ear with hearing difficulties
\end{itemize}

After surgery:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sore throat:} pain that may last up to two weeks
    \item \textbf{Ear pain:} referred pain in the ears is common
    \item \textbf{Slight fever:} mild temperature in the first day or two
    \item \textbf{Healing coating:} a white or yellow layer where the tonsils were is normal
    \item \textbf{Voice change:} temporary changes and some difficulty swallowing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The decision for surgery must weigh whether symptoms are really due to the tonsils and adenoids:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Viral throat infections, which resolve on their own
    \item Allergic rhinitis causing mouth breathing and snoring
    \item Obesity-related obstructive sleep apnoea
    \item Asthma or other respiratory conditions
    \item Enlarged lymph nodes from other causes
    \item Craniofacial conditions affecting the airway
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the surgical team promptly if, after the operation, the child:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Has any fresh bleeding from the mouth or nose
    \item Cannot drink enough fluid or is showing signs of dehydration
    \item Has a fever, increasing pain, or a foul smell from the mouth
    \item Has difficulty breathing or very noisy breathing
    \item Is unusually drowsy or difficult to wake
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Any bright red bleeding from the mouth, even if small
    \item Difficulty breathing or choking
    \item Severe drowsiness, confusion, or loss of consciousness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the pattern of sore throats, infections, sleep, and breathing symptoms
    \item \textbf{Examination:} inspection of the throat, tonsils, and nose
    \item \textbf{Polysomnography:} a sleep study to confirm and grade obstructive sleep apnoea
    \item \textbf{Nasendoscopy:} a small camera to view the adenoids and airway
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} rarely needed, except where bleeding risk is a concern
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative assessment:} review of general health and fitness for anaesthetic
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} removal of the tonsils and adenoids under a general anaesthetic
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} regular paracetamol and ibuprofen for the first week or two
    \item \textbf{Encourage fluids:} drinking and eating a soft diet helps healing and reduces pain
    \item \textbf{Rest:} staying home from school and avoiding strenuous play for about two weeks
    \item \textbf{Post-operative review:} a follow-up appointment to check healing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding after surgery, the most serious complication, occurring in roughly 1 in 100 to 1 in 200 children
    \item Dehydration from pain and poor drinking
    \item Infection, which can cause a throat infection after surgery
    \item Ear pain and temporary voice changes
    \item Very rarely, breathing problems or injury to teeth, lips, or other structures
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For most children the operation is very successful. Children with obstructive sleep apnoea usually sleep better, snore less, and often show improvements in behaviour and concentration. Children with recurrent tonsillitis typically have far fewer episodes of sore throat in the following years.

Most children recover fully within two to three weeks, and serious complications are rare. Families should be alert to bleeding, which is most likely in the first 24 hours and again around 7 to 10 days after surgery when the healing scabs separate.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Give pain relief regularly, including before meals, to encourage eating and drinking
    \item Offer cool, soft foods and plenty of fluids after surgery
    \item Keep the child away from crowds and from people with colds during recovery
    \item Avoid hot or scratchy foods for the first week
    \item Contact the team promptly with any concerns about bleeding or pain
    \item Follow the surgeon's advice about school and activity
\end{itemize}
