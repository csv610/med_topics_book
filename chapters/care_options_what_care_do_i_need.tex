\chapter{Care Options: What Care Do I Need}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
This chapter explains the range of health care options available in the community and in hospital, and how to decide which level of care is right for a particular problem. Care is organised along a spectrum, from looking after yourself at home, through care from a general practitioner (GP) or community pharmacist, to specialist, hospital, and emergency care. Options also include allied health services such as physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and podiatry, mental health services, home-based nursing care, residential aged care, and palliative care. Understanding these options helps people seek the right care at the right time, avoid unnecessary delay, and use health services efficiently.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Most health problems are managed outside hospital. In many countries, the majority of people visit a GP at least once a year, and a large share of the population uses a pharmacy, allied health service, or community health programme at some point. Emergency departments see very large numbers of patients, although many attendances are for problems that could have been managed by a GP or by self-care. Use of hospital and emergency services rises with age and with chronic illness. Older adults, people with multiple long-term conditions, and those living alone are more likely to need community and residential support services. Access to care also varies with location, income, and health insurance, which influences which options are realistically available.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The level of care a person needs depends on the type, severity, and urgency of the illness or injury, rather than on a single underlying cause. Factors that influence the decision include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The nature of the problem:} minor and self-limiting compared with serious and potentially life-threatening
    \item \textbf{How rapidly symptoms develop:} gradual onset compared with sudden and severe
    \item \textbf{The person's age, frailty, and general health,} including chronic conditions
    \item \textbf{Immune status, pregnancy, or recent surgery,} which lower the threshold for hospital care
    \item \textbf{Whether the person can safely be monitored and treated at home,} including access to transport and family support
    \item \textbf{What treatments are needed:} for example, oral medicines and simple dressings can usually be given at home, while intravenous therapy or surgery requires hospital
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Different care settings are appropriate at different points in an illness:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Self-care:} minor coughs and colds, small wounds, mild aches and pains, and everyday symptom management
    \item \textbf{Pharmacy:} advice on over-the-counter medicines, minor ailments, and repeat prescriptions
    \item \textbf{GP:} new or worsening symptoms, ongoing health problems, prescriptions, referrals, and routine health checks
    \item \textbf{Allied health:} physiotherapy for injuries, podiatry, dietetics, and speech therapy as needed
    \item \textbf{Emergency or urgent care:} injuries, severe pain, breathing difficulty, bleeding, or sudden serious symptoms
    \item \textbf{Community and residential services:} nursing care, personal care, respite, and aged care for people who need ongoing support
    \item \textbf{Palliative care:} comfort and symptom control for people with advanced, incurable illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Choosing a care option is not a medical diagnosis, but the choice can be complicated by overlapping symptoms and services:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain: angina compared with musculoskeletal pain, anxiety, or indigestion -- these point to very different care settings
    \item Shortness of breath: asthma compared with heart failure, pneumonia, or panic
    \item Abdominal pain: gastroenteritis compared with appendicitis, constipation, or gallstones
    \item Headache: tension headache compared with migraine, meningitis, or stroke
    \item Back pain: muscle strain compared with disc prolapse, kidney infection, or emergency conditions such as an aortic problem
\end{itemize}
When the cause is unclear, it is safest to be assessed by a doctor rather than to assume a minor diagnosis at home.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical attention if symptoms are new, severe, persistent, or worsening, or if they are interfering with daily life, and particularly if the person is very young, very old, frail, pregnant, or has a weakened immune system.
\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, difficulty breathing, or severe bleeding
    \item Sudden weakness, numbness, confusion, or difficulty speaking
    \item Loss of consciousness, a seizure, or anaphylaxis
    \item A sudden, severe headache or abdominal pain
    \item Any sudden, severe symptom that worries you
\end{itemize}
For less urgent problems, a telehealth service, community pharmacist, or GP can advise on the most appropriate option.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Determining which care option is appropriate involves assessment rather than laboratory testing:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Triage:} nurses and doctors assess urgency, severity, and risk to direct people to the right service
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} history and examination to judge whether the problem can be safely managed at home
    \item \textbf{Vital signs:} temperature, heart rate, breathing rate, and blood pressure to identify serious illness
    \item \textbf{Investigations as needed:} for example, urine testing, blood tests, or X-rays arranged by the treating clinician
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} age, chronic illness, medications, and social circumstances are considered when deciding between admission and home care
    \item \textbf{Referral pathways:} letters and plans that move care between GP, specialist, allied health, and community services
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management depends on the care option chosen:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Self-care:} rest, fluids, simple first aid, and appropriately used over-the-counter medicines
    \item \textbf{Primary care:} diagnosis, prescriptions, referrals, health screening, and ongoing management of chronic conditions
    \item \textbf{Medication management:} prescription and over-the-counter medicines, with review by a pharmacist for safety
    \item \textbf{Allied health:} physiotherapy, occupational therapy, dietetics, podiatry, and other therapies
    \item \textbf{Community services:} district nursing, home care packages, respite, and meals and transport services
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} emergency treatment, surgery, and specialist management of serious illness
    \item \textbf{Residential and palliative care:} supported living, personal care, and end-of-life symptom management
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Choosing the wrong level of care can have consequences:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Delay in a serious condition, such as appendicitis, stroke, or sepsis, leading to worse outcomes
    \item Overuse of emergency departments for problems better managed in primary care, adding cost and wait times
    \item Missed opportunities for prevention, screening, or early treatment when care is not accessed
    \item Financial burden and anxiety from unnecessary or avoidable hospitalisation
    \item Medication errors when medicines are started or changed without medical review
    \item Fragmented care when services do not communicate, leading to duplication or gaps
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Outcomes depend on the underlying problem and on how promptly and appropriately care is accessed. Many common, self-limiting illnesses resolve with self-care or primary care alone. Early presentation for serious symptoms, such as chest pain or stroke-like features, is associated with substantially better outcomes. Most people who need community or residential support find that coordinated care improves safety, comfort, and quality of life. Ongoing engagement with a regular GP is associated with better management of chronic disease and fewer hospital admissions.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain routine health checks, screening, and vaccinations through your regular GP
    \item Manage chronic conditions such as diabetes, asthma, and high blood pressure with a written care plan
    \item Learn to recognise warning signs that need urgent care, and keep emergency numbers accessible
    \item Build a relationship with a regular GP and a preferred pharmacy for continuity of care
    \item Use telehealth and pharmacist advice for minor problems before attending an emergency department
    \item Plan ahead for after-hours care, including knowing local services and transport options
    \item Make an advance care plan and discuss your preferences for care with family and doctors
    \item Keep a current list of medicines, allergies, and past medical history to share with any health service
\end{itemize}
