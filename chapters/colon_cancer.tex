\section*{Definition and Overview}
Colon cancer is a cancer that starts in the large intestine, specifically the colon, which is the main part of the bowel between the small intestine and the rectum. It usually develops slowly from benign growths called polyps that form on the inner lining of the bowel, and a small proportion of these polyps become cancerous over a number of years. Colon cancer and rectal cancer are often grouped together as colorectal cancer. The disease is very treatable when found early, and screening aims to detect polyps and early cancers before they cause symptoms. Advanced cancer can spread to the liver, lungs, and other organs.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Colon cancer is one of the most common cancers worldwide and the second most common cause of cancer death in many countries. Around one in 20 people will be diagnosed with bowel cancer during their lifetime. The risk rises steeply with age, and most cases occur in people over 50 years, although rates in younger adults have been increasing. Men are affected slightly more often than women. The disease is more common in developed countries, which is thought to reflect diet and lifestyle. Family history and inherited conditions account for a small proportion of cases. Survival has improved steadily with screening, earlier diagnosis, and better treatments.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Colon cancer arises when cells in the lining of the bowel accumulate genetic damage that allows them to grow out of control. Most cancers develop through the adenoma--carcinoma sequence, in which a benign polyp progressively acquires mutations and becomes an invasive cancer over many years. Factors that increase risk include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Age:} risk rises markedly after age 50
    \item \textbf{Diet and lifestyle:} a diet high in processed and red meat, low in fibre, together with obesity, physical inactivity, smoking, and heavy alcohol use
    \item \textbf{Family history:} close relatives with colon cancer or polyps increase risk
    \item \textbf{Inherited syndromes:} conditions such as Lynch syndrome and familial adenomatous polyposis cause a high lifetime risk
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory bowel disease:} long-standing ulcerative colitis or Crohn disease
    \item \textbf{Diabetes:} people with type 2 diabetes appear to have a modestly increased risk
\end{itemize}

Cancer cells can invade through the bowel wall, spread to nearby lymph nodes, and travel through the bloodstream to the liver, lungs, and elsewhere.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Early colon cancer often causes no symptoms, which is why screening is important. When symptoms do occur, they can include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A change in bowel habit lasting more than a few weeks, such as looser stools, more frequent stools, or constipation
    \item Blood in the stool or bleeding from the bottom
    \item Unexplained weight loss
    \item Tiredness and paleness from anaemia caused by slow, hidden bleeding
    \item Abdominal pain, bloating, or a feeling of incomplete emptying after a bowel motion
    \item A lump in the abdomen in more advanced disease
    \item Bowel obstruction, causing severe pain, vomiting, and inability to pass wind or stool
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of colon cancer can be caused by many more common conditions, including:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Haemorrhoids, a very common cause of rectal bleeding
    \item Irritable bowel syndrome, which causes changes in bowel habit and abdominal pain
    \item Diverticular disease, pockets in the bowel wall that can bleed or become inflamed
    \item Inflammatory bowel disease such as ulcerative colitis or Crohn disease
    \item Benign polyps of the bowel
    \item Anal fissures, which cause bleeding and pain on passing stool
    \item Gastroenteritis or infections that cause temporary bowel symptoms
    \item Cancer of the rectum, which shares features but starts lower in the bowel
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor promptly if you notice blood in your stool, a change in bowel habit that persists for more than two to three weeks, unexplained weight loss, or persistent abdominal pain. These symptoms are more likely to be caused by something other than cancer, but they always need checking.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain with vomiting and an inability to pass wind or stool (possible bowel obstruction)
    \item Heavy bleeding from the bowel
    \item Collapse, severe dizziness, or signs of serious illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} questions about symptoms, risk factors, and family history, followed by examination including a rectal examination
    \item \textbf{Faecal occult blood test:} a home test that detects hidden blood in the stool, used for screening
    \item \textbf{Colonoscopy:} a thin, flexible camera is passed through the bowel; polyps can be removed and biopsies taken during the same procedure
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} a sample of tissue examined under the microscope confirms the diagnosis and grade
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count for anaemia, liver function tests, and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), a tumour marker used to monitor treatment
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis to stage the cancer and look for spread
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} offered when a hereditary syndrome is suspected, particularly for younger people or those with a strong family history
    \item \textbf{Staging:} the extent of the cancer (its size, lymph node involvement, and spread) is classified after surgery and imaging
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on the stage and location of the cancer, and is planned by a multidisciplinary team that includes a surgeon, oncologist, and other specialists:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} the main treatment for early colon cancer, removing the section of bowel containing the tumour and nearby lymph nodes, and rejoining the bowel
    \item \textbf{Polypectomy:} removal of polyps during colonoscopy can be curative for very early cancers confined to a polyp
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy:} used after surgery when there is a risk of the cancer returning, and for advanced cancer
    \item \textbf{Targeted therapy:} newer medicines that act on specific features of the cancer cells, often combined with chemotherapy
    \item \textbf{Immunotherapy:} used for cancers with particular genetic features, such as mismatch repair deficiency
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} more often used for rectal cancer than colon cancer, before or after surgery
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} symptom control, nutrition, and psychological support at all stages of the illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bowel obstruction, caused by the tumour narrowing the bowel
    \item Bleeding from the tumour, which can cause anaemia or, rarely, heavy bleeding
    \item Perforation of the bowel, a surgical emergency
    \item Spread (metastasis) to the liver, lungs, or other organs
    \item Complications of surgery, such as infection, bleeding, or leaking of the bowel join
    \item Side effects of chemotherapy, including fatigue, nausea, and nerve damage
    \item Psychological effects, including anxiety and depression after diagnosis and treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Outcomes for colon cancer depend strongly on the stage at diagnosis. When found early and confined to the bowel lining, the cancer is often cured by surgery alone, with five-year survival well above 90\% for the earliest stages. Survival falls as the cancer spreads, but treatment for advanced disease continues to improve. Roughly half of people with colon cancer will be alive five years after diagnosis overall, and this figure has risen steadily. Regular follow-up after treatment, including colonoscopy and blood tests, detects recurrence early. Many people live with the disease or return to full health after treatment.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take part in the bowel-cancer screening programme available where you live; eligibility, test type, and interval vary by country
    \item Eat a diet rich in fibre, vegetables, fruit, and whole grains
    \item Limit red meat and avoid processed meats
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and be physically active
    \item Limit alcohol and do not smoke
    \item See a doctor promptly about bleeding or changes in bowel habit
    \item If you have a family history or inflammatory bowel disease, ask your doctor about earlier or more frequent screening
    \item Attend all follow-up appointments after treatment for polyps or cancer
\end{itemize}
