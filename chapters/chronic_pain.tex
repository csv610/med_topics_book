\chapter{Chronic Pain}
\index{Chronic Pain}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Chronic pain is pain that persists beyond the expected time for healing -- usually three months or more -- or pain that continues after the underlying injury or illness has resolved. It differs from acute pain, which is a normal, short-lived warning signal of injury or disease. Chronic pain serves no useful protective purpose and becomes a condition in its own right.

Chronic pain may be continuous or come and go, and it can range from mild to severe. It affects far more than the site of the pain: it commonly disturbs sleep, mood, energy, and the ability to work and socialise. Because the nervous system changes over time, chronic pain is a complex condition that usually needs a broad, multi-pronged approach rather than a simple cure.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Chronic pain is one of the most common reasons people consult doctors and is a leading cause of disability worldwide. Studies suggest that roughly one in five adults lives with chronic pain, and the proportion rises with age, so that about a third of older adults are affected. Chronic pain is somewhat more common in women than in men.

Common types include back pain, arthritis, headache and migraine, and nerve pain (neuropathic pain). Chronic pain is more frequent among people with lower incomes, in those with physically demanding jobs, and in people living with mental health conditions, obesity, or multiple chronic illnesses. Its economic and personal costs are very large, including lost work, healthcare use, and reduced quality of life.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Chronic pain can start for many reasons, and in many cases the original trigger is no longer identifiable:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nociceptive pain:} from ongoing tissue damage or inflammation, for example arthritis, or an old injury that has not fully healed
    \item \textbf{Neuropathic pain:} from damage or disease of the nerves themselves, such as diabetic neuropathy, shingles, or a trapped nerve
    \item \textbf{Post-surgical or post-injury pain:} pain that persists after healing, sometimes because nerves were damaged during the procedure or accident
    \item \textbf{Central sensitisation:} the spinal cord and brain become more sensitive, so that ordinary signals are amplified and even light touch can hurt
    \item \textbf{Changes in the pain system:} after repeated pain, the nervous system adapts, and pain becomes maintained by the system itself rather than by ongoing damage
    \item \textbf{Psychological and social factors:} stress, low mood, fear of movement, and poor sleep can all amplify pain and make it harder to live with
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Chronic pain presents in many ways, but common features include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain lasting more than three months, in one or several parts of the body
    \item Pain that is described as aching, burning, stabbing, shooting, or electric-like
    \item Pain made worse by certain activities, weather, stress, or fatigue
    \item Sleep disturbance and waking unrefreshed
    \item Fatigue and low energy
    \item Low mood, irritability, or anxiety
    \item Reduced physical activity and muscle deconditioning
    \item Difficulty concentrating (sometimes called brain fog)
    \item Interference with work, relationships, and daily activities
    \item Sensitivity in the painful area, including to light touch (allodynia)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because chronic pain is a symptom rather than a diagnosis, doctors look for underlying causes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Arthritis and other musculoskeletal conditions (osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, gout)
    \item Back and neck problems, including disc disease, spinal stenosis, and nerve root compression
    \item Neuropathic conditions (diabetic neuropathy, post-herpetic neuralgia, sciatica)
    \item Migraine and other primary headache disorders
    \item Fibromyalgia and other central sensitisation syndromes
    \item Chronic regional pain syndrome
    \item Inflammatory, autoimmune, or rheumatic diseases
    \item Malignancy, when pain is new, severe, or progressive -- although most chronic pain is not due to cancer
    \item Referred pain from organs (e.g.\ heart, gallbladder, kidneys)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone whose pain has lasted more than three months, is not improving, or is interfering with daily life should see a doctor. It is also important to seek review if a chronic pain pattern changes, becomes much worse, or develops new features, since this may signal a new problem rather than the old one.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe chest pain or difficulty breathing
    \item Severe pain with collapse, fainting, or confusion
    \item Sudden severe abdominal pain
    \item Pain with signs of a serious injury, such as deformity or inability to move a limb
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Chronic pain is assessed mostly through a careful history and examination, with tests used to look for treatable underlying causes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detailed history:} where the pain is, how it feels, when it started, what makes it better or worse, and how it affects sleep, mood, work, and relationships
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} assessment of movement, strength, reflexes, sensation, and tenderness
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} inflammatory markers, full blood count, thyroid function, vitamin D, and tests for conditions such as diabetes or gout where indicated
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} X-ray, ultrasound, CT, or MRI to look for structural causes
    \item \textbf{Nerve conduction studies:} where nerve damage is suspected
    \item \textbf{Pain and mood questionnaires:} to measure the impact of pain and screen for depression and anxiety
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} to a pain clinic, rheumatologist, neurologist, or other specialist when the cause is unclear
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Chronic pain is best managed with a combination of approaches, usually including self-care and often involving several health professionals:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Self-management and pacing:} balancing activity and rest, and learning to do things within limits rather than pushing through
    \item \textbf{Exercise:} graded physical activity and physiotherapy improve function and reduce pain for many conditions
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} simple analgesics, anti-inflammatories, medicines for nerve pain (such as certain antidepressants and anticonvulsants), and topical preparations -- prescribed by a doctor with careful review
    \item \textbf{Psychological therapies:} cognitive behaviour therapy and acceptance and commitment therapy help people live better with pain
    \item \textbf{Procedures:} nerve blocks, injections, or other interventions in selected cases, usually through a pain clinic
    \item \textbf{Sleep and mood support:} treating insomnia and depression often reduces pain
    \item \textbf{Complementary approaches:} some people find acupuncture, massage, or mindfulness helpful alongside medical care
    \item \textbf{Opioids:} only occasionally appropriate, under strict medical supervision, because long-term use carries significant risks of dependence and harm
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Depression, anxiety, and chronic stress
    \item Sleep disturbance and chronic fatigue
    \item Physical deconditioning and weight gain from inactivity
    \item Reduced work capacity and financial difficulties
    \item Social isolation and relationship strain
    \item Overuse of medicines, including dependence on opioids
    \item Reduced quality of life and independence
    \item Avoidance of activity that, in the long run, worsens the pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Chronic pain can be a long-term condition, and for many people complete relief is not achieved. However, most people can learn to manage their pain well enough to function and enjoy a reasonable quality of life. Improvements in pain, sleep, mood, and function are realistic goals even when the pain itself does not fully go away.

Outcomes tend to be better with early, comprehensive management; active participation in treatment; and approaches that address the physical, psychological, and social aspects together. Many people find that pain fluctuates over the years, with good and bad periods, rather than following a single course, and maintaining activity and social connection supports resilience.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Not all chronic pain is preventable, but the risk and impact can be reduced:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Stay active and build strength, flexibility, and fitness
    \item Maintain a healthy weight
    \item Use good posture and safe techniques at work and during sport
    \item Manage acute injuries well, and avoid over-immobilising or over-resting
    \item Protect sleep and develop good sleep habits
    \item Manage stress with relaxation, mindfulness, or counselling
    \item Keep socially connected and maintain enjoyable activities
    \item Pace yourself: plan activity and rest rather than pushing to exhaustion
    \item Seek early help if pain is persistent, rather than waiting for it to worsen
\end{itemize}
