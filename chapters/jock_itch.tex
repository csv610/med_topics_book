\chapter{Jock Itch (Tinea Cruris)}
\index{Jock Itch (Tinea Cruris)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Jock itch, medically called tinea cruris, is a common fungal infection of the skin in the groin area, caused by the same fungi that cause athlete's foot and ringworm. It produces an itchy, red, ring-shaped rash on the inner thighs, groin, and buttocks, which can spread if untreated. It is more common in men, in warm humid conditions, and in people who sweat heavily, wear tight clothing, or are overweight. The infection responds well to antifungal creams and good hygiene, and it can be prevented by keeping the area dry and avoiding sharing towels and clothing.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Jock itch is one of the most common fungal skin infections, affecting men far more often than women, and is particularly common in athletes, military personnel, and people who work in hot or humid conditions. It is more common in summer, in tropical regions, and in people with diabetes or weakened immunity. Many people also have athlete's foot, and the fungi can spread from the feet to the groin via towels and clothing. Recurrence is common without treatment of predisposing factors and of any coexisting foot infection.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Tinea cruris is caused by dermatophyte fungi, most commonly Trichophyton rubrum, which feed on keratin in the skin. The fungi thrive in warm, moist environments, so the groin, with its heat, sweat, and friction from clothing, is an ideal site. Infection begins when fungi from the feet, contaminated towels, clothing, or surfaces reach the groin, and it spreads outward in a ring pattern, with inflammation at the advancing edge. The skin becomes itchy, red, and scaly. The fungus does not penetrate deeply, but scratching can cause secondary bacterial infection, and the rash can spread to the buttocks and upper thighs.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Intense itching and burning in the groin and inner thighs
    \item A red, scaly, ring-shaped rash with a well-defined edge
    \item The rash may spare the scrotum, which helps distinguish it from other conditions
    \item Flaking, cracking, or peeling skin
    \item A musty odour in some cases
    \item Worsening with sweating, exercise, and tight clothing
    \item Associated athlete's foot in many people
    \item Secondary infection with pain, weeping, or pus when scratched
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other rashes in the groin can resemble jock itch.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Candidal intertrigo:} yeast infection, often with satellite lesions and involvement of skin folds
    \item \textbf{Eczema and dermatitis:} contact or seborrhoeic dermatitis
    \item \textbf{Psoriasis:} can affect the groin without scaling
    \item \textbf{Erythrasma:} a bacterial infection with brown-red patches
    \item \textbf{Hidradenitis suppurativa:} chronic inflamed nodules in the groin
    \item \textbf{Scabies:} intense itching with burrows and a generalised rash
    \item \textbf{Skin cancer:} rare, but persistent or unusual lesions need assessment
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Jock itch often responds to over-the-counter antifungal creams, but medical assessment is appropriate for persistent, severe, or spreading rashes, for rashes with pain, pus, or blisters, and for people with diabetes or weakened immunity. Recurrent infections warrant review and treatment of any foot infection. Any rash that does not improve after two weeks of treatment should be assessed.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rapidly spreading redness, pain, and swelling, which may indicate cellulitis
    \item Fever, chills, or feeling generally unwell
    \item Pus, weeping, or severe pain in the groin
    \item Red streaks extending from the rash
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the appearance, site, and pattern of the rash
    \item \textbf{Skin scrapings:} microscopy and culture to confirm the fungus
    \item \textbf{Examination of the feet:} for athlete's foot, a common source
    \item \textbf{Blood glucose testing:} in recurrent or severe cases, to exclude diabetes
    \item \textbf{Assessment of immune status:} where infections are frequent or severe
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antifungal creams:} applied to the rash and surrounding skin for two to four weeks
    \item \textbf{Treatment of athlete's foot:} to prevent reinfection
    \item \textbf{Oral antifungals:} for extensive, resistant, or recurrent infection
    \item \textbf{Keeping the area dry:} drying thoroughly, wearing cotton underwear, and changing after exercise
    \item \textbf{Avoiding shared towels and clothing:} and washing towels and underwear in hot water
    \item \textbf{Weight management:} reducing skin folds and sweating
    \item \textbf{Treating secondary infection:} antibiotics where bacteria have entered scratched skin
    \item \textbf{Steroid combinations:} short-term use for severe inflammation, under medical advice
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Spread of the rash to the buttocks, thighs, and other areas
    \item Secondary bacterial infection and cellulitis
    \item Chronic and recurrent infection
    \item Skin changes from chronic scratching and strong treatments
    \item Discomfort and embarrassment affecting daily life
    \item Spread to other household members through shared items
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Jock itch responds well to antifungal treatment, with most infections clearing within two to four weeks. Recurrence is common unless the underlying factors are addressed, including athlete's foot, sweating, tight clothing, and diabetes control. With good hygiene and early treatment, most people keep the infection under control and prevent spread to others.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep the groin clean and dry, and dry thoroughly after showering
    \item Wear loose cotton underwear and change out of sweaty clothes promptly
    \item Avoid sharing towels, clothing, and sports gear
    \item Wash towels, underwear, and sports clothing in hot water
    \item Treat athlete's foot at the same time
    \item Use antifungal powder or cream as prevention if you are prone
    \item Maintain a healthy weight
    \item Apply cream to the whole affected area, not just the centre
    \item Seek medical advice for persistent or severe symptoms
\end{itemize}
