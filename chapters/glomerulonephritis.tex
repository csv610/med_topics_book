\chapter{Glomerulonephritis}
\index{Glomerulonephritis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Glomerulonephritis is a group of kidney diseases in which the glomeruli, the tiny filtering units of the kidneys, become inflamed and damaged. The inflammation can be caused by immune system problems, infections, or other underlying diseases, and it impairs the kidneys' ability to filter waste and fluid from the blood. Glomerulonephritis can be acute, coming on suddenly with blood in the urine, swelling, and high blood pressure, or chronic, developing slowly over years with few symptoms until significant kidney damage has occurred. Treatment depends on the cause and severity and aims to control inflammation, manage blood pressure, and preserve kidney function.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Glomerulonephritis is a relatively common cause of kidney disease and is one of the leading causes of end-stage kidney disease worldwide. In many countries, glomerulonephritis accounts for around 20--25\% of people starting dialysis. It affects all ages, and specific types have different demographic patterns: IgA nephropathy is the most common type worldwide and often presents in young adults, post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis mainly affects children after a throat or skin infection, and lupus nephritis affects mostly young women. The incidence of some types has declined in developed countries as infections and socioeconomic factors have changed.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The different forms of glomerulonephritis share a common theme: inflammation and damage to the glomeruli, often through the immune system.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immune complexes:} antibodies and antigens can deposit in the glomeruli, triggering inflammation (seen in post-streptococcal, lupus, and IgA nephropathy)
    \item \textbf{Antibodies against the kidney:} in anti-GBM disease, antibodies attack the basement membrane of the glomerulus directly
    \item \textbf{ANCA-associated vasculitis:} antibodies against white blood cells cause inflammation of small blood vessels, including the glomeruli
    \item \textbf{IgA nephropathy:} deposits of immunoglobulin A accumulate in the glomeruli, often after respiratory or gut infections
    \item \textbf{Consequences:} inflammation reduces filtration, allows protein and blood into the urine, and if persistent causes scarring (sclerosis)
    \item \textbf{Progression:} scarring leads to chronic kidney disease, high blood pressure, and eventually kidney failure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms vary with the type and speed of onset of the disease.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Blood in the urine, which may be visible (red, smoky, or cola-coloured) or only microscopic
    \item Frothy urine from protein leaking into the urine
    \item Swelling of the face, hands, feet, and ankles (oedema)
    \item High blood pressure
    \item Reduced urine output
    \item Fatigue and general malaise
    \item Nephrotic syndrome: heavy protein loss causing marked swelling and low blood protein
    \item Nephritic syndrome: blood in the urine, high blood pressure, and reduced kidney function
    \item In chronic disease, symptoms of kidney failure appear only at an advanced stage
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of glomerulonephritis overlap with other kidney and urinary tract conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Urinary tract infection and pyelonephritis, which cause blood and protein in the urine
    \item Kidney stones, which cause blood in the urine and pain
    \item Diabetic nephropathy and hypertensive nephropathy
    \item Polycystic kidney disease
    \item Interstitial nephritis, an inflammation of the kidney tissue between the tubules
    \item Minimal change disease and other causes of nephrotic syndrome
    \item Amyloidosis and other systemic diseases affecting the kidney
    \item Exercise-induced blood in the urine and other benign causes
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor promptly if you notice blood in your urine, frothy urine, swelling, or new high blood pressure. Anyone with a family history of kidney disease, diabetes, or autoimmune disease should have regular kidney checks.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden reduction in urine output or inability to pass urine
    \item Severe swelling with breathlessness, which may indicate fluid in the lungs
    \item Very high blood pressure with severe headache, chest pain, or visual disturbance
    \item Confusion, seizures, or other signs of kidney failure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis requires urine, blood, and imaging tests, and often a kidney biopsy.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urine tests:} dipstick and laboratory testing for blood, protein, and casts
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} kidney function (creatinine and eGFR), albumin, and inflammatory markers
    \item \textbf{Serology:} tests for antibodies that identify the cause, including ANCA, anti-GBM, ANA (lupus), and complement levels
    \item \textbf{Blood glucose and other screens:} to exclude diabetes and other causes
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} checks kidney size, structure, and any obstruction
    \item \textbf{Kidney biopsy:} the definitive test, examining a small sample of kidney tissue under a microscope to identify the specific type of glomerulonephritis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is directed at the underlying cause, controlling inflammation, and protecting the kidneys.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure control:} ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers lower blood pressure and reduce protein loss, protecting the kidneys
    \item \textbf{Immunosuppression:} corticosteroids and other immunosuppressant medicines for immune-mediated types such as lupus nephritis, ANCA vasculitis, and severe IgA nephropathy
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} for post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis and any underlying infection
    \item \textbf{Dietary measures:} a dietitian may advise on salt, protein, and fluid restrictions
    \item \textbf{Diuretics:} reduce swelling from fluid retention
    \item \textbf{Lipid control:} statins may be used in nephrotic syndrome
    \item \textbf{Kidney replacement therapy:} dialysis or kidney transplantation for those who progress to kidney failure
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular checks of blood pressure, urine, and kidney function
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic kidney disease and progression to end-stage kidney failure requiring dialysis or transplant
    \item High blood pressure and its complications, including heart disease and stroke
    \item Nephrotic syndrome with severe swelling, infections, and blood clots
    \item Acute kidney injury in rapidly progressive forms
    \item Hyperkalaemia and other electrolyte disturbances
    \item Anaemia and bone disease from advanced kidney failure
    \item Fluid overload causing breathlessness and heart failure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of glomerulonephritis varies widely by type. Some forms, particularly post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis in children, resolve completely. Others, such as IgA nephropathy, follow a slowly progressive course, with a significant proportion of patients developing kidney failure over 10--20 years. Lupus nephritis and ANCA vasculitis respond to modern immunosuppression but need long-term management. The key predictors of outcome are the specific diagnosis, the amount of protein in the urine, the degree of kidney damage at presentation, and the control of blood pressure. Early diagnosis and treatment substantially improve outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Treat sore throats and skin infections promptly and completely
    \item Control blood pressure and blood glucose if you have diabetes or hypertension
    \item Attend regular kidney checks if you have risk factors or a family history of kidney disease
    \item Take prescribed medicines, especially blood pressure medicines, exactly as directed
    \item Limit salt, and follow dietary advice from your kidney team
    \item Avoid medicines that can harm the kidneys, including high-dose over-the-counter anti-inflammatories (NSAIDs)
    \item Do not smoke, and limit alcohol
    \item Report new blood in the urine, swelling, or breathlessness promptly
\end{itemize}
