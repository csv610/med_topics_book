\chapter{Congenital heart disease}
\index{Congenital heart disease}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Educational information; seek professional advice for individual care.}

\section*{What it is}
Congenital heart disease (CHD) is a structural difference in the heart or major blood vessels that is present from birth. It ranges from mild defects that need monitoring to complex conditions requiring treatment early in life. Some people have few symptoms, while others need lifelong specialist follow-up.

\section*{Possible signs}
In a baby, signs can include blue or grey lips or nails, fast or difficult breathing, sweating or tiring during feeds, poor weight gain, unusual sleepiness, or swelling. Older children may have poor exercise tolerance, fainting, palpitations, chest symptoms, or delayed growth. These signs have many possible causes and need medical assessment.

\section*{Diagnosis}
Some defects are identified during pregnancy or newborn screening; others are found later. Assessment may include examination, oxygen measurement, electrocardiography, echocardiography, chest imaging, or cardiac catheterization. A paediatric or adult congenital cardiology team can explain the specific defect and expected follow-up.

\section*{Treatment and living with CHD}
Treatment depends on the defect and may include observation, medicines, catheter procedures, surgery, or a combination. Repair may improve blood flow but does not always remove the need for lifelong follow-up. Ask the cardiology team about activity, dental care, pregnancy, medicines, vaccinations, and infection prevention rather than assuming restrictions.

\section*{When to seek urgent care}
Seek urgent help for blue or grey lips, severe breathing difficulty, collapse, fainting with ongoing symptoms, severe chest pain, or sudden marked deterioration. Babies who are feeding poorly, breathing fast, unusually sleepy, or not gaining weight should be assessed promptly.
