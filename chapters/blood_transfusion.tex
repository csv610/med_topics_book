\chapter{Blood Transfusion}
\index{Blood Transfusion}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A blood transfusion is the transfer of blood or blood products from a donor into a person's bloodstream. It is used to replace blood lost through injury or surgery, to treat severe anaemia, and to correct clotting problems. Blood products include red cells, platelets, plasma, and concentrated clotting factors. Transfusions are given only when the benefits clearly outweigh the risks, and donated blood is carefully screened and matched before use. In modern practice, restrictive transfusion thresholds and patient blood management programs aim to ensure that each transfusion is truly necessary.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Blood transfusion is one of the most common procedures performed in hospitals, with millions of units of blood transfused worldwide every year. Red cell transfusions are most often given to people with major bleeding, anaemia of chronic illness, or blood disorders such as sickle cell disease. Platelet and plasma transfusions support people with leukaemia, liver disease, or severe bleeding. As surgical techniques and patient blood management have improved, the number of unnecessary transfusions has fallen, and voluntary donation remains essential to keep supplies adequate for emergencies, surgery, and chronic disease.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
A transfusion replaces a missing component of the blood:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Red cells} carry oxygen; they are transfused when the haemoglobin level is dangerously low, such as after major blood loss or in severe anaemia
    \item \textbf{Platelets} help the blood clot; they are given to people with very low platelet counts or platelet dysfunction
    \item \textbf{Plasma} contains clotting factors; it is used to treat bleeding in liver disease, warfarin over-anticoagulation, or major trauma
    \item \textbf{Cryoprecipitate} and concentrated clotting factors are used in specific bleeding disorders such as haemophilia
    \item \textbf{Blood group matching:} donor and recipient are matched for the ABO and Rhesus (Rh) blood groups so that incompatible blood is not given
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
A transfusion is given through an intravenous line, usually over one to four hours. During and after it, the following are normal:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A feeling of coolness in the arm where the drip is placed
    \item Mild discomfort at the cannula site
    \item Close monitoring of pulse, blood pressure, and temperature by nursing staff
    \item Most people feel no different during the transfusion itself
    \item The benefit is felt over hours to days as symptoms of anaemia, such as fatigue and breathlessness, improve
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Not every person who is anaemic needs a transfusion, and the reasons for low blood counts must be considered:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Iron, vitamin B12, or folate deficiency can be treated with supplements instead of transfusion
    \item Bleeding may be from trauma, surgery, or internal sources such as the gut, and the cause must be found
    \item Low platelets may be immune-mediated, medication-related, or due to bone marrow problems
    \item Chronic kidney disease and cancer treatments cause anaemia that is often managed without transfusion if symptoms allow
    \item Reactions to transfusion are distinguished from worsening of the underlying illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most reactions, if they occur, begin during or soon after the transfusion while the patient is under observation. Tell staff immediately if you develop:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever, chills, or shaking
    \item Itching, rash, or hives
    \item Difficulty breathing, chest pain, or back pain
    \item Nausea, headache, or a general feeling of discomfort
    \item Dark urine or any new bleeding after the transfusion
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, severe chest pain, or collapse during or after a transfusion
    \item Any sudden severe symptom while the transfusion is running
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Before a transfusion, several checks are completed:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full blood count:} to confirm the haemoglobin or platelet level is low enough to need transfusion
    \item \textbf{Blood group and antibody screen:} determines the recipient's ABO and Rh type and checks for unexpected antibodies
    \item \textbf{Cross-match:} donor cells and the recipient's serum are mixed in the laboratory to confirm compatibility
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} symptoms, signs of bleeding, and how quickly the count has fallen are all considered
    \item \textbf{Review of alternatives:} iron, folate, or vitamin B12 treatment, and stopping blood-thinning medicines where possible
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Appropriate use:} transfusion is reserved for clinical need rather than for a number on a blood test alone
    \item \textbf{Red cell transfusion:} given for significant bleeding or symptomatic anaemia, commonly using restrictive thresholds
    \item \textbf{Platelets and plasma:} given to treat or prevent bleeding in people with low levels
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} temperature, pulse, and blood pressure are checked throughout; the first part of the transfusion is given slowly
    \item \textbf{Treatment of reactions:} a transfusion reaction is managed promptly by stopping the transfusion and treating symptoms
    \item \textbf{Patient blood management:} programs minimise transfusion by treating anaemia, reducing surgical blood loss, and using blood-salvage techniques
    \item \textbf{Informed consent:} the benefits, risks, and alternatives are discussed before transfusion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Most transfusions are safe, but complications can occur:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fever and allergic reactions:} the most common, usually mild and treatable
    \item \textbf{Transfusion-related acute lung injury:} a rare but serious reaction causing breathing difficulty
    \item \textbf{Haemolytic reactions:} when incompatible blood is given, causing breakdown of red cells
    \item \textbf{Transfusion-associated circulatory overload:} fluid overload, mainly in people with heart or kidney problems
    \item \textbf{Infection:} the risk is extremely low because of donor screening and testing, but not zero
    \item \textbf{Development of antibodies:} can make future transfusions or pregnancies more complicated
    \item \textbf{Iron overload:} with many repeated transfusions, iron accumulates and must be treated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For most recipients, a transfusion is a safe and effective treatment that restores strength and well-being. Transfusion for major bleeding can be life-saving, and regular transfusions allow people with conditions such as sickle cell disease or thalassaemia to live well. Serious reactions are rare, particularly when transfusions are given only when needed and under close observation. Overall outcomes depend mainly on the underlying illness being treated, rather than on the transfusion itself.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Treat the cause of anaemia where possible, such as replacing iron, vitamin B12, or folate
    \item Eat an iron-rich diet and manage heavy menstrual bleeding with medical advice
    \item Tell your doctor about any previous transfusion reactions
    \item Consider donating blood regularly if you are eligible, to help keep supplies available
    \item Carry a card or document stating your blood group if you have a rare type
    \item Attend follow-up blood tests to check the response to transfusion and to monitor for complications of repeated transfusions
\end{itemize}
