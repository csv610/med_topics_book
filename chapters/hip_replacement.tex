\chapter{Hip Replacement}
\index{Hip Replacement}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hip replacement surgery, or total hip arthroplasty, is an operation in which a damaged hip joint is removed and replaced with an artificial joint made of metal, plastic, and ceramic components. It is one of the most successful and commonly performed operations in the world, most often for severe osteoarthritis, but also for rheumatoid arthritis, fractures of the hip, and other conditions. Hip replacement reliably relieves pain and restores mobility, with most people walking normally within weeks and returning to full activity. More than 50,000 hip replacements are performed in many countries each year.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Around 50,000--60,000 hip replacements are performed in many countries annually, and the number is rising with the ageing population and increasing obesity. Osteoarthritis is the indication in most cases, and most operations are in people over 60, though younger and older patients are increasingly treated. The operation is highly successful: over 95\% of hip replacements last more than 10 years and over 80\% last 20 years or more. Patient satisfaction rates are among the highest of any surgical procedure.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Osteoarthritis:} the commonest cause, with progressive loss of joint cartilage and bone-on-bone contact
    \item \textbf{Rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory disease:} destroy the joint surface
    \item \textbf{Hip fracture:} displaced femoral neck fractures are treated with replacement in older people
    \item \textbf{Avascular necrosis:} death of the femoral head from impaired blood supply
    \item \textbf{Developmental dysplasia and childhood conditions:} lead to early arthritis
    \item \textbf{The prosthesis:} a stem in the femur, a socket in the pelvis, and a bearing surface between them
    \item \textbf{Indication:} surgery is considered when pain limits daily life and conservative treatment has failed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe hip or groin pain, worse with walking and weight-bearing
    \item Pain at night disturbing sleep
    \item Stiffness and reduced range of movement
    \item A limp and difficulty with stairs, shoes, and socks
    \item Reduced ability to work, exercise, and perform daily activities
    \item X-ray changes: narrowed joint space, bone spurs, and deformity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Back pain with referred pain to the hip, including spinal stenosis
    \item Hip bursitis and tendinopathy
    \item Osteonecrosis of the femoral head
    \item Paget's disease and other bone conditions
    \item Peripheral vascular disease causing leg pain
    \item The pattern of pain, examination, and imaging distinguish these
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for persistent hip pain that limits activity, and ask about referral to an orthopaedic surgeon when conservative treatment no longer helps.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A fall with severe hip pain and inability to bear weight (possible fracture)
    \item A shortened, rotated leg after trauma
    \item Fever, redness, or severe pain in a joint after recent surgery
    \item Chest pain or breathlessness after surgery (possible blood clot)
    \item A swollen, painful calf, which may indicate a deep vein thrombosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} confirm the diagnosis and severity of arthritis
    \item \textbf{MRI:} assesses cartilage, bone, and soft tissue in complex cases
    \item \textbf{Preoperative assessment:} blood tests, ECG, and evaluation of fitness for anaesthesia
    \item \textbf{Bone density testing:} where osteoporosis is suspected
    \item \textbf{Templating:} X-ray planning of the components
    \item \textbf{Postoperative review:} clinical and X-ray follow-up at intervals
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conservative treatment first:} physiotherapy, weight loss, activity modification, and pain relief
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} the femoral head and socket are replaced with prosthetic components
    \item \textbf{Approaches:} posterior, lateral, and anterior approaches, with muscle-sparing techniques reducing recovery time
    \item \textbf{Anaesthesia:} spinal or general anaesthesia with enhanced recovery programs
    \item \textbf{Day of surgery:} most patients walk with support within hours
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy, home exercises, and graduated activity
    \item \textbf{Anticoagulation:} medicines to prevent blood clots after surgery
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular review to monitor the implant
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Blood clots: deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, reduced by blood thinners and early mobilisation
    \item Infection of the joint, the most serious complication, occurring in under 1--2\% of cases
    \item Dislocation of the new joint, more common in the first months
    \item Leg length difference and limp
    \item Nerve injury, causing numbness or weakness
    \item Loosening or wear of components over time, requiring revision
    \item Anaesthesia and medical complications
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Hip replacement is one of the most effective operations in medicine. Around 90--95\% of patients report excellent pain relief and improved function, and most return to walking, driving, work, and recreational activity. Implants last 15--25 years in most patients, with younger and more active patients at higher risk of eventual revision. Outcomes are best when surgery is performed before severe deconditioning, and patient satisfaction rates remain among the highest of any procedure.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and strong muscles to delay arthritis
    \item Consider hip replacement when pain seriously limits your life, rather than waiting
    \item Prepare for surgery: stop smoking, optimise fitness, and arrange support at home
    \item Follow physiotherapy and exercise programs after surgery
    \item Take anticoagulation and antibiotics as prescribed
    \item Use precautions to avoid dislocation in the early months
    \item Attend follow-up appointments and seek advice for any new symptoms
    \item Prevent falls by making the home safe
\end{itemize}
