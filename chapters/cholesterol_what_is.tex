\chapter{Cholesterol (what is)}
\index{Cholesterol (what is)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cholesterol is a vital lipid (waxy fat-like substance) produced naturally by the liver and also obtained from certain foods. It is a critical component of eukaryotic cell membranes, where it regulates membrane fluidity and permeability, and serves as an essential precursor for the synthesis of steroid hormones (including cortisol, aldosterone, oestrogen, progesterone, and testosterone), vitamin D, and bile acids that aid in dietary fat digestion. Because lipids are insoluble in water, cholesterol is transported through the bloodstream packaged inside lipid-protein complexes called lipoproteins. These include low-density lipoprotein (LDL) and high-density lipoprotein (HDL). Maintaining balanced cholesterol levels is of paramount clinical importance; elevated levels of LDL cholesterol (often termed 'bad' cholesterol) are major contributors to plaque formation in arteries (atherosclerosis), which significantly elevates the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), including coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, and ischaemic stroke.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Abnormal cholesterol levels, or dyslipidaemia, represent one of the most common risk factors for cardiovascular disease globally and in many countries. Approximately one in three adults have elevated blood cholesterol levels. The prevalence increases significantly with age, with a substantial proportion of individuals over 55 requiring lipid-lowering therapies. While men are statistically more likely to experience elevated cholesterol and associated cardiovascular events at a younger age, post-menopausal women experience a rapid rise in LDL cholesterol due to declining oestrogen levels, narrowing the gender gap. Globally, dyslipidaemia contributes to millions of deaths annually from coronary heart disease and stroke, with risk patterns strongly tied to dietary habits, physical inactivity, genetic predispositions, and socioeconomic factors.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Although cholesterol is essential for physiological function, dyslipidaemia arises from a combination of genetic susceptibility and environmental factors that disrupt lipid homeostasis.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary intake:} High consumption of saturated fats and trans-unsaturated fatty acids increases circulating LDL levels by downregulating hepatic LDL receptors, thereby reducing the clearance of cholesterol from the blood.
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} Hereditary conditions, such as familial hypercholesterolaemia, involve mutations in the LDL receptor gene ($LDLR$), apolipoprotein B ($APOB$), or proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 ($PCSK9$), leading to profoundly elevated LDL levels and premature cardiovascular events.
    \item \textbf{Hepatic regulation:} The liver regulates cholesterol synthesis via the HMG-CoA reductase pathway. Decreased expression of hepatic LDL receptors impairs the liver's ability to clear LDL-C from circulation.
    \item \textbf{Sedentary lifestyle:} A lack of physical activity reduces lipoprotein lipase activity in skeletal muscle, which lowers cardioprotective HDL levels and increases circulating triglycerides and LDL levels.
    \item \textbf{Secondary medical conditions:} Endocrine disorders such as hypothyroidism, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and Cushing's syndrome, as well as renal pathologies like nephrotic syndrome, alter systemic lipid metabolism and clearance.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological agents:} Certain medications, including atypical antipsychotics, glucocorticoids, and immunosuppressants, can adversely alter lipid profiles.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Hypercholesterolaemia is predominantly a silent condition, producing no direct symptoms until significant vascular damage or plaque deposition has occurred.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asymptomatic presentation:} The vast majority of individuals with high cholesterol remain entirely asymptomatic until they experience a cardiovascular event.
    \item \textbf{Xanthomas:} Deposition of cholesterol-rich material in tendons or skin, most commonly presenting as tendon xanthomas on the Achilles tendons or extensor tendons of the hand, particularly in familial hypercholesterolaemia.
    \item \textbf{Xanthelasmas:} Yellowish plaques on or around the eyelids caused by localised cholesterol deposition.
    \item \textbf{Corneal arcus:} A greyish-white ring or arc around the corneal margin, which is normal in older adults (arcus senilis) but indicates severe hypercholesterolaemia if present in individuals under 45 years of age.
    \item \textbf{Symptoms of vascular insufficiency:} Angina pectoris (chest discomfort), transient ischaemic attacks, or intermittent claudication in the lower limbs, indicating compromised arterial blood flow due to atherosclerosis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When evaluating elevated cholesterol, clinicians must distinguish between primary genetic disorders and secondary causes of dyslipidaemia.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Familial hypercholesterolaemia:} A primary genetic disorder marked by severely elevated LDL cholesterol ($>4.9$\,mmol/L in adults) and a strong family history of premature coronary artery disease.
    \item \textbf{Hypothyroidism:} Thyroid hormones regulate LDL receptor expression; an underactive thyroid reduces clearance, simulating primary hypercholesterolaemia.
    \item \textbf{Nephrotic syndrome:} Renal protein loss triggers compensatory hepatic synthesis of proteins and lipids, leading to severe secondary hypercholesterolaemia.
    \item \textbf{Obstructive liver disease:} Biliary obstruction impairs the excretion of cholesterol in bile, leading to elevated circulating levels.
    \item \textbf{Poorly managed diabetes mellitus:} Insulin resistance alters lipoprotein metabolism, typically presenting as elevated triglycerides, low HDL, and small, dense LDL particles (diabetic dyslipidaemia).
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Adults should undergo routine lipid screening as part of a general cardiovascular health assessment. Anyone with a family history of premature heart disease, tendon xanthomas, or physical features of lipid deposits should consult a general practitioner.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, crushing, or squeezing chest pain that may radiate to the jaw, neck, back, or left arm.
    \item Acute shortness of breath, sudden sweating, dizziness, or nausea.
    \item Sudden weakness or numbness in the face, arm, or leg, especially on one side of the body.
    \item Difficulty speaking, slurred speech, or sudden visual disturbances.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is made through a blood test and comprehensive cardiovascular risk calculation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fasting or non-fasting lipid profile:} Measures total cholesterol, HDL-C, LDL-C, and triglycerides. While non-fasting tests are acceptable for general screening, fasting profiles are preferred for precise assessments.
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular risk calculator:} Tools such as the local Cardiovascular Risk Calculator estimate the 5-year risk of cardiovascular disease based on lipid levels, blood pressure, age, smoking status, and diabetes.
    \item \textbf{Thyroid function tests:} Conducted to exclude secondary dyslipidaemia caused by hypothyroidism.
    \item \textbf{Renal function tests:} Performed to screen for chronic kidney disease or nephrotic syndrome.
    \item \textbf{Liver function tests:} Required prior to starting lipid-lowering therapies (like statins) to establish a baseline.
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} Indicated if familial hypercholesterolaemia is suspected based on clinical criteria (e.g., Dutch Lipid Clinic Network criteria).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management focuses on reducing cardiovascular risk through a combination of lifestyle interventions and pharmacotherapy.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary modifications:} Reducing saturated and trans fats while increasing soluble fibre, plant sterols, and omega-3 fatty acids to lower LDL cholesterol.
    \item \textbf{Physical activity:} Aiming for at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise per week to raise cardioprotective HDL and lower triglycerides.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacotherapy (Statins):} HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors (e.g., atorvastatin, rosuvastatin) are the first-line pharmacological treatment to lower LDL cholesterol.
    \item \textbf{Ezetimibe:} A cholesterol absorption inhibitor often added to statin therapy when LDL targets are not met.
    \item \textbf{PCSK9 inhibitors:} Monoclonal antibodies (e.g., evolocumab) reserved for patients with familial hypercholesterolaemia or high cardiovascular risk who cannot tolerate or achieve targets with standard therapies.
    \item \textbf{Weight management and smoking cessation:} Key strategies to improve overall cardiovascular risk and lipid profiles.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated dyslipidaemia leads to systemic arterial changes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Atherosclerosis:} The progressive narrowing and hardening of arteries due to lipid plaque deposition.
    \item \textbf{Coronary artery disease:} Plaque buildup in the arteries supplying the heart, which can cause angina or myocardial infarction (heart attack).
    \item \textbf{Cerebrovascular disease:} Plaque rupture or thromboembolism leading to transient ischaemic attacks or stroke.
    \item \textbf{Peripheral arterial disease:} Reduced blood flow to the limbs, causing claudication and poor wound healing.
    \item \textbf{Acute pancreatitis:} Extremely high triglyceride levels (typically $>10$\,mmol/L) can trigger severe pancreatic inflammation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals with elevated cholesterol is highly favourable if detected early and managed proactively. Appropriate lipid-lowering therapy and lifestyle adjustments can slow, halt, or even partially reverse the progression of atherosclerosis, reducing the relative risk of major adverse cardiovascular events by up to 20--30\% per mmol/L reduction in LDL cholesterol. For individuals with familial hypercholesterolaemia, early diagnosis and lifelong treatment are critical to prevent premature coronary artery disease. Adherence to prescribed therapies and regular monitoring are the primary determinants of long-term outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adopt a Mediterranean diet:} Focus on vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, olive oil, and lean proteins like fish.
    \item \textbf{Limit processed foods:} Minimise consumption of foods high in saturated fats, trans fats, and refined sugars.
    \item \textbf{Engage in regular exercise:} Incorporate brisk walking, cycling, or swimming into your daily routine.
    \item \textbf{Avoid tobacco use:} Stop smoking to prevent damage to blood vessel walls and improve HDL levels.
    \item \textbf{Monitor alcohol intake:} Limit alcohol consumption to prevent elevated triglycerides and blood pressure.
    \item \textbf{Attend routine health checks:} Have your cholesterol and blood pressure checked regularly as advised by your general practitioner.
\end{itemize}
