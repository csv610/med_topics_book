\chapter{Opioid Overdose}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Opioid overdose is an acute, life-threatening medical emergency caused by the excessive consumption of or exposure to natural, semi-synthetic, or synthetic opioid agonists. Opioids include prescription analgesics (such as oxycodone, morphine, codeine, tramadol, and fentanyl), pharmacotherapies for opioid dependence (methadone and buprenorphine), and illicit substances (such as heroin). The primary pathophysiological consequence of opioid toxicity is severe respiratory depression, which occurs when opioids bind to and overstimulate mu-opioid receptors in the brainstem's respiratory control centres. Without immediate intervention, this leads to profound hypoxia, brain injury, cardiac arrest, and death. Rapid recognition of the classic opioid overdose triad and the prompt administration of the antagonist naloxone are key to saving lives.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Opioid overdose represents a major global public health crisis and is a leading cause of accidental death in many developed nations, including Australia. According to the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW), there are more than 1,000 drug-induced deaths involving opioids in Australia each year, representing over 60\% of all drug-induced fatalities. Historically driven by heroin, the epidemiology has shifted over the past two decades, with pharmaceutical opioids (primarily oxycodone, fentanyl, and methadone) now responsible for the majority of overdoses. Accidental overdoses are common, occurring in the context of chronic pain management, therapeutic errors, or substance misuse. The crisis is further exacerbated by the emerging contamination of the illicit drug market with highly potent synthetic opioids, such as nitazenes and fentanyl analogues.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The toxic effects of opioids are mediated by their interaction with specific G-protein-coupled receptors throughout the central and peripheral nervous systems.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mu-opioid receptor activation:} Opioids act primarily on mu-opioid receptors. In the pre-Bötzinger complex within the medulla oblongata, this activation suppresses sensitivity to rising carbon dioxide levels, slowing the respiratory rate and decreasing tidal volume.
    \item \textbf{Synergistic depression:} The risk of overdose is severely compounded by the concurrent use of other central nervous system (CNS) depressants, such as alcohol, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids, or sedating antihistamines, which act via distinct pathways to further depress respiratory drive.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacokinetics and half-life:} The duration and onset of toxicity depend on the route of administration (intravenous injection causing immediate toxicity, whereas oral ingestion is slower) and the drug's half-life (e.g. methadone has a long, unpredictable half-life, leading to delayed or prolonged overdose states).
    \item \textbf{Renal and hepatic clearance:} Impaired organ function reduces the clearance of active metabolites (e.g. morphine-6-glucuronide), increasing the risk of cumulative toxicity and accidental overdose in chronic users.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
An acute opioid overdose is characterized by a distinct clinical triad, alongside secondary signs of systemic hypoxia.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Respiratory depression:} Bradypnoea (respiratory rate under 8 to 10 breaths per minute), shallow breathing, or complete apnoea. Cyanosis (blue or purple lips and fingertips) is often visible.
    \item \textbf{Depressed consciousness:} Stupor, unresponsive coma, or inability to be aroused by painful stimuli.
    \item \textbf{Miosis:} Bilateral pinpoint pupils (pupillary constriction). Note that miosis may be absent in cases of mixed overdose, severe terminal hypoxia, or co-ingestion with atypical opioids like pethidine.
    \item \textbf{Snoring or gurgling sounds:} Indicative of partial airway obstruction by the tongue or secretions.
    \item \textbf{Bradycardia and hypotension:} Occurring secondary to profound hypoxia.
    \item \textbf{Hypothermia:} Cold, clammy skin and reduced core body temperature.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When presenting with depressed consciousness and respiratory depression, several alternative conditions must be ruled out.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Non-opioid sedative overdose:} Benzodiazepine, barbiturate, or alcohol toxicity; presents with similar stupor but typically lacks pinpoint pupils and responds poorly to naloxone.
    \item \textbf{Hypoglycaemia:} Exceptionally low blood glucose levels presenting with confusion or coma; ruled out immediately via finger-prick blood glucose measurement.
    \item \textbf{Acute ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke:} Especially brainstem strokes, which can present with coma and pinpoint pupils; diagnosed via urgent CT head scan.
    \item \textbf{Severe postictal state:} Depressed consciousness following a grand mal seizure; identified through clinical history or witness reports.
    \item \textbf{Hypothermia or carbon monoxide poisoning:} Environmental toxicities presenting with altered mental state and respiratory changes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Suspected opioid overdose is a critical, time-sensitive medical emergency. If a person is unresponsive, has slow breathing, or has blue lips, emergency services must be contacted immediately.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) immediately for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unresponsiveness to shaking or shouting
    \item Unusually slow, shallow, or irregular breathing, or complete absence of breathing
    \item Snoring, choking, or gurgling noises in an unresponsive person
    \item Pale, cold, or clammy skin, or blue/grey discolouration of the lips, tongue, or nail beds
    \item Pinpoint or unresponsive pupils
\end{itemize}
\textit{In Australia, if the person is conscious but you suspect they have taken an unsafe amount of medication, contact the Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26 for guidance.}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is primarily clinical, based on physical signs and history, and treatment must not be delayed for laboratory confirmation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical presentation and history:} The presence of the classic triad (coma, miosis, bradypnoea) in the context of known opioid use or drug paraphernalia.
    \item \textbf{Naloxone response:} Rapid improvement in respiratory rate and consciousness following naloxone administration serves as diagnostic confirmation.
    \item \textbf{Arterial Blood Gas (ABG):} Reveals respiratory acidosis with hypoxaemia and hypercapnia.
    \item \textbf{Serum toxicology screen:} Detects the presence of opioids and co-ingested substances, though results are rarely available in the acute resuscitation window.
    \item \textbf{Electrocardiogram (ECG):} Essential to monitor for QT prolongation (associated with methadone toxicity) or myocardial ischaemia secondary to hypoxia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Emergency management focuses on maintaining airway patency, supporting ventilation, and reversing opioid binding.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First aid (DRSABCD):} Assess for danger, response, send for help, open the airway, check for breathing, perform CPR if breathing is absent, and apply a defibrillator. If breathing but unconscious, place the patient in the recovery position.
    \item \textbf{Airway management and oxygenation:} Maintain a patent airway using head-tilt/chin-lift or jaw-thrust manoeuvres. Deliver supplemental high-flow oxygen, or support ventilation using a bag-valve-mask device if apnoeic.
    \item \textbf{Naloxone administration:} Administer naloxone, a competitive opioid antagonist. In community settings, intranasal naloxone (Nyxoid) or intramuscular injection is used. In clinical settings, intravenous naloxone is titrated (e.g. 0.4 mg to 2 mg) to restore adequate spontaneous respiration without inducing severe acute withdrawal.
    \item \textbf{Observation and monitoring:} Patients must be observed for at least 2 to 4 hours after the last dose of naloxone, as the half-life of naloxone (30 to 90 minutes) is often shorter than that of the offending opioid, risking recurrence of respiratory depression (re-narcotisation).
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} Manage complications such as non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema or aspiration pneumonia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Anoxic brain injury secondary to prolonged cerebral hypoxia.
    \item Aspiration pneumonia due to inhalation of gastric contents during coma or vomiting.
    \item Non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, a rare but serious reaction to severe hypoxia or rapid naloxone reversal.
    \item Acute withdrawal syndrome, characterized by agitation, tachycardia, severe nausea, vomiting, shivering, and piloerection, triggered by excessive naloxone doses.
    \item Compartment syndrome or rhabdomyolysis resulting from prolonged immobility on a hard surface.
    \item Death due to asphyxiation and cardiac arrest.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for opioid overdose is excellent if naloxone is administered promptly and ventilation is supported before hypoxic brain damage or cardiac arrest occurs. Normal neurological function is typically restored immediately upon reversal. However, if respiration is compromised for more than a few minutes, permanent neurological deficits or death may result. Following recovery, patients require comprehensive assessment for substance use disorder and referral to addiction specialists or methadone/buprenorphine maintenance programmes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Obtain and carry a take-home naloxone kit (available free without a prescription in Australia under the Take Home Naloxone program) if you or a family member uses opioids.
    \item Take prescription opioids strictly as directed by your clinician; do not increase doses without medical advice.
    \item Avoid mixing opioids with alcohol, benzodiazepines, or other sedatives.
    \item Educate family members and friends on how to recognize the signs of an overdose and how to administer naloxone.
    \item Never use illicit opioids alone; ensure someone is present who can call emergency services or administer naloxone.
\end{itemize}
