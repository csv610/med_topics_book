\chapter{Arthroscopic Release of Frozen Shoulder}
\index{Arthroscopic Release of Frozen Shoulder}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Arthroscopic release of the frozen shoulder, also known as adhesive capsulitis, is a keyhole operation that divides the tight, scarred joint capsule of the shoulder so that the joint can move more freely. It is commonly combined with a manipulation under anaesthesia, in which the surgeon gently moves the arm through its full range of movement to break down adhesions. The procedure is considered when a frozen shoulder has not improved despite physiotherapy, corticosteroid injections, and the passage of time. It is a day-case or short-stay procedure performed under a general anaesthetic or regional block.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Frozen shoulder affects roughly two to five people in every hundred over a lifetime, most commonly between the ages of 40 and 60, and it is slightly more frequent in women. It is considerably more common in people with diabetes, affecting around one in five of them at some point, and it may follow an injury, a period of immobility, or previous shoulder surgery. In a minority of people the condition affects both shoulders, either simultaneously or one after the other. Approximately 10\% of frozen shoulders do not resolve spontaneously and go on to require intervention.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary (idiopathic) frozen shoulder:} no clear precipitating cause; the capsule undergoes an inflammatory process that proceeds to fibrosis and scarring
    \item \textbf{Secondary causes:} diabetes mellitus, thyroid disease, cardiac disease, and shoulder trauma or previous shoulder surgery
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} the joint capsule thickens, becomes inflamed, and develops contracted bands of scar-like tissue that shrink the joint volume and restrict glenohumeral movement
    \item The condition classically passes through three phases: a painful \textbf{freezing} phase, a stiff \textbf{frozen} phase with less pain, and a gradual \textbf{thawing} phase
    \item Surgery is most often offered in the frozen phase, once pain has settled but stiffness persists
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent shoulder pain, typically worse at night and disturbing sleep
    \item Progressive stiffness with difficulty reaching overhead, behind the back, and out to the side
    \item Marked, proportionate loss of both active (self-initiated) and passive (assisted) movement, unlike a rotator cuff tear in which passive movement is preserved
    \item Loss of external rotation is usually the earliest and most severe restriction
    \item Pain and restriction that can last many months if untreated, interfering with dressing, hygiene, driving, and work
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rotator cuff tear or tendinopathy
    \item Osteoarthritis of the glenohumeral or acromioclavicular joint
    \item Shoulder dislocation or recurrent instability
    \item Calcific tendinopathy of the rotator cuff
    \item Subacromial bursitis or impingement syndrome
    \item Pain referred from the cervical spine or nerve root irritation
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if shoulder pain or stiffness lasts more than a few weeks, is worsening, or is affecting sleep and daily activities. Seek advice before the shoulder becomes severely stiff, because treatment is easier and more effective in the earlier phases of the condition.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe shoulder pain after an injury or fall
    \item An obviously deformed or dislocated shoulder
    \item Shoulder pain accompanied by chest pain, breathlessness, or collapse
    \item Numbness or weakness spreading down the arm
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the nature and duration of pain, night pain, and the specific movements that are difficult
    \item \textbf{Examination:} comparison of active and passive range of movement in all planes, with a characteristic global restriction in frozen shoulder
    \item \textbf{X-ray:} plain films to exclude arthritis, calcification, or an old dislocation
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} to assess the rotator cuff and subacromial bursa for alternative pathology
    \item \textbf{MRI or MR arthrogram:} used when the diagnosis is uncertain or to plan surgery and exclude other intra-articular problems
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} blood glucose, glycated haemoglobin, and thyroid function may be checked if diabetes or thyroid disease is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} stretching and graded exercises, usually tried first for several months
    \item \textbf{Analgesia and anti-inflammatories:} non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and simple analgesics to control pain in the freezing phase
    \item \textbf{Corticosteroid injection:} into the glenohumeral joint to reduce inflammation and relieve pain, sometimes repeated
    \item \textbf{Manipulation under anaesthesia:} controlled stretching of the shoulder to break down adhesions, alone or before arthroscopy
    \item \textbf{Arthroscopic capsular release:} division of the contracted capsule and any adhesions through small keyhole incisions, usually combined with gentle manipulation
    \item \textbf{Post-operative rehabilitation:} a structured physiotherapy programme, begun early and sustained for several months, is essential to maintain the movement gained
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Incomplete improvement or recurrence of stiffness
    \item Infection of the joint or surgical wounds, which is rare
    \item Nerve or blood vessel injury during surgery
    \item Damage to cartilage or bone during manipulation
    \item Persistent pain even when movement improves
    \item Anaesthetic-related risks, as with any operation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Many frozen shoulders improve on their own over one to three years, but recovery is often slow and sometimes incomplete. Arthroscopic release typically restores a useful range of movement much more quickly, usually within weeks to months, and is generally most successful when followed by committed physiotherapy. Results are usually good, with most patients regaining near-normal function, although some residual stiffness or discomfort may persist and recurrence is possible. People with diabetes have a somewhat higher risk of a less complete result.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep the shoulder moving gently after an injury, a fall, or shoulder surgery to prevent secondary stiffness
    \item Follow prescribed physiotherapy and home stretching programmes consistently
    \item Manage diabetes and other underlying conditions carefully
    \item Protect the shoulder from repeated strain and overuse
    \item Seek early review for persistent shoulder pain rather than waiting for severe stiffness to develop
    \item Maintain good posture and regular general exercise
\end{itemize}
