\chapter{Medicines safety for older people}
\index{Medicines safety for older people}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Medicines safety for older people involves the safe and appropriate use of medications in individuals aged 65 and older to prevent adverse drug events. Older adults are highly vulnerable to medication-related problems due to physiological changes associated with ageing, polypharmacy (taking multiple medications simultaneously), and multiple co-existing medical conditions. Promoting medication safety involves regular reviews, identifying potentially inappropriate medications, and modifying drug doses to match altered organ function.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Older people represent approximately 16\% of the the population but consume over 30\% of all prescription medicines. Polypharmacy is highly prevalent, with over 30--40\% of older adults taking five or more medications daily. Adverse drug events are a major cause of hospitalisation in this age group, accounting for up to 15--20\% of all unplanned geriatric admissions. Up to half of these medication-related admissions are considered preventable.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Age-related physiological changes alter how the body processes and responds to medications (pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reduced renal clearance:} Ageing leads to a natural decline in glomerular filtration rate (GFR), which delays the excretion of hydrophilic drugs (e.g., digoxin, metformin) and increases the risk of toxicity.
    \item \textbf{Altered body composition:} An increase in body fat percentage and decrease in total body water and muscle mass alter the volume of distribution, causing lipophilic drugs (e.g., diazepam) to persist in the body longer.
    \item \textbf{Decreased hepatic metabolism:} Reduced liver blood flow and mass can slow down first-pass metabolism, increasing the bioavailability and half-life of many medications.
    \item \textbf{Increased sensitivity (pharmacodynamics):} The ageing brain is more sensitive to central nervous system drugs (like benzodiazepines and opioids), leading to enhanced side effects such as sedation and confusion.
    \item \textbf{Impaired homeostatic mechanisms:} Blunted baroreceptor reflexes make older adults highly susceptible to orthostatic hypotension from antihypertensive drugs, increasing the risk of falls.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Medication-related issues in older adults often present atypically or mimic common geriatric syndromes rather than typical drug side effects:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cognitive impairment:} Sudden or worsening confusion, delirium, memory loss, or sedation, often caused by anticholinergic or sedative medicines.
    \item \textbf{Falls and instability:} Unsteadiness, dizziness, or loss of balance, frequently linked to psychotropic drugs or blood-pressure-lowering medications.
    \item \textbf{Postural hypotension:} A drop in blood pressure upon standing, leading to lightheadedness or fainting (syncope).
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal issues:} Constipation (often aggravated by opioid analgesics or anticholinergics) or gastrointestinal bleeding (linked to NSAIDs or anticoagulants).
    \item \textbf{Non-specific decline:} Fatigue, loss of appetite, dehydration, or functional decline that is incorrectly attributed to "old age".
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When evaluating an older patient with new symptoms, clinicians must distinguish drug-induced adverse events from other age-related conditions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dementia vs. Drug-induced cognitive impairment:} Progressive, irreversible neurodegenerative decline compared to acute, reversible confusion caused by medications.
    \item \textbf{Idiopathic Parkinson's disease vs. Drug-induced parkinsonism:} Primary motor disease compared to extrapyramidal symptoms caused by antipsychotics or metoclopramide.
    \item \textbf{Chronic vestibular disease vs. Drug-induced dizziness:} Inner ear dysfunction compared to orthostatic hypotension or drug-induced vestibular toxicity.
    \item \textbf{Urinary tract infection vs. Anticholinergic delirium:} Acute confusion caused by infection compared to medication side effects.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Older adults, their families, or carers should consult their GP or pharmacist if they notice new symptoms after starting, stopping, or changing a medication, or if the drug regimen becomes too complex to manage. Regular medication reviews (such as a Home Medicines Review in many countries) are recommended at least annually.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe confusion, hallucinations, or inability to wake up (delirium or severe toxicity)
    \item A fall resulting in severe pain, inability to move, or a suspected head injury
    \item Signs of severe bleeding, such as vomiting blood, coughing up blood, or passing black, tarry stools
    \item Severe dizziness, fainting, or chest pain
    \item Difficulty breathing or severe allergic reactions with swelling of the face or throat
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Identifying medication-related problems in older adults requires a systematic clinical approach:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive medication reconciliation:} Creating an accurate list of all prescription, over-the-counter, and complementary medicines the patient is actually taking.
    \item \textbf{Beers Criteria and STOPP/START criteria:} Using validated clinical tools to identify potentially inappropriate medications in older adults.
    \item \textbf{Estimated Glomerular Filtration Rate (eGFR):} Regularly calculating renal function to adjust drug dosages.
    \item \textbf{Orthostatic blood pressure measurement:} Checking blood pressure while lying flat and after standing for 2--3 minutes to detect orthostatic hypotension.
    \item \textbf{Serum drug levels:} Monitoring levels of high-risk drugs with narrow therapeutic windows (e.g., digoxin, warfarin INR, phenytoin).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Optimising medicine safety involves coordination between the patient, family, GP, pharmacist, and specialists:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Structured medication reviews:} Annual Home Medicines Review (HMR) or residential medication management reviews conducted by an accredited pharmacist.
    \item \textbf{Deprescribing:} The planned, supervised process of reducing or stopping medications that may no longer be beneficial or are causing harm.
    \item \textbf{Dose adjustment:} Adhering to the principle of "start low and go slow" when initiating new therapies in older adults.
    \item \textbf{Adherence aids:} Using dose administration aids (like Webster-paks or pill organisers) and simplified dosing schedules to prevent errors.
    \item \textbf{Patient and carer education:} Providing clear, written instructions about the purpose of each medicine, correct dosages, and potential side effects.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe falls resulting in hip fractures, subdural haematomas, or loss of independence
    \item Acute kidney injury or chronic renal decline due to drug-drug interactions (e.g., the "triple whammy" of NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics)
    \item Severe gastrointestinal haemorrhage or stroke from inappropriate anticoagulant or NSAID use
    \item Delirium, falls, and functional decline leading to premature admission to residential aged care facilities
    \item Anticholinergic toxicity, leading to dry mouth, blurred vision, urinary retention, constipation, and cognitive decline
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for older people who undergo regular medication reviews and deprescribing is excellent, often resulting in improved cognitive function, fewer falls, and enhanced quality of life. Resolving drug side effects and simplifying regimens can reverse what was previously assumed to be irreversible physical or cognitive decline. Active management of medication safety significantly reduces the risk of emergency hospitalisations and drug-related deaths.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep an up-to-date list of all your medications, including vitamins and herbal supplements, and carry it with you to all medical appointments
    \item Do not share medications with others or take expired medicines
    \item Ask your GP or pharmacist to explain the reason for any new medicine, its potential side effects, and how long you need to take it
    \item Use a single pharmacy for all your prescriptions so the pharmacist can monitor for drug interactions
    \item Avoid self-medicating with over-the-counter sleeping pills, strong pain relievers (like ibuprofen), or allergy medicines without consulting a pharmacist first
\end{itemize}
