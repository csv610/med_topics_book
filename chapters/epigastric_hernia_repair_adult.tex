\chapter{Epigastric Hernia Repair (Adult)}
\index{Epigastric Hernia Repair (Adult)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An epigastric hernia is a hernia that occurs through a weakness in the midline of the abdominal wall, between the breastbone and the belly button. Through the gap, fat or, less commonly, part of the bowel can push out, forming a lump in the upper abdomen. Epigastric hernias are often small and may cause no symptoms, but repair is recommended when they cause pain, enlarge, or are at risk of complications. Repair is a straightforward operation, usually performed laparoscopically or through a small incision, and recovery is quick.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Epigastric hernias are common and can occur at any age, affecting both men and women, and they may be present from birth or develop later. They are often found incidentally on examination or imaging. Most epigastric hernias are small, and while many never cause problems, those that are painful or enlarging are repaired surgically.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Epigastric hernias develop through weaknesses in the abdominal wall.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anatomy:} the midline of the upper abdomen has natural gaps where blood vessels pass through, and hernias can develop at these points
    \item \textbf{Weakness:} the connective tissue of the midline may be weak or stretched
    \item \textbf{Pressure:} increased pressure inside the abdomen, from straining, lifting, coughing, or pregnancy, pushes tissue through the gap
    \item \textbf{Contents:} usually a pad of fat (preperitoneal fat); bowel is less common
    \item \textbf{Multiple hernias:} people with one epigastric hernia often have several, which is why the area is checked carefully
    \item \textbf{Complications:} fat can become trapped (incarcerated) or, rarely, the blood supply can be cut off (strangulated)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Epigastric hernias produce characteristic symptoms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A lump in the upper midline of the abdomen
    \item The lump may be tender or painless
    \item Pain, which may worsen with straining, coughing, or lifting
    \item The lump may flatten or disappear when lying down
    \item A dragging or burning sensation
    \item Nausea or a feeling of fullness in some cases
    \item Symptoms of a complication: sudden pain, a lump that cannot be pushed back, and in strangulation, vomiting and severe pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions can produce lumps or pain in the upper abdomen.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Diastasis recti, separation of the abdominal muscles without a hernia
    \item Lipoma and other benign lumps of the abdominal wall
    \item Muscle strains and other musculoskeletal pain
    \item Peptic ulcer disease and other causes of upper abdominal pain
    \item Swelling of lymph nodes or other masses
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with a lump in the upper abdomen that is painful, enlarging, or concerning should be assessed by a GP and referred to a surgeon. Hernias are repaired when they cause symptoms or carry a risk of complications.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe pain at the hernia site
    \item A hernia that becomes firm, tender, and cannot be pushed back
    \item Nausea, vomiting, or an inability to pass gas or stools
    \item Fever and a painful, red lump, which may indicate strangulation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is usually clinical.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Examination:} a lump in the upper midline is felt, and the person is examined standing and lying
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} confirms the hernia and its contents when the diagnosis is unclear
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} for large, multiple, or complex hernias
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative assessment:} general health, medicines, and anaesthetic review
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Repair is a routine operation with a short recovery.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observation:} small, symptom-free hernias may be watched, though repair is often offered to prevent problems
    \item \textbf{Keyhole surgery:} laparoscopic repair with a mesh, through small incisions
    \item \textbf{Open repair:} a small incision over the hernia, with the gap closed and often reinforced with mesh
    \item \textbf{Mesh:} a synthetic patch that strengthens the repair and reduces recurrence
    \item \textbf{Day surgery:} most people go home the same day
    \item \textbf{Recovery:} return to light activity within days and to full activity within a few weeks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Recurrence of the hernia
    \item Bruising, swelling, and discomfort at the site
    \item Infection of the wound or mesh
    \item A collection of fluid (seroma)
    \item Numbness around the scar
    \item Rarely, damage to nearby structures
    \item Complications of anaesthesia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Epigastric hernia repair is very effective, and most people make a full recovery with a low risk of recurrence when mesh is used. The operation is safe, performed as day surgery, and most people return to work and normal activity within a few weeks. Serious complications are uncommon.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Follow the post-operative advice on lifting and activity
    \item Use good lifting technique to protect the repair
    \item Manage constipation and coughing, which strain the repair
    \item Keep the wound clean and watch for infection
    \item Attend follow-up appointments
    \item Seek care promptly if the lump becomes painful or cannot be pushed back
\end{itemize}
