\chapter{Cataract Surgery}
\index{Cataract Surgery}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cataract surgery is a procedure that removes the clouded natural lens of the eye and replaces it with a clear artificial lens implant, restoring vision in people with cataracts. It is one of the most commonly performed and successful operations worldwide. The standard technique is phacoemulsification, in which the cataract is broken up with ultrasound and removed through a tiny incision, after which a folded artificial intraocular lens is inserted and positioned within the lens capsule.

Most cataract surgery is performed as a day procedure under local anaesthetic with sedation, and the vast majority of people notice improved vision within days. The operation is generally offered when the cataract causes symptoms that affect daily life, such as blurred vision, glare, or difficulty driving, rather than at a fixed degree of cloudiness.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cataract is the leading cause of treatable blindness worldwide, and cataract surgery is among the most frequently performed operations in developed countries, with hundreds of thousands of procedures carried out each year. The need for surgery increases with age, and most operations are performed in people over the age of 65.

Demand is growing as populations age, and improving surgical techniques and safety have lowered the threshold for operating. Cataract surgery restores vision in the vast majority of cases and is associated with very high rates of patient satisfaction, although access to surgery remains unequal, and untreated cataract remains a major cause of blindness in low-income regions.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Cataract surgery is performed because the natural lens of the eye has become cloudy, interfering with the passage of light to the retina:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Age-related cataract:} the most common type, caused by gradual changes in the lens proteins with ageing
    \item \textbf{Congenital cataract:} present at birth or developing in childhood, sometimes due to inherited conditions or infection during pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Traumatic cataract:} following injury to the eye
    \item \textbf{Secondary cataract:} associated with diabetes, long-term steroid use, or previous eye surgery
    \item \textbf{Radiation or toxin exposure:} ultraviolet light, radiation therapy, and some medicines
\end{itemize}

The procedure removes the cloudy lens material through a small capsular opening, preserving the lens capsule to hold the new artificial lens. The operation is usually completed through an incision of around two to three millimetres, which in modern techniques is small enough to be self-sealing and usually needs no stitches.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The typical clinical picture is of symptoms rather than signs, because the eye usually looks normal from the outside:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Gradually worsening blurred or hazy vision
    \item Glare and halos around lights, especially at night
    \item Colours that appear faded or yellowed
    \item Difficulty reading, seeing in dim light, or judging distances
    \item Frequent changes to glasses prescriptions without improvement
    \item Double vision in one eye in some cases
    \item Problems with night driving and increased sensitivity to bright light
\end{itemize}

Surgery is considered when these symptoms interfere with daily activities, work, or driving, or when the cataract is dense enough to prevent examination or treatment of other eye conditions.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Other causes of gradual visual loss:} macular degeneration, glaucoma, or diabetic retinopathy, which may coexist with cataract
    \item \textbf{Refractive error:} simple short-sightedness or long-sightedness correctable with glasses
    \item \textbf{Corneal problems:} clouding or scarring of the front surface of the eye
    \item \textbf{Vitreal or retinal disease:} floaters, vitreous degeneration, or retinal detachment
    \item \textbf{Amblyopia:} reduced vision from a "lazy eye", particularly in children
    \item \textbf{Medication or systemic disease effects:} such as steroid-related or diabetic lens changes
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See an optometrist or ophthalmologist if you notice blurred vision, glare, halos, or difficulty with daily activities such as reading and driving. Regular eye examinations are especially important after the age of 60, and anyone with sudden vision loss, pain, or flashes and floaters should be reviewed urgently.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:} sudden loss of vision, severe eye pain, or sudden flashing lights with a curtain-like loss of vision, which may indicate retinal detachment or another eye emergency.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} symptoms, their duration and impact, and any conditions affecting the eyes
    \item \textbf{Visual acuity testing:} measurement of reading and distance vision with and without glasses
    \item \textbf{Slit-lamp examination:} a detailed view of the lens to confirm the type and severity of the cataract
    \item \textbf{Retinal examination:} inspection of the back of the eye after the pupil is dilated
    \item \textbf{Biometry:} precise measurement of the length and curvature of the eye to calculate the power of the intraocular lens implant
    \item \textbf{Corneal and general health checks:} assessing the surface of the eye and any systemic conditions such as diabetes that may affect outcomes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative assessment:} eye measurements, general health review, and discussion of lens options, including monofocal, multifocal, and toric lenses
    \item \textbf{Anaesthesia:} typically local anaesthetic eye drops or an injection around the eye, often with light sedation
    \item \textbf{Phacoemulsification:} breaking up and removing the cataract through a small incision
    \item \textbf{Lens implantation:} insertion of a folded artificial lens, which opens inside the eye
    \item \textbf{Day surgery:} most people go home the same day with an eye shield and eye drops
    \item \textbf{Post-operative care:} antibiotic and anti-inflammatory eye drops for several weeks, and avoidance of heavy lifting, rubbing the eye, and water in the eye for a time
    \item \textbf{Review:} early follow-up to check healing, and later review to measure the final refractive result
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Posterior capsule opacification:} clouding of the membrane behind the lens implant months or years later, treatable with a quick laser procedure
    \item \textbf{Infection (endophthalmitis):} rare but serious, requiring urgent treatment
    \item \textbf{Bleeding or swelling} inside the eye, including cystoid macular oedema
    \item \textbf{Retinal detachment:} an uncommon but serious complication, more likely in people who are short-sighted
    \item \textbf{Increased pressure in the eye} in the early post-operative period
    \item \textbf{Lens-related problems:} a slightly imperfect position of the implant or residual refractive error, often needing glasses
    \item \textbf{Corneal swelling or damage} in the early period, usually resolving
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Cataract surgery is highly successful, and the majority of people achieve significantly better vision, with many able to see well without glasses for distance after surgery. Most people notice improvement within days and can return to normal activities within a week or two. Complications are uncommon, and when they do occur most are treatable. The final visual result depends partly on the health of the rest of the eye, so people with coexisting conditions such as macular degeneration or glaucoma may have limits to their best vision even after a technically perfect operation.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Protect the eyes from ultraviolet light with sunglasses and hats
    \item Do not smoke, as smoking is linked to earlier cataract formation
    \item Control diabetes and attend regular eye checks
    \item Eat a balanced diet with plenty of fruit and vegetables
    \item Have regular eye examinations after the age of 60, or earlier if you notice symptoms
    \item After surgery, follow instructions about eye drops, shielding the eye, and avoiding strenuous activity and swimming
    \item Report any pain, redness, sudden visual loss, or increasing floaters after surgery without delay
\end{itemize}
