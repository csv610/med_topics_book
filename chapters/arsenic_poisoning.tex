\chapter{Arsenic Poisoning}
\index{Arsenic Poisoning}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Arsenic poisoning is the clinical illness that results from exposure to a toxic amount of arsenic, a naturally occurring metalloid element. It presents in two distinct forms: acute poisoning, which follows a single large dose and is a medical emergency that can be rapidly fatal, and chronic poisoning, which follows months to years of lower-level exposure and causes gradual damage to the skin, nerves, blood vessels, and internal organs. Chronic exposure is also associated with a substantially increased risk of several cancers. The recognition of arsenic poisoning depends on a careful exposure history and confirmatory laboratory testing, because the presentation can closely resemble many common illnesses.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Acute arsenic poisoning is uncommon in most developed countries and is usually the result of accidental ingestion, occupational accidents, or deliberate poisoning with an arsenic compound. Chronic arsenic poisoning is far more widespread. In regions where groundwater is naturally contaminated, such as parts of Bangladesh, India, and China, millions of people have been exposed through drinking water over many years, making it one of the largest public health problems of chemical origin. Occupational exposure in mining, smelting, and pesticide manufacture adds to the burden, and the use of arsenic in traditional medicines continues to cause poisoning in some communities.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Arsenic can enter the body through the mouth, the lungs, or the skin. Once absorbed, it interferes with the enzymes that cells use to produce energy, damages the lining of small blood vessels, and harms DNA, which underlies both the acute toxicity and the long-term risk of cancer.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Contaminated water:} the leading cause of chronic poisoning worldwide
    \item \textbf{Contaminated food:} rice, seafood, and crops grown in contaminated soil or irrigated with contaminated water
    \item \textbf{Occupational exposure:} dust and fumes in mining, smelting, and certain manufacturing processes
    \item \textbf{Deliberate ingestion:} poisoning with arsenic compounds, which have been used historically as poisons
    \item \textbf{Industrial or household products:} old pesticides, some pigments, and pressure-treated timber
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} arsenic binds to thiol groups in mitochondrial enzymes, blocking cellular energy production; it also damages capillary endothelium, causing fluid leakage, shock, and organ injury, and produces oxidative DNA damage that can initiate cancer
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features depend on whether the poisoning is acute or chronic.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute:} nausea, vomiting, and severe abdominal pain, beginning within minutes to hours
    \item \textbf{Acute:} watery or bloody diarrhoea, sometimes described as rice-water stools
    \item \textbf{Acute:} a garlic-like odour on the breath in some cases
    \item \textbf{Acute severe:} hypotension, a rapid or weak pulse, and cardiovascular collapse (shock)
    \item \textbf{Acute severe:} confusion, seizures, acute kidney injury, and respiratory difficulty
    \item \textbf{Chronic:} fatigue, anaemia, and weight loss
    \item \textbf{Chronic:} skin pigmentation changes, thickening of the palms and soles, and white bands (Mees' lines) across the nails
    \item \textbf{Chronic:} tingling, numbness, and weakness of the hands and feet from peripheral neuropathy
    \item \textbf{Chronic:} liver, heart, and circulatory disease, and an increased risk of cancer of the skin, bladder, and lung
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Arsenic poisoning can be mistaken for a number of common conditions, particularly early in its course.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute gastroenteritis or cholera during the vomiting and diarrhoea phase
    \item Sepsis or other causes of shock and multiorgan failure
    \item Poisoning by other heavy metals, such as lead or mercury
    \item Guillain--Barré syndrome or diabetic neuropathy in patients with nerve symptoms
    \item Anaemia or chronic fatigue syndrome of other cause
    \item Dermatological conditions that cause skin pigmentation, such as Addison's disease
    \item Chronic liver disease from alcohol, viruses, or other toxins
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any suspected ingestion of arsenic is an emergency. If poisoning is suspected, do not wait for symptoms to develop, and do not induce vomiting at home.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Known or suspected ingestion of arsenic, insecticide, or an unknown powder or liquid
    \item Severe vomiting, bloody diarrhoea, or signs of shock such as faintness, a rapid pulse, or confusion
    \item Seizures, difficulty breathing, or collapse
\end{itemize}

Seek medical advice promptly if you have been exposed to a contaminated water source or workplace and develop skin changes, numbness, tingling, or persistent unexplained fatigue.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is based on a history of exposure and laboratory confirmation, and in acute cases it must be made quickly because treatment is time-critical.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} potential sources of arsenic, the timing of exposure, and the pattern of symptoms
    \item \textbf{Urine arsenic level:} the principal test; levels are very high in acute poisoning and raised in chronic exposure, with speciation helping to exclude harmless organic seafood forms
    \item \textbf{Blood arsenic level:} helpful in the first hours after acute poisoning
    \item \textbf{Hair and nail analysis:} confirms exposure that occurred in previous months
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, electrolytes, renal function, and liver function
    \item \textbf{ECG:} to detect cardiac rhythm abnormalities and QT prolongation
    \item \textbf{Nerve conduction studies:} for people with symptoms of peripheral neuropathy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Acute arsenic poisoning is treated in hospital, frequently in an intensive care setting, while chronic poisoning is managed mainly by removing the source of exposure and treating the damage it has already caused.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute care:} intravenous fluids, close monitoring, and support of the circulation, breathing, and kidneys
    \item \textbf{Decontamination:} gastric lavage may be considered very early after ingestion of a large dose, but it is rarely helpful and can be hazardous
    \item \textbf{Chelation therapy:} agents such as dimercaprol or succimer (DMSA) that bind to arsenic and promote its excretion, most effective when started promptly
    \item \textbf{Dialysis:} required for severe acute kidney injury with fluid overload or biochemical disturbance
    \item \textbf{Stopping exposure:} the single most important step in chronic poisoning
    \item \textbf{Symptom treatment:} management of established nerve, skin, haematological, and organ damage
    \item \textbf{Cancer surveillance:} regular skin examination and monitoring for bladder and lung cancer after significant exposure
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} repeat arsenic testing and long-term medical review to detect late effects
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Death from shock, kidney failure, or respiratory failure in severe acute poisoning
    \item Permanent peripheral nerve damage with numbness, weakness, and difficulty walking
    \item Skin, bladder, lung, and other cancers appearing years after exposure
    \item Heart and circulatory disease, including damage to small arteries and gangrene
    \item Liver damage, diabetes, and hypertension
    \item Anaemia and impaired immunity
    \item Developmental effects in children exposed during pregnancy or early life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Acute arsenic poisoning can be fatal, but with rapid hospital care and early chelation many patients survive; the outcome depends on the dose and on how quickly treatment begins. Chronic poisoning causes cumulative damage, and some effects, such as peripheral neuropathy and the elevated risk of cancer, can persist for years even after exposure stops. Early removal of the source can halt progression and allow partial recovery of the skin and blood abnormalities. People who have been poisoned need long-term follow-up for the late complications of their exposure.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Never taste, drink, or handle unknown powders, liquids, or containers
    \item Keep pesticides, wood preservatives, and industrial products secure and out of reach of children
    \item Test well water for arsenic and use an alternative source if it is contaminated
    \item Follow workplace safety rules and wear protective equipment where arsenic exposure is possible
    \item Wash hands thoroughly after handling treated timber or industrial products
    \item Avoid unregulated herbal remedies and traditional medicines of unknown composition
    \item If exposure has occurred, attend follow-up and screening appointments
\end{itemize}
