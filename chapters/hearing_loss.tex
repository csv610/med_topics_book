\chapter{Hearing Loss}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hearing loss is a reduction in the ability to hear sounds, ranging from mild difficulty understanding speech in noisy environments to profound deafness. It is classified by the part of the ear affected: conductive loss involves the outer or middle ear, sensorineural loss involves the inner ear or auditory nerve, and mixed loss involves both. Hearing loss may be present at birth, develop gradually with age, or occur suddenly. Because hearing underpins communication, learning, and social connection, untreated hearing loss has profound effects across the lifespan.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hearing loss is among the most common chronic health conditions. Around 1.5 billion people worldwide live with some degree of hearing loss, and the number is growing as populations age. In Australia, approximately one in six people is affected, with prevalence rising from about 15\% of adults to more than half of those over 75. Around one to three babies in every thousand are born with significant hearing loss, which is why newborn hearing screening is now routine. Noise exposure, including occupational noise and loud personal audio, is a leading preventable cause in younger adults.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ageing (presbycusis):} gradual loss of cochlear hair cells and auditory nerve fibres, typically affecting high frequencies first
    \item \textbf{Noise exposure:} loud sounds damage the delicate hair cells of the inner ear, causing permanent sensorineural loss
    \item \textbf{Genetic causes:} hundreds of gene changes cause congenital, childhood, or adult-onset hearing loss
    \item \textbf{Infections:} meningitis, measles, mumps, rubella, cytomegalovirus, and chronic middle ear infection (otitis media)
    \item \textbf{Conductive causes:} earwax, foreign bodies, middle ear fluid (glue ear), eardrum perforation, and otosclerosis
    \item \textbf{Medications:} some antibiotics, chemotherapy drugs, diuretics, and other agents are ototoxic
    \item \textbf{Other causes:} head injury, tumours such as acoustic neuroma, and chronic conditions including diabetes and cardiovascular disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty hearing in conversations, especially with background noise
    \item Asking for repetition and misunderstanding what is said
    \item Turning up volume on televisions and devices
    \item Tinnitus: ringing, buzzing, or hissing sounds in the ears
    \item A sensation of fullness or blockage in the ear with conductive loss
    \item In children: speech delay, poor school performance, inattention, and behaviour problems
    \item Sudden hearing loss develops rapidly, usually in one ear, and is a medical emergency
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conductive vs sensorineural:} distinguishing the site of the problem guides treatment; conductive loss is often reversible
    \item \textbf{Sudden sensorineural hearing loss:} requires urgent treatment with steroids and must not be assumed to be wax or infection
    \item \textbf{Earwax impaction and middle ear infection:} common, treatable causes of reduced hearing
    \item \textbf{Cognitive impairment:} difficulty understanding speech can be mistaken for (or coexist with) early dementia
    \item \textbf{Autism and attention disorders:} in children, apparent not hearing may reflect other developmental conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who notices declining hearing should have it assessed, and parents should seek advice if a child appears not to hear or responds inconsistently. Sudden hearing loss requires urgent review, ideally within 24 hours.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden hearing loss, particularly if accompanied by severe dizziness, ringing, or a feeling of fullness
    \item Hearing loss with facial weakness, severe headache, or other neurological symptoms
    \item Hearing loss after a head injury
    \item A foreign object lodged deeply in the ear in a young child
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Newborn screening:} automated hearing tests in the first weeks of life detect congenital loss early
    \item \textbf{Audiometry:} determines the type, degree, and pattern of hearing loss
    \item \textbf{Examination:} inspection of the ear canal and eardrum to identify wax, fluid, or perforation
    \item \textbf{Tympanometry and acoustic reflexes:} assess middle ear function
    \item \textbf{Otoacoustic emissions and auditory brainstem response:} used in infants and when detailed assessment is needed
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT or MRI for suspected structural causes such as otosclerosis or acoustic neuroma
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} may be offered, especially in children or where family history exists
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conductive causes:} wax removal, treatment of infection, grommets for glue ear, and surgery for otosclerosis or perforations
    \item \textbf{Hearing aids:} the mainstay for sensorineural loss, programmed to the individual's audiogram
    \item \textbf{Assistive devices:} amplified telephones, alerting systems, and FM systems for classrooms
    \item \textbf{Cochlear implants:} surgically implanted devices that stimulate the auditory nerve, for severe to profound loss
    \item \textbf{Communication strategies:} lip-reading, sign language, and a hearing-friendly environment
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} auditory training and support for adults and children with hearing loss
    \item \textbf{Management of underlying conditions:} control of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and ototoxic medication review
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Social isolation, loneliness, and reduced participation in work and community life
    \item Depression and anxiety, which are substantially more common in people with untreated hearing loss
    \item Cognitive decline: hearing loss is one of the most important modifiable risk factors for dementia
    \item Falls and accidents from reduced awareness of the environment
    \item In children, delayed speech, language, and educational development
    \item Economic impact through reduced employment and productivity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the cause and timing of intervention. Conductive losses are frequently reversible with treatment. For sensorineural loss, hearing aids and other technologies provide substantial functional benefit, and early intervention in children dramatically improves language and educational outcomes. Even in older adults, treating hearing loss improves quality of life, mood, and cognitive outcomes. Sudden sensorineural hearing loss carries a better prognosis when treated urgently. Ongoing support and realistic expectations are associated with the best long-term results.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Protect ears from loud noise using earplugs or earmuffs, and limit exposure
    \item Keep personal audio devices at safe volumes, and consider noise-cancelling headphones
    \item Take breaks from noisy environments and move away from loudspeakers
    \item Have hearing tested regularly, especially from middle age or if exposed to noise
    \item Treat ear infections promptly and have wax removed professionally rather than with cotton buds
    \item Immunise against infections that cause hearing loss, including measles, mumps, rubella, and Hib
    \item See a doctor promptly for sudden hearing loss or persistent ear symptoms
\end{itemize}
