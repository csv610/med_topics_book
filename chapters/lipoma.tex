\chapter{Lipoma}
\index{Lipoma}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A lipoma is a benign, slow-growing tumour composed of mature fat cells (adipocytes). It is the most common soft-tissue tumour in adults and typically presents as a soft, mobile, painless subcutaneous nodule. Lipomas are most frequently located in the subcutaneous tissues of the trunk, neck, shoulders, upper back, and proximal limbs, though they can occasionally develop in deeper structures such as the muscles, internal organs, or gastrointestinal tract. While they are histologically benign and carry no inherent risk of malignant transformation, lipomas are clinically significant because they can mimic malignant mesenchymal tumours (such as liposarcomas), cause cosmetic distress, or produce pain and functional impairment if they compress adjacent nerves or blood vessels.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Lipomas occur in approximately 1\% of the general population. They are most commonly diagnosed in adults between the ages of 40 and 60, although they can develop in individuals of any age, including children. While solitary lipomas affect males and females equally, multiple lipomas (lipomatosis) are more frequently observed in males. The majority of lipomas are solitary, but in 5\% to 10\% of patients, multiple lesions occur. Multiple lipomas may be inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern or associated with specific rare conditions, such as Familial Multiple Lipomatosis, Dercum's disease (adiposis dolorosa), Madelung's disease (benign symmetric lipomatosis), and Gardner syndrome.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The exact cause of lipomas remains unknown, though genetic factors play a significant role. Pathologically, they are well-circumscribed, encapsulated masses of mature white adipose tissue that differ from normal fat by having a distinct fibrous capsule and lobulated architecture.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} Up to two-thirds of benign lipomas exhibit cytogenetic abnormalities, most commonly rearrangements or deletions on chromosome 12q13-15, which affect the high-mobility group AT-hook 2 (\textit{HMGA2}) gene.
    \item \textbf{Familial predisposition:} A family history of lipomas is common, especially in individuals presenting with multiple lesions, suggesting an inherited genetic susceptibility.
    \item \textbf{Metabolic factors:} While lipomas are composed of fat cells, their growth is independent of the body's overall nutritional status; they do not regress with weight loss or diet.
    \item \textbf{Local trauma:} It has been proposed that local blunt trauma may trigger the release of inflammatory cytokines that stimulate adipocyte differentiation, or cause herniation of existing subcutaneous fat, though this link remains controversial.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Lipomas typically present as slow-growing, asymptomatic masses that are detected incidentally by the patient or clinician during routine examination.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Consistency and mobility:} On palpation, lipomas have a characteristically soft, doughy, or rubbery consistency and are freely mobile over the underlying deep fascia.
    \item \textbf{Overlying skin:} The skin covering the lipoma is normal in appearance, temperature, and texture, without attachment or tethering to the lump (no dimpling when pinched).
    \item \textbf{Size:} Most lipomas are small, typically ranging from 1 to 5 cm in diameter. Those exceeding 5 cm are classified as "giant lipomas" and are more likely to extend into deeper muscular compartments.
    \item \textbf{Location:} Primarily subcutaneous, affecting the shoulders, neck, trunk, and arms. Deep-seated lipomas are less common and may be firm and less mobile.
    \item \textbf{Asymptomatic nature:} Generally painless unless they compress adjacent peripheral nerves, are located near joints where they restrict movement, or contain prominent vascular channels (such as angiolipomas, which are frequently tender).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
It is clinically vital to distinguish lipomas from other benign skin lesions and, most importantly, from malignant soft-tissue sarcomas.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Liposarcoma:} A malignant lipomatous tumour. Unlike lipomas, liposarcomas are typically firm, fixed to surrounding tissues, rapidly growing, larger than 5 cm, and often located deep within the retroperitoneum or thigh muscles.
    \item \textbf{Epidermoid cyst:} A common benign cyst containing keratin. It is distinguished by its firmer texture, attachment to the overlying skin, and the presence of a central punctum.
    \item \textbf{Abscess:} An acute, painful infectious collection characterised by overlying erythema, warmth, tenderness, and fluctuance, often accompanied by systemic symptoms such as fever.
    \item \textbf{Ganglion cyst:} A fluid-filled swelling associated with joint capsules or tendon sheaths, typically firm and transilluminates under light.
    \item \textbf{Fibroma or neurofibroma:} Benign connective tissue or nerve sheath tumours that are generally firmer, less mobile, and may be tender or associated with neurological symptoms.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should consult a general practitioner for an evaluation of any new, persistent, or changing subcutaneous lump to confirm the diagnosis and rule out malignancy.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A lump in the neck or throat that causes acute difficulty breathing, swallowing, or speaking.
    \item Rapid swelling or pain associated with high fever, rigors, or signs of spreading infection.
    \item Sudden, severe pain or loss of sensation in a limb due to acute vascular or nerve compression by a mass.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of a lipoma is primarily clinical, based on history and physical examination. However, investigations are warranted if atypical features are present.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical evaluation:} Assessing the mobility, consistency, size, and rate of growth of the lump during physical examination.
    \item \textbf{Ultrasonography:} The initial imaging modality of choice. Lipomas typically appear as well-circumscribed, homogeneous, hyperechoic masses in the subcutaneous tissue, without internal blood flow on Doppler assessment.
    \item \textbf{Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):} Indicated for deep-seated, rapidly growing, or giant masses (greater than 5 cm) to differentiate a lipoma from a liposarcoma. MRI provides excellent tissue characterisation, showing high signal intensity on T1- and T2-weighted sequences that suppresses with fat-saturation techniques.
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} Core needle biopsy or excision biopsy is performed if malignancy is suspected. Histopathological examination confirms the presence of mature adipocytes surrounded by a thin fibrous capsule.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Most lipomas are benign and asymptomatic, requiring no intervention. Management is individualised based on the size, location, and symptoms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observation and reassurance:} Appropriate for small, painless, stable lipomas where the diagnosis is clear. Periodic monitoring for any changes is advised.
    \item \textbf{Surgical excision:} The definitive treatment, usually performed under local anaesthetic. The surgeon makes an incision over the mass, dissects it free from surrounding tissues, and removes the lipoma along with its fibrous capsule to minimise the risk of recurrence.
    \item \textbf{Minimal-incision extraction:} A technique where a very small incision is made, and the lipoma is squeezed or pulled out, resulting in minimal scarring.
    \item \textbf{Liposuction:} Used for larger lipomas or those in cosmetically sensitive areas. It involves suctioning the fat tissue through a small cannula, though it carries a higher recurrence rate because the fibrous capsule may not be completely removed.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Complications arising from untreated lipomas are rare, but post-treatment complications can occur.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nerve and vascular compression:} Local pressure on adjacent nerves leading to paraesthesia, numbness, or chronic pain, or compression of local blood vessels.
    \item \textbf{Cosmetic disfigurement:} Large or multiple lipomas in visible areas can cause psychological distress and body image concerns.
    \item \textbf{Post-operative complications:} Infection of the surgical site, haematoma or seroma formation, scarring, and injury to local nerves during dissection.
    \item \textbf{Recurrence:} Return of the lipoma, usually due to incomplete excision of the fibrous capsule.
    \item \textbf{Misdiagnosis:} A malignant liposarcoma misidentified as a benign lipoma, delaying appropriate oncological treatment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals with lipomas is excellent. Lipomas are entirely benign and do not undergo malignant transformation. Surgical excision is highly effective and usually curative. The recurrence rate following complete surgical excision is less than 5\%. For patients with multiple lipomatosis syndromes, the prognosis remains good, though management is more complex due to the ongoing development of new lesions.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
There are no established measures to prevent the development of lipomas, but self-care practices can help manage the condition safely.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Regular self-examination:} Monitor existing lumps monthly for any changes in size, firmness, shape, or the development of pain.
    \item \textbf{Avoid self-treatment:} Do not attempt to squeeze, puncture, pop, or cut the lump at home, as this can cause severe infection, bleeding, and permanent scarring.
    \item \textbf{Differentiate from weight:} Recognise that dieting, weight loss, or exercise will not shrink a lipoma, and maintain a balanced lifestyle for overall health.
    \item \textbf{Professional assessment:} Always seek professional medical evaluation for any new lump to ensure an accurate diagnosis.
\end{itemize}
