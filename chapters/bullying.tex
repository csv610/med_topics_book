\chapter{Bullying}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bullying is repeated aggressive behaviour in which an individual or a group deliberately hurts, intimidates, or excludes someone who has less power to defend themselves. It may be physical, verbal, or relational, and in recent years much of it occurs online in the form of cyberbullying. Bullying is not a normal rite of passage; it is a recognised risk to physical and mental health that can cause lasting psychological harm to those targeted, and it can also have damaging long-term effects for those who bully. Effective responses involve stopping the behaviour, supporting the person affected, and addressing the environments in which bullying occurs.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bullying is common in schools, with roughly one in three children worldwide reporting having been bullied and a similar proportion admitting to having bullied others. It peaks in the early teenage years and occurs most visibly in the schoolyard, but it is also prevalent in workplaces, within families, and online. Boys are more often involved in physical bullying, while girls are more often involved in relational and cyberbullying. Bullying affects people of every background, but those perceived as different in appearance, ethnicity, sexual orientation, or disability status are more frequently targeted.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Bullying arises from a complex mix of individual, family, school, and societal factors. Those who bully may act out of a desire for power and status, a learned pattern from home, or a lack of empathy, while those targeted are often simply seen as different, less assertive, or socially isolated.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Risk factors for being targeted:} being perceived as different, shyness, low self-esteem, disability, or membership of a minority group
    \item \textbf{Risk factors for bullying others:} witnessing violence at home, harsh or inconsistent parenting, or peer groups that reward aggression
    \item \textbf{Environmental factors:} schools or workplaces with weak supervision, unclear rules, or a culture that tolerates aggression
    \item \textbf{Online factors:} the anonymity, permanence, and reach of social media make cyberbullying continuous and difficult to escape
\end{itemize}

Repeated bullying acts as a chronic stressor. It keeps the stress response switched on, raising levels of stress hormones, which over time can alter mood, sleep, appetite, and the body's handling of anxiety, and can change brain development in children and adolescents.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The effects of bullying can appear as behavioural change and physical symptoms, particularly in children.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Repeated incidents of physical, verbal, or online harassment, or being systematically left out and isolated
    \item Reluctance to go to school or work, with a drop in performance and attendance
    \item Unexplained physical symptoms such as headaches, stomach aches, and disturbed sleep
    \item Anxiety, low mood, irritability, and tearfulness
    \item Loss of appetite or overeating, with changes in weight
    \item Withdrawal from friends, family, and previously enjoyed activities
    \item Unexplained injuries, torn clothing, or damaged belongings
    \item In severe cases, panic attacks, self-harm, or thoughts of suicide
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of being bullied overlap with many other problems, so the underlying cause must be carefully explored.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anxiety disorders and depression:} can cause similar mood, sleep, and somatic symptoms
    \item \textbf{School refusal for other reasons:} such as learning difficulties or separation anxiety
    \item \textbf{Social anxiety or autism-related social difficulties:} may present with withdrawal and distress at school
    \item \textbf{Abuse or neglect within the home:} a serious alternative or coexisting cause of distress
    \item \textbf{Family stress:} divorce, bereavement, or illness at home
    \item \textbf{Physical illness:} conditions causing headaches, abdominal pain, or fatigue
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if a child shows persistent distress, physical symptoms with no clear cause, a sudden change in behaviour, or signs of self-harm, so the situation can be explored and support arranged. Adults experiencing workplace bullying should seek help from their employer's human resources team, a doctor, or a counsellor.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) or seek urgent help for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Thoughts of suicide or self-harm -- call 000 or Lifeline on 13 11 14
    \item The child being in immediate danger of physical harm
    \item The development of severe symptoms such as panic attacks, fainting, or marked withdrawal
    \item The child refusing to leave home or becoming severely distressed about going to school
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Bullying is not diagnosed with tests; it is identified by listening carefully to the person affected and to those around them.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conversation with the child or adult:} asking directly about friendships, school, and online activity in a safe, private setting
    \item \textbf{Information from parents and teachers:} accounts of incidents, changes in behaviour, and patterns of attendance
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} checking for injuries and looking for other causes of symptoms
    \item \textbf{Mental health assessment:} screening for anxiety, depression, and risk of self-harm
    \item \textbf{Review of records:} school or workplace records of reported incidents and responses
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Stopping the bullying and supporting the person's recovery are the immediate priorities.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immediate action:} report incidents to the school or workplace and keep a written record of what has happened
    \item \textbf{School measures:} a clear anti-bullying plan, closer supervision, and involvement of the school counsellor
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} counselling or cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT) to rebuild confidence and manage anxiety
    \item \textbf{Online safety:} blocking and reporting cyberbullies, adjusting privacy settings, and limiting exposure to the abuse
    \item \textbf{Family involvement:} keeping communication open and reassuring the child that they are not to blame
    \item \textbf{Medical care:} treatment of associated symptoms such as sleep problems, anxiety, or depression
    \item \textbf{Workplace action:} for adult bullying, formal complaints and involvement of human resources
    \item \textbf{Community resources:} helplines such as Kids Helpline (1800 551 800) for children and young people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Without intervention, bullying can have long-lasting effects on health, education, and relationships.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress symptoms
    \item Low self-esteem that persists into adulthood
    \item Self-harm and an increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviour
    \item School refusal, poor academic performance, and dropout
    \item Strained family relationships and social isolation
    \item Long-term health effects of chronic stress, including sleep disturbance and physical illness
    \item For those who bully, an increased risk of later antisocial behaviour and relationship difficulties
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people who are bullied recover well once the bullying stops and they receive appropriate support, and many grow stronger through the experience. The risk of lasting harm is greater when the bullying is severe, prolonged, or allowed to continue without intervention. Early recognition, a firm response from the school or workplace, and supportive relationships all greatly improve the outcome. Some children and adults continue to benefit from professional help for a time after the bullying has ended.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Prevention works best when schools, workplaces, families, and communities act together.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Schools: implement clear anti-bullying policies, promote respectful behaviour, and provide effective supervision
    \item Parents: talk openly with children about their day, take reports seriously, and teach digital safety
    \item Encourage children to stay connected with friends and to seek help from a trusted adult
    \item Build self-esteem and assertiveness through activities the person enjoys
    \item Teach and model kindness, empathy, and the willingness to stand up for others
    \item Monitor online activity and keep shared devices in communal family spaces where possible
    \item Respond to online abuse calmly: save evidence, block the person, and report the content
    \item Adults: know your workplace rights and report bullying through the proper channels
\end{itemize}
