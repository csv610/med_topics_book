\chapter{Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), the most common form of motor neurone disease (MND), is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder in which the motor neurones that control voluntary muscles gradually degenerate and die. The name reflects its two pathological hallmarks: \emph{amyotrophy}, the wasting of muscles from denervation, and \emph{lateral sclerosis}, the scarring in the lateral columns of the spinal cord seen at autopsy. The disease causes progressive weakness, muscle wasting, and loss of function, eventually involving speech, swallowing, and breathing. The sensory systems and the mind are usually preserved, although mild cognitive changes occur in a proportion of people. There is no cure, but multidisciplinary care, symptom control, and disease-modifying treatment can slow progression and substantially improve quality of life.

\section*{Epidemiology}
ALS is uncommon, affecting roughly 1.5 to 2.5 people per 100,000 per year, with a lifetime risk of about 1 in 300 to 1 in 400. It most often begins between the ages of 50 and 70, and men are affected slightly more often than women. About 5 to 10\% of cases are familial, inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern in most families, while the remainder are sporadic with no clear family history. The disease occurs in all populations, although historical clusters such as the high incidence reported on the Pacific island of Guam attracted particular attention. Global incidence is expected to rise as populations age.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The cause of ALS is not fully understood, but a combination of genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers is thought to drive motor neurone death through a common final pathway. Both familial and sporadic forms converge on the same process of progressive upper and lower motor neurone loss.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Familial genes:} mutations in genes including \textit{C9orf72}, \textit{SOD1}, \textit{TARDBP}, and \textit{FUS} account for the majority of inherited cases and have illuminated key mechanisms in sporadic disease.
    \item \textbf{Cellular damage:} abnormal protein aggregation, impaired RNA processing, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation all contribute to neuronal injury.
    \item \textbf{Spread of disease:} degeneration typically begins in a single region and spreads along connected nerve pathways, which explains the gradual expansion of symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} older age, male sex, and possibly smoking increase risk; head trauma and strenuous physical exertion have been studied, but their contributions remain uncertain.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The first symptoms depend on which muscle groups are affected and usually begin insidiously, often on one side of the body:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lower motor neurone signs:} weakness, wasting, muscle twitching (fasciculations), and cramps.
    \item \textbf{Upper motor neurone signs:} stiffness, slowness of movement, and overactive reflexes, including an extensor plantar response.
    \item \textbf{Limb onset:} difficulty with fine movements such as buttoning a shirt, tripping, or foot drop.
    \item \textbf{Bulbar onset:} slurred speech (dysarthria) and difficulty swallowing (dysphagia), with choking episodes.
    \item \textbf{Respiratory involvement:} breathlessness on exertion and poor sleep quality as the breathing muscles weaken.
    \item \textbf{Other features:} fatigue, weight loss, and, in a minority, mild cognitive or behavioural change including frontotemporal dysfunction.
\end{itemize}

Sensation, eye movements, and bladder function are characteristically preserved, which is a helpful diagnostic distinction.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Several conditions can mimic early ALS, and many are treatable:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cervical spondylosis with spinal cord compression.
    \item Multifocal motor neuropathy, an immune-mediated nerve disorder that responds to treatment.
    \item Myasthenia gravis, causing fatigable weakness.
    \item Benign fasciculation syndrome, with muscle twitching but no true weakness.
    \item Inherited spinal muscular atrophy and other motor neuropathies.
    \item Thyroid disease, vitamin B12 deficiency, and other metabolic causes of weakness.
    \item Structural lesions such as stroke, tumour, or syringomyelia affecting the brain or cord.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor promptly if you develop persistent, progressive weakness, muscle wasting, slurred speech, swallowing difficulty, or unexplained muscle twitching, because early referral to a neurologist is important for diagnosis and management.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden difficulty breathing.
    \item Sudden weakness, numbness, or speech difficulty, which may indicate a stroke that can mimic ALS.
    \item Choking or an inability to swallow food or fluids.
    \item Any sudden, severe symptom that causes concern.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
There is no single test for ALS; the diagnosis is clinical, supported by investigations that exclude other conditions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurological examination:} assessment of weakness, wasting, fasciculations, reflexes, and plantar responses to document both upper and lower motor neurone involvement.
    \item \textbf{Electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction studies:} demonstrate the pattern of acute and chronic denervation characteristic of ALS and exclude neuropathy.
    \item \textbf{MRI of the brain and spine:} excludes tumours, cord compression, stroke, and other structural mimics.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} exclude thyroid disease, vitamin deficiencies, and other metabolic causes.
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture:} occasionally used if inflammatory or infectious causes are suspected.
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} offered when a familial form is suspected, and increasingly in sporadic cases to inform prognosis and reproductive decisions.
    \item \textbf{Lung function tests:} measurement of breathing strength, used to time respiratory support.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
ALS is best managed by a specialist multidisciplinary team aiming to slow progression, control symptoms, and preserve quality of life:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Disease-modifying medication:} riluzole modestly slows progression, and newer agents such as edaravone are used in selected people.
    \item \textbf{Symptom control:} medicines for cramps, drooling, spasticity, mood, and emotional lability.
    \item \textbf{Nutritional support:} dietary advice, high-calorie supplements, and gastrostomy feeding when swallowing becomes unsafe or weight loss is marked.
    \item \textbf{Breathing support:} non-invasive ventilation, particularly at night, which improves survival and quality of life.
    \item \textbf{Speech and swallowing therapy:} communication aids, including eye-gaze technology, and strategies for safe swallowing.
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy and occupational therapy:} to maintain strength, mobility, and independence, and to provide equipment and home adaptations.
    \item \textbf{Palliative care:} expert symptom control, psychosocial support, and advance care planning for the person and their family.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
As the disease advances, several complications can arise:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive loss of mobility with wheelchair dependence.
    \item Malnutrition and weight loss from swallowing difficulties.
    \item Aspiration pneumonia from food or fluid entering the airway.
    \item Respiratory failure from weakened breathing muscles.
    \item Falls and fractures related to weakness and fatigue.
    \item Anxiety, depression, and emotional lability, affecting the person and their carers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
ALS is progressive, and most people live three to five years from symptom onset, although around one in ten survive ten years or more and a few live considerably longer. Older age at onset, bulbar-onset disease, and more rapid progression are associated with a shorter course. Non-invasive ventilation, gastrostomy feeding, and coordinated specialist care improve quality of life and extend survival. The individual outlook varies greatly and should be discussed openly and sensitively with the treating neurologist and the multidisciplinary team.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
There is no proven way to prevent ALS. For people living with the disease, self-care focuses on maintaining comfort, function, and quality of life:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Follow the multidisciplinary care plan and attend regular reviews.
    \item Use aids and home adaptations early to conserve energy and prevent falls.
    \item Maintain nutrition and hydration, and plan ahead for feeding support.
    \item Keep the chest clear using assisted coughing techniques as breathing weakens.
    \item Discuss advance care planning with family and clinicians.
    \item Seek practical and emotional support for carers, who face major physical and psychological demands.
\end{itemize}
