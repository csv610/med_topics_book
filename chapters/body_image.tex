\chapter{Body Image}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Body image is the way a person perceives, thinks, and feels about their own body and appearance. It is a multidimensional construct that includes perceptual elements (how a person sees their size and shape), affective elements (how they feel about their looks), and behavioural elements (how they act on those feelings). Body image exists on a spectrum. A positive body image is associated with acceptance, appreciation, and respect for the body, whereas a negative body image involves persistent dissatisfaction, shame, or a distorted perception of appearance. Poor body image is not a diagnosis in itself, but it is one of the strongest risk factors for eating disorders, depression, and unhealthy weight-control behaviours, and it deserves the same seriousness as any other health concern.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Body dissatisfaction is extremely common, particularly among adolescents and young adults. Community surveys consistently report that a substantial proportion of teenage girls, and a growing number of boys, are unhappy with some aspect of their appearance. Weight and shape are the most frequent sources of concern, but skin, hair, muscularity, and facial features also matter. Body image problems affect people across the whole weight range, including those of normal or low weight, and are increasingly reported in children under twelve. Rates of body dissatisfaction have risen over recent decades, and heavy use of image-based social media has been linked in longitudinal studies to worse body image, especially in girls and young women.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Media and cultural ideals:} pervasive, digitally altered images of extreme thinness and muscularity set standards that most people cannot realistically meet.
    \item \textbf{Social media:} constant upward comparison with peers and with edited self-images intensifies dissatisfaction and normalises appearance-fixing behaviours.
    \item \textbf{Family and peer influences:} teasing, criticism, and parental over-emphasis on appearance or weight are strongly and consistently associated with poor body image.
    \item \textbf{Personality traits:} perfectionism, low self-esteem, neuroticism, and a strong tendency to compare oneself with others increase vulnerability.
    \item \textbf{Biological changes:} the rapid growth, weight gain, and hormonal shifts of puberty occur at a developmental stage when social evaluation is especially salient.
    \item \textbf{Psychological mechanisms:} biased attention to perceived flaws, selective recall of critical comments, and a distorted internal mental picture of the body act to maintain negative body image over time.
    \item \textbf{Sociocultural pressure:} weight stigma, sexism, and professions or sports that reward a particular physique create additional pressure on vulnerable individuals.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Body image problems are largely internal, but they typically show themselves through behaviours and mood:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent dissatisfaction with weight, shape, or specific body parts
    \item Frequent weighing, measuring, or mirror checking, or conversely, avoidance of mirrors entirely
    \item Avoiding situations where the body is visible, such as swimming, changing rooms, or intimacy
    \item Wearing loose or baggy clothing to hide the body
    \item Constant comparison of one's own body with others, both in person and online
    \item Restrictive dieting, skipping meals, over-exercising, or purging
    \item Self-deprecating comments about appearance and perceived lack of discipline
    \item Low mood, anxiety, social withdrawal, and reduced performance at school or work
    \item In severe cases, the preoccupation meets criteria for body dysmorphic disorder or an eating disorder
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal, mild body dissatisfaction:} transient or minor concerns that do not impair functioning or cause significant distress.
    \item \textbf{Body dysmorphic disorder:} intense preoccupation with a specific, usually minor or imagined defect, with repetitive checking or concealing behaviours.
    \item \textbf{Muscle dysmorphia:} a subtype of body dysmorphic disorder in which a person believes they are not sufficiently muscular or lean.
    \item \textbf{Eating disorders:} anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, and binge eating disorder, which involve disturbed eating and weight-control behaviour alongside body image disturbance.
    \item \textbf{Depression and anxiety disorders:} these can present with low self-worth and negative self-evaluation that centres on appearance.
    \item \textbf{Gender dysphoria:} distress arising from a mismatch between one's experienced gender identity and the physical body.
    \item \textbf{Weight stigma and bullying:} situations where the problem is primarily the attitudes and behaviour of others rather than the person's own perception.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a GP or a mental health professional if poor body image is causing significant distress, is interfering with school, work, or relationships, or is driving restrictive dieting, over-exercising, or other unhealthy behaviours. Seek help promptly if body image concerns are accompanied by dramatic weight loss, binge eating, vomiting, or withdrawal from usual activities, because these can signal an emerging eating disorder. Parents and teachers should take body image complaints in young people seriously and arrange assessment early rather than waiting for the problem to resolve on its own.

\textbf{Seek immediate help if there are thoughts of self-harm or suicide.} Call 000 (or local emergency services), contact a crisis line such as Lifeline on 13 11 14, or attend the nearest hospital emergency department.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical interview:} a sensitive discussion of eating habits, exercise, weight-control behaviours, and the impact of body image on mood and daily life.
    \item \textbf{Screening questionnaires:} validated tools that assess body dissatisfaction and eating-disorder risk, which can structure the assessment.
    \item \textbf{Mental health assessment:} screening for depression, anxiety, eating disorders, and body dysmorphic disorder, including a risk assessment for self-harm.
    \item \textbf{Physical assessment:} weight, height, and BMI plotted over time, along with examination for signs of malnutrition, vomiting, or excessive exercise.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} may be indicated where restrictive eating or purging is present, to check electrolytes, iron, and thyroid function.
    \item \textbf{Family involvement:} with the young person's consent, interviews with parents or partners give context and support engagement in treatment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychoeducation:} teaching about the diversity of healthy bodies and the misleading nature of edited and filtered images.
    \item \textbf{Cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT):} the best-evidenced approach; it helps identify and challenge unhelpful thoughts and gradually reduces checking, avoidance, and comparison behaviours.
    \item \textbf{Media literacy:} developing critical skills to deconstruct advertising and social media content.
    \item \textbf{Mindfulness-based approaches:} techniques for noticing body-related thoughts and urges without acting on them.
    \item \textbf{Family-based treatment:} for young people with eating-disorder behaviour, involving the family in meals and recovery is highly effective.
    \item \textbf{Treating co-morbid conditions:} addressing underlying depression or anxiety improves overall outcomes.
    \item \textbf{Allied health support:} a dietitian supports balanced, flexible nutrition and safe approaches to weight, while a psychologist addresses the emotional dimension.
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular review of mood, weight, eating behaviour, and safety throughout treatment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Development of anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, or binge eating disorder
    \item Depression, anxiety disorders, and social withdrawal
    \item Unhealthy weight-control practices, including crash dieting, fasting, laxative misuse, and compulsive exercise
    \item Impaired academic or occupational performance from preoccupation and low confidence
    \item Use of unregulated diet pills, supplements, or other risky products
    \item Unnecessary cosmetic procedures performed to fix perceived flaws
    \item In the most severe cases, self-harm and suicidal thoughts or behaviour
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Body image is changeable and can improve substantially with time and appropriate support. Many young people find their concerns lessen as they mature and develop a more stable identity. Psychological treatment, particularly CBT, produces meaningful and durable improvement in the majority of cases, and family-based treatment has excellent results for young people with eating-disorder behaviour. Where a full eating disorder or body dysmorphic disorder has developed, specialist treatment is required, and outcomes are consistently better with early intervention. Without help, negative body image tends to persist and may intensify, so seeking assistance early -- even for concerns that seem minor -- is strongly recommended.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Practise self-compassion; speak to yourself as you would to a close friend.
    \item Limit time on image-heavy social media and unfollow accounts that promote unrealistic ideals.
    \item Focus on what the body can do -- strength, movement, and health -- rather than only how it looks.
    \item Eat regularly and flexibly, and avoid labelling foods as "good" or "bad".
    \item Surround yourself with people who do not criticise bodies, including your own.
    \item Refuse to comment on other people's size or shape, and challenge such comments when you hear them.
    \item Model a positive, accepting attitude to food and bodies in front of children and young people.
    \item Seek help early if body concerns are affecting your mood, eating, or behaviour.
\end{itemize}
