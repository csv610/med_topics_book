\chapter{Cognitive behaviour therapy}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{This chapter provides an AI‑assisted educational overview of cognitive behavioural therapy. It is not a substitute for professional medical advice; please consult a qualified healthcare provider for diagnosis or treatment.}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT) is a structured, time-limited psychological treatment that focuses on the links between thoughts, feelings, physical sensations, and behaviours. Rather than treating a condition as an illness to be cured, CBT works on the idea that unhelpful patterns of thinking and behaviour can maintain emotional distress, and that these patterns can be learned and changed. It is one of the most widely studied psychological therapies and is a recommended first-line treatment for many common mental health conditions, including anxiety and depressive disorders. CBT can be delivered individually, in groups, online, or through self-help programs, and is often used alongside medication.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Mental health conditions for which CBT is commonly used affect a large proportion of the population. Around one in five adults experiences a common mental health problem in any given year, with anxiety and depression the most frequent. CBT is among the most widely accessed psychological treatments in high-income countries, and demand for it typically exceeds availability. It is used across all age groups, including children, adolescents, and older adults. Access can be limited by cost, waiting times, and a shortage of trained therapists, which has prompted growth in digital and low-intensity CBT programs.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
CBT is not a disease with a single cause; instead, it is a treatment built on a model of how psychological problems are maintained. Key elements of that model include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Thoughts (cognitions):} distorted or unhelpful interpretations of events, such as catastrophising, overgeneralising, or black-and-white thinking
    \item \textbf{Behaviours:} avoidance, withdrawal, or safety-seeking actions that prevent people from testing their fears or learning that situations are manageable
    \item \textbf{Emotions:} distress such as anxiety, low mood, anger, or shame that is intensified by unhelpful thoughts and avoidance
    \item \textbf{Physical sensations:} bodily responses such as a racing heart, muscle tension, or fatigue, which can be misinterpreted as danger
    \item \textbf{Learning and conditioning:} earlier experiences can associate certain situations with threat, so that later similar situations trigger the same reactions
\end{itemize}

CBT aims to break this cycle by helping the person identify and reappraise unhelpful thoughts and gradually change avoidant behaviours, so that new learning replaces the old patterns.

\section*{Clinical Features}
CBT itself is not a condition with symptoms; rather, the features that suggest someone may benefit from CBT are the symptoms of the underlying problem:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent worry, low mood, irritability, or panic
    \item Avoidance of situations that provoke fear or distress
    \item Difficulty concentrating or intrusive negative thoughts
    \item Low self-esteem, excessive guilt, or self-criticism
    \item Sleep disturbance, fatigue, or physical tension
    \item Withdrawal from social, work, or family activities
    \item Behaviours such as repeated checking, compulsive rituals, or substance use used to cope
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before starting CBT, a clinician will usually determine whether the person has a diagnosable condition and whether CBT is the appropriate treatment. Considerations include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Depression vs.\ bipolar disorder, where CBT is used differently and mood stabilisers are often needed
    \item Anxiety disorders vs.\ underlying medical conditions such as thyroid disease or cardiac problems
    \item Psychotic disorders, where more specialised therapy and medication are usually required
    \item Substance use disorders, which may need to be addressed before or alongside therapy
    \item Neurodevelopmental conditions such as ADHD or autism, which may require adapted approaches
    \item Normal reactions to life stress or grief, which may not require formal therapy
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Speak to a GP or mental health professional if emotional or behavioural problems persist for more than two weeks, are causing significant distress, or are affecting work, school, or relationships. A GP can arrange a mental health treatment plan, which provides a Medicare rebate for sessions with a psychologist.

\textbf{Seek immediate help for thoughts of suicide or self-harm.} Call 000, contact Lifeline on 13 11 14, or go to the nearest hospital emergency department. Crisis care takes priority over starting therapy.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical interview:} a detailed history of the presenting problem, its triggers, and its impact on daily life
    \item \textbf{Mental state examination:} assessment of mood, thinking, perception, and risk
    \item \textbf{Standardised questionnaires:} tools such as the PHQ-9 for depression and GAD-7 for anxiety to measure severity and track progress
    \item \textbf{Functional analysis:} identifying the situations, thoughts, feelings, and behaviours that maintain the problem
    \item \textbf{Physical assessment:} blood tests or other checks to exclude medical causes of symptoms
    \item \textbf{Formulation:} the therapist works with the person to build an individualised explanation of how the problem developed and is maintained
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Core CBT techniques:} thought records, cognitive restructuring, behavioural experiments, and problem-solving skills
    \item \textbf{Exposure therapy:} gradual, structured facing of feared situations to reduce avoidance
    \item \textbf{Behavioural activation:} scheduling pleasant and valued activities to lift mood
    \item \textbf{Relapse prevention:} identifying early warning signs and planning how to manage setbacks
    \item \textbf{Homework between sessions:} practising skills in everyday life is central to CBT
    \item \textbf{Combined treatment:} CBT is often used with antidepressant medication for moderate to severe depression
    \item \textbf{Low-intensity options:} guided self-help, online programs, and group courses where suitable
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Worsening of distress early in therapy, particularly when first confronting avoided situations
    \item Anxiety about undertaking homework or exposure tasks
    \item Frustration or disappointment if progress is slower than hoped
    \item A focus on thinking styles may not suit people with complex trauma or severe mental illness, who may need other approaches
    \item Risk of deterioration if therapy is delayed or discontinued and the person does not practise the skills
    \item Suicidality or self-harm in severe depression, which requires urgent care rather than waiting for therapy sessions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
CBT is an effective treatment for many people. Studies show that a substantial proportion of people with anxiety and depressive disorders improve significantly, and improvements often last after therapy ends. Outcomes depend on the condition, its severity, the person's engagement with homework, and the quality of the therapeutic relationship. Some people need further sessions or a top-up course later; others may need medication or a different therapy if CBT does not help. In many cases, CBT reduces the risk of relapse compared with no treatment, especially when skills are maintained.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Learn to recognise unhelpful thinking patterns and question their accuracy
    \item Keep up relaxation, breathing exercises, and mindfulness as daily habits
    \item Maintain regular sleep, physical activity, and a balanced diet
    \item Practise gradually facing feared situations rather than avoiding them
    \item Build a support network of family, friends, or peer groups
    \item Use self-help books and reputable online CBT programs to reinforce skills
    \item Seek help early when symptoms first appear, rather than waiting for them to worsen
\end{itemize}
