\chapter{Medicines to treat nicotine dependence}
\index{Medicines to treat nicotine dependence}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Medicines to treat nicotine dependence are pharmacotherapeutic agents clinically proven to assist individuals in achieving smoking cessation. These medicines work by mitigating the physiological cravings and distressing withdrawal symptoms associated with tobacco and nicotine abstinence. They are divided into two main categories: nicotine replacement therapy (NRT), which delivers controlled, clean doses of nicotine without the toxic gases and carcinogens found in tobacco smoke; and non-nicotine prescription medicines, which target specific neurotransmitter pathways in the brain. Incorporating these medicines into a cessation plan significantly increases the likelihood of long-term abstinence, particularly when combined with structured behavioural support.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Tobacco smoking remains the leading cause of preventable disease, disability, and death in many countries, accounting for approximately 20,000 deaths annually. While approximately 11\% of adults smoke daily, the majority express a desire to quit. However, attempting to quit without assistance (often referred to as "cold turkey") has a very low long-term success rate of only 3\% to 5\% at 12 months. Clinical trials show that using first-line cessation medicines doubles or triples the success rate. Consequently, guidelines in many countries recommend offering pharmacotherapy to all nicotine-dependent smokers who are ready to make a quit attempt.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Nicotine dependence is driven by the drug's action on the brain's reward system, and cessation medicines are designed to target these neurochemical pathways:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nicotinic receptor activation:} Inhaled nicotine binds to nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) in the brain, triggering the release of dopamine in the mesolimbic pathway, which reinforces smoking behaviour.
    \item \textbf{Nicotine replacement therapy (NRT):} By providing a slow, controlled release of nicotine that binds to nAChRs, NRT reduces withdrawal symptoms and cravings without inducing the rapid, high-concentration dopamine spikes caused by cigarette smoke.
    \item \textbf{Varenicline:} A selective alpha-4 beta-2 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor partial agonist. It binds to the receptor, producing a mild dopaminergic effect that relieves withdrawal symptoms, while simultaneously blocking inhaled nicotine from binding, reducing the rewarding effect of smoking.
    \item \textbf{Bupropion:} A selective reuptake inhibitor of dopamine and noradrenaline. It increases the synaptic concentrations of these neurotransmitters, mimicking the neurochemical effects of nicotine to suppress withdrawal symptoms and reduce cravings.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Individuals undergoing treatment for nicotine dependence present with a range of symptoms, including withdrawal effects and potential side effects from the medicines:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nicotine withdrawal symptoms:} Intense cravings, irritability, anxiety, mood swings, difficulty concentrating, insomnia, restlessness, and an increased appetite or weight gain.
    \item \textbf{Local NRT side effects:} Skin erythema and pruritus at the site of nicotine patches; localized irritation of the mouth or throat, hiccups, and dyspepsia associated with oral NRT (gum, lozenges, inhalators, or sprays).
    \item \textbf{Varenicline side effects:} Nausea (which occurs in up to 30\% of patients but often resolves over time), insomnia, vivid or abnormal dreams, headaches, and rare changes in mood or behaviour.
    \item \textbf{Bupropion side effects:} Insomnia (the most common side effect, which can be managed by avoiding pre-bedtime dosing), dry mouth, headaches, dizziness, and agitation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When managing patients during a quit attempt, it is important to differentiate between drug side effects, nicotine withdrawal, and other underlying disorders:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nicotine withdrawal vs. primary mood disorder:} Distinguishing the transient anxiety, depression, and irritability of nicotine withdrawal (which typically peaks within 1 week and resolves in 2 to 4 weeks) from a pre-existing psychiatric illness.
    \item \textbf{Medication-induced neuropsychiatric symptoms vs. smoking cessation-induced depression:} Differentiating rare psychiatric side effects of varenicline or bupropion from clinical depression triggered by the psychological loss of smoking.
    \item \textbf{NRT-related dyspepsia vs. gastrointestinal pathology:} Ruling out peptic ulcer disease or gastro-oesophageal reflux disease in patients who experience dyspepsia due to swallowing nicotine-containing saliva rather than using correct oral NRT administration techniques.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Smokers should consult a GP or pharmacist before starting cessation medicines, particularly if they are pregnant, breastfeeding, under 18, or have chronic medical conditions.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anaphylaxis:} Swelling of the lips, tongue, or throat, severe dyspnoea, wheezing, or collapse after taking a medicine.
    \item \textbf{Acute suicidal ideation:} Sudden, severe changes in mood, depression, or thoughts of self-harm while taking varenicline or bupropion.
    \item \textbf{Seizures:} A rare but serious complication of bupropion therapy, requiring immediate emergency intervention.
    \item \textbf{Acute cardiovascular symptoms:} Sudden crushing chest pain, severe shortness of breath, or palpitations, particularly in patients with unstable cardiovascular disease using NRT.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Before initiating pharmacotherapy, a clinician must evaluate the patient's level of nicotine dependence and screen for contraindications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fagerstr\"{o}m Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND):} A validated questionnaire measuring smoking behaviours (e.g., time to first cigarette in the morning) to determine the severity of addiction and guide NRT dosing.
    \item \textbf{Assessment of psychiatric history:} Screening for a history of depression, bipolar disorder, or schizophrenia, as quitting smoking can alter the metabolism of psychiatric drugs (e.g., clozapine) and requires close monitoring.
    \item \textbf{Screening for seizure risk factors:} Evaluating the patient for a history of epilepsy, eating disorders (bulimia or anorexia nervosa), or alcohol withdrawal, all of which contraindicate the use of bupropion.
    \item \textbf{Renal function assessment:} Calculating eGFR, as varenicline is renally cleared and requires a lower dose in patients with severe renal impairment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of nicotine dependence involves selecting the appropriate medicine, optimizing its dose, and combining it with behavioural support:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Combination Nicotine Replacement Therapy:} The recommended NRT approach, combining a long-acting nicotine patch (for steady, 24-hour background control) with a short-acting product (gum, lozenge, or spray) used as-needed for sudden, intense cravings.
    \item \textbf{Varenicline therapy:} Initiated 1 to 2 weeks before the target quit date, starting at a low dose (0.5 mg daily) and titrating up to 1 mg twice daily, continued for a standard course of 12 to 24 weeks.
    \item \textbf{Bupropion therapy:} Commenced 1 to 2 weeks before the quit date, starting at 150 mg daily for 3 days, then increasing to 150 mg twice daily (separated by at least 8 hours), continued for 7 to 12 weeks.
    \item \textbf{Behavioural counselling:} Referral to structured support programmes (such as Quitline or cognitive behavioural therapy), which significantly enhances the efficacy of all cessation medicines.
    \item \textbf{Follow-up and support:} Scheduling regular weekly or fortnightly clinical reviews to monitor adherence, manage side effects, and prevent relapse.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The primary complications relate to treatment failure, medication side effects, or drug-specific risks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Relapse to smoking:} Returning to regular smoking, which should be viewed as a common part of the quitting process, prompting a re-evaluation of treatment.
    \item \textbf{Seizures (bupropion):} A dose-related risk that increases significantly if bupropion is prescribed to patients with pre-existing risk factors or taken in overdose.
    \item \textbf{Severe neuropsychiatric reactions:} Rare but serious reports of agitation, hostility, depression, and suicidal behaviour, requiring immediate cessation of the suspected medicine.
    \item \textbf{Incorrect oral NRT usage:} Swallowing nicotine gum or lozenges too quickly, leading to poor systemic absorption and local side effects like throat irritation and hiccups.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for achieving long-term smoking cessation is significantly improved with the use of medicines. One-year continuous abstinence rates are approximately 15\% to 20\% for single NRT or bupropion, 25\% to 30\% for combination NRT, and 30\% to 35\% for varenicline. Successful cessation at any age yields immediate and profound health benefits: within 24 hours, carbon monoxide levels return to normal; within 1 to 9 months, coughing and shortness of breath decrease; and by 10 to 15 years, the risk of coronary heart disease and lung cancer is halved compared to a continuing smoker.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Patients can implement self-care strategies to manage side effects and support their quit attempt:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Learn proper NRT techniques:} Chew nicotine gum slowly until the taste is strong, then "park" it between the cheek and gums; suck nicotine lozenges rather than chewing or swallowing them.
    \item \textbf{Manage varenicline-induced nausea:} Take varenicline with food and a full glass of water to reduce stomach irritation.
    \item \textbf{Utilise the "4 Ds" for cravings:} Practice Delay (waiting 5 minutes for the urge to pass), Deep breathing, Drinking water slowly, and Distraction (finding an activity).
    \item \textbf{Prepare the environment:} Remove all tobacco products, lighters, and ashtrays from the home, car, and workplace prior to starting pharmacotherapy.
\end{itemize}
