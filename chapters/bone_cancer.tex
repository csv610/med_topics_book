\chapter{Bone Cancer}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bone cancer is a malignant tumour that arises within bone. Primary bone cancers begin in the bone itself and are uncommon; secondary bone cancer, or bone metastasis, occurs when cancer from another site -- most often the breast, lung, prostate, kidney, or thyroid -- spreads to the skeleton, and it is far more common. The major primary bone tumours are osteosarcoma, which mainly affects adolescents and young adults and usually arises around the knee; Ewing sarcoma, a highly malignant tumour of children and young people; and chondrosarcoma, which develops from cartilage cells and typically affects older adults. Primary bone cancer is treated by specialist teams using surgery, chemotherapy, and sometimes radiotherapy, while secondary bone cancer is managed as part of treatment for the underlying disease, with the aim of controlling symptoms and preserving quality of life.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Primary bone cancers are rare, accounting for well under one percent of all cancers. Osteosarcoma has an incidence of roughly two to three cases per million people per year and shows a bimodal age distribution, peaking in the second decade of life and again in older adults, often in the setting of Paget's disease. Ewing sarcoma is seen almost exclusively in children and young adults. Chondrosarcoma occurs mainly after the age of 40. There is a slight male predominance for most primary bone tumours. Secondary bone cancer, in contrast, is very common: bone is the third most frequent site of cancer spread, and the skeleton is affected in the majority of people who die of advanced breast or prostate cancer.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Osteosarcoma:} arises from primitive bone-forming cells (osteoblasts); risk is increased by rapid bone growth in adolescence, prior radiotherapy, and conditions such as Paget's disease, retinoblastoma, and Li-Fraumeni syndrome.
    \item \textbf{Ewing sarcoma:} characterised by a translocation involving the \textit{EWSR1} gene on chromosome 22; its precise cell of origin is uncertain but is thought to be a primitive neuroectodermal cell.
    \item \textbf{Chondrosarcoma:} develops from cartilage cells; it is more common in older adults and may arise secondarily within an enchondroma or osteochondroma, particularly in conditions such as Ollier disease.
    \item \textbf{Genetic syndromes:} inherited cancer predisposition syndromes and some benign bone disorders increase the risk of primary bone tumours.
    \item \textbf{Secondary bone cancer:} cancer cells travel via the bloodstream or lymphatics and colonise the bone marrow, where they stimulate osteoclasts (bone-destroying cells) to resorb bone; the result is painful, weakened bone prone to fracture.
    \item \textbf{Ionising radiation:} prior therapeutic radiation and, less commonly, occupational exposure increase the risk of primary bone sarcoma.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent or progressive bone pain, often worse at night and at rest, is the hallmark symptom.
    \item A palpable lump or swelling over the affected bone
    \item Reduced range of movement in a nearby joint
    \item Limping or pathological fracture through a weakened bone
    \item Fever, weight loss, and fatigue, particularly with Ewing sarcoma
    \item Localised warmth and tenderness over the tumour
    \item In secondary bone cancer, bone pain, new-onset fractures, and sometimes features of hypercalcaemia such as thirst, confusion, and constipation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Osteomyelitis:} bone infection causing pain, swelling, fever, and sometimes a sinus; it can mimic tumours radiologically.
    \item \textbf{Benign bone tumours:} osteochondroma, enchondroma, osteoid osteoma, and giant cell tumour, which are commoner than malignant tumours and usually less painful.
    \item \textbf{Bone cysts:} simple and aneurysmal bone cysts present with pain or fracture.
    \item \textbf{Stress fracture:} an overuse injury that produces localised pain in athletes.
    \item \textbf{Growing pains:} benign leg pain in children, which is typically bilateral and settles with activity.
    \item \textbf{Eosinophilic granuloma:} a benign lesion of Langerhans cell histiocytosis that can present as a painful lytic bone lesion in children.
    \item \textbf{Metabolic bone disease:} hyperparathyroidism and Paget's disease can produce bone pain and radiological changes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any persistent bone pain, particularly in a child or young adult, or pain that is worse at night, waking the person from sleep, or associated with a swelling, should be assessed promptly. A new fracture that occurs with minimal trauma, or recurrent pain at a known site of cancer, also warrants medical review. Do not assume that bone pain is a sporting injury until it has been properly investigated.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe pain after a fall or minor injury that may indicate a pathological fracture
    \item Numbness, weakness, or loss of bladder or bowel control, which may signal spinal cord compression
    \item Sudden confusion, which can occur with dangerously high calcium levels
    \item Severe breathlessness or chest pain, which may indicate spread of cancer to the lungs
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} careful assessment of the character of the pain, age, associated symptoms, and any family or environmental risk factors.
    \item \textbf{Plain radiographs:} the first imaging test; primary bone tumours often show characteristic appearances such as a "sunburst" periosteal reaction in osteosarcoma and "onion-skin" layering in Ewing sarcoma.
    \item \textbf{MRI:} the key investigation for assessing the extent of the tumour within the bone and surrounding soft tissues, and for surgical planning.
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} defines the bony anatomy, detects lung metastases, and guides biopsy.
    \item \textbf{Bone scan and PET-CT:} whole-body imaging to detect additional bone lesions and distant spread.
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} a needle or open biopsy is essential to confirm the diagnosis and determine the tumour grade; it must be performed by, or in consultation with, the treating sarcoma team to avoid compromising later surgery.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, inflammatory markers, alkaline phosphatase, and serum calcium; in secondary disease, tumour markers for the underlying cancer.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Primary bone cancer is treated by specialist sarcoma multidisciplinary teams:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} the mainstay of curative treatment; modern limb-salvage techniques with endoprostheses or bone grafts preserve the limb in most patients, with amputation reserved for extensive tumours.
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy:} given before and after surgery for osteosarcoma and Ewing sarcoma to shrink the tumour and eradicate microscopic spread; chondrosarcoma is largely chemotherapy-resistant.
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} used for Ewing sarcoma, for tumours that cannot be completely excised, and to relieve pain in advanced or secondary disease.
    \item \textbf{Secondary bone cancer:} management focuses on the underlying cancer and includes radiotherapy, surgical stabilisation of fractures, and drugs such as bisphosphonates or denosumab to protect bone and reduce skeletal events.
    \item \textbf{Pain control:} analgesics, radiotherapy, and nerve blocks as appropriate.
    \item \textbf{Hypercalcaemia:} intravenous fluids and bisphosphonates or denosumab for calcium disturbance from secondary disease.
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy, prosthetics, and psychological support after surgery to restore function and quality of life.
    \item \textbf{Surveillance:} regular imaging and review to detect recurrence or spread early.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pathological fracture through tumour-weakened bone
    \item Spinal cord compression when tumours affect the vertebral column
    \item Hypercalcaemia in metastatic disease, causing thirst, confusion, and kidney problems
    \item Complications of treatment, including infection, bleeding, and poor wound healing after surgery
    \item Chemotherapy side effects such as neutropenia, infection, and infertility
    \item Growth impairment in children treated for bone cancer
    \item Amputation and its psychological and functional consequences
    \item Tumour recurrence, locally or at distant sites
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Prognosis depends on the tumour type, stage, and response to treatment. With modern chemotherapy and surgery, around 60--70\% of patients with localised osteosarcoma and a similar proportion with localised Ewing sarcoma are alive five years after diagnosis. Chondrosarcoma is cured by complete surgical excision in the majority of low- and intermediate-grade cases, but high-grade lesions have a poorer outlook. Metastatic disease at presentation carries a worse prognosis, although some patients with limited lung metastases are cured with aggressive treatment. For secondary bone cancer, the outlook reflects the behaviour of the primary tumour, and treatment aims to control pain, preserve mobility, and maintain quality of life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item There is no reliable way to prevent primary bone cancer; awareness of symptoms allows earlier diagnosis and better outcomes.
    \item Report persistent bone pain, swelling, or a painless lump to a doctor promptly, especially in children and young adults.
    \item For people living with cancer, maintain good bone health through adequate calcium and vitamin D, weight-bearing activity, and avoidance of smoking and excess alcohol.
    \item Attend recommended cancer screening and follow-up appointments to detect secondary spread early.
    \item People at risk of pathological fracture can be offered prophylactic stabilisation and bone-protecting treatment.
    \item After treatment, engage with rehabilitation and survivorship services, including physiotherapy and psychological support.
\end{itemize}
