\chapter{Leukemia Treatment}
\index{Leukemia Treatment}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Leukemia treatment refers to the comprehensive, multi-modal therapeutic strategies designed to manage, control, and cure the various forms of leukaemia. Because leukaemia comprises a heterogeneous group of haematological malignancies, treatment is highly individualised, guided by the specific subtype (acute myeloid, acute lymphoblastic, chronic myeloid, or chronic lymphocytic leukaemia), genetic and molecular markers, patient age, comorbidities, and overall performance status. The primary objectives of treatment range from achieving deep molecular remission and long-term cure in acute leukaemias, to managing disease progression and maintaining quality of life in chronic, indolent cases. Modern treatment paradigms have shifted from sole reliance on non-specific cytotoxic chemotherapy toward precise targeted therapies, immunotherapies, and cellular engineering.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The landscape of leukaemia treatment has evolved significantly over the last several decades, altering survival statistics globally and in many countries. Historically, acute leukaemias carried a very poor prognosis; however, with modern treatment protocols, the 5-year survival rate for paediatric acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) now exceeds 90\%. In adults, survival rates for acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) remain lower but have improved due to targeted agents and improved stem cell transplant protocols. In many countries, access to highly specialised treatments, such as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T-cell therapies and novel tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), is subsidized through the public medicines subsidy (public medicines subsidy), ensuring high standards of care. Despite these advances, disparities exist in treatment outcomes, often correlated with advanced age at diagnosis, geographical access to tertiary haematology centres, and socio-economic factors.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Leukemia treatments are designed to target the specific cellular and genetic abnormalities that drive malignant haematopoiesis.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Targeting rapid cell division:} Cytotoxic chemotherapy agents disrupt DNA replication and cell division, preferentially killing rapidly proliferating leukaemic blast cells.
    \item \textbf{Tyrosine kinase inhibition:} Targeted molecular therapies, such as imatinib, nilotinib, and dasatinib, bind to the ATP-binding site of the abnormal BCR-ABL1 fusion protein in CML, blocking downstream signals that promote cell survival and growth.
    \item \textbf{Differentiation induction:} Retinoids like all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) and arsenic trioxide target the PML-RARA fusion protein in acute promyelocytic leukaemia (APL), forcing immature promyelocytes to differentiate into mature, functional granulocytes.
    \item \textbf{Monoclonal antibody targeting:} Antibodies such as rituximab target specific cell-surface proteins (e.g.\ CD20 on B-lymphocytes) to induce complement-dependent cytotoxicity and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity.
    \item \textbf{Epigenetic modification:} Hypomethylating agents (e.g.\ azacitidine and decitabine) inhibit DNA methyltransferases, reversing abnormal gene silencing and promoting normal differentiation in myelodysplastic syndromes and AML.
    \item \textbf{Cellular immunotherapy:} CAR T-cell therapy involves genetically engineering a patient's own T-lymphocytes to express receptors targeting leukaemia-specific antigens, such as CD19, leading to direct T-cell-mediated destruction of leukaemic cells.
    \item \textbf{Marrow eradication and reconstitution:} High-dose myeloablative chemotherapy followed by haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) completely eliminates the patient's diseased marrow, replacing it with donor stem cells that establish a new, healthy immune system capable of attacking residual leukaemia (graft-versus-leukaemia effect).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features associated with leukaemia treatment include both the indications for commencing therapy and the clinical manifestations of treatment-related toxicities.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Indications for acute therapy:} Rapidly rising white blood cell count, profound cytopenias (anaemia, thrombocytopenia), organomegaly, constitutional symptoms, or life-threatening coagulopathy (especially in APL).
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy-induced cytopenias:} Severe fatigue, pallor, and dyspnea (due to anaemia); easy bruising, petechiae, and mucosal bleeding (due to thrombocytopenia); and recurrent or opportunistic infections (due to neutropenia).
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal toxicity:} Severe mucositis presenting as painful oral ulcers and dysphagia, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, and anorexia.
    \item \textbf{Infusion reactions and CRS:} Fever, hypotension, hypoxia, and multi-organ dysfunction associated with cytokine release syndrome (CRS), commonly seen following CAR T-cell therapy or bispecific antibody infusions.
    \item \textbf{Tumour lysis syndrome signs:} Oliguria, muscle cramps, cardiac arrhythmias, and seizures arising from rapid cell breakdown immediately after initiating therapy.
    \item \textbf{Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) features:} In patients undergoing allogeneic stem cell transplant, GVHD presents with a maculopapular rash, watery or bloody diarrhoea, elevated liver enzymes, and dry eyes or mouth.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
During leukaemia treatment, distinguishing between treatment side effects, disease progression, and secondary complications is critical for safe management.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neutropenic fever versus drug-induced fever:} Differentiating an underlying bacterial or fungal infection from a febrile reaction to chemo-therapeutic agents or blood transfusions.
    \item \textbf{Leukaemia relapse versus secondary marrow suppression:} Distinguishing the return of the primary malignant clone from transient myelosuppression caused by chemotherapy or a secondary myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) induced by prior therapy.
    \item \textbf{Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) versus septic shock:} Differentiating the systemic inflammatory response of immune-targeted therapies from septic shock caused by bacterial pathogen entry.
    \item \textbf{GVHD skin rash versus drug eruption:} Differentiating the cutaneous manifestations of graft-versus-host disease from allergic skin reactions to antibiotics or chemotherapy.
    \item \textbf{Veno-occlusive disease (VOD) versus hepatic GVHD:} Distinguishing hepatic sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (characterized by ascites, weight gain, and painful hepatomegaly) from direct immunological attack on the bile ducts post-transplant.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients undergoing active leukaemia treatment require close monitoring. They must contact their haematology team immediately or seek emergency care if they experience any signs of treatment complications.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A temperature of $38^\circ$C or higher, or chills and shivering (neutropenic sepsis).
    \item Sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, or rapid heart rate.
    \item Uncontrolled bleeding, such as a nosebleed lasting more than 30 minutes, vomiting blood, or blood in the stool.
    \item Sudden, severe headache, confusion, difficulty speaking, or weakness on one side of the body.
    \item Signs of severe allergic reaction, including swelling of the face, lips, or tongue, and difficulty swallowing or breathing.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Monitoring the efficacy and safety of leukaemia treatment requires frequent, specialised laboratory and imaging assessments.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Serial full blood counts (FBC):} Performed daily or weekly to monitor cytopenias, guide transfusion support, and track haematopoietic recovery.
    \item \textbf{Repeat bone marrow biopsies:} Essential to assess response to therapy (e.g.\ at day 14 and day 28 of induction) and to confirm morphological complete remission.
    \item \textbf{Minimal residual disease (MRD) monitoring:} Uses highly sensitive flow cytometry or polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to detect residual leukaemic cells down to 1 in 10,000 or 100,000 cells, guiding decisions on transplant or consolidation intensity.
    \item \textbf{Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM):} Utilised for certain drugs (e.g.\ busulfan or cyclosporine) to ensure therapeutic levels are achieved while minimizing renal or hepatic toxicity.
    \item \textbf{Tumour lysis panel:} Frequent monitoring of serum potassium, phosphate, calcium, uric acid, and creatinine levels during the initial phase of treatment.
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular assessment:} Echocardiogram or MUGA scan to evaluate left ventricular ejection fraction before and during treatment with cardiotoxic anthracyclines.
    \item \textbf{Infectious screening:} Blood cultures, sputum cultures, chest X-rays, and PCR assays for viral (e.g.\ CMV, EBV) or fungal pathogens when infection is suspected.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of leukaemia spans multiple phases, integrating systemic chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and supportive interventions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Induction chemotherapy:} The intensive first phase of treatment aimed at rapidly clearing leukaemic blasts from the blood and bone marrow to restore normal haematopoiesis.
    \item \textbf{Consolidation (Post-remission) therapy:} Treatment administered after remission is achieved to eliminate hidden, residual leukaemic cells and prevent relapse. This may include further chemotherapy, high-dose therapy with stem cell rescue, or targeted drugs.
    \item \textbf{Targeted and oral therapies:} Ongoing administration of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) for CML, or venetoclax (a BCL-2 inhibitor) combined with hypomethylating agents for older adults with AML.
    \item \textbf{Maintenance therapy:} Lower-dose, long-term chemotherapy or targeted therapy (often lasting 2 to 3 years) used in certain leukaemias, such as childhood ALL or acute promyelocytic leukaemia, to maintain remission.
    \item \textbf{Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT):} Replacing diseased marrow with healthy donor cells, typically reserved for high-risk, relapsed, or refractory leukaemias.
    \item \textbf{Supportive and prophylactic care:} Administration of antiemetics, intravenous hydration, rasburicase (for TLS prevention), colony-stimulating factors (like G-CSF), and prophylactic antimicrobials (acyclovir, cotrimoxazole, posaconazole).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neutropenic sepsis:} A life-threatening systemic infection resulting from severe treatment-induced neutropenia.
    \item \textbf{Organ toxicity:} Long-term damage to organs, such as anthracycline-induced cardiomyopathy, bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis, or methotrexate-induced neurotoxicity.
    \item \textbf{Infertility and gonadal failure:} Chemotherapy and total body irradiation frequently impair reproductive capacity, requiring pre-treatment fertility counselling and gamete cryopreservation.
    \item \textbf{Secondary malignancies:} Increased risk of developing secondary cancers, such as treatment-related AML or myelodysplastic syndrome, years after chemotherapy or radiation exposure.
    \item \textbf{Stem cell transplant-related complications:} Severe acute or chronic GVHD, graft failure, veno-occlusive disease of the liver, and infectious complications.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals undergoing leukaemia treatment depends heavily on the specific disease subtype, patient age, genetic alterations, and response to initial therapy. Pediatric patients with ALL have an outstanding prognosis, with long-term survival rates exceeding 90\%. Adults with acute leukaemias face a more challenging course, but outcomes have steadily improved with targeted agents and safer transplant techniques. CML patients receiving TKIs have a life expectancy that approaches that of the general population. Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia remains largely incurable but can be managed successfully for many years or even decades using novel oral targeted therapies, with many patients dying of unrelated causes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Strict neutropenic hygiene:} Wash hands frequently, avoid contact with sick individuals, avoid large crowds, and ensure all food is thoroughly washed and cooked.
    \item \textbf{Central venous access device (CVAD) care:} Maintain and protect PICC lines or ports according to clinical instructions, checking daily for redness, swelling, or discharge.
    \item \textbf{Gentle oral care:} Use a soft-bristled toothbrush and prescribed non-alcoholic mouthwashes (such as saline or bicarbonate rinses) to prevent and manage oral mucositis.
    \item \textbf{Meticulous skin care:} Keep skin moisturized to prevent cracking, which can act as a portal of entry for pathogens, and protect skin from sun exposure due to increased drug-induced photosensitivity.
    \item \textbf{Hydration adherence:} Maintain a high intake of oral fluids as directed by the clinical team to support renal excretion of chemotherapy metabolites.
    \item \textbf{Peer and psychological support:} Access hospital social work services, oncology psychologists, and leukaemia support groups to address the emotional distress of treatment.
\end{itemize}
