\chapter{Vaginal discharge}
\index{Vaginal discharge}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Vaginal discharge is fluid produced by glands in the cervix and vagina, together with cells shed from the vaginal wall. A normal vaginal discharge is clear, white, or slightly creamy, has little or no smell, and changes in amount and consistency through the menstrual cycle, around ovulation, during pregnancy, and with sexual arousal. It is a healthy and natural process that helps keep the vagina clean, moist, and protected against infection. What matters is a change from a woman's usual pattern: a discharge that becomes much heavier, changes colour to yellow, green, or grey, develops a strong or fishy smell, or is accompanied by itchiness, soreness, or pain may indicate an infection or, rarely, another problem. Common causes of abnormal discharge include thrush (candidiasis), bacterial vaginosis, and sexually transmitted infections such as chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and trichomoniasis. Most causes are easily treated, but some can spread to the uterus and fallopian tubes if left untreated.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Vaginal discharge is one of the most common reasons women seek medical care. Bacterial vaginosis is the most common cause of abnormal vaginal discharge, affecting around 1 in 4 to 1 in 3 women of reproductive age at some point, and it is especially common during pregnancy. Thrush is extremely common, with around 3 in 4 women experiencing at least one episode in their lifetime, and many having recurrent episodes. Sexually transmitted infections are also frequent: chlamydia is the most commonly notified STI in many countries, with rates highest in people under 30, and many infections are symptom-free. Trichomoniasis affects millions of people worldwide. Abnormal discharge is more common at certain times of life, including the reproductive years, pregnancy, after antibiotics, and around menopause, when changing hormone levels alter the balance of organisms in the vagina.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The vagina normally contains a balanced community of bacteria, dominated by lactobacilli, which produce lactic acid and keep the vaginal pH slightly acidic. This acidity suppresses harmful organisms. Abnormal discharge occurs when this balance is disturbed.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bacterial vaginosis:} overgrowth of mixed bacteria such as \textit{Gardnerella vaginalis} as the lactobacilli are displaced, causing a thin, grey, fishy-smelling discharge; often triggered by new sexual partners, douching, and hormonal changes
    \item \textbf{Thrush (candidiasis):} overgrowth of \textit{Candida} yeasts, usually \textit{Candida albicans}, causing a thick, white, cottage-cheese-like discharge with intense itching; triggered by antibiotics, pregnancy, diabetes, tight clothing, and some hormonal treatments
    \item \textbf{Chlamydia and gonorrhoea:} sexually transmitted bacteria that infect the cervix, often causing a yellow or mucopurulent discharge, frequently with little or no other symptom
    \item \textbf{Trichomoniasis:} a sexually transmitted parasite (\textit{Trichomonas vaginalis}) causing a frothy, yellow-green, itchy discharge with a strong odour
    \item \textbf{Cervicitis:} inflammation of the cervix, which can be infectious or non-infectious and produces a purulent discharge
    \item \textbf{Foreign bodies:} a forgotten tampon, condom, or intrauterine device string can cause an offensive discharge
    \item \textbf{Atrophic vaginitis:} thinning and dryness of the vaginal lining after menopause due to low oestrogen, which can cause a watery or irritating discharge
    \item \textbf{Rare causes:} cervical or endometrial cancer can rarely present with a blood-stained or watery discharge
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The character of the discharge and its associated symptoms point towards the likely cause.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bacterial vaginosis:} thin, grey or white discharge with a strong fishy odour, worse after sex or menstruation, usually without itching or soreness
    \item \textbf{Thrush:} thick, white, lumpy discharge like cottage cheese, with intense vulval itching, redness, soreness, and pain on sex or urination
    \item \textbf{Chlamydia and gonorrhoea:} increased discharge that may be yellow or cloudy, sometimes with bleeding after sex, pain passing urine, or lower abdominal pain
    \item \textbf{Trichomoniasis:} frothy, yellow-green discharge with a strong smell, vulval irritation, and redness
    \item \textbf{Normal discharge:} clear to white, variable in amount, without odour, itch, or pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Normal physiological discharge, including ovulation and pregnancy-related changes
    \item Bacterial vaginosis
    \item Vulvovaginal candidiasis (thrush)
    \item Trichomoniasis
    \item Chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and other sexually transmitted infections
    \item Pelvic inflammatory disease, when discharge is accompanied by pelvic pain and fever
    \item Atrophic vaginitis after menopause
    \item Foreign body such as a retained tampon
    \item Cervical polyps, cervicitis, or, rarely, cervical or endometrial cancer
    \item A urinary tract infection, which can cause similar discomfort
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
A woman should seek medical advice if she notices a change in her discharge that is new, persistent, smells unusual, or is accompanied by itching, soreness, bleeding, pain passing urine, or pelvic pain. Anyone with possible symptoms of a sexually transmitted infection should be tested, even if symptoms are mild or absent, because many infections are silent.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discharge together with severe pelvic or abdominal pain and fever, which may indicate pelvic inflammatory disease or another serious infection
    \item Discharge with heavy bleeding or faintness in pregnancy
    \item Sudden severe pain during pregnancy with discharge, which may indicate a pregnancy emergency such as rupture of the membranes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A specific diagnosis is important, because the treatments for different causes are completely different.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the colour, consistency, odour, and amount of the discharge, its relation to the cycle and to sex, itching, pain, recent antibiotics, contraceptive use, and sexual history
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} inspection of the vulva, vagina, and cervix
    \item \textbf{Vaginal pH testing:} a high pH suggests bacterial vaginosis or trichomoniasis; a normal low pH suggests thrush
    \item \textbf{Microscopy:} a sample of discharge is examined under a microscope for yeasts, clue cells (bacterial vaginosis), and trichomonads
    \item \textbf{STI testing:} swabs or urine tests for chlamydia and gonorrhoea, because these can cause discharge but also need to be excluded when the discharge has another cause
    \item \textbf{Testing in pregnancy:} bacterial vaginosis and trichomoniasis are assessed in the same way, with additional attention because infection in pregnancy can have effects on the pregnancy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is directed at the specific cause.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bacterial vaginosis:} antibiotic treatment, usually with metronidazole as tablets or a vaginal gel; recurrence is common and may need repeated or longer courses
    \item \textbf{Thrush:} antifungal medicines, as vaginal pessaries and cream or as an oral tablet; recurrent thrush may need a longer course and treatment of triggers
    \item \textbf{Chlamydia and gonorrhoea:} antibiotic treatment, with testing and treatment of sexual partners and a follow-up test to confirm the infection has cleared
    \item \textbf{Trichomoniasis:} oral antibiotics, with partner treatment and a test of cure
    \item \textbf{Atrophic vaginitis:} vaginal oestrogen or lubricants under medical advice
    \item \textbf{Foreign body:} removal of the retained object, followed by reassurance or treatment if inflammation is present
    \item \textbf{Normal discharge:} reassurance and advice that no treatment is needed
    \item \textbf{During pregnancy:} treatment of the specific cause is safe and important, because some infections can increase the risk of pregnancy complications
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Untreated chlamydia and gonorrhoea can ascend to cause pelvic inflammatory disease, tubal scarring, infertility, and ectopic pregnancy
    \item Bacterial vaginosis increases the risk of pelvic inflammatory disease and, in pregnancy, is associated with an increased risk of preterm birth
    \item Trichomoniasis can cause discomfort and is associated with an increased risk of transmitting other infections
    \item Recurrent thrush can cause persistent discomfort and reduced quality of life
    \item In pregnancy, some untreated infections can be passed to the baby during delivery
    \item Rarely, a persistent offensive discharge can indicate a missed foreign body or, very rarely, an underlying cancer
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for most causes of abnormal vaginal discharge is excellent. Thrush and bacterial vaginosis are easily treated, although both may recur, and recurrent cases can usually be controlled with tailored management. Chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and trichomoniasis are curable with antibiotics, provided they are diagnosed and treated promptly and partners are treated to prevent reinfection; this also prevents the long-term complications of tubal damage and infertility. Discharge associated with menopause and hormonal change responds well to treatment. The most important message is that symptoms should be assessed and diagnosed accurately, because treating the wrong cause, or assuming a discharge is thrush when it is actually chlamydia, can delay effective treatment and allow complications to develop.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Do not use douches, perfumed soaps, or vaginal deodorants, which disturb the natural balance of the vagina
    \item Wash the vulva gently with water or a mild, unscented soap, and avoid bubble baths
    \item Wear cotton underwear and avoid very tight clothing, especially in warm weather
    \item Wipe from front to back after using the toilet
    \item Use condoms to reduce the risk of sexually transmitted infections
    \item Have regular STI screening if you have new or multiple sexual partners
    \item After antibiotics, be aware that thrush may develop and seek early treatment
    \item Manage blood sugar carefully if you have diabetes, since high sugar levels promote thrush
    \item Seek medical review for any persistent change in discharge rather than self-treating repeatedly
\end{itemize}
