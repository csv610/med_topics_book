\section*{Definition and Overview}
Syphilis is a bacterial infection caused by the organism Treponema pallidum, which is spread almost entirely through close sexual contact and from mother to baby during pregnancy. It is one of the oldest known sexually transmitted infections and remains important today because it can affect almost any part of the body, can be silent for years, and produces serious complications if untreated. Syphilis is famous for being "the great imitator", because its rashes, ulcers, and other features can look like many unrelated conditions, which is one reason it is sometimes missed or diagnosed late.

The infection develops in distinct stages. Primary syphilis begins with a single painless ulcer, usually in the genital area, that heals on its own even without treatment. Secondary syphilis appears weeks later with a rash, often involving the palms and soles, along with fever, sore throat, and swollen glands. If still untreated, the infection enters a long latent period with no symptoms, and a minority of people eventually develop tertiary syphilis, which can damage the heart, blood vessels, brain, and nervous system many years later. A pregnant woman with untreated syphilis can also pass the infection to her baby, causing congenital syphilis, which can result in stillbirth, prematurity, or serious illness in the newborn.

The good news is that syphilis is easily detected with a blood test and cured completely with simple, cheap antibiotic treatment, especially when it is caught early. This chapter explains the stages, how the infection is transmitted, the symptoms to watch for, and the treatment and follow-up that cure it and prevent its complications.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Syphilis has increased in many regions after falling when penicillin became available. It affects people of all genders and sexual orientations, including men who have sex with men and people having heterosexual sex. Rates may be higher in communities facing barriers to testing and treatment. Screening, partner notification, and prompt treatment are important public-health measures.

Two important consequences of these trends are the return of congenital syphilis, with infected babies born to mothers who did not receive antenatal screening or treatment, and increased cases of syphilis among women of reproductive age. Public health authorities in many countries now recommend routine syphilis testing in early pregnancy, and repeat testing for women at increased risk. Because syphilis is largely curable and entirely preventable with condom use and screening, these recent increases are seen as a preventable setback that strengthened screening, contact tracing, and education can reverse.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Treponema pallidum is a thin, spiral-shaped bacterium that cannot survive outside the human body and is transmitted almost exclusively by intimate contact with an infected person's lesions, or from mother to child across the placenta. The organism enters the body through tiny breaks in the skin or mucous membranes and spreads rapidly through the bloodstream and lymphatics, which is why it can reach the brain, eyes, and other organs very early, even in the first stage.

The immune system responds, but imperfectly: the organism has a remarkable ability to evade immune clearance, and the disease persists in a chronic, waxing-and-waning state. The painless ulcer of primary syphilis reflects inflammation at the entry site, while the rash of secondary syphilis reflects the organism seeding the skin and tissues. In untreated infection, the organism can remain dormant for years before reactivating in tertiary syphilis, where the damage is caused as much by the body's own immune response as by the bacterium itself.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sexual transmission:} direct contact with primary or secondary lesions during vaginal, anal, or oral sex
    \item \textbf{Congenital transmission:} across the placenta at any stage of pregnancy, with the highest risk in untreated early infection
    \item \textbf{Blood transfusion:} rare in many countries because of rigorous blood donor screening
    \item \textbf{Immune evasion:} the bacterium's outer membrane is poorly immunogenic, allowing chronic, lifelong infection if untreated
    \item \textbf{HIV co-infection:} syphilis and HIV often occur together, and genital ulcers increase the risk of HIV transmission
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features of syphilis depend on the stage of infection, and a person can pass through all of them, or remain entirely asymptomatic in the latent stage.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary chancre:} a single, painless, firm ulcer at the site of entry, appearing three to ninety days after exposure; often in the genital area but can occur in the mouth or anus, and may be unnoticed if internal
    \item \textbf{Swollen lymph nodes:} firm, painless glands near the ulcer
    \item \textbf{Secondary rash:} a widespread, often non-itchy rash, classically on the palms and soles, appearing two to twelve weeks after the ulcer
    \item \textbf{General symptoms:} fever, sore throat, headache, muscle aches, fatigue, and hair loss in patches
    \item \textbf{Condylomata lata:} moist, wart-like lesions in skin folds that are highly infectious
    \item \textbf{Mouth ulcers:} painless greyish patches or sores inside the mouth
    \item \textbf{Latent stage:} no symptoms at all, sometimes for years, while the blood test remains positive
    \item \textbf{Tertiary features:} gummas (soft, destructive lumps in skin, bone, or organs), aortic damage, and neurosyphilis with dementia-like changes, stroke-like events, or eye problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because syphilis can mimic so many conditions, the list below covers the main alternatives for its two most common presentations, the genital ulcer and the widespread rash.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genital herpes:} causes painful, multiple small blisters and ulcers, often recurrent, and is confirmed by viral swab
    \item \textbf{Chancroid:} a painful soft ulcer caused by a different bacterium, rare in many countries
    \item \textbf{Lymphogranuloma venereum:} a chlamydia-related infection causing swollen groin glands and proctitis
    \item \textbf{Pityriasis rosea:} a harmless scaly rash that can resemble the secondary rash, but with a typical "herald patch" and no palmar-plantar involvement
    \item \textbf{Psoriasis:} chronic scaly plaques, occasionally confused with secondary syphilis when widespread
    \item \textbf{Drug eruptions:} rashes from medicines, identified by the drug history
    \item \textbf{Infectious mononucleosis and other viruses:} can cause fever, sore throat, and rash, distinguished by specific blood tests
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who notices a genital ulcer, an unusual rash on the palms or soles, or a sore throat with swollen glands after possible exposure should be tested for syphilis without waiting for the symptoms to improve. Testing is also recommended after unprotected sex with a new or casual partner, and routinely during pregnancy. Because syphilis can be entirely silent, people at higher risk, including men who have sex with men and anyone with HIV, are advised to test regularly even when well.

\textbf{Seek urgent medical care for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Confusion, memory problems, difficulty walking, weakness, or numbness that could indicate neurosyphilis
    \item Sudden vision changes, eye pain, or redness that could indicate ocular syphilis
    \item Chest pain or symptoms of aortic disease in late-stage infection
\end{itemize}
These features warrant immediate assessment, and calling 000 (or local emergency services) is appropriate if symptoms are severe or sudden.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Syphilis is diagnosed mainly with blood tests, which are simple, reliable, and routinely available through GPs and sexual health clinics. If you have been tested, remember that the screening test can be negative in the very early primary stage.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Screening serology:} an enzyme immunoassay (EIA) blood test detects antibodies to Treponema pallidum
    \item \textbf{Confirmatory tests:} treponemal tests such as TPHA or TPPA confirm a positive screening result
    \item \textbf{Activity tests:} RPR or VDRL measure antibody levels, which rise with active infection and fall after treatment, and are used to monitor response
    \item \textbf{Lesion testing:} a swab of a primary ulcer can be tested with polymerase chain reaction (PCR) or dark-field microscopy to find the organism directly
    \item \textbf{Repeat testing:} early-stage infection can show a false-negative blood test, so repeat testing a few weeks later is sometimes needed
    \item \textbf{Cerebrospinal fluid testing:} a lumbar puncture is arranged if neurological, eye, or ear symptoms suggest neurosyphilis
    \item \textbf{Testing for other infections:} because syphilis often coexists with HIV and other sexually transmitted infections, a full sexual health screen is recommended
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Syphilis is one of the most curable infections in medicine when treated correctly. Penicillin remains the treatment of choice and is used in the form of an intramuscular injection.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Benzathine penicillin G:} a single injection for primary, secondary, and early latent syphilis; three weekly injections for late latent syphilis
    \item \textbf{Penicillin alternatives:} doxycycline taken by mouth for fourteen to twenty-eight days is used for people allergic to penicillin
    \item \textbf{Neurosyphilis treatment:} higher-dose intravenous penicillin given over a course of days is used when the nervous system is involved
    \item \textbf{Congenital syphilis treatment:} infected babies receive intravenous penicillin in hospital shortly after birth
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} women diagnosed in pregnancy are treated urgently, as treatment early in pregnancy largely prevents congenital infection
    \item \textbf{Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction:} fever, chills, and aching within hours of the first injection are common and harmless, and reassure the person in advance
    \item \textbf{Partner notification and contact tracing:} sexual partners are informed and offered testing and treatment, often confidentially through health services
    \item \textbf{Follow-up blood tests:} antibody levels are checked at three, six, and twelve months to confirm the infection has cleared
    \item \textbf{Testing for HIV and other infections:} offered at the same visit, as co-infection is common
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated or late-treated syphilis can cause serious, permanent harm, which is why prompt diagnosis matters so much.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurosyphilis:} brain and spinal cord damage causing memory loss, personality change, stroke, balance problems, and blindness
    \item \textbf{Ocular syphilis:} inflammation of the eye that can cause permanent vision loss
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular syphilis:} weakening of the aorta and heart valves, most often appearing decades after infection
    \item \textbf{Gummas:} destructive inflammatory lumps in skin, bone, or internal organs
    \item \textbf{Congenital syphilis:} stillbirth, premature birth, and serious illness in babies, including bone and brain damage, if the mother is untreated
    \item \textbf{Increased HIV risk:} genital ulcers make it easier for HIV to be transmitted and acquired
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With correct treatment, the outlook for syphilis is excellent. Primary, secondary, and early latent syphilis are cured completely by a single course of treatment, symptoms resolve, and the person becomes non-infectious within days. The infection's late complications are almost entirely preventable, because they occur only after years of untreated disease, which gives people a very large window in which to be cured. Even in late latent syphilis, treatment prevents progression to tertiary disease. The main predictor of a poor outcome is delay: people who are treated before the nervous system or heart are involved make a full recovery.

For babies with congenital syphilis, outcomes depend on how early in pregnancy the mother is treated and how severe the infection is at birth; early antenatal diagnosis and treatment prevent the vast majority of cases. Overall, syphilis is a reminder that a simple blood test and a single injection can prevent a lifetime of disability, and that sexual health screening is one of the most effective preventive measures in modern medicine.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use condoms consistently, which greatly reduce but do not completely eliminate the risk of syphilis
    \item Have regular sexual health checks if you have a new partner or multiple partners
    \item Ask to be tested for syphilis as part of routine antenatal care in every pregnancy
    \item Practise safer sex with partners and consider testing before starting a new relationship
    \item If you are treated for syphilis, avoid sex until your doctor confirms you are no longer infectious
    \item Cooperate with partner notification so that contacts are tested and treated early
    \item Reduce or stop the use of alcohol and other drugs before sex, which are linked to riskier sexual behaviour
\end{itemize}
