\chapter{Pityriasis Rosea}
\index{Pityriasis Rosea}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{This chapter is for general education. A new or unusual rash should be assessed if the diagnosis is uncertain.}

\section*{What it is}
Pityriasis rosea is a common rash that usually settles without treatment. It is different from pityriasis versicolor and is not usually spread by close contact.

\section*{Symptoms}
It often begins with one oval, scaly patch, followed days or weeks later by smaller patches on the trunk or limbs. Itching can occur, and some people have tiredness, sore throat, headache, or a fever beforehand. Skin colour changes can look different on different skin tones and may persist after the rash settles.

\section*{Diagnosis and treatment}
A clinician diagnoses it from the appearance and history, while considering eczema, psoriasis, ringworm, drug reactions, and other rashes. Most cases improve within a few months. Emollients, avoiding irritating products, and pharmacist-advised antihistamines may reduce itch; severe cases may need prescribed treatment.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek prompt advice for a new rash during pregnancy, a rapidly spreading or blistering rash, fever with serious illness, involvement of the eyes or mouth, or a rash that is painful rather than itchy. Arrange review if the rash persists beyond about 3 months or the diagnosis is unclear.
