\chapter{Excision of a Ganglion}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A ganglion is a benign, fluid-filled swelling that develops near joints and tendons, most commonly on the wrist, hand, or foot. Excision is the surgical removal of a ganglion and is one option for treatment when the ganglion causes pain, interferes with movement, or is unsightly. Surgery is performed under local or general anaesthetic, and the ganglion is removed along with its connection to the joint or tendon. Most people do well, although ganglions can return after any treatment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Ganglions are common, affecting people of all ages, with a peak in young and middle-aged adults, and they are more common in women. They often appear after injury or overuse of a joint. Many ganglions cause no symptoms and are simply observed, while others are treated because of pain or appearance. Excision is the most definitive treatment, but it is not always needed.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
A ganglion forms from a weakness in the lining of a joint or tendon sheath.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} fluid from the joint or tendon sheath leaks through a small defect and collects as a cyst under the skin
    \item \textbf{The fluid:} thick, clear, jelly-like fluid similar to joint fluid
    \item \textbf{Location:} the back of the wrist, the front of the wrist, the base of a finger, and the top of the foot are common sites
    \item \textbf{Relationship to the joint:} the ganglion has a stalk connecting it to the joint or tendon
    \item \textbf{Causes:} not fully understood; injury and repetitive stress may play a part
    \item \textbf{Behaviour:} ganglions can change size, disappear, and recur
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms depend on the size and location.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A visible, smooth lump under the skin
    \item The lump is usually firm, round, and may be tender
    \item Pain, which may worsen with joint movement
    \item Weakness or altered sensation if the ganglion presses on a nerve
    \item Difficulty with activities involving the joint
    \item Many ganglions are painless and found incidentally
    \item The lump may transilluminate, or glow, with a light, because it is fluid-filled
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other lumps must be distinguished from a ganglion.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Lipoma and other benign soft tissue tumours
    \item A mucous cyst at the finger joint
    \item A giant cell tumour of the tendon sheath
    \item An enlarged lymph node
    \item A bone or joint abnormality
    \item Rarely, a soft tissue sarcoma, which is why persistent or growing lumps are investigated
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Lumps should be assessed by a GP to confirm they are ganglions. Referral for excision is appropriate when the ganglion causes pain, grows, interferes with function, or is of concern.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe pain, redness, or swelling at the site
    \item Signs of infection after surgery, including fever
    \item Numbness or weakness in the hand or foot
    \item Heavy bleeding after surgery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is usually clinical.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Examination:} the characteristic firm, smooth, fluid-filled lump
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} confirms the fluid-filled nature and excludes other lumps
    \item \textbf{MRI:} rarely needed, for unusual cases
    \item \textbf{Aspiration:} draining the fluid with a needle can confirm the diagnosis and provide temporary relief
    \item \textbf{Decision to treat:} based on symptoms and the person's preferences
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Excision is one of several treatment options.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observation:} many ganglions need no treatment
    \item \textbf{Aspiration:} draining the fluid, which is simple but has a high recurrence rate
    \item \textbf{Excision surgery:} the definitive treatment, removing the ganglion and its stalk
    \item \textbf{Anaesthesia:} local or general anaesthetic, depending on the site and the person
    \item \textbf{The operation:} a small incision, removal of the cyst and stalk, and closure with stitches
    \item \textbf{Recovery:} a dressing and splint for a period, with return to full activity over weeks
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} wound review and removal of stitches
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Recurrence, which occurs in a small proportion of people even after surgery
    \item Infection and bleeding
    \item Scarring and tenderness at the site
    \item Nerve injury causing numbness, which is uncommon but possible
    \item Stiffness of the joint after surgery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Excision is effective, and most people have no further trouble after surgery, although a minority experience recurrence. The procedure is safe, the recovery is short, and the result is generally good both functionally and cosmetically. Many ganglions also resolve on their own without any treatment.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Have any new lump assessed by a doctor
    \item Rest the affected joint and avoid aggravating activities
    \item Follow the post-operative advice on splinting, wound care, and activity
    \item Keep the wound dry and clean
    \item Attend follow-up and report any signs of infection
    \item Be aware that recurrence is possible and seek early review
\end{itemize}
