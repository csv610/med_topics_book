\chapter{Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori)}
\index{Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Helicobacter pylori, commonly called H. pylori, is a spiral-shaped bacterium that colonises the lining of the stomach. It is the most common chronic bacterial infection in humans and a major cause of gastritis, peptic ulcers, and stomach cancer. Most people infected with H. pylori have no symptoms at all, but in a significant minority the infection damages the stomach lining, leading to ulcers and other disease. Infection is diagnosed by breath, stool, or biopsy tests and is treated with a short course of combination antibiotic therapy, which cures most people.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Around half of the world's population carries H. pylori, making it one of the most common infections worldwide. Prevalence is higher in developing countries and in older generations; in many countries, around 15--25\% of adults are infected, with higher rates in some communities. Infection is usually acquired in childhood, often within families. H. pylori is responsible for the majority of duodenal ulcers and most gastric ulcers, and it is the strongest known risk factor for stomach cancer, which is classified as a group 1 carcinogen. Infection rates have been falling with improved sanitation and living conditions.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} spread by the faecal--oral or oral--oral route, usually in childhood, through contaminated food, water, or close family contact
    \item \textbf{Colonisation:} the bacterium burrows into the mucus layer of the stomach, where it survives the acid environment
    \item \textbf{Urease:} the bacterium produces urease, which neutralises acid around it and enables survival
    \item \textbf{Inflammation:} toxins and the immune response cause chronic inflammation of the stomach lining (gastritis)
    \item \textbf{Ulcer formation:} inflammation damages the mucosa, and acid digestion leads to ulcers in the stomach and duodenum
    \item \textbf{Cancer risk:} long-standing infection can lead to atrophic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia, and, rarely, stomach cancer and MALT lymphoma
    \item \textbf{Most are asymptomatic:} host, bacterial, and environmental factors determine who develops disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The majority of infected people have no symptoms
    \item Dyspepsia: pain or discomfort in the upper abdomen, bloating, nausea, and early fullness
    \item Peptic ulcer pain: burning or gnawing upper abdominal pain, often between meals and at night
    \item Pain that is relieved by eating or antacids in duodenal ulcer, or worsened by eating in gastric ulcer
    \item Nausea, vomiting, and loss of appetite
    \item Complications present with vomiting blood, black stools, or sudden severe abdominal pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Functional dyspepsia and non-ulcer dyspepsia cause identical symptoms without infection
    \item Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease and heartburn
    \item Peptic ulcer disease from NSAIDs and aspirin, which can occur without H. pylori
    \item Gallbladder disease and pancreatitis cause upper abdominal pain
    \item Stomach cancer, which may present with weight loss, bleeding, or difficulty swallowing
    \item The presence of H. pylori is confirmed by testing, not assumed from symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for persistent upper abdominal pain, bloating, nausea, or reflux symptoms. Testing for H. pylori is recommended for people with ulcers, dyspepsia, a family history of stomach cancer, or other indications.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vomiting blood or material resembling coffee grounds
    \item Black, tarry, or bloody stools
    \item Sudden, severe abdominal pain, which may indicate a perforated ulcer
    \item Fainting, dizziness, or collapse from blood loss
    \item Difficulty swallowing or unexplained weight loss
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urea breath test:} the patient drinks a labelled urea solution; H. pylori's urease releases labelled carbon dioxide in the breath. Highly accurate for diagnosis and confirming cure
    \item \textbf{Stool antigen test:} detects bacterial proteins in stool; convenient and accurate
    \item \textbf{Blood antibody test:} indicates past or present infection, but cannot distinguish current from cleared infection, so is less useful
    \item \textbf{Endoscopy with biopsy:} allows visualisation of ulcers and gastritis, with biopsy for histology, culture, and rapid urease testing
    \item \textbf{Choice of test:} depends on whether endoscopy is otherwise indicated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Eradication therapy:} a 7--14 day course combining two or three antibiotics with a proton pump inhibitor
    \item \textbf{Common regimens:} triple therapy with amoxicillin, clarithromycin, and a PPI, adjusted for antibiotic resistance
    \item \textbf{Treatment success:} eradicates the infection in around 85--90\% of people
    \item \textbf{Confirmation of cure:} a breath or stool test at least four weeks after finishing treatment, and after stopping acid suppression
    \item \textbf{Retreatment:} if the first course fails, a different regimen is used, ideally guided by antibiotic sensitivity
    \item \textbf{Ulcer management:} healing of ulcers and long-term acid suppression where needed
    \item \textbf{Family and food hygiene:} reduces reinfection, which is uncommon in adults
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Peptic ulcers with bleeding, which can be life-threatening
    \item Perforation of the stomach or duodenum, a surgical emergency
    \item Gastric outlet obstruction from ulcer scarring
    \item Atrophic gastritis and intestinal metaplasia, precursors to stomach cancer
    \item Stomach cancer and gastric MALT lymphoma, which are strongly linked to H. pylori
    \item Antibiotic side effects and the growing problem of antibiotic resistance
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Successful eradication cures the infection and greatly reduces the risk of future ulcers, ulcer complications, and stomach cancer. Around 85--90\% of people are cured with first-line therapy, and most of the remainder with retreatment. Ulcers heal and dyspepsia improves in many, though some people continue to have symptoms unrelated to the infection. Eradication is especially important in people with a family history of stomach cancer or with established pre-cancerous changes. Without treatment, the infection persists for life in most people, carrying a long-term risk of ulcers and stomach cancer.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Practise good hygiene: wash hands after toileting and before eating
    \item Drink safe water and eat properly cooked food
    \item Complete the full course of eradication therapy exactly as prescribed
    \item Avoid alcohol and NSAIDs during and after treatment
    \item Attend the follow-up test to confirm eradication
    \item Limit use of NSAIDs and aspirin, which damage the stomach
    \item Discuss testing with your doctor if a family member has been infected or has stomach cancer
\end{itemize}
