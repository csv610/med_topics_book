\chapter{Condoms}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Condoms are barrier devices designed to be worn during sexual intercourse to prevent unintended pregnancy and reduce the transmission of sexually transmitted infections (STIs). They are classified into external (male) condoms, which fit over the erect penis, and internal (female) condoms, which are inserted into the vagina or anus. Typically manufactured from latex, polyurethane, polyisoprene, or natural lambskin, condoms are highly accessible, non-hormonal, and serve as a cornerstone of public health strategies targeting sexual health, family planning, and infectious disease control.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Condoms are the most widely used barrier contraceptive globally. In Australia, condoms are the second most common form of contraception used by sexually active young adults, following the oral contraceptive pill. According to national sexual health surveys, condom usage is highest among individuals aged 16 to 24 and decreases with age and in long-term monogamous relationships. Despite high awareness, consistent and correct condom use remains suboptimal, with studies indicating that up to 30\% of individuals report condom slippage, breakage, or failure due to improper application or lack of lubrication.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The effectiveness and mechanical properties of condoms depend on their material, correct application, and biological barrier function.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mechanical barrier function:} Condoms create a physical, impermeable barrier that prevents the exchange of semen, vaginal fluids, and blood, thereby preventing fertilization and stopping pathogens from reaching mucosal surfaces.
    \item \textbf{Latex condoms:} Made from natural rubber latex, providing excellent elasticity and durability. Latex is impermeable to sperm and most viral and bacterial pathogens, including HIV, herpes simplex virus (HSV), and \textit{Chlamydia trachomatis}.
    \item \textbf{Synthetic alternatives:} Polyurethane and polyisoprene condoms are available for individuals with latex allergies. Polyurethane is thinner and conducts heat well but is less elastic; polyisoprene is highly elastic but compatible only with water- or silicone-based lubricants.
    \item \textbf{Natural skin condoms:} Manufactured from sheep caecum membrane. While effective at preventing pregnancy, they contain microscopic pores that allow the passage of viruses (e.g.\ HIV, HBV, HPV), meaning they do \textbf{not} protect against viral STIs.
    \item \textbf{Lubricant compatibility:} Latex and polyisoprene condoms are degraded by oil-based lubricants (e.g.\ petroleum jelly, mineral oil, baby oil, or cooking oils), leading to microscopic tears and rapid mechanical failure. Only water- or silicone-based lubricants should be used.
    \item \textbf{Causes of failure:} Incorrect sizing, improper storage (exposure to heat or UV light), opening the packet with sharp objects, failure to leave space at the tip for semen collection, and reusing condoms.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical evaluation of condom use involves assessing user behaviour, correct usage techniques, and signs of condom failure or hypersensitivity.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Correct application signs:} The external condom must be rolled completely to the base of the erect penis before any genital contact occurs, with the reservoir tip pinched to exclude air.
    \item \textbf{Condom failure presentation:} Visible breakage or tearing of the barrier, slippage off the penis during intercourse, or retention of the condom within the vagina or anus after withdrawal. Withdrawal must occur immediately after ejaculation while holding the rim of the condom.
    \item \textbf{Allergic or hypersensitivity reactions:} Localized erythema, pruritus, burning sensation, oedema, or contact dermatitis of the genitalia, groin, or inner thighs in individuals sensitive to latex or added chemicals (e.g.\ spermicides, fragrances, or warming agents).
    \item \textbf{Systemic latex allergy signs:} Urticaria, rhinitis, conjunctivitis, wheezing, and in severe cases, systemic anaphylaxis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When a patient presents with symptoms of localized genital irritation or allergic reaction after condom use, other conditions must be considered:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Seminal fluid hypersensitivity:} A rare allergic reaction to proteins in human semen, presenting with vaginal burning, erythema, and swelling post-intercourse, which is actually \textbf{prevented} by condom use.
    \item \textbf{Sexually transmitted infections:} Chlamydia, gonorrhoea, trichomoniasis, or genital herpes, which can cause burning, discharge, and pruritus, potentially mimicking or complicating a contact allergy.
    \item \textbf{Candidiasis (thrush):} Vaginal or penile yeast infection presenting with itching, redness, and a thick white discharge, often exacerbated by friction during intercourse.
    \item \textbf{Irritant contact dermatitis:} Caused by soaps, douches, personal lubricants, or synthetic underwear, rather than the condom material itself.
    \item \textbf{Vaginismus or dyspareunia:} Pain during intercourse due to pelvic floor muscle spasms or inadequate lubrication, which may be falsely attributed to the condom.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Individuals who experience a condom break or slip during intercourse, particularly if there is a risk of pregnancy or STI transmission, should seek immediate medical advice (within 72 hours) regarding emergency contraception and post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP).

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of a severe systemic allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) following contact with latex: swelling of the lips, tongue, or throat, difficulty breathing, wheezing, dizziness, or collapse.
    \item Acute, severe abdominal or pelvic pain post-intercourse.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Investigations are directed toward evaluating potential exposure to STIs or pregnancy following condom failure, or confirming latex allergy.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy testing:} Urine or serum beta-hCG test performed to assess for pregnancy if a condom fails, repeated 10 to 14 days later if necessary.
    \item \textbf{Sexually transmitted infection screening:} Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAAT) for Chlamydia and gonorrhoea, and serology for HIV, Syphilis, Hepatitis B, and Hepatitis C, performed at baseline and repeated at recommended windows (e.g.\ 2 to 12 weeks).
    \item \textbf{Latex-specific IgE testing:} Serum RAST or skin prick testing to confirm IgE-mediated latex allergy in patients experiencing systemic symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Patch testing:} Indicated to identify delayed hypersensitivity reactions (Type IV) to chemicals used in condom manufacturing, such as rubber accelerators (e.g.\ thiurams, carbamates).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management involves promoting correct usage, treating allergic reactions, and implementing emergency measures in the event of condom failure.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Emergency contraception:} Administration of oral emergency contraceptive pills (e.g.\ levonorgestrel within 72 hours or ulipristal acetate within 120 hours) or insertion of a copper intrauterine device (IUD) within 5 days of condom failure to prevent pregnancy.
    \item \textbf{Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP):} Antiretroviral therapy started within 72 hours of potential HIV exposure due to condom failure with a high-risk partner.
    \item \textbf{Management of latex allergy:} Switching to synthetic condoms (polyurethane or polyisoprene) and using latex-free lubricants and gloves.
    \item \textbf{Symptomatic relief of contact dermatitis:} Short-term application of low-potency topical corticosteroids (e.g.\ 1\% hydrocortisone cream) to the affected external skin, along with oral antihistamines to reduce itching.
    \item \textbf{Correct usage education:} Detailed instruction on proper storage (cool, dry place), opening, application, use of appropriate lubricants, and safe disposal.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Unintended pregnancy:} Occurs due to condom slippage, breakage, or inconsistent use, with a typical-use failure rate of approximately 13\% to 18\% annually.
    \item \textbf{STI transmission:} Increased risk of infection with pathogens such as HIV, chlamydia, gonorrhoea, HSV, and HPV if condoms are not used consistently or fail.
    \item \textbf{Anaphylaxis:} A rare but life-threatening complication in individuals with severe IgE-mediated natural rubber latex allergy.
    \item \textbf{Psychosexual distress:} Anxiety regarding condom failure, reduced sensation, or difficulty maintaining an erection, which can lead to avoidant sexual behaviour or non-use.
    \item \textbf{Retained foreign body:} A slipped condom remaining in the vaginal vault or rectum, which can lead to bacterial vaginosis or infection if not removed promptly.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
When used consistently and correctly, condoms provide excellent protection. The perfect-use failure rate for pregnancy is only 2\% per year. They reduce the risk of HIV transmission by approximately 85\% and offer substantial protection against bacterial STIs. Prognosis for individuals experiencing minor contact allergy or irritation is excellent, with symptoms resolving within a few days of discontinuing the offending condom. Long-term outcomes are highly positive when condoms are integrated with comprehensive sexual health practices.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Check the expiry date:} Always inspect the condom wrapper for the expiration date and the presence of an air pocket, which indicates the seal is intact.
    \item \textbf{Use proper lubricants:} Ensure that only water- or silicone-based lubricants are used with latex condoms to prevent structural degradation.
    \item \textbf{Avoid double-condoming:} Never use two condoms simultaneously (e.g.\ two external condoms or an external and an internal condom), as the friction increases the risk of both breaking.
    \item \textbf{Store safely:} Keep condoms in a cool, dark place. Avoid storing them in wallets, glove compartments, or direct sunlight, as heat degrades the materials.
    \item \textbf{Apply before contact:} Ensure the condom is applied before any genital-to-genital contact occurs to maximise protection against both STIs and pre-ejaculatory sperm.
\end{itemize}
