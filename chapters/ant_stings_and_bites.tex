\chapter{Ant Stings and Bites}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Ant stings and bites are injuries caused by ants. Some ants bite with their jaws, and others sting with a stinger at the rear of the body, injecting venom; many species do both, biting to hold on and then stinging. Most ant encounters cause only minor, localised pain, redness, and swelling that settle quickly without treatment.

However, in some people the venom triggers a severe allergic reaction called anaphylaxis, which can be life-threatening. In parts of the world, ants such as fire ants and jack jumper ants are a significant public health concern because of the frequency of stings and the risk of allergic reactions. Treatment depends on the severity of the local reaction and on whether the person has a venom allergy.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Ant stings are common worldwide and are more frequent in warm climates where large ant populations thrive and where people spend time outdoors or work in affected areas. In Australia, the jack jumper ant and other bull ants are among the leading causes of venom allergy and anaphylaxis, particularly in Tasmania and south-eastern Australia. In the United States and elsewhere, imported fire ants sting very large numbers of people each year, and their venom is a common cause of allergic reactions.

Children, gardeners, outdoor workers, and people living in newly developed areas where ant nests are numerous are at higher risk. Severe reactions are uncommon relative to the total number of stings, but they can occur at any age and sometimes on first known exposure.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Ant venom contains a complex mixture of proteins, peptides, and small molecules. When the venom is injected, the body reacts in two distinct ways:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Local reaction:} venom components directly damage tissue and trigger the release of histamine, causing pain, redness, and swelling at the site
    \item \textbf{Allergic reaction:} in susceptible people, the immune system produces antibodies (IgE) against the venom, so that a later sting causes the release of large amounts of histamine and other chemicals throughout the body
    \item \textbf{Secondary infection:} scratching stings can introduce skin bacteria, leading to wound infection
    \item \textbf{Large local reactions:} extensive swelling around the sting site caused by inflammation, usually developing within hours
\end{itemize}

In anaphylaxis, the sudden release of immune mediators causes blood vessels to widen and leak fluid, the airways to narrow, and blood pressure to fall -- a pattern that constitutes a medical emergency.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on whether the reaction is local or systemic:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Immediate, sharp pain and burning at the sting site
    \item Redness, swelling, and itching at the site, typically lasting hours to a couple of days
    \item Fire ant stings often form small blisters or pus-filled lesions within a day
    \item Large local reactions: marked swelling of the whole limb that can persist for several days
    \item Anaphylaxis: hives or flushing, swelling of the face, lips, or throat, difficulty breathing or wheezing, vomiting, dizziness, or collapse
    \item Fever and lethargy with multiple stings, especially in young children
    \item Signs of secondary infection: increasing pain, spreading redness, pus, or fever developing days later
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other insect and environmental causes of similar skin reactions include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bee and wasp stings:} similar local and allergic reactions, but with different venom
    \item \textbf{Mosquito bites:} itchy lumps without the sharp initial sting
    \item \textbf{Tick bites:} may embed in the skin and later transmit infections
    \item \textbf{Spider bites:} can cause necrotic or systemic effects depending on the species
    \item \textbf{Allergic reactions to other triggers:} foods, drugs, or latex, which can also cause anaphylaxis
    \item \textbf{Skin infection or folliculitis:} which can mimic an infected sting
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most ant stings can be managed at home, but medical assessment is warranted if:

\begin{itemize}
    \item There is difficulty breathing, wheezing, or swelling of the face, lips, or throat
    \item There are widespread hives or vomiting after a sting
    \item The person has previously had a severe allergic reaction to any sting
    \item A sting site becomes increasingly red, swollen, painful, or starts discharging pus
    \item There is a large local reaction involving the whole limb
    \item Multiple stings have occurred, especially in a small child
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing or noisy breathing (wheezing)
    \item Swelling of the tongue or throat, or difficulty speaking
    \item Dizziness, collapse, or unconsciousness
    \item A known allergy to ant venom with symptoms developing after a sting
\end{itemize}

If a prescribed adrenaline (epinephrine) auto-injector is available, use it promptly for anaphylaxis and still seek emergency medical care.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is usually made from the history of being stung and the appearance of the skin:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} where and how the person was stung, the time since the sting, and whether there have been allergic reactions before
    \item \textbf{Examination:} assessment of the pattern and extent of swelling, blisters, or any signs of anaphylaxis
    \item \textbf{Allergy testing:} skin prick tests or blood tests for specific ant venom antibodies, arranged by an allergy specialist after a significant reaction
    \item \textbf{Observation:} after an allergic reaction, the person may be observed in a clinic or emergency department until symptoms settle
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management depends on the severity of the reaction:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Minor stings:} wash the area, remove any ants carefully, and apply a cold pack to reduce pain and swelling
    \item \textbf{Itch and swelling:} oral antihistamines and simple pain relief may help; a short course of topical steroid cream can be used for persistent local reactions
    \item \textbf{Blister care:} keep blisters clean and dry, and do not burst them
    \item \textbf{Anaphylaxis:} emergency treatment with intramuscular adrenaline, followed by hospital care, oxygen, and fluids
    \item \textbf{Allergy action plan:} after a severe reaction, an allergy specialist may prescribe an adrenaline auto-injector and provide a written plan
    \item \textbf{Venom immunotherapy:} a course of desensitising injections that dramatically reduces the risk of a severe reaction to future stings in people with confirmed ant venom allergy
    \item \textbf{Secondary infection:} antibiotics may be needed if the site becomes infected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anaphylaxis:} a life-threatening allergic reaction that can be fatal without prompt treatment
    \item \textbf{Secondary bacterial infection:} of sting sites, especially after scratching or multiple stings
    \item \textbf{Scarring or pigment changes:} after blisters or infected stings
    \item \textbf{Large local reactions:} painful swelling that can last for days and limit use of the limb
    \item \textbf{Effects of multiple stings:} fever, nausea, and rarely more serious effects from a large venom load in vulnerable people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For most people, ant stings cause discomfort for a few hours to a few days and then resolve completely, usually without scarring. People who have had a severe allergic reaction remain at risk of a similarly severe reaction with future stings unless they receive venom immunotherapy, which is highly effective at reducing that risk. Once immunotherapy is completed, the great majority of people can be stung with little or no reaction. Blisters and large local reactions typically settle within a few days to a week.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Learn to recognise and avoid ant nests in gardens, parks, and rural areas
    \item Wear enclosed shoes and long trousers when outdoors in ant-prone areas
    \item Check clothing, footwear, and bedding for ants before putting them on
    \item If ants climb on you, brush them off quickly rather than crushing them, since crushing provokes more stings
    \item Treat known ant nests carefully, using professional pest control if needed
    \item If you have had a severe allergic reaction, carry your prescribed adrenaline auto-injector and wear a medical alert device
    \item Keep a written allergy action plan and ensure family, school, and work contacts know how to use the auto-injector
    \item Ask an allergy specialist about venom immunotherapy if you have had a significant reaction
\end{itemize}
