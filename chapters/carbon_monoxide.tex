\chapter{Carbon Monoxide}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Carbon monoxide (CO) is a colourless, odourless, and tasteless gas produced by the incomplete burning of fuels such as gas, wood, coal, and petrol. It is highly poisonous: when breathed in, it binds to haemoglobin in the blood far more strongly than oxygen does, blocking the delivery of oxygen to the body's tissues and causing cellular suffocation. Carbon monoxide poisoning is a medical emergency that can cause anything from mild headache and nausea to unconsciousness and death. It is often called the silent killer because it cannot be detected by the senses, and exposure can occur during sleep or without anyone being aware of it until symptoms develop.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Carbon monoxide poisoning is one of the most common causes of poisoning worldwide and a leading cause of poisoning-related death. In Australia it leads to hundreds of hospitalisations and many deaths each year. Most poisonings occur in the home, from faulty heaters, blocked flues, gas appliances, portable generators, and charcoal or gas barbecues used indoors or in enclosed spaces. There are seasonal peaks in winter, when heaters are running, and during power outages, when portable generators are used indoors. Unintentional exposures greatly outnumber intentional ones, and sleeping people, young children, older adults, and pregnant women are especially vulnerable to harm.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Carbon monoxide is produced whenever fuel burns without enough oxygen. Common sources include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Faulty or poorly ventilated gas, wood, or kerosene heaters
    \item Blocked chimneys, flues, or exhaust pipes
    \item Portable generators or car engines run in garages, tents, or other enclosed areas
    \item Charcoal braziers, gas barbecues, or camping stoves used indoors
    \item Smoke from fires, including house fires
    \item Tobacco smoke, which contains carbon monoxide
    \item Occupational exposure in industries using internal combustion engines, such as mining and warehousing
\end{itemize}
Once inhaled, carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin about 250 times more strongly than oxygen does, forming carboxyhaemoglobin. This reduces the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity, so the heart, brain, and other organs become starved of oxygen. The brain and heart, which use the most oxygen, are the first to suffer damage. Breathing high-concentration oxygen shortens the half-life of carboxyhaemoglobin considerably, which is the basis of treatment.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms of carbon monoxide poisoning are nonspecific and easily mistaken for flu or food poisoning:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Headache, dizziness, and confusion
    \item Nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain
    \item Fatigue, weakness, and shortness of breath on exertion
    \item Chest pain, especially in people with underlying heart disease
    \item Poor concentration, irritability, and memory problems
    \item Loss of coordination and impaired judgement
    \item In more severe poisoning: fainting, seizures, or unconsciousness
    \item A rosy or pink complexion in some cases, although this is not a reliable sign
\end{itemize}
Symptoms often affect everyone in a household at the same time, improve when the person leaves the source, and are typically worse in pets because they are smaller and exposed closer to the floor.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the symptoms are vague, carbon monoxide poisoning is easily confused with other conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Influenza, COVID-19, or other viral illnesses
    \item Food poisoning or gastroenteritis
    \item Migraine or tension-type headache
    \item Alcohol or drug intoxication
    \item Heart attack or other cardiac problems
    \item Other poisonings, such as carbon dioxide or methane exposure
    \item Stroke or other neurological conditions
\end{itemize}
A key clue is that several people or animals in the same building fall ill at the same time, and that symptoms improve when they leave the building.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical care urgently if you suspect carbon monoxide exposure, even if symptoms are mild. If symptoms improve when you leave the building, carbon monoxide poisoning is strongly suspected and should be investigated.
\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, fainting, or loss of consciousness
    \item Seizures
    \item Chest pain or palpitations
    \item Difficulty breathing
    \item Anyone who is pregnant, a child, or an older adult with any symptoms after a suspected exposure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} details of symptoms, fuel-burning appliances, whether several people are affected, and whether symptoms improve away from home
    \item \textbf{Pulse oximetry:} a finger clip reading; note that standard pulse oximeters can overestimate oxygen levels in carbon monoxide poisoning and are not reliable
    \item \textbf{Blood test for carboxyhaemoglobin:} the definitive test, taken as soon as possible after exposure
    \item \textbf{Blood gas and other blood tests:} to check oxygen levels, acid-base balance, and for metabolic complications
    \item \textbf{ECG and cardiac monitoring:} in moderate or severe cases, because the heart is particularly vulnerable to oxygen deprivation
    \item \textbf{Brain imaging:} CT or MRI where there is confusion, unconsciousness, or focal neurological signs
    \item \textbf{Identifying the source:} the fire service or a qualified gas fitter should inspect the property, since the danger remains until the source is found and fixed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Carbon monoxide poisoning is treated in hospital:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Remove the person from the source:} get into fresh air immediately and open doors and windows
    \item \textbf{High-flow oxygen:} given through a tight-fitting mask as soon as possible and continued until carboxyhaemoglobin levels fall
    \item \textbf{Hyperbaric oxygen:} treatment in a pressurised chamber, considered in severe cases, pregnant women, and people with serious symptoms or heart problems
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} monitoring of the heart, brain, and breathing, with treatment of any complications
    \item \textbf{Observation:} in hospital for several hours to detect deterioration and delayed symptoms
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} assessment for late neurological effects in the weeks and months afterwards
\end{itemize}
At home, the most urgent step after leaving the building is to have the source inspected and fixed by a qualified tradesperson before anyone returns.

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Immediate death from severe or prolonged exposure
    \item Heart damage, including abnormal rhythms and heart attack, especially in people with existing heart disease
    \item Brain injury causing memory loss, personality change, and difficulty with concentration and coordination
    \item Delayed neurological syndrome, in which new symptoms appear days to weeks after apparent recovery
    \item Effects on the unborn baby in pregnancy, including reduced oxygen supply and developmental harm
    \item Permanent physical or cognitive disability in the most severe cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Mild poisoning usually resolves fully with fresh air and oxygen, and most people recover without lasting problems. Outcomes depend on the severity and duration of exposure, with unconsciousness and prolonged exposure carrying the worst outlook. A significant minority of people with moderate or severe poisoning develop delayed neurological symptoms weeks later, some of which improve with time and treatment. Recovery can be slow, and some people are left with permanent memory, concentration, or movement problems.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Install a carbon monoxide alarm near bedrooms and test it regularly
    \item Have gas appliances, wood heaters, and chimney flues serviced by a qualified technician every year
    \item Never run a car, generator, barbecue, or camping stove inside a garage, home, or tent
    \item Never use a gas oven or hob to heat a room
    \item Ensure appliances are correctly ventilated and that flues and vents are clear
    \item Do not sleep in rooms with unvented gas or kerosene heaters
    \item Know the symptoms and act quickly if several people or pets in the house feel unwell at once
    \item Call a gas fitter immediately if you suspect a leak, and get out of the building until it has been cleared
\end{itemize}
