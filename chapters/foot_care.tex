\chapter{Foot Care}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Foot care is the daily attention to the feet that keeps them healthy, comfortable, and free of problems, and it becomes increasingly important with age and with conditions such as diabetes, in which foot problems can become serious quickly. The feet carry the body's entire weight, take thousands of steps a day, and are often neglected until they hurt. Good foot care means wearing well-fitting shoes, keeping the feet clean and dry, trimming nails correctly, treating corns and calluses properly, and inspecting the feet regularly, especially for people with diabetes, who can lose sensation and not notice injuries. This chapter explains how to care for the feet and how to recognise problems that need professional help.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Foot problems are extremely common. Around three-quarters of Australians experience a foot problem at some point, and foot pain affects a large share of older adults, limiting mobility and increasing falls risk. Common problems include corns, calluses, ingrown toenails, fungal infections such as tinea and athlete's foot, and arthritis. For people with diabetes, the stakes are higher: foot ulcers affect a significant proportion over their lifetime, and diabetes is a leading cause of amputation. Many of these problems are preventable with simple daily care, and podiatry services treat millions of Australians each year.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Footwear:} ill-fitting shoes are the leading cause of foot problems, causing corns, calluses, bunions, and deformities
    \item \textbf{Pressure and friction:} repeated pressure creates thickened skin (calluses) and corns, which become painful and can ulcerate
    \item \textbf{Nail problems:} incorrect trimming causes ingrown toenails, and injury or fungal infection affects the nail plate
    \item \textbf{Fungal infection:} tinea (athlete's foot) thrives in warm, moist shoes, causing itching, scaling, and cracking between the toes
    \item \textbf{Diabetes:} nerve damage (neuropathy) removes protective sensation, and poor circulation impairs healing, so minor injuries become ulcers and can progress to amputation
    \item \textbf{Poor circulation:} peripheral artery disease reduces blood flow, slowing healing and causing cold, painful feet
    \item \textbf{Skin changes with age:} thinner skin, dryness, and reduced cushioning increase vulnerability
    \item \textbf{Structure and gait:} flat feet, high arches, and arthritis change pressure distribution
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Clean, dry, healthy skin between the toes and on the soles
    \item Nails trimmed straight across and not into the corners
    \item Shoes that fit well, with room for the toes and a firm sole
    \item No pain, calluses, corns, or ingrown nails
    \item Signs needing attention: persistent pain, numbness, tingling, or coldness
    \item Signs of infection: redness, swelling, heat, discharge, or a non-healing sore
    \item In diabetes: the need to inspect the feet daily, because injury may not be felt
    \item Cracking, dryness, and discolouration of the skin or nails may indicate fungal or circulatory problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Foot symptoms need distinguishing.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Calluses and corns from friction versus verrucae (plantar warts), which are viral
    \item Fungal nail infection versus injury to the nail
    \item Ingrown toenail, which is painful and may be infected
    \item Bunions and hammer toes, which are structural deformities
    \item Neuropathy from diabetes or other causes, with numbness and burning
    \item Peripheral artery disease, with cramping on walking and cold feet
    \item Arthritis, gout, and plantar fasciitis, with specific patterns of pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most foot problems respond to good self-care, but a podiatrist or doctor should be consulted for persistent pain, ingrown nails, corns that do not respond, fungal infections, and any foot problem in a person with diabetes, reduced circulation, or reduced sensation.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A foot that is pale, cold, numb, or blue, which may indicate a blocked artery
    \item A deep, dark, or rapidly spreading ulcer, especially in diabetes
    \item Fever with a red, hot, swollen foot, which may indicate spreading infection
    \item Signs of sepsis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Examination:} skin, nails, circulation, and sensation, with assessment of shoes and gait
    \item \textbf{Foot checks in diabetes:} the annual diabetic foot check includes sensation testing with a monofilament and circulation checks
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} for diabetes control and cholesterol in vascular disease
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} X-rays for fractures, arthritis, and deformities; ultrasound for circulation
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a podiatrist for ongoing foot care, especially in diabetes and older adults
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Washing:} daily washing with warm water and soap, drying thoroughly, especially between the toes
    \item \textbf{Moisturising:} lotion on the soles to prevent dryness and cracking, but not between the toes
    \item \textbf{Nail care:} trim nails straight across, not too short, and file edges smooth
    \item \textbf{Footwear:} well-fitting shoes with a wide toe box, low heel, and good support, broken in gradually
    \item \textbf{Correct footwear for activity:} different shoes for sport, work, and everyday use
    \item \textbf{Calluses and corns:} gentle pumice use after bathing and professional treatment by a podiatrist rather than cutting at home
    \item \textbf{Fungal infection:} antifungal creams and keeping feet dry
    \item \textbf{Diabetes care:} daily inspection, professional foot care, and immediate attention to any injury
    \item \textbf{Circulation:} regular exercise, not smoking, and treatment of vascular disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Foot ulcers and amputation in people with diabetes who do not care for their feet
    \item Infections spreading from ingrown nails and untreated wounds
    \item Chronic pain and reduced mobility from corns, bunions, and arthritis
    \item Falls in older adults from painful or unstable feet
    \item Fungal infections spreading to nails and family members
    \item Loss of independence when foot problems limit walking
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With daily care, most foot problems are preventable and treatable. Good footwear, hygiene, and nail care keep the feet healthy, and early treatment of corns, fungal infections, and ingrown nails prevents complications. For people with diabetes, regular podiatry care and daily inspection reduce the risk of ulcers and amputation dramatically. The feet respond well to attention, and people who practise good foot care maintain mobility and independence throughout life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wear shoes that fit properly and match the activity
    \item Wash and dry the feet daily, including between the toes
    \item Moisturise dry skin, avoiding between the toes
    \item Trim nails straight across and not into the corners
    \item Inspect the feet daily if you have diabetes, and use a mirror for the soles
    \item Never cut corns or calluses at home; see a podiatrist
    \item Change socks daily and alternate shoes to keep them dry
    \item See a podiatrist regularly for diabetes, older age, or recurring problems
\end{itemize}
