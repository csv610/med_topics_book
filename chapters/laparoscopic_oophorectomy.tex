\chapter{Laparoscopic oophorectomy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Laparoscopic oophorectomy is a minimally invasive surgical procedure performed to remove one (unilateral) or both (bilateral) ovaries. Utilising a laparoscope---a thin, lighted tube with a camera attached---inserted through small abdominal incisions, the surgeon visualises and dissects the ovarian tissue. This procedure is frequently performed in conjunction with the removal of the fallopian tubes, a combined surgery known as a salpingo-oophorectomy. Laparoscopic oophorectomy is commonly indicated for both benign and malignant conditions, including severe endometriosis, ovarian cysts, ovarian torsion, pelvic inflammatory disease, and as a prophylactic risk-reducing measure in individuals with a high hereditary risk of ovarian cancer. Compared to open laparotomy, the laparoscopic approach offers substantial clinical benefits, including reduced post-operative pain, shorter hospital stays, quicker recovery times, and lower rates of surgical site infections.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The incidence of oophorectomy varies globally, closely linked to regional clinical guidelines and rates of hysterectomy. In Australia and other Western nations, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (BSO) is one of the most frequently performed gynaecological surgeries, often undertaken concurrently with a hysterectomy for benign disease. Approximately 20\% to 30\% of women undergoing a hysterectomy for benign indications in high-income nations have a concurrent oophorectomy. The peak age for performing this procedure is between 40 and 50 years. Prophylactic or risk-reducing bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (RRBSO) is specifically targeted at individuals carrying pathogenic germline mutations in the BRCA1 or BRCA2 genes, or those with Lynch syndrome. In these high-risk cohorts, RRBSO is recommended by age 35 to 40 for BRCA1 carriers and by age 40 to 45 for BRCA2 carriers, reducing their ovarian and fallopian tube cancer risk by up to 80\% to 90\%.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The underlying clinical indications and pathophysiological states that necessitate a laparoscopic oophorectomy are diverse and range from benign structural lesions to hereditary cancer syndromes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ovarian neoplasia and cysts:} Benign epithelial, germ cell, or sex cord-stromal tumours, as well as large or persistent complex ovarian cysts that pose a risk of rupture, haemorrhage, or malignancy.
    \item \textbf{Hereditary cancer syndromes:} Genetic predisposition due to BRCA1/2 mutations or mismatch repair gene mutations (Lynch syndrome), which dramatically elevate the lifetime risk of ovarian, fallopian tube, and peritoneal carcinomas.
    \item \textbf{Ovarian torsion:} An acute gynaecological emergency where the ovary rotates on its ligamentous supports, compromising the ovarian blood supply and leading to ischaemia, necrosis, and subsequent tissue death if not resolved promptly.
    \item \textbf{Endometriosis:} Severe pelvic endometriosis leading to endometriomas (chocolate cysts) that destroy normal ovarian parenchyma, cause debilitating chronic pelvic pain, and fail to respond to conservative medical therapies.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID):} Chronic or severe upper genital tract infections leading to a tubo-ovarian abscess (TOA) that is refractory to intravenous antibiotic therapy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of patients requiring laparoscopic oophorectomy depends primarily on the underlying pathology, ranging from acute symptoms to asymptomatic screening findings.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chronic pelvic pain:} Dull, aching, or sharp localized pain in the lower abdomen or pelvis, which may worsen during menstruation (dysmenorrhea) or intercourse (dyspareunia).
    \item \textbf{Acute abdominal pain:} Sudden-onset, severe, unilateral lower abdominal pain, often accompanied by nausea and vomiting, which is highly characteristic of ovarian torsion or cyst rupture.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic mass effect:} Abdominal bloating, early satiety, urinary frequency, or palpable pelvic fullness caused by a large ovarian mass or cyst compressing adjacent pelvic organs.
    \item \textbf{Asymptomatic presentation:} Discovery of an ovarian mass incidentally during routine pelvic examinations or transvaginal ultrasonography, or a known high-risk genetic carrier status without active symptoms.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
In patients presenting with symptoms suggestive of ovarian pathology, several other gynaecological and non-gynaecological conditions must be considered.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ectopic pregnancy:} A potentially life-threatening condition presenting with unilateral pelvic pain and abnormal vaginal bleeding, differentiated by a positive beta-hCG test.
    \item \textbf{Appendicitis:} Acute inflammation of the appendix presenting with right lower quadrant pain, fever, and leukocytosis, which can mimic right-sided ovarian torsion or cyst rupture.
    \item \textbf{Uterine fibroids:} Benign smooth muscle tumours of the uterus that can cause pelvic pressure, pain, and heavy menstrual bleeding, differentiated by pelvic ultrasound.
    \item \textbf{Functional ovarian cysts:} Normal physiological cysts (follicular or corpus luteum cysts) that usually resolve spontaneously within one to two menstrual cycles.
    \item \textbf{Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS):} A functional gastrointestinal disorder presenting with abdominal pain, bloating, and altered bowel habits, lacking structural pelvic pathology on imaging.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients scheduled for or recovering from a laparoscopic oophorectomy, as well as those experiencing symptoms of underlying ovarian disease, must be educated on clinical red flags. Prompt medical attention should be sought if there is a recurrence of severe pelvic pain, high fevers, or signs of surgical wound infection such as purulent discharge or spreading redness.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe, and intractable lower abdominal or pelvic pain.
    \item Signs of severe post-operative haemorrhage, such as feeling faint, dizzy, or losing consciousness.
    \item Shortness of breath, chest pain, or coughing up blood, which may indicate a pulmonary embolism.
    \item High fever accompanied by chills, confusion, or rapid breathing, suggestive of systemic sepsis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A structured diagnostic workup is required to characterise the ovarian pathology, assess the risk of malignancy, and plan the surgical approach.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transvaginal ultrasound:} The primary imaging modality used to evaluate the size, internal structure, and vascularity of the ovary, distinguishing solid components, septations, and fluid collections.
    \item \textbf{Tumour markers:} Serum blood tests, including Cancer Antigen 125 (CA125), Carcinoembryonic Antigen (CEA), and CA19-9, to assist in evaluating the risk of epithelial ovarian cancer.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic MRI:} Utilised in indeterminate cases to provide superior soft-tissue characterisation of complex ovarian masses and assess local tissue invasion.
    \item \textbf{Beta-hCG test:} Performed in all premenopausal women presenting with acute pelvic pain to exclude intrauterine or ectopic pregnancy.
    \item \textbf{Genetic screening:} Referral for genetic testing for BRCA1, BRCA2, and Lynch syndrome mismatch repair genes in patients with a strong family history of breast, ovarian, or colorectal cancers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management involves a combination of pre-operative optimisation, standardised surgical techniques, and post-operative support, including hormone replacement.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgical procedure:} Laparoscopic excision involving the insertion of a laparoscope and instruments, ligation of the infundibulopelvic ligament (containing the ovarian vessels) and the utero-ovarian ligament, and removal of the ovary via an endocatch bag to prevent tissue seeding.
    \item \textbf{Post-operative pain management:} Multimodal analgesia including paracetamol, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and short-acting opioids if necessary, to facilitate early mobilisation.
    \item \textbf{Hormone replacement therapy (HRT):} Discussed and initiated in premenopausal patients undergoing bilateral oophorectomy to mitigate the sudden onset of surgical menopause, cardiovascular risk, and osteoporosis, unless contraindicated by hormone-receptor-positive malignancies.
    \item \textbf{Thromboprophylaxis:} Administered based on risk assessment, using mechanical compression stockings and pharmacological agents (e.g., low-molecular-weight heparin) to prevent deep vein thrombosis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While laparoscopic oophorectomy is generally safe, potential surgical and systemic risks must be recognised and managed.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Haemorrhage:} Significant intra-operative or post-operative bleeding from the ovarian vessels, which may require blood transfusion or surgical re-intervention.
    \item \textbf{Injury to adjacent pelvic structures:} Accidental damage to the ureters, urinary bladder, bowel loops, or major pelvic blood vessels during dissection or trocar insertion.
    \item \textbf{Surgical site infection:} Infection of the umbilical or abdominal port-site incisions, or deep pelvic organ-space infections such as abscesses.
    \item \textbf{Surgical menopause:} The immediate onset of menopause in premenopausal women undergoing BSO, characterised by hot flushes, vaginal dryness, mood lability, and long-term risks of osteopenia and cardiovascular disease.
    \item \textbf{Conversion to open laparotomy:} The necessity to abort the laparoscopic approach and perform an open abdominal incision due to extensive adhesions, bleeding, or anatomical distortion.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis following laparoscopic oophorectomy is highly favourable for benign conditions, with most patients experiencing complete resolution of pelvic pain or symptoms related to mass effect. The recovery period is relatively short, with patients typically returning to normal light activities within 1 to 2 weeks, and full physical activity within 4 to 6 weeks. For patients undergoing prophylactic bilateral oophorectomy due to genetic risk, the procedure provides peace of mind and reduces the risk of ovarian and fallopian tube cancers by approximately 80\% to 90\%. In cases where the surgery was performed for malignant indications, the long-term prognosis depends heavily on the stage and histological type of the tumour, as well as the success of adjuvant oncological therapies.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Post-operative care and lifestyle adaptations are critical to promote healing and prevent complications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Wound management:} Keeping the port-site incisions clean and dry, checking daily for signs of infection, and avoiding soaking in baths or swimming until incisions are fully healed.
    \item \textbf{Physical activity restrictions:} Avoiding heavy lifting (greater than 5 kilograms), strenuous exercise, and intense abdominal strain for at least 4 to 6 weeks.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic rest:} Refraining from sexual intercourse, douching, and using tampons for 4 to 6 weeks post-operatively to allow the vaginal vault and pelvic tissues to heal.
    \item \textbf{Constipation prevention:} Maintaining a high-fibre diet, drinking adequate fluids, and taking stool softeners to prevent straining, which can exacerbate pelvic discomfort.
    \item \textbf{Bone health maintenance:} Engaging in regular weight-bearing exercises and ensuring adequate intake of calcium and vitamin D, particularly for patients entering surgical menopause.
\end{itemize}
