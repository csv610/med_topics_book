\chapter{Mononucleosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Infectious mononucleosis, commonly referred to as glandular fever in Australia and the United Kingdom, is a acute viral syndrome most frequently caused by the Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), a member of the herpesvirus family. The condition is classically characterised by a clinical triad of fever, severe pharyngitis (sore throat), and lymphadenopathy (swollen lymph nodes, particularly affecting the posterior cervical chain). The virus primarily infects B-lymphocytes and is transmitted through contact with infected saliva. While glandular fever is typically a self-limiting illness that resolves within several weeks, it can cause prolonged, debilitating fatigue and transient enlargement of the spleen (splenomegaly), necessitating specific modifications in physical activity to prevent traumatic complications.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Epstein-Barr virus is extremely common, infecting over 90\% of the global population by adulthood. The clinical manifestation of primary infection is highly age-dependent: in infants and young children, primary EBV infection is usually asymptomatic or presents as a mild, non-specific respiratory illness. However, when primary infection is delayed until adolescence or young adulthood (ages 15 to 25), it manifests as clinical infectious mononucleosis in 35--50\% of cases. Glandular fever is a frequent diagnosis in high-density environments, such as university dormitories and boarding schools. The incidence is equal between males and females, and there is no significant seasonal variation, although outbreaks may occur in close-knit communities.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The primary pathogen and cellular mechanisms involved in the transmission and development of mononucleosis include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Epstein-Barr virus (EBV):} Also known as human herpesvirus 4, this double-stranded DNA virus is the causative agent in over 90\% of mononucleosis cases.
    \item \textbf{Salivary transmission:} The virus is shed in the saliva of infected individuals and is transmitted via close oral contact (e.g. kissing) or by sharing saliva-contaminated objects such as cups, utensils, or toothbrushes.
    \item \textbf{Infection of B-lymphocytes:} The virus replicates in the epithelial cells of the pharynx and then infects local B-lymphocytes by binding to the CD21 receptor, subsequently spreading throughout the lymphatic system.
    \item \textbf{Cytotoxic T-cell response:} The host immune system responds by producing CD8+ cytotoxic T-lymphocytes to eliminate the infected B-cells. These activated, hypertrophied T-cells appear as atypical lymphocytes (Downey cells) on a peripheral blood film.
    \item \textbf{Non-EBV mononucleosis:} Approximately 10\% of mononucleosis-like syndromes are caused by other pathogens, including cytomegalovirus (CMV), toxoplasmosis, human herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6), and acute HIV seroconversion.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The incubation period of EBV ranges from 4 to 6 weeks, after which symptoms emerge progressively:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fever:} Persistent, moderate-to-high fever (often peaking at 39--40 degrees Celsius), which typically lasts for 1 to 2 weeks.
    \item \textbf{Exudative pharyngitis:} Severe, painful sore throat accompanied by marked tonsillar hypertrophy covered with thick, white-to-grey tonsillar exudates, often mimicking streptococcal tonsillitis.
    \item \textbf{Lymphadenopathy:} Swollen and tender lymph nodes, most prominently involving the posterior cervical chain, though generalized lymphadenopathy can occur.
    \item \textbf{Profound fatigue:} Debilitating lethargy and malaise that interfere with daily activities, often persisting for weeks or months after the fever and sore throat have resolved.
    \item \textbf{Splenomegaly:} Enlargement of the spleen, occurring in 50--60\% of patients, which can cause left upper quadrant abdominal fullness, pain, or early satiety.
    \item \textbf{Aminopenicillin-induced rash:} A widespread, pruritic, maculopapular rash that develops in up to 90\% of patients with mononucleosis if they are mistakenly treated with amoxicillin or ampicillin.
    \item \textbf{Periorbital oedema:} Transient swelling around the eyes, which can occur in the early stages of the illness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the clinical features of mononucleosis overlap with other acute febrile and pharyngeal illnesses, clinicians must differentiate:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Streptococcal pharyngitis (Group A Strep):} Presents with severe sore throat and tonsillar exudates, but is distinguished by the absence of posterior cervical lymphadenopathy, absence of splenomegaly, and a positive throat swab or rapid antigen test.
    \item \textbf{Acute HIV infection (seroconversion):} Presents with fever, pharyngitis, rash, and lymphadenopathy, distinguished by a history of high-risk exposure, presence of mucosal ulcers, and positive HIV viral load.
    \item \textbf{Cytomegalovirus (CMV) mononucleosis:} Causes a highly similar syndrome but typically features milder pharyngitis and lymphadenopathy, and is characterised by a negative heterophile antibody (Monospot) test.
    \item \textbf{Acute leukaemia:} Can present with fatigue, fever, lymphadenopathy, and sore throat, distinguished by a full blood count showing anaemia, thrombocytopenia, and circulating blast cells.
    \item \textbf{Diphtheria:} A rare infectious disease presenting with a grey, adherent pseudomembrane over the tonsils and pharynx, distinguished by travel history and throat culture.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients experiencing symptoms of glandular fever should consult a general practitioner for an accurate diagnosis and guidance on managing recovery.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe, or sharp pain in the left upper quadrant of the abdomen, which may radiate to the left shoulder (indicating potential splenic rupture).
    \item Difficulty breathing, stridor (noisy breathing), or an inability to swallow liquids or saliva (indicating impending airway obstruction).
    \item Sudden collapse, severe dizziness, or loss of consciousness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis is established through blood tests confirming lymphocytosis and heterophile antibodies or specific viral serologies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full blood count (FBC) and blood film:} Demonstrates absolute lymphocytosis (lymphocytes $>50\%$ of white blood cells) with atypical lymphocytes (Downey cells) accounting for $>10\%$ of the total white cell count.
    \item \textbf{Heterophile antibody test (Monospot):} A rapid slide latex agglutination test that detects heterophile antibodies. It is highly specific in adolescents and adults but can yield a false-negative result in the first week of illness and in children under 4 years.
    \item \textbf{EBV-specific serology:} Performed if the Monospot is negative but clinical suspicion remains. Acute infection is confirmed by the presence of IgM anti-Viral Capsid Antigen (VCA) and the absence of IgG anti-Epstein-Barr Nuclear Antigen (EBNA).
    \item \textbf{Liver function tests (LFTs):} Typically show mild to moderate elevations in transaminases (AST and ALT), indicating a transient reactive hepatitis.
    \item \textbf{Throat swab:} Performed to rule out or diagnose a co-existing Group A Streptococcal tonsillitis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is primarily supportive, aimed at symptom control and the prevention of mechanical complications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rest and hydration:} Adequate rest during the acute phase and maintaining fluid intake to prevent dehydration.
    \item \textbf{Analgesia and antipyretics:} Paracetamol and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs, e.g. ibuprofen) to alleviate throat pain and control fever. Aspirin must be avoided in children and adolescents due to the risk of Reye's syndrome.
    \item \textbf{Avoidance of physical exertion:} Strict avoidance of contact sports, heavy lifting, or vigorous exercise for a minimum of 3 to 4 weeks from symptom onset to reduce the risk of traumatic splenic rupture.
    \item \textbf{Corticosteroids:} A short course of oral corticosteroids (e.g. prednisolone) is reserved only for severe complications, such as impending upper airway obstruction, severe thrombocytopenia, or haemolytic anaemia.
    \item \textbf{Avoidance of aminopenicillins:} Withholding amoxicillin and ampicillin to prevent the development of a drug-induced maculopapular rash.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While most cases of mononucleosis recover uneventfully, serious complications can occur:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Splenic rupture:} A rare ( $<0.5\%$) but life-threatening emergency, typically occurring between days 4 and 21 of illness, often precipitated by minor abdominal trauma.
    \item \textbf{Upper airway obstruction:} Severe swelling of the tonsils and adenoids blocking the pharynx.
    \item \textbf{Haematological complications:} Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, mild thrombocytopenia, or neutropenia.
    \item \textbf{Neurological disorders:} Encephalitis, aseptic meningitis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, or cranial nerve palsies (e.g. Bell's palsy).
    \item \textbf{Chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS):} A subset of patients experience prolonged, disabling post-viral fatigue that meets the criteria for ME/CFS.
    \item \textbf{Myocarditis and pericarditis:} Rare cardiac inflammatory complications.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for patients with infectious mononucleosis is excellent. The acute symptoms, such as fever and pharyngitis, usually resolve within 1 to 2 weeks, while lymphadenopathy and splenomegaly settle by 3 to 4 weeks. The most prolonged symptom is fatigue, which typically improves gradually over 1 to 2 months. However, approximately 10\% of patients report persistent fatigue and malaise lasting 6 months or longer. Once the infection has cleared, the virus remains latent in memory B-cells, and the individual develops lifelong immunity, making recurrent episodes of clinical mononucleosis extremely rare.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Practical self-care and hygiene habits can help prevent transmission and support a smooth recovery:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Avoid sharing personal items:} Do not share cups, water bottles, cutlery, toothbrushes, or lip balms with others.
    \item \textbf{Meticulous hand hygiene:} Wash hands regularly with soap and water, especially after blowing your nose, coughing, or contact with saliva.
    \item \textbf{Refrain from kissing:} Avoid kissing or close contact with others while actively unwell or during the early recovery phase.
    \item \textbf{Soothe throat pain:} Gargle with warm salt water, suck on throat lozenges, or drink warm fluids (like tea with honey) to ease throat irritation.
    \item \textbf{Graduated return to activity:} Return to work, school, and light exercise gradually, avoiding overexertion, and obtain medical clearance before resuming contact sports.
\end{itemize}
