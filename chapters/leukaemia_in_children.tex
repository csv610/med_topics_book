\chapter{Leukaemia in children}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Leukaemia in children refers to a group of malignant haematological disorders characterised by the uncontrolled clonal proliferation and accumulation of abnormal immature white blood cells (leukaemic blasts) in the bone marrow and peripheral blood. It is the most common cancer diagnosed in children and adolescents, accounting for approximately one-third of all paediatric malignancies. Paediatric leukaemia is broadly categorised into acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) and acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), with ALL representing the vast majority of cases. These malignant cells crowd out normal haematopoietic cells in the bone marrow, leading to bone marrow failure and consequent anaemia, thrombocytopenia, and neutropenia. Over the past few decades, advances in risk-directed multi-agent chemotherapy, supportive care, and targeted therapies have transformed paediatric leukaemia from a once universally fatal disease to one with high cure rates, particularly for ALL.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Paediatric leukaemia is the leading paediatric oncological diagnosis worldwide. In Australia, approximately 300 to 400 new cases are diagnosed annually in children aged 0 to 14 years. Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia comprises approximately 80\% of paediatric leukaemia cases, with a peak incidence observed in children aged 2 to 5 years and a slightly higher prevalence in boys than girls. Acute myeloid leukaemia is less common, representing about 15--20\% of cases, with a relatively stable incidence throughout childhood and a slight rise during adolescence. Chronic leukaemias, such as juvenile myelomonocytic leukaemia or chronic myeloid leukaemia, are rare in children, representing less than 5\% of cases. While the exact cause remains unknown for most patients, risk patterns show associations with genetic syndromes (such as Down syndrome, which carries a significantly increased risk for both ALL and AML) and rare inherited immune deficiency states.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Paediatric leukaemia arises from a combination of genetic alterations and environmental exposures, although the majority of cases occur without a clear predisposing factor. The pathophysiology involves somatic genetic mutations in a single haematopoietic progenitor cell, leading to dysregulated cell survival, proliferation, and a block in normal differentiation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic predisposition:} Children with Down syndrome (trisomy 21) have a 10-to-20-fold increased risk of developing leukaemia, particularly acute megakaryoblastic leukaemia (a subtype of AML) and ALL. Other predisposing genetic conditions include Fanconi anaemia, Li-Fraumeni syndrome, neurofibromatosis type 1, and ataxia-telangiectasia.
    \item \textbf{Somatic genetic mutations:} Acquired chromosomal translocations, deletions, and aneuploidy are hallmarks of paediatric leukaemia. Common ALL-associated translocations include t(12;21) [\textit{ETV6::RUNX1}], which carries a favourable prognosis, and t(9;22) [\textit{BCR::ABL1}], which represents Philadelphia chromosome-positive ALL and requires targeted tyrosine kinase inhibitors.
    \item \textbf{Bone marrow infiltration:} The rapid expansion of leukaemic blasts within the rigid bone marrow space compresses and suppresses healthy haematopoietic stem cells, leading to cytopenias (anaemia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia).
    \item \textbf{Extramedullary involvement:} Leukaemic cells can migrate from the bone marrow to infiltrate other organs, including the spleen, liver, lymph nodes, central nervous system, and testes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of paediatric leukaemia is highly variable, reflecting the replacement of normal bone marrow components and extramedullary infiltration. Symptoms often develop rapidly over days to weeks.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fatigue, lethargy, and progressive pallor resulting from anaemia.
    \item Easy bruising, petechiae (tiny red or purple spots on the skin), and recurrent mucosal bleeding (such as epistaxis or bleeding gums) due to severe thrombocytopenia.
    \item Recurrent or persistent fevers, bone marrow necrosis, or severe infections resulting from neutropenia and functional white blood cell deficiency.
    \item Bone and joint pain, often causing a limp or refusal to bear weight, caused by the expansion of leukaemic cells within the marrow cavity.
    \item Lymphadenopathy (painless swelling of cervical, axillary, or inguinal lymph nodes) and hepatosplenomegaly (enlarged liver and spleen), presenting as abdominal fullness.
    \item Mediastinal mass features, particularly in T-cell ALL, which can cause superior vena cava syndrome, respiratory distress, and wheezing.
    \item Central nervous system involvement, presenting with signs of increased intracranial pressure such as morning headaches, vomiting, irritability, or cranial nerve palsies.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The initial signs of paediatric leukaemia can be non-specific and overlap with several benign and malignant conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infectious mononucleosis and viral infections:} Epstein-Barr virus or cytomegalovirus infections can cause fever, atypical lymphocytosis, lymphadenopathy, and splenomegaly, but do not typically cause severe cytopenias or bone pain.
    \item \textbf{Juvenile idiopathic arthritis:} Can present with bone and joint pain, fever, and a limp, but joint pain in leukaemia is often disproportionate to physical findings of arthritis and is typically accompanied by abnormal blood counts.
    \item \textbf{Aplastic anaemia:} Characterised by pancytopenia due to bone marrow failure, but lacks the presence of circulating blast cells, organomegaly, or bone pain.
    \item \textbf{Immune thrombocytopenia:} Causes sudden bruising and petechiae due to isolated thrombocytopenia, but is not accompanied by anaemia, neutropenia, bone pain, or organomegaly.
    \item \textbf{Neuroblastoma and other paediatric solid tumours:} Can metastasise to the bone marrow and cause bone pain and cytopenias, but are distinguished by specific imaging findings and urinary catecholamine assays.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents should seek immediate medical assessment if they notice signs of unexplained bruising, persistent bone pain, or a pale, lethargic child, particularly if accompanied by fever.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Extreme difficulty breathing, grunting, or stridor (suggesting a mediastinal mass compressing the airway).
    \item Uncontrolled, heavy bleeding or features of severe haemorrhage.
    \item A fever in a child known to be undergoing chemotherapy for leukaemia (oncological emergency).
    \item Altered consciousness, seizures, or sudden weakness in the limbs.
\end{itemize}

Other non-emergency reasons to seek medical attention include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unexplained petechiae or bruising without a history of injury.
    \item Persistent, worsening bone or joint pain that wakes the child from sleep.
    \item Prolonged, unexplained fever or recurrent infections.
    \item Progressive, unexplained pale skin, fatigue, and loss of appetite.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Establishing a diagnosis of leukaemia requires detailed blood tests, imaging, and a definitive bone marrow biopsy.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full blood count and peripheral blood smear:} Typically shows cytopenias (anaemia, thrombocytopenia) and may show marked leukocytosis or leukopenia; the peripheral smear often reveals circulating leukaemic blasts.
    \item \textbf{Bone marrow aspirate and biopsy:} The gold standard diagnostic test, performed under sedation. A diagnosis of acute leukaemia is confirmed when blast cells constitute 20\% or more of all nucleated cells in the marrow.
    \item \textbf{Flow cytometry and immunophenotyping:} Performed on bone marrow samples to identify specific cell surface markers (CD markers) and determine whether the leukaemia is of B-cell, T-cell, or myeloid lineage.
    \item \textbf{Cytogenetic and molecular testing:} Identifies specific chromosomal rearrangements, karyotypic abnormalities, and gene mutations (e.g., \textit{ETV6::RUNX1}, \textit{BCR::ABL1}, \textit{KMT2A} rearrangements) to assist in risk stratification.
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture:} Performed to collect cerebrospinal fluid to check for the presence of leukaemic cells in the central nervous system, helping determine the need for intrathecal chemotherapy.
    \item \textbf{Serum biochemistry and tumour lysis markers:} Evaluation of renal function, liver enzymes, electrolytes, uric acid, lactate dehydrogenase, and phosphate to screen for spontaneous tumour lysis syndrome.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of paediatric leukaemia is highly complex and must be coordinated by a paediatric oncologist in a specialised tertiary paediatric hospital.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy:} The cornerstone of treatment, divided into distinct phases: induction (to achieve remission), consolidation/intensification (to eradicate subclinical disease), and maintenance (low-dose oral and intravenous chemotherapy for 2 to 3 years to prevent recurrence).
    \item \textbf{Intrathecal chemotherapy:} Delivery of chemotherapy drugs (such as methotrexate, cytarabine, and hydrocortisone) directly into the cerebrospinal fluid via lumbar puncture to prevent or treat central nervous system relapse.
    \item \textbf{Targeted therapy:} Use of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (e.g., imatinib, dasatinib) for children with Philadelphia chromosome-positive (\textit{BCR::ABL1}) ALL, or FLT3 inhibitors for specific AML subtypes.
    \item \textbf{Immunotherapy:} Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy (e.g., tisagenlecleucel) or bispecific antibodies (e.g., blinatumomab) for relapsed or refractory B-cell ALL.
    \item \textbf{Haematopoietic stem cell transplant:} Indicated for high-risk AML, relapsed leukaemia, or high-risk ALL that fails to respond to induction chemotherapy.
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} Prophylactic antibiotics, antifungal agents, granulocyte colony-stimulating factors, blood and platelet transfusions, and intensive hydration to prevent and manage infection, bleeding, and tumour lysis syndrome.
    \item \textbf{Psychosocial support:} Multidisciplinary support including social work, psychology, play therapy, and educational support to help the child and family navigate the emotional and social impacts of cancer treatment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tumour lysis syndrome:} A metabolic emergency caused by the rapid breakdown of cancer cells, releasing intracellular potassium, phosphate, and uric acid into the blood, potentially causing acute kidney injury and cardiac arrhythmias.
    \item \textbf{Febrile neutropenia:} Severe, life-threatening infection risk due to chemotherapy-induced bone marrow suppression; requires immediate hospitalisation and broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics.
    \item \textbf{Haemorrhage:} Serious bleeding risks, particularly intracranial or pulmonary haemorrhage, due to severe thrombocytopenia.
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy toxicities:} Acute toxicities include nausea, hair loss, mucositis (painful mouth ulcers), peripheral neuropathy (e.g., from vincristine), and haemorrhagic cystitis (e.g., from cyclophosphamide).
    \item \textbf{Long-term late effects:} Chronic health issues arising years after treatment, including cardiac dysfunction (from anthracyclines), growth retardation, endocrine dysfunction, infertility, cognitive impairments, and secondary malignancies.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for children with leukaemia has improved dramatically over the last half-century. For paediatric acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, the overall 5-year survival rate now exceeds 90\%, and the majority of these children are considered cured. Acute myeloid leukaemia carries a more guarded prognosis, with a 5-year survival rate of approximately 65--70\% and a higher risk of treatment resistance and relapse. Prognosis is heavily influenced by patient age, initial white blood cell count, immunophenotype, cytogenetic features, and the speed of response to induction chemotherapy (measured by minimal residual disease). Long-term survivorship clinics are essential to monitor for late treatment toxicities and support survivors' quality of life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Follow strict hygiene measures, including frequent handwashing, avoiding crowds, and ensuring all household members are fully vaccinated, to protect the child during periods of neutropenia.
    \item Avoid administering live vaccines (e.g., MMR, varicella) to the child while they are immunocompromised, and consult the clinical team regarding vaccination schedules for family members.
    \item Ensure the child consumes only pasteurised foods and avoids raw, unwashed fruit and vegetables or undercooked meats when neutropenic.
    \item Monitor the child's temperature regularly and contact the oncology unit immediately if it rises above 38 degrees Celsius or if the child shivers or becomes lethargic.
    \item Care for central venous access devices (such as port-a-caths or Hickman lines) according to clinical protocols to prevent bloodstream infections and line displacement.
    \item Encourage gentle physical activity and support returning to school when clinically safe, using specialised educational support programmes.
\end{itemize}
