\chapter{HPV Vaccine}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The HPV vaccine protects against the human papillomavirus (HPV) types that cause most cervical, anal, and other HPV-related cancers, and the types that cause most genital warts. In Australia, it is offered free to girls and boys aged 12--13 through school-based vaccination programs, and it is most effective when given before sexual activity begins. The vaccine is safe, highly effective, and has dramatically reduced HPV infection, genital warts, and cervical pre-cancer in vaccinated cohorts. It does not treat existing infection, so it is best given before exposure.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Australia was one of the first countries to fund HPV vaccination, beginning with girls in 2007 and extending to boys in 2013. Vaccination rates are high, with around 80\% of adolescents fully vaccinated. The impact has been remarkable: infections with the vaccine types have fallen by over 90\% in young people, genital warts have almost disappeared in vaccinated cohorts, and cervical pre-cancer rates have fallen sharply. Australia is projected to virtually eliminate cervical cancer by 2035.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vaccine types:} the current vaccines protect against 9 HPV types, including the high-risk types 16 and 18 and the wart types 6 and 11
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} the vaccine stimulates antibodies against the HPV surface proteins, preventing the virus from establishing infection
    \item \textbf{Virus-like particles:} the vaccine contains no live virus and cannot cause HPV infection
    \item \textbf{Timing:} best given before sexual debut, but catch-up vaccination is beneficial up to age 26 and sometimes beyond
    \item \textbf{Effectiveness:} over 95\% effective against infection and disease caused by the vaccine types
    \item \textbf{Duration:} protection lasts many years, and booster doses are not currently recommended
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} preventing infection prevents the cell changes and cancers that follow persistent infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Routinely offered to girls and boys at age 12--13 through school programs
    \item Given as two doses six to twelve months apart at this age
    \item Catch-up vaccination is available for older adolescents and young adults
    \item Adults up to age 26, and some older adults, can discuss vaccination with their doctor
    \item The vaccine is given into the upper arm
    \item Mild side effects: soreness at the injection site, headache, and fatigue
    \item Serious side effects are extremely rare
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vaccination is recommended even for people who have already had HPV, as it protects against types not yet encountered
    \item Vaccinated women still need cervical screening, because the vaccine does not cover all cancer-causing types
    \item The vaccine is not a treatment for existing warts or cell changes
    \item Vaccination during pregnancy is avoided, but it is safe if given inadvertently
    \item Vaccination is safe and recommended for people with most medical conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents should arrange vaccination for their children through school programs or their GP, and young adults up to age 26 should discuss catch-up vaccination. Pregnant women wait until after pregnancy.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe allergic reaction after vaccination: difficulty breathing, swelling, or collapse
    \item Fainting after vaccination that does not recover
    \item Any life-threatening emergency
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immunisation history:} checking vaccination status
    \item \textbf{National register:} vaccinations are recorded on the Australian Immunisation Register
    \item \textbf{Catch-up schedules:} for adolescents and adults who missed school vaccination
    \item \textbf{Cervical screening reminder:} vaccinated women still attend screening from age 25
    \item \textbf{No pre-vaccination HPV testing is needed}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{School program:} free vaccination for girls and boys aged 12--13
    \item \textbf{Catch-up:} free programs for some older adolescents and young adults
    \item \textbf{GP and pharmacy vaccination:} for those who miss school programs
    \item \textbf{Two-dose schedule:} for those under 15; three doses for older recipients
    \item \textbf{Recording:} doses are recorded on the register
    \item \textbf{Reminders:} families are notified of missed doses
    \item \textbf{Screening continues:} cervical screening remains essential regardless of vaccination
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Injection-site soreness, headache, and fatigue, which settle quickly
    \item Fainting, which is common in adolescents after any vaccination
    \item Extremely rare severe allergic reactions
    \item Missed vaccination leaves people unprotected
    \item False reassurance if vaccinated people skip screening
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The HPV vaccine is one of the most effective vaccines ever developed. Vaccinated cohorts in Australia have seen near-elimination of genital warts and dramatic falls in cervical pre-cancer, and the program is projected to make cervical cancer virtually extinct. Protection is long-lasting, and the vaccine is extremely safe. Combined with screening, the HPV vaccine has put cervical cancer elimination within reach.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vaccinate girls and boys at age 12--13
    \item Arrange catch-up vaccination for adolescents and young adults who missed it
    \item Attend all scheduled doses
    \item Continue cervical screening from age 25 even after vaccination
    \item Report concerns about side effects to your doctor
    \item Encourage eligible family members to be vaccinated
    \item Discuss vaccination with your doctor if you are over 26 and at risk
\end{itemize}
