\chapter{Diffuse Idiopathic Skeletal Hyperostosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Diffuse idiopathic skeletal hyperostosis (DISH), also called Forestier's disease, is a condition in which new bone forms along the spine and at the sites where tendons and ligaments attach to bone. It most commonly affects the thoracic spine, where flowing bone bridges develop between vertebrae, but it can also affect other areas. DISH is common in older people and is often found incidentally on imaging. Many people have no symptoms, while others develop stiffness, pain, and reduced movement. DISH is not the same as osteoarthritis or ankylosing spondylitis, and it is generally managed with symptom relief and maintaining mobility.

\section*{Epidemiology}
DISH becomes more common with age, affecting around 10--25\% of people over 50 and a higher proportion over 70. It is more common in men than women and is associated with obesity, diabetes, and metabolic syndrome. Many cases are discovered incidentally on X-rays or CT scans performed for other reasons. DISH is a common cause of back stiffness and reduced spinal movement in older adults, although it often causes no symptoms at all.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{New bone formation:} calcification and ossification of ligaments and tendons where they attach to bone (entheses)
    \item \textbf{Spinal bridges:} flowing ossification along the front and sides of the vertebrae, most often in the thoracic spine
    \item \textbf{Metabolic associations:} strongly linked to obesity, diabetes, high blood pressure, and elevated uric acid
    \item \textbf{Growth factors:} increased levels of growth factors may drive bone formation
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} chronic inflammation at entheses and metabolic changes promote bone deposition
    \item \textbf{Extraspinal involvement:} ossification can affect the pelvis, heels, elbows, and knees
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Many people have no symptoms and the condition is found incidentally
    \item Stiffness of the back, especially in the morning, which improves with activity
    \item Reduced range of movement of the spine
    \item Pain in the back, neck, or affected joints
    \item Difficulty swallowing in rare cases when cervical bone spurs press on the oesophagus
    \item Difficulty breathing in very rare cases of upper airway involvement
    \item Heel pain from enthesitis at the heel
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ankylosing spondylitis, an inflammatory spinal condition with different features and associations
    \item Osteoarthritis of the spine, which affects the discs and facet joints
    \item Spondylosis and degenerative disc disease
    \item Diffuse bone disease such as Paget's disease
    \item DISH versus ossification of the posterior longitudinal ligament, a related condition
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for persistent back pain or stiffness, reduced mobility, or new symptoms. Imaging findings of DISH should be discussed with a doctor, especially if there is any new pain or difficulty swallowing.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe back pain with leg weakness, numbness, or loss of bladder or bowel control (possible spinal emergency)
    \item Difficulty swallowing or breathing
    \item A fall with significant injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} show the characteristic flowing ossification along the spine
    \item \textbf{CT or MRI:} used when symptoms are atypical or complications are suspected
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} inflammatory markers are usually normal, helping distinguish DISH from inflammatory arthritis
    \item \textbf{Assessment of associated conditions:} testing for diabetes, high blood pressure, and metabolic syndrome
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} assessment of spinal movement and enthesitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} paracetamol and anti-inflammatory medicines for pain and stiffness
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} exercises to maintain spinal mobility and posture
    \item \textbf{Heat:} warm packs and heat therapy ease stiffness
    \item \textbf{Weight management:} addressing obesity and metabolic factors
    \item \textbf{Managing associated conditions:} treatment of diabetes and high blood pressure
    \item \textbf{Foot care:} treatment of heel pain and enthesitis
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} rarely needed, for severe complications such as swallowing or airway problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive stiffness and reduced mobility
    \item Increased risk of spinal fractures after falls, because the spine is stiff
    \item Rarely, difficulty swallowing or breathing from large cervical bone spurs
    \item Pain and reduced quality of life
    \item Associated metabolic conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
DISH is generally a slowly progressive condition, and most people maintain reasonable function with symptom management and exercise. It does not cause the severe inflammation or deformity of ankylosing spondylitis, and many people live with it without significant disability. Maintaining mobility, managing weight, and treating associated conditions give the best long-term outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep the spine moving with regular exercise, including stretching
    \item Maintain a healthy weight
    \item Manage diabetes, blood pressure, and cholesterol
    \item Use good posture and ergonomics
    \item Use heat and gentle exercise to manage stiffness
    \item Prevent falls, as the stiff spine is more prone to fracture
    \item See a doctor for persistent pain or new symptoms
\end{itemize}
