\chapter{Microdochectomy}
\index{Microdochectomy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A microdochectomy (sometimes spelled microductectomy) is a localised surgical procedure performed to excise a single, specific lactiferous duct of the breast. It is generalised as the standard treatment for pathological nipple discharge---which is typically unilateral, spontaneous, and arises from a single duct opening, often containing blood or serous fluid. The most common underlying cause is a benign intraductal papilloma, but the procedure is crucial to rule out ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) or invasive breast cancer.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Pathological nipple discharge is the third most common breast symptom for which women seek medical evaluation, after breast pain (mastalgia) and breast lumps. Microdochectomy is most frequently performed in premenopausal women aged 30 to 50. While the vast majority of patients undergoing this procedure are diagnosed with a benign lesion (such as an intraductal papilloma or duct ectasia), approximately 10--15\% of cases of bloody nipple discharge may be associated with malignancy, with the risk increasing with age.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Pathological discharge occurs when a lesion arises within the lining of a major lactiferous duct:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intraductal papilloma:} A benign, finger-like growth of epithelial cells within the duct lumen that easily bleeds, causing bloody or serous discharge.
    \item \textbf{Duct ectasia:} Chronic inflammation and dilatation of a subareolar duct, leading to thick, multi-coloured or sticky discharge.
    \item \textbf{Malignant transformation:} Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) or invasive papillary carcinoma arising from the ductal epithelium, shedding blood cells and fluid.
    \item \textbf{Localised excision:} By identifying and removing only the single discharging duct, the surgeon achieves a definitive histopathological diagnosis while preserving the surrounding breast tissue and remaining ducts.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Patients scheduled for or recovering from a microdochectomy present with specific localised signs:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pathological nipple discharge:} Spontaneous, persistent discharge from a single duct opening on one nipple, which may be bloody (haematic), pink, or clear (serous).
    \item \textbf{Absence of a palpable mass:} In many cases, the underlying ductal lesion is small and cannot be felt during physical examination.
    \item \textbf{Trigger zone:} A specific area on the areola that, when pressed, consistently reproduces the nipple discharge.
    \item \textbf{Post-operative findings:} A small incision, typically along the edge of the areola (periareolar incision), with mild bruising and localised discomfort.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before performing a microdochectomy, other causes of nipple discharge must be excluded:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physiological nipple discharge:} Bilateral, multi-duct discharge that occurs only upon expression, often white, green, or yellow, and typically related to hormonal changes.
    \item \textbf{Galactorrhoea:} Bilateral, milky discharge from multiple ducts, caused by elevated prolactin levels (hyperprolactinaemia), often due to medications or a pituitary adenoma.
    \item \textbf{Mammary duct ectasia:} Involving multiple ducts, usually presenting with thicker, non-bloody discharge, often managed conservatively.
    \item \textbf{Fibrocystic breast changes:} Benign changes causing diffuse breast pain and multi-duct discharge.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Women who notice any new, spontaneous, unilateral, or bloody nipple discharge should consult their GP or a breast specialist for clinical evaluation. Immediate emergency care is not required for nipple discharge itself, but surgical complications or systemic signs require urgent attention.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, worsening redness, warmth, or swelling of the breast accompanied by a high fever (signs of acute breast infection or abscess)
    \item Significant, active bleeding or discharge from the surgical wound
    \item Severe chest pain or sudden shortness of breath post-operatively
    \item Sudden loss of consciousness or severe dizziness
    \item Any acute, life-threatening symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A triple assessment is carried out before a microdochectomy is planned:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical breast examination:} Detailed inspection of both breasts and the nipples, identifying the specific discharging duct.
    \item \textbf{Breast imaging:} Mammography (for women over 35) and high-resolution ultrasound to identify any underlying masses or ductal dilatation.
    \item \textbf{Ductography (galactography):} Injecting a small amount of contrast dye into the discharging duct followed by X-rays to visualise filling defects or blockages.
    \item \textbf{Nipple discharge cytology:} Examining a smear of the fluid under a microscope, though this has low sensitivity for ruling out cancer.
    \item \textbf{Ductoscopy:} Inserting a micro-endoscope into the duct to visualise the lesion directly (available in specialised centres).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management consists of pre-operative marking, the surgical procedure, and post-operative care:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative cannulation:} Inserting a fine lacrimal probe or injecting a small amount of sterile blue dye into the discharging duct opening just before surgery to guide the surgeon.
    \item \textbf{Surgical excision:} Under general or local anaesthesia, a circumareolar incision is made, and the marked duct is dissected out and excised down to its base.
    \item \textbf{Histopathological analysis:} Sending the excised duct for microscopic examination to confirm the diagnosis (e.g., papilloma vs. malignancy).
    \item \textbf{Post-operative wound care:} Keeping the dressing dry for 48 hours, wearing a supportive, non-wired bra, and using simple analgesics (like paracetamol) for pain control.
    \item \textbf{Activity restriction:} Avoiding heavy lifting and vigorous exercise for 1 to 2 weeks to promote wound healing.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wound infection or localised haematoma requiring antibiotics or drainage
    \item Nipple numbness or altered sensation (usually temporary, but can be permanent)
    \item Inability to breastfeed from the affected duct in the future (though other ducts and the opposite breast are unaffected)
    \item Nipple retraction or minor cosmetic deformity of the subareolar area
    \item Recurrence of nipple discharge if the abnormal duct was not completely excised or if another duct develops a lesion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis after a microdochectomy is excellent. For the vast majority of patients, the procedure successfully cures the nipple discharge and confirms a benign diagnosis (such as a solitary intraductal papilloma). If a malignancy is detected, the microdochectomy serves as the initial diagnostic step, and further treatment (such as wider excision, sentinel node biopsy, radiotherapy, or endocrine therapy) is planned based on the pathology results.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend routine breast screening (such as BreastScreen the affected region) as recommended for your age group
    \item Avoid squeezing or stimulating the nipple, as this can worsen discharge and inflammation
    \item Monitor the surgical wound daily for signs of infection, such as redness, warmth, or pus
    \item Wear a supportive, comfortable bra without underwires during the early healing phase to minimise pain and friction
    \item Perform regular breast self-awareness checks and report any new lumps or changes to a doctor immediately
\end{itemize}
