\chapter{Developmental Milestones}
\index{Developmental Milestones}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Developmental milestones are the skills and abilities that most children achieve by certain ages, across the areas of motor skills (movement), language and communication, social and emotional skills, and cognitive and problem-solving abilities. Milestones provide a guide to typical development, helping parents and health professionals recognise when a child is developing normally and when extra support is needed. Every child develops at their own pace, and there is a wide range of normal, but significant delays warrant early assessment because early intervention improves outcomes. This chapter outlines key milestones and how to support a child's development.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Most children reach milestones within the expected age ranges, but developmental delays are common: around 5--15\% of children have some form of delay, and about 1--2\% of children are diagnosed with a developmental disability such as autism or intellectual disability. Speech and language delays are the most common concerns raised by parents. Early identification and intervention significantly improve outcomes, which is why routine developmental surveillance at child health checks is important. In many countries, the child health checks and the Blue Book are used to track development.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal variation:} children develop at different rates within a broad normal range
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} genes influence the timing and pattern of development
    \item \textbf{Environment:} stimulation, interaction, nutrition, and opportunity shape development
    \item \textbf{Prematurity:} premature babies may reach milestones later than their chronological age suggests, and are usually assessed by corrected age
    \item \textbf{Causes of delay:} hearing or vision problems, autism, intellectual disability, cerebral palsy, and other conditions
    \item \textbf{Family history:} some variation in development runs in families
    \item \textbf{Nutrition and health:} good nutrition, sleep, and physical health support development
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{By 6 months:} smiles, rolls over, reaches for objects, babbles, and responds to sounds
    \item \textbf{By 12 months:} sits, stands, takes first steps, says first words, waves, and shows attachment to caregivers
    \item \textbf{By 18 months:} walks well, says several words, points, and plays simple games
    \item \textbf{By 2 years:} runs, builds towers, uses two-word phrases, and begins pretend play
    \item \textbf{By 3 years:} climbs, speaks in short sentences, rides a tricycle, and plays with others
    \item \textbf{By 4--5 years:} hops, draws shapes, tells stories, and manages self-care tasks such as dressing
    \item Red flags include loss of previously acquired skills, no speech by certain ages, and lack of response to the child's name
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Normal variation in development, where the child is within the broad normal range
    \item Hearing or vision impairment causing apparent developmental delay
    \item Autism spectrum disorder, which affects social communication and behaviour
    \item Intellectual disability and other neurodevelopmental conditions
    \item Speech and language disorders that occur with otherwise normal development
    \item Motor disorders, including cerebral palsy
    \item Environmental factors, including limited stimulation or neglect
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents should seek advice if they are worried about their child's development, if the child has not reached expected milestones, or if there is any loss of skills. Early concerns should be discussed with a GP, child health nurse, or paediatrician, since early assessment and support make a real difference. Trust parental concerns: they are often the first sign of a problem.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden loss of skills, seizures, or a sudden change in behaviour or consciousness
    \item The child stops breathing, has a serious fall, or shows signs of serious illness
    \item Any sudden neurological symptoms such as weakness, facial droop, or difficulty waking
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Developmental surveillance:} milestones are tracked at routine child health checks
    \item \textbf{Standardised screening tools:} questionnaires such as the Ages and Stages Questionnaire (ASQ) and the PEDS
    \item \textbf{Hearing and vision tests:} essential in any child with a suspected delay
    \item \textbf{Developmental assessment:} formal assessment by a paediatrician or psychologist
    \item \textbf{Autism assessment:} specialist assessment where social communication concerns exist
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing and imaging:} where specific conditions are suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Early intervention:} therapy services such as speech therapy, occupational therapy, and physiotherapy are highly effective when started early
    \item \textbf{Parent support:} coaching parents to respond to and stimulate their child
    \item \textbf{Medical treatment:} management of hearing, vision, or other medical causes of delay
    \item \textbf{Educational support:} kindergarten and school support programs
    \item \textbf{Family services:} connection with services such as early childhood intervention providers in each state
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular reassessment to track progress
    \item \textbf{Support for the family:} counselling, information, and peer support
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Missed early intervention when delays are not recognised
    \item Difficulties at school with learning, behaviour, and social skills
    \item Low self-esteem and frustration in the child
    \item Parental stress and uncertainty
    \item Progression of an underlying condition without treatment
    \item Family and financial strain from navigating services
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The vast majority of children develop within the normal range, and many children with mild or transient delays catch up with support. Early intervention significantly improves outcomes for children with delays or disabilities, including speech, learning, and social functioning. Outcomes depend on the cause and severity of any delay, but timely assessment and support give every child the best chance. With the right help, most children reach their potential and lead happy, fulfilling lives.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend routine child health checks and developmental surveillance
    \item Talk, read, and sing to your child from birth
    \item Provide plenty of floor play and tummy time for babies
    \item Encourage play, outdoor activity, and social interaction
    \item Ensure good nutrition, sleep, and physical health
    \item Limit screen time, especially for young children
    \item Seek advice early if you have concerns about development
    \item Trust your instincts: no concern is too small to raise
\end{itemize}
