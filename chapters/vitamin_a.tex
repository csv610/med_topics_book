\chapter{Vitamin a}
\index{Vitamin a}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Vitamin A is a fat-soluble micronutrient consisting of a group of structurally related organic compounds. These include preformed Vitamin A (retinol, retinal, and retinyl esters) and provitamin A carotenoids, most notably beta-carotene, alpha-carotene, and beta-cryptoxanthin. Preformed Vitamin A is highly bioavailable and found in animal-sourced foods, whereas provitamin A carotenoids are pigments in plant-sourced foods that the human body converts into retinol.

Physiologically, Vitamin A is crucial for multiple biological functions. In the visual system, retinal binds to the protein opsin in rod cells to form rhodopsin, the light-sensitive pigment required for vision under low-light conditions. As its active metabolite retinoic acid, Vitamin A functions as a hormone-like regulator of gene expression by binding to nuclear receptors (Retinoic Acid Receptors and Retinoid X Receptors). This regulates cellular differentiation, growth, and the maintenance of mucosal epithelial barriers in the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and genitourinary tracts. Furthermore, Vitamin A is essential for systemic immunity, maintaining T-lymphocyte and B-lymphocyte function, and supporting reproductive health, including spermatogenesis and embryonic development. Its discovery in 1913 by Elmer McCollum and Marguerite Davis established the field of fat-soluble vitamin research.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Vitamin A deficiency (VAD) is a major global public health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income regions such as Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that approximately 190 million preschool-aged children and 19 million pregnant women suffer from subclinical deficiency, defined by a serum retinol level below 0.70 $\mu$mol/L (20 $\mu$g/dL). VAD remains the leading cause of preventable childhood blindness worldwide, contributing to 250,000 to 500,000 cases annually. Approximately half of these children die within a year of becoming blind due to increased vulnerability to severe infections.

The demographic distribution highlights that infants, young children (6--59 months), and pregnant or lactating women are at the greatest risk due to elevated physiological demands. In high-income countries, VAD is rare and usually secondary to severe malabsorptive disorders. Conversely, Vitamin A toxicity (hypervitaminosis A) is more prevalent in developed countries, typically resulting from excessive intake of high-dose dietary supplements or treatment with therapeutic retinoid medications (such as isotretinoin) for dermatological conditions.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The development of Vitamin A disorders is driven by dietary, gastrointestinal, and systemic factors. The primary causes are detailed below:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary Insufficiency}: Inadequate consumption of foods rich in preformed Vitamin A or carotenoids. This is common in regions reliant on starchy staple foods (e.g., polished white rice) and among infants who are not breastfed or weaned appropriately.
    \item \textbf{Malabsorption Syndromes}: Impaired fat absorption due to gastrointestinal diseases such as celiac disease, Crohn's disease, cystic fibrosis, pancreatic insufficiency, bariatric surgery, or cholestatic liver disease.
    \item \textbf{Infectious Diseases}: Febrile infections, especially measles and diarrheal illnesses, which accelerate Vitamin A utilization, increase urinary excretion, and temporarily suppress hepatic synthesis of retinol-binding protein (RBP).
    \item \textbf{Excessive Supplementation}: Ingestion of large amounts of preformed Vitamin A, causing acute toxicity (single dose of more than 150 mg for adults) or chronic toxicity (daily intake exceeding 10,000 mcg RAE over months).
\end{itemize}

Pathophysiologically, dietary retinyl esters and carotenoids are absorbed in the small intestine, packaged into chylomicrons, and transported to the liver. The liver is the primary storage site, storing Vitamin A as retinyl esters within hepatic stellate cells. When needed, retinol is released into circulation bound to RBP and transthyretin. Once inside target cells, retinol is oxidized to retinoic acid, which enters the nucleus to bind to Retinoic Acid Receptors (RARs) and Retinoid X Receptors (RXRs), modifying the transcription of target genes.

In VAD, the visual cycle is disrupted by a lack of 11-cis-retinal, leading to impaired rod function. In epithelial tissues, a lack of retinoic acid causes squamous metaplasia, replacing mucus-secreting goblet cells with keratinized squamous cells. This compromises mucosal immunity and tissue integrity.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Vitamin A disorders manifest through progressive ocular changes, skin lesions, immune suppression, or signs of systemic toxicity.

Ocular manifestations of deficiency (xerophthalmia) progress through the following stages:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nyctalopia}: Night blindness, which is the earliest clinical symptom of VAD.
    \item \textbf{Conjunctival Xerosis}: Dry, dull, and wrinkled conjunctival membranes due to the loss of goblet cells.
    \item \textbf{Bitot's Spots}: Dry, triangular, foamy, white or gray patches on the bulbar conjunctiva.
    \item \textbf{Corneal Xerosis}: Hazy, dry, and rough appearance of the cornea.
    \item \textbf{Keratomalacia}: Rapid necrosis, softening, and liquefaction of the cornea, leading to perforation and permanent blindness.
\end{itemize}

Extra-ocular clinical features of VAD include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dermatological Changes}: Dry, rough skin and follicular hyperkeratosis (phrynoderma), characterized by keratin plugs in hair follicles.
    \item \textbf{Systemic Infections}: Increased frequency and severity of respiratory, gastrointestinal, and measles infections.
    \item \textbf{Growth Failure}: Delayed skeletal development and growth retardation in children.
\end{itemize}

Clinical features of Vitamin A toxicity include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute Toxicity}: Increased intracranial pressure (pseudotumor cerebri) presenting with severe headache, nausea, projectile vomiting, drowsiness, and bulging fontanelles in infants, followed by skin desquamation.
    \item \textbf{Chronic Toxicity}: Dry, peeling skin, cheilosis, alopecia, bone pain, hyperostosis, hepatosplenomegaly, and hypercalcemia.
    \item \textbf{Teratogenicity}: Congenital malformations (Fetal Retinoid Syndrome) if high doses are ingested during early pregnancy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Clinicians must differentiate Vitamin A disorders from conditions with overlapping clinical features:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Retinitis Pigmentosa}: An inherited retinal dystrophy causing progressive night blindness and bone-spicule pigmentation on fundoscopy, which is not responsive to Vitamin A.
    \item \textbf{Congenital Stationary Night Blindness}: A genetic, non-progressive night blindness characterized by a normal fundus and normal serum Vitamin A levels.
    \item \textbf{Zinc Deficiency}: Zinc is necessary for RBP synthesis and retinal conversion; deficiency can mimic Vitamin A deficiency-induced night blindness.
    \item \textbf{Sj\"{o}gren's Syndrome}: An autoimmune condition causing dry eyes and mouth, diagnosed by positive autoantibodies and a Schirmer's test, and unresponsive to Vitamin A.
    \item \textbf{Keratosis Pilaris}: A benign genetic skin condition presenting with follicular papules, resembling phrynoderma but unrelated to nutrition.
    \item \textbf{Idiopathic Intracranial Hypertension}: Elevated intracranial pressure without a space-occupying lesion or toxicity, primarily affecting young, obese women.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Medical evaluation is recommended for persistent night blindness, chronic dry eyes, dry bumpy skin, or recurrent, severe infections.

Seek immediate emergency care for the following critical symptoms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden loss of vision, severe eye pain, or corneal cloudiness.
    \item Signs of elevated intracranial pressure, such as sudden severe headache, projectile vomiting, confusion, or a bulging fontanelle.
    \item Accidental ingestion of massive doses of Vitamin A supplements.
\end{itemize}

In life-threatening situations, contact emergency services immediately:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{local emergency services} in many countries.
    \item \textbf{911} in the United States and Canada.
    \item \textbf{999} in the United Kingdom.
    \item \textbf{112} in the European Union.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis requires a combination of clinical, biochemical, and functional assessments:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical and Ocular Exams}: Assessing dietary history, fat absorption, and performing slit-lamp examinations to detect corneal changes and Bitot's spots.
    \item \textbf{Serum Retinol Concentration}: Measured via HPLC. Normal levels range from 1.05 to 3.00 $\mu$mol/L. Levels below 0.70 $\mu$mol/L indicate deficiency, while levels above 3.50 $\mu$mol/L suggest toxicity.
    \item \textbf{Serum Retinol-Binding Protein (RBP)}: Used as a surrogate for retinol. Because RBP is a negative acute-phase reactant, inflammatory markers like CRP and AGP must be measured concurrently to rule out false-positive deficiency during infections.
    \item \textbf{Relative Dose Response (RDR) Tests}: Measures the percentage increase in serum retinol 5 hours after a small oral dose of Vitamin A. A rise of 20\% or more indicates low hepatic stores.
    \item \textbf{Functional Tests}: Dark adaptometry to assess rod threshold and electroretinography (ERG) to detect delayed rod-mediated responses.
    \item \textbf{Liver Biopsy}: The gold standard for measuring hepatic retinyl ester stores, but rarely performed due to its invasiveness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment strategies differ based on the diagnosis of deficiency or toxicity:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{WHO Supplementation Protocol}: For children with xerophthalmia, severe malnutrition, or measles:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Infants under 6 months: 50,000 IU orally once.
        \item Infants 6--11 months: 100,000 IU orally once.
        \item Children 12 months and older: 200,000 IU orally on days 1, 2, and 14.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Maternal Supplementation}: Pregnant women should receive lower doses (10,000 IU daily or 25,000 IU weekly for 4 weeks) to avoid teratogenicity.
    \item \textbf{Parenteral Administration}: Water-soluble intramuscular injections for patients with severe malabsorption or vomiting.
    \item \textbf{Ocular Support}: Preservative-free lubricants, topical antibiotics, and protective eye shields for corneal involvement.
    \item \textbf{Toxicity Management}: Immediate cessation of Vitamin A and retinoid intake.
    \item \textbf{Hypercalcemia Treatment}: Intravenous hydration with normal saline, loop diuretics, bisphosphonates, or calcitonin, along with corticosteroids to limit calcium absorption.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated Vitamin A disorders can cause permanent organ damage:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Irreversible Blindness}: Corneal melting (keratomalacia) leading to perforation and phthisis bulbi.
    \item \textbf{Infectious Mortality}: A 30\% to 50\% increase in childhood mortality from measles, diarrhea, and pneumonia.
    \item \textbf{Growth and Developmental Retardation}: Stunted skeletal development in children.
    \item \textbf{Fetal Retinoid Syndrome}: Birth defects (craniofacial, cardiac, CNS) from maternal retinoid exposure during the first trimester.
    \item \textbf{Hepatic Fibrosis}: Stellate cell hypertrophy leading to cirrhosis, portal hypertension, and liver failure in chronic toxicity.
    \item \textbf{Skeletal Integrity Loss}: Premature epiphyseal closure in children and increased osteoporotic fractures in adults.
    \item \textbf{Renal Injury}: Nephrocalcinosis and acute kidney injury secondary to severe hypercalcemia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for early Vitamin A deficiency is excellent, with night blindness resolving within 24 to 48 hours and ocular lesions clearing in 1 to 2 weeks. However, once keratomalacia develops, the visual prognosis is poor, often leaving permanent blindness or severe scarring. Systemically, universal Vitamin A supplementation in deficient areas decreases overall under-five child mortality by approximately 24\%.

The prognosis for Vitamin A toxicity is favorable upon cessation of the supplement or medication. Signs of intracranial pressure and skin desquamation resolve in days to weeks. Hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis, however, may be irreversible, and congenital anomalies resulting from gestational exposure are permanent.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventative strategies focus on adequate dietary intake and avoiding excessive supplementation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary Diversity}: Regular consumption of animal-sourced foods (liver, eggs, dairy) and provitamin A carotenoid-rich plants (sweet potatoes, carrots, spinach) cooked with a source of fat.
    \item \textbf{Supplementation Campaigns}: Participation in periodic child supplementation programs in endemic areas.
    \item \textbf{Adherence to RDA}: Maintaining daily intake near recommended levels (900 $\mu$g RAE for men, 700 $\mu$g RAE for women).
    \item \textbf{Monitoring Upper Limits}: Avoiding preformed Vitamin A supplements exceeding the Tolerable Upper Intake Level (3,000 $\mu$g RAE/day).
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy Safety}: Avoiding high-dose supplements and retinoid medications during pregnancy, and utilizing beta-carotene-based prenatal vitamins.
    \item \textbf{Food Fortification}: Utilizing Vitamin A-fortified staple foods such as cooking oils and flours.
\end{itemize}
