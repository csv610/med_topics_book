\chapter{Memory concerns}
\index{Memory concerns}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Memory concerns encompass a range of subjective complaints or objective observations regarding cognitive decline, specifically involving forgetfulness, difficulty retaining new information, or impaired retrieval of past events. Memory concerns are highly prevalent in clinical practice, particularly among older adults. They represent a diagnostic spectrum that spans from normal, age-associated memory decline to Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI), and ultimately to various forms of dementia. A comprehensive clinical evaluation is essential to distinguish between benign, age-related changes, reversible medical conditions (such as thyroid disorders, nutritional deficiencies, or depression), and progressive, neurodegenerative illnesses (such as Alzheimer's disease). Early detection allows for the initiation of therapies to slow cognitive decline, support lifestyle changes, and facilitate advanced care planning.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Memory concerns are common reasons for primary care consultations, especially as life expectancy increases. In many countries, dementia is the second leading cause of death overall and the leading cause of death among women. Currently, over 400,000 people live with dementia, a figure projected to double by 2050. Approximately 1 in 10 individuals over the age of 65, and nearly 3 in 10 over the age of 85, have a diagnosis of dementia. Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) is estimated to affect 10\% to 20\% of adults aged 65 and older. While some individuals with MCI remain stable or even revert to normal cognition, approximately 10\% to 15\% of those with MCI progress to dementia each year.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The causes of memory concerns are diverse, classification being split into neurodegenerative, structural, metabolic, and psychiatric categories.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurodegenerative diseases:} Alzheimer's disease is the most common cause of progressive memory loss, characterised by the extracellular accumulation of beta-amyloid plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles of hyperphosphorylated tau protein, leading to synaptic loss and cortical atrophy. Other neurodegenerative causes include Lewy body dementia and frontotemporal lobar degeneration.
    \item \textbf{Vascular cognitive impairment:} Vascular dementia arises from chronic cerebrovascular disease, multiple cortical or lacunar infarcts, or subcortical ischaemic leukoencephalopathy, leading to a stepwise decline in cognitive function.
    \item \textbf{Reversible metabolic and endocrine conditions:} Hypothyroidism (slowing of cellular metabolism), Vitamin B12 deficiency (causing demyelination in the central and peripheral nervous systems), and normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH).
    \item \textbf{Psychiatric disorders (pseudodementia):} Major depressive disorder, chronic stress, and severe anxiety can significantly impair attention, concentration, and memory processing, presenting as cognitive impairment in older adults.
    \item \textbf{Medications and substances:} Central nervous system active drugs, particularly benzodiazepines, sedatives, tricyclic antidepressants, and medications with strong anticholinergic properties, can impair memory. Chronic alcohol abuse can lead to Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome, caused by thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Memory concerns present with varying degrees of severity and may be accompanied by other cognitive or behavioural symptoms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Forgetfulness of recent events:} Misplacing personal items, repeating questions or statements in a short period, and forgetting appointments, conversations, or recent news.
    \item \textbf{Language difficulties:} Difficulty finding the correct words (aphasia), substituting inappropriate words, or struggling to follow a conversation.
    \item \textbf{Disorientation:} Getting lost in familiar surroundings, or confusion regarding the current date, season, or time of day.
    \item \textbf{Impairment in executive function:} Difficulty planning, organising, managing finances, or executing complex, multi-step tasks (such as preparing a meal).
    \item \textbf{Changes in mood and personality:} Apathy, social withdrawal, irritability, anxiety, depression, and loss of empathy.
    \item \textbf{Apraxia and agnosia:} Inability to perform purposeful, learned movements despite intact motor function, or failure to recognise familiar objects or people.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Clinicians must systematically differentiate between the various stages of cognitive decline and rule out acute, reversible conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Age-associated memory impairment vs. Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI):} Age-associated decline is characterised by occasional forgetfulness (e.g., misplacing keys, forgetting names temporarily) that does not impact daily functioning or independence. MCI involves objective memory impairment confirmed by testing, but with preserved activities of daily living.
    \item \textbf{MCI vs. Dementia:} Dementia is diagnosed when cognitive deficits are severe enough to interfere significantly with independence in daily activities (such as cooking, driving, or managing medications).
    \item \textbf{Depression (pseudodementia) vs. Dementia:} Patients with depression-related cognitive impairment often express detailed complaints about their memory loss, may exhibit poor effort on testing (answering ``I don't know''), and experience cognitive improvement once their depression is treated.
    \item \textbf{Delirium:} An acute, fluctuating state of confusion, inattention, and altered level of consciousness, typically triggered by an underlying medical issue (such as a urinary tract infection, pneumonia, or medication toxicity) rather than a chronic neurodegenerative process.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Individuals experiencing persistent memory difficulties or their family members should consult a general practitioner for an assessment.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, acute onset of memory loss, confusion, or disorientation (suggestive of an acute stroke, transient ischaemic attack, or delirium).
    \item Memory loss accompanied by sudden weakness, numbness, difficulty speaking, or facial drooping.
    \item Acute confusion following a head injury or fall.
    \item An individual with known cognitive impairment who has wandered from home, is lost, or is in an unsafe environment.
    \item Sudden, severe agitation, aggression, or hallucinations causing immediate safety risks.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A diagnostic workup for memory concerns combines detailed history-taking, cognitive screening, laboratory tests, and neuroimaging.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical history:} A detailed interview with the patient and, crucially, a collateral history from a family member or close contact who can describe the onset, progression, and impact of cognitive changes on daily life.
    \item \textbf{Cognitive screening tests:} Validated office-based assessments, including the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), or the General Practitioner Assessment of Cognition (GPCOG).
    \item \textbf{Laboratory screening:} Blood tests to rule out reversible causes, including a full blood count (FBC), thyroid function tests (TSH), serum Vitamin B12 and folate levels, renal and liver function tests, electrolytes, and calcium. Syphilis serology may be performed in select cases.
    \item \textbf{Neuroimaging:} A CT scan or MRI of the brain is recommended to rule out structural lesions (such as brain tumours, subdural haematomas, or normal pressure hydrocephalus), identify vascular pathology (infarcts, white matter disease), and evaluate for patterns of regional atrophy (e.g., hippocampal atrophy in Alzheimer's disease).
    \item \textbf{Neuropsychological assessment:} In-depth testing by a neuropsychologist to evaluate multiple cognitive domains, helpful in distinguishing early-stage dementia, atypical presentations, or mapping cognitive strengths and weaknesses.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is tailored to the underlying cause and focuses on maximizing cognitive function, managing symptoms, and supporting carers.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological treatment for dementia:} Cholinesterase inhibitors (such as donepezil, galantamine, or rivastigmine) are indicated to treat mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's disease and Lewy body dementia by increasing acetylcholine levels. Memantine, an NMDA receptor antagonist, is used for moderate-to-severe Alzheimer's disease.
    \item \textbf{Addressing reversible factors:} Correcting hypothyroidism with levothyroxine, administering Vitamin B12 supplementation, and treating co-existing depression with psychological therapies or non-anticholinergic antidepressants.
    \item \textbf{Cognitive stimulation and lifestyle measures:} Engaging in cognitive rehabilitation, social activities, and regular physical exercise. Managing cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, and diabetes) is crucial to prevent the progression of vascular cognitive impairment.
    \item \textbf{Environmental modifications:} Implementing structured daily routines, using memory aids (calendars, whiteboards, pill organisers, and automated reminders), and ensuring a safe home environment (installing handrails, removing trip hazards, and disabling stove gas lines if necessary).
    \item \textbf{Carer support and legal planning:} Providing education and resources to carers to reduce stress and prevent burnout. Facilitating early completion of legal documents, including an Enduring Power of Attorney, Enduring Guardianship, and Advance Care Directives.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Progressive cognitive decline leads to several physical, psychological, and social complications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Loss of independence:} Gradual inability to perform basic activities of daily living (such as bathing, dressing, eating, and toileting), requiring transitioning to residential aged care.
    \item \textbf{Behavioural and Psychological Symptoms of Dementia (BPSD):} Manifestations such as agitation, physical or verbal aggression, wandering, anxiety, depression, apathy, sleep disturbances, and psychosis (delusions and hallucinations).
    \item \textbf{Safety hazards:} Accidental injury due to falls, medication errors (accidental overdose or omission), wandering and exposure to extreme weather, and vulnerability to financial abuse.
    \item \textbf{Physical decline:} In late-stage dementia, dysphagia (swallowing difficulties) can lead to aspiration pneumonia, malnutrition, pressure ulcers, and complete immobility.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for memory concerns depends on the etiology. Reversible conditions can resolve completely, with patients regaining baseline cognitive function. For Mild Cognitive Impairment, the clinical course is variable; some remain stable or improve, but a significant portion progress to dementia. Most neurodegenerative dementias are progressive, irreversible, and life-limiting diseases. The rate of decline varies, with survival after an Alzheimer's diagnosis typically ranging from three to ten years, though some patients live for much longer. Modern management focuses on maintaining function and independence for as long as possible.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Evidence-based lifestyle strategies can help protect cognitive function and reduce the risk of developing dementia.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Regular physical exercise:} Participate in moderate-intensity aerobic exercise (such as brisk walking, swimming, or cycling) for at least 150 minutes per week to promote neuroplasticity and cardiovascular health.
    \item \textbf{Adopt a brain-healthy diet:} Follow a Mediterranean-style diet high in vegetables, fruits, whole grains, fish, nuts, and olive oil, while limiting saturated fats and refined sugars.
    \item \textbf{Stay cognitively active:} Engage in mentally stimulating activities throughout life, such as reading, playing musical instruments, learning a new language, solving puzzles, or playing strategic games.
    \item \textbf{Maintain social connections:} Participate regularly in social events, volunteer work, or hobby clubs to reduce isolation, which is a known risk factor for cognitive decline.
    \item \textbf{Manage cardiovascular health:} Work with your GP to monitor and control blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood glucose, and avoid smoking and excessive alcohol consumption.
\end{itemize}
