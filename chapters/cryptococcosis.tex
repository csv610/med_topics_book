\chapter{Cryptococcosis}
\index{Cryptococcosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cryptococcosis is an infection caused by the fungus \textit{Cryptococcus}, most commonly \textit{Cryptococcus neoformans}. The fungus is found in soil, bird droppings, and some trees. Most people who breathe in the fungus do not become ill, but people with weakened immune systems -- particularly those with HIV/AIDS, organ transplants, or on immunosuppressive medicines -- can develop serious infection. Cryptococcosis most often affects the lungs and the brain (meningitis). It is a serious but treatable infection, and prompt diagnosis and antifungal therapy greatly improve outcomes.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cryptococcosis occurs worldwide and is a major opportunistic infection in people with advanced HIV, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, where it is one of the leading causes of death in people with AIDS. In many countries, cases are less common and mostly occur in people with immune suppression, although the related species \textit{Cryptococcus gattii} can cause disease in otherwise healthy people, particularly in tropical regions. Meningitis is the most serious form, and without treatment it is usually fatal. With antifungal therapy, outcomes have improved substantially.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Cryptococcosis is caused by inhalation of fungal spores from the environment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\textit{Cryptococcus neoformans}:} found worldwide in soil contaminated with bird droppings, particularly pigeon droppings
    \item \textbf{\textit{Cryptococcus gattii}:} associated with certain trees, especially eucalypts, and can affect healthy people
    \item \textbf{Inhalation:} spores are breathed in and reach the lungs
    \item \textbf{Lung infection:} the fungus can cause pneumonia or nodules in the lungs
    \item \textbf{Dissemination:} from the lungs the fungus can spread through the blood to the brain
    \item \textbf{Meningitis:} infection of the membranes around the brain and spinal cord
    \item \textbf{Immune status:} people with low CD4 counts in HIV, organ transplants, and immunosuppression are at highest risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on the site of infection.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lung infection:} cough, fever, chest pain, shortness of breath, and weight loss
    \item \textbf{Meningitis:} headache, fever, neck stiffness, nausea and vomiting, sensitivity to light, confusion, and drowsiness
    \item \textbf{Skin:} sores, ulcers, or nodules on the skin in disseminated disease
    \item \textbf{Other organs:} bone, joint, prostate, and eye involvement in disseminated disease
    \item \textbf{Slow onset:} symptoms often develop gradually over weeks
    \item \textbf{In healthy people:} a self-limiting lung infection or no symptoms at all
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms overlap with many other infections and conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Tuberculosis, with similar lung and meningeal symptoms
    \item Other fungal infections, such as histoplasmosis or aspergillosis
    \item Bacterial meningitis or brain abscess
    \item Viral meningitis or encephalitis
    \item Lung cancer or other causes of lung nodules
    \item HIV-related conditions, such as toxoplasmosis or lymphoma
    \item Non-infectious causes of headache and confusion
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with HIV, transplants, or immune suppression who develop cough, fever, headache, or confusion should seek medical review promptly, as cryptococcosis is treatable but serious.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A severe headache with fever, neck stiffness, or sensitivity to light
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or a seizure
    \item Difficulty breathing or coughing up blood
    \item Sudden neurological symptoms such as weakness or difficulty speaking
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis requires laboratory confirmation, and urgent assessment is needed when meningitis is suspected.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} immune status, exposure history, and symptoms
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray or CT:} for lung infection
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture:} analysis of cerebrospinal fluid for meningitis, including India ink stain, antigen testing, and culture
    \item \textbf{Cryptococcal antigen test:} a blood test that detects the fungus rapidly
    \item \textbf{Blood cultures:} for disseminated infection
    \item \textbf{Brain imaging:} CT or MRI to assess for complications
    \item \textbf{Immune testing:} HIV testing and CD4 count in appropriate people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is intensive and prolonged, especially for meningitis.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Induction therapy:} amphotericin B with flucytosine, given intravenously in hospital for meningitis
    \item \textbf{Consolidation and maintenance:} fluconazole for many months to clear the infection and prevent relapse
    \item \textbf{Antifungal therapy for lung disease:} fluconazole for milder lung infections
    \item \textbf{Management of raised pressure:} repeated lumbar punctures or drainage for raised brain pressure in meningitis
    \item \textbf{Antiretroviral therapy:} for people with HIV, started carefully to avoid immune reconstitution reactions
    \item \textbf{Reduction of immunosuppression:} where possible, in transplant and other immunosuppressed patients
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular blood tests and cerebrospinal fluid checks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Raised pressure inside the skull, which can cause vision loss
    \item Hydrocephalus (fluid buildup in the brain)
    \item Permanent neurological damage
    \item Vision or hearing loss
    \item Relapse after treatment
    \item Immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) in HIV patients starting antiretrovirals
    \item Complications of antifungal drugs, including kidney damage from amphotericin B
    \item Death, especially in untreated meningitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the site and severity of infection, the underlying immune status, and how quickly treatment starts. With modern induction and maintenance therapy, the majority of people with cryptococcal meningitis survive, though the treatment is demanding and requires months of follow-up. Lung infection in people with mild immune suppression usually responds well to oral antifungals. Outcomes are best when the diagnosis is made early, which is why people at risk should seek review for persistent symptoms.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item People with advanced HIV should take preventive antifungals as recommended and maintain antiretroviral therapy
    \item Avoid exposure to pigeon droppings and disturbed soil where possible, especially if immunocompromised
    \item Wear protective masks when cleaning areas with heavy bird droppings
    \item Seek early medical review for persistent cough, fever, or headache if immunocompromised
    \item Take the full course of antifungal treatment and attend all follow-up
    \item Follow advice about reducing immunosuppression where possible
\end{itemize}
