\chapter{Medicines for babies and children}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Medicines for babies and children refers to the specialised field of paediatric pharmacotherapy, encompassing the safe prescribing, dispensing, and administration of medications to patients from birth through to 18 years of age. Paediatric patients are not simply "miniature adults"; they undergo rapid, continuous physiological growth and developmental changes that profoundly affect how drugs are absorbed, distributed, metabolised, and excreted. Consequently, drug dosing in children must be carefully calculated based on weight, body surface area, and developmental stage rather than utilising standardised adult doses. Safe paediatric medication management requires strict adherence to safety protocols, the use of appropriate delivery devices, and clear caregiver education to prevent dosing errors, which represent the most common type of preventable medical error in paediatric healthcare.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Children are major consumers of both prescription and over-the-counter (OTC) medications. In Australia, epidemiological surveys indicate that approximately 40\% to 50\% of children receive at least one medication in any given month, with analgesics/antipyretics (paracetamol and ibuprofen), antibiotics, and asthma medications being the most frequently administered. Paediatric medication errors occur at a rate up to three times higher than in adult medicine, with the majority occurring in the home environment due to caregiver dosing mistakes, confusion between different concentrations of liquid formulations, or the use of inaccurate measuring tools like household spoons. Accidental ingestion of unsecured medications remains a leading cause of paediatric poisoning admissions in Australian emergency departments.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The clinical challenges and risks associated with paediatric medicines are rooted in age-related pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic differences.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Altered drug absorption:} Neonates and young infants have higher gastric pH (less acidic), delayed gastric emptying, and immature intestinal enzymes, which alter the bioavailability of oral drugs. Additionally, their thin stratum corneum and high surface-area-to-weight ratio lead to significantly increased absorption of topical medications.
    \item \textbf{Variable drug distribution:} The total body water content of a neonate is approximately 80\% of body weight, compared to 60\% in adults. This requires higher weight-based doses of hydrophilic (water-soluble) drugs. Conversely, lower plasma protein binding in infants increases the free, active fraction of protein-bound drugs, raising the risk of toxicity.
    \item \textbf{Immature hepatic metabolism:} Hepatic enzyme systems, particularly the cytochrome P450 pathway, are immature at birth. This delays the clearance of medications like paracetamol or anti-epileptics in neonates, requiring longer dosing intervals. In contrast, older children (toddlers and young children) often metabolise certain drugs faster than adults, requiring higher or more frequent doses.
    \item \textbf{Developing renal clearance:} Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and tubular secretion are significantly reduced at birth (especially in preterm infants) and do not reach adult levels until approximately 6 to 12 months of age, necessitating cautious dosing of renally excreted drugs to prevent accumulation.
    \item \textbf{Cognitive and behavioural barriers:} Infants and young children cannot swallow tablets, frequently spit out bitter liquid medicines, or actively refuse administration, leading to incomplete dosing.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features of paediatric medication management include monitoring for therapeutic responses and identifying early signs of drug-induced toxicity.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Therapeutic indications:} Relief of fever and pain, resolution of bacterial infections, control of airway inflammation (asthma), and management of allergic reactions.
    \item \textbf{Accidental overdose signs:} Drowsiness, respiratory depression (e.g. from accidental ingestion of opioids or sedating antihistamines), hypothermia, or persistent vomiting.
    \item \textbf{Paracetamol toxicity features:} Often asymptomatic in the first 24 hours, followed by nausea, vomiting, right upper quadrant abdominal pain, and progressive jaundice indicating hepatotoxicity.
    \item \textbf{NSAID toxicity features:} Epigastric pain, vomiting, lethargy, and in severe cases, oliguria (reduced urine output) indicating acute kidney injury.
    \item \textbf{Allergic reactions:} Sudden onset of hives (urticaria), facial swelling, wheezing, or generalized skin rashes (drug exanthems) following drug administration.
    \item \textbf{Paradoxical reactions:} Increased agitation, hyperactivity, and insomnia in response to medications that typically cause sedation (e.g. certain antihistamines).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When a child presents with symptoms after taking a medicine, clinicians must determine whether the presentation is a drug reaction, a dosing error, or a progression of the underlying illness.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Drug-induced hepatotoxicity vs.\ viral hepatitis:} Differentiating elevated liver enzymes caused by paracetamol overdose from primary viral infections (e.g. adenovirus, Epstein-Barr virus).
    \item \textbf{Drug eruption vs.\ viral exanthem:} Distinguishing an allergic drug rash from common childhood viral rashes (e.g. roseola infantum, measles, or hand, foot, and mouth disease).
    \item \textbf{Drug-induced drowsiness vs.\ meningitis or sepsis:} Differentiating lethargy caused by accidental ingestion of sedatives or antihistamines from life-threatening central nervous system or systemic infections.
    \item \textbf{Reye's syndrome vs.\ acute viral encephalopathy:} Differentiating Reye's syndrome (a rare, catastrophic hepatic encephalopathy linked to aspirin use in children) from primary encephalitis or inborn errors of metabolism.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Caregivers should consult a pharmacist, general practitioner, or call the Poisons Information Centre if they suspect a dosing error, if the child spits up a dose, or if the child develops a new rash or vomiting after taking a medicine.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Suspected ingestion of a highly toxic medication (e.g. adult heart medications, oral hypoglycaemics, or iron supplements) or any large drug overdose.
    \item Difficulty breathing, throat tightness, blue lips, or a high-pitched wheeze (suggesting anaphylaxis).
    \item Extreme lethargy, difficulty waking the child, or complete loss of consciousness.
    \item Seizures, convulsions, or severe muscle twitching.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Investigating paediatric medication issues involves precise clinical measurements and, when necessary, laboratory analysis.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Accurate weight measurement:} Weighing the child naked or in a clean nappy using a calibrated electronic scale; estimation of weight should be avoided as it leads to dosing errors.
    \item \textbf{Serum drug level monitoring:} Measuring blood concentrations of drugs with narrow therapeutic indices (e.g. gentamicin, vancomycin, digoxin, or phenytoin) to guide dosage adjustments.
    \item \textbf{Toxicological serum assays:} In cases of suspected overdose, measuring serum paracetamol levels (plotted on a Rumack-Matthew nomogram) or salicylate levels to determine the need for antidotes.
    \item \textbf{Renal and hepatic biomarker panel:} Testing serum creatinine, urea, electrolytes, and liver transaminases (ALT, AST) to monitor for drug-induced organ injury.
    \item \textbf{Caregiver interview and bottle audit:} Reviewing the medication bottle, concentration details, and estimating the remaining volume to calculate the maximum potential dose ingested.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Paediatric medication management involves strict adherence to safety guidelines, precise calculations, and supportive care.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Weight-based dose calculation:} Calculating doses using the child's exact weight (e.g. paracetamol at 15 mg/kg per dose; ibuprofen at 10 mg/kg per dose) and cross-checking against pediatric dosing manuals (e.g. AMH Children's Dosing Companion).
    \item \textbf{Use of oral dosing syringes:} Administering liquid medicines using a marked oral syringe rather than kitchen spoons or dosing cups to ensure accurate volume delivery.
    \item \textbf{Double-checking protocols:} In clinical settings, performing independent double-checks of calculations and drawn-up volumes for high-risk medications (e.g. insulin, heparin, intravenous potassium).
    \item \textbf{Antidote administration:} Administering N-acetylcysteine (NAC) intravenously for confirmed paracetamol poisoning to replenish hepatic glutathione stores.
    \item \textbf{Activated charcoal:} Administered in a hospital setting within 1 to 2 hours of a toxic ingestion if the patient has a secure airway and the drug is adsorbable.
    \item \textbf{Caregiver counselling:} Providing written instructions detailing the medicine name, purpose, dose in millilitres (not milligrams), frequency, and maximum daily limit.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Incorrect administration or toxic exposure to medicines in childhood can result in severe organ injury or death.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute liver failure:} The primary complication of severe paracetamol toxicity, potentially requiring urgent liver transplantation.
    \item \textbf{Acute kidney injury:} Caused by NSAID-induced renal vasoconstriction, particularly in children who are dehydrated due to gastroenteritis.
    \item \textbf{Respiratory arrest:} Severe hypoventilation and hypoxia resulting from overdose of opioids, sedatives, or cough and cold preparations.
    \item \textbf{Reye's syndrome:} A rare but often fatal complication characterised by acute encephalopathy and fatty liver infiltration, secondary to aspirin administration during a viral illness.
    \item \textbf{Choking and aspiration:} Occurring when liquid medications are forced down a child's throat too quickly, causing the fluid to enter the lungs.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for children receiving appropriate paediatric medications is excellent, with high rates of recovery from common acute illnesses. In cases of accidental poisoning or overdose, early recognition and prompt medical care carry an extremely favourable prognosis. The implementation of standardised hospital guidelines, independent double-checking, and community campaigns regarding safe storage have significantly reduced the incidence and severity of paediatric medication errors over the past decade.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Caregivers can prevent medication errors and poisoning through structured safety habits.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Locked and high storage:} Storing all medications—including vitamins, creams, and household cleaners—in a locked cupboard located at least 1.5 metres above the ground.
    \item \textbf{Avoid "double-dosing":} Checking active ingredients on all packaging to ensure the child is not receiving the same drug from two different products (e.g. cold medicine and pain liquid both containing paracetamol).
    \item \textbf{Keep a written log:} Writing down the date, time, dose, and name of the medicine given to prevent duplicate doses by different family members.
    \item \textbf{Avoid OTC cough and cold medicines:} Refraining from giving OTC cough and cold preparations to children under 6 years of age, as they have no proven efficacy and carry significant risk of harm.
    \item \textbf{Ask the pharmacist:} Verifying the correct dose with a pharmacist whenever purchasing a new over-the-counter medicine or picking up a prescription.
\end{itemize}
