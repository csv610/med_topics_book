\chapter{Injuries}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An injury is physical harm to the body caused by an external force, such as a fall, a motor vehicle crash, a burn, a cut, or a blow. Injuries range from minor cuts and bruises that need no treatment to life-threatening trauma requiring urgent hospital care. They are a leading cause of death and disability in Australia, particularly among young people, and they place a large burden on individuals, families, and the health system. This chapter provides an overview of the common types of injury, first aid, when to seek emergency care, and the many ways in which injuries can be prevented.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Injuries are the leading cause of death in Australians aged 1 to 44 and account for around 10 per cent of all deaths. Falls are the most common cause of injury-related hospitalisation, especially in older people, followed by transport crashes and intentional self-harm. Men are injured more often than women in almost every category, and rates are higher in rural and remote areas and among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. Around one in ten hospitalisations in Australia is due to injury, and injuries are a major cause of long-term disability, with traumatic brain injury and spinal cord injury among the most devastating.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Injuries result from the transfer of energy to the body in an amount or at a rate that exceeds tissue tolerance. The energy may be mechanical, as in falls, crashes, and assaults; thermal, in burns and scalds; electrical or chemical; or from radiation. The resulting damage depends on the force, the body part struck, and factors such as age, bone strength, and protective equipment. In children, injuries reflect development and supervision; in older people, they follow falls linked to frailty, medicines, and home hazards. Understanding the chain of events, from the agent of injury to the environment and the person, is the basis for prevention through road safety, workplace safety, home modification, and public policy.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Soft tissue injuries: cuts, bruises, sprains, and strains
    \item Fractures and dislocations, with pain, swelling, deformity, and inability to use the limb
    \item Head injuries: from mild concussion to serious brain injury
    \item Burns and scalds, with redness, blistering, or charring
    \item Wounds with bleeding, which may be severe
    \item Pain, swelling, and loss of function in the affected area
    \item In serious trauma: unconsciousness, difficulty breathing, shock, and multiple injuries
    \item Psychological effects: distress, fear, and post-traumatic stress
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The nature and severity of an injury are assessed to guide treatment.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Soft tissue injury versus fracture:} often distinguished by examination and X-ray
    \item \textbf{Concussion versus more serious head injury:} assessed with neurological observation
    \item \textbf{Sprain versus ligament rupture:} severity of joint instability
    \item \textbf{Superficial versus deep burns:} depth and extent determine treatment
    \item \textbf{Bleeding versus bruising:} the site and severity of vascular injury
    \item \textbf{Injury versus medical cause:} collapse or confusion may be due to a medical event rather than trauma
    \item \textbf{Suspected abuse or non-accidental injury:} considered in children with inconsistent histories
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Many minor injuries can be managed at home with first aid, but assessment is needed for injuries that are severe, involve the head, eyes, joints, or bones, or do not settle. Any injury with significant bleeding, deformity, loss of function, or concern about deeper damage should be reviewed. Older people who fall, and anyone with bleeding while taking blood-thinning medicines, should be assessed.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unconsciousness, confusion, or difficulty breathing after injury
    \item Severe bleeding that does not stop with pressure
    \item Suspected spinal injury, especially after a fall or crash
    \item A severe head injury, or a head injury in someone taking blood thinners
    \item A large burn, or a burn to the face, hands, or genitals
    \item Signs of shock: pale, clammy skin, rapid pulse, or faintness
    \item Any injury to a child or baby that is severe or unexplained
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First aid assessment:} checking the airway, breathing, and circulation, and controlling bleeding
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the mechanism of injury and a systematic assessment
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} to detect fractures and dislocations
    \item \textbf{CT scanning:} for head, chest, abdominal, and spinal injuries in significant trauma
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} in the emergency assessment of internal bleeding
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} including blood group, clotting, and assessment of blood loss
    \item \textbf{Specialist assessment:} by surgeons, orthopaedic, burns, and rehabilitation teams as needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First aid:} rest, ice, compression, and elevation for soft tissue injuries; direct pressure for bleeding
    \item \textbf{Wound care:} cleaning, closure, dressings, and tetanus immunisation as needed
    \item \textbf{Fracture care:} splinting, plaster casts, or surgery to align and fix broken bones
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple analgesics to strong medicines according to severity
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} surgery, intensive care, and blood transfusion for serious trauma
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and graded return to activity
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} counselling for distress and post-traumatic reactions
    \item \textbf{Secondary prevention:} assessment of risks to prevent recurrence, especially falls in older people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection of wounds and surgical sites
    \item Bleeding, both external and internal
    \item Delayed or non-union of fractures
    \item Compartment syndrome in limb injuries
    \item Chronic pain and stiffness
    \item Traumatic brain injury and spinal cord injury with lifelong disability
    \item Post-traumatic stress disorder and other psychological effects
    \item Long-term effects on work, sport, and quality of life
    \item Death from severe trauma
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outcome depends on the type and severity of the injury. Most minor injuries heal completely within days to weeks. Fractures usually heal well with proper treatment, though some leave stiffness or require surgery. Severe trauma carries significant mortality and disability, but modern emergency care and rehabilitation achieve good recoveries in many cases. Early first aid, prompt emergency care, and structured rehabilitation all improve outcomes. Many serious injuries are entirely preventable with safety measures.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wear seatbelts and helmets, and obey road rules
    \item Child-proof the home and supervise children near roads, water, and heights
    \item Prevent falls in older people: remove hazards, improve lighting, and review medicines
    \item Use safe handling and protective equipment at work
    \item Store medicines, chemicals, and tools safely
    \item Learn first aid and CPR
    \item Keep up to date with tetanus immunisation
    \item Exercise to maintain strength, balance, and bone health
    \item Drink responsibly and never drive after alcohol or drugs
\end{itemize}
