\chapter{Wheezing}
\index{Wheezing}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Wheezing is a high-pitched, whistling, or musical sound produced during breathing, most commonly heard during expiration, though it can also occur during inspiration. This distinctive sound is generated by the rapid, turbulent passage of air through narrowed, compressed, or partially obstructed intrathoracic airways. It is not a disease in itself, but rather a clinical sign---an objective manifestation of an underlying pathological process affecting the respiratory system. From a physiological standpoint, wheezing occurs when the airflow velocity increases through a narrowed lumen, causing the airway walls to oscillate or vibrate. This phenomenon is closely related to the physical principles of fluid dynamics, specifically the Bernoulli principle, where a restriction in flow area results in an increase in fluid velocity and a localized drop in pressure, causing airway collapse and subsequent flutter.

Understanding wheezing is of paramount clinical significance because it serves as a critical indicator of airway dysfunction. It is one of the most common respiratory symptoms encountered in both primary care and emergency settings, spanning all age groups from neonates to the elderly. In children, it is frequently associated with viral infections or transient developmental factors, whereas in adults, it often points to chronic obstructive lung diseases, cardiovascular anomalies, or occupational exposures. Because the underlying causes of wheezing range from benign, self-limiting conditions to life-threatening medical emergencies, a comprehensive understanding of its pathophysiology, clinical presentation, and diagnostic pathways is essential for healthcare providers. For consumers and patients, recognizing the nature of wheezing, identifying potential triggers, and knowing when to seek urgent medical attention are vital components of effective health self-management and can significantly reduce the morbidity and mortality associated with acute respiratory distress.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of wheezing is highly variable and depends heavily on age, geographical location, environmental exposures, and underlying health conditions. It represents a major public health concern globally, accounting for millions of healthcare visits, emergency department admissions, and significant economic burdens associated with lost productivity and treatment costs.

Among infants and young children, wheezing is remarkably prevalent. Epidemiological cohorts indicate that approximately 30\% to 50\% of all children experience at least one episode of wheezing before their third birthday. This high incidence in early childhood is primarily attributed to the smaller caliber of infant airways, increased airway compliance, and a high susceptibility to viral respiratory tract infections, particularly Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) and rhinovirus. While many of these children are ``transient wheezers'' who outgrow the symptom as their lungs develop, a subset goes on to develop persistent asthma. The Asthma Predictive Index (API) is often utilized to estimate the probability of persistent asthma in these young populations, taking into account factors such as parental history of asthma, personal history of atopic dermatitis (eczema), and sensitization to aeroallergens.

In the adult population, the prevalence of wheezing remains substantial, though its etiology shifts. It is estimated that between 8\% and 15\% of adults experience wheezing globally. The demographic distribution in adults is heavily influenced by smoking status, occupational exposures, and socioeconomic status. Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD), which is characterized by persistent respiratory symptoms and airflow limitation, is a major contributor to wheezing in older adults, particularly those with a history of tobacco smoking. Occupational wheezing, triggered by exposure to sensitizers or irritants in the workplace (such as chemical fumes, wood dust, or animal proteins), affects an estimated 10\% to 15\% of adult-onset wheezing cases. Furthermore, gender disparities exist: in childhood, wheezing is more common in boys, whereas in adulthood, women are more frequently diagnosed with asthma and report higher rates of wheezing than men.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The production of a wheeze requires a critical velocity of airflow through a narrowed airway lumen, which induces oscillation of the airway walls. The reduction in airway diameter can occur through several distinct pathological mechanisms: bronchoconstriction (smooth muscle contraction), mucosal oedema (swelling of the airway lining), excessive mucus secretion, or structural changes (such as airway remodeling or external compression).

The primary causes and pathophysiological pathways of wheezing include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asthma:} This is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the airways characterized by bronchial hyperresponsiveness, reversible airflow obstruction, and airway remodeling. Exposure to allergens or irritants triggers an IgE-mediated type I hypersensitivity reaction, leading to mast cell degranulation and the release of inflammatory mediators like histamines, leukotrienes, and prostaglandins. This results in acute bronchoconstriction, mucosal inflammation, and thick mucus production, causing widespread airway narrowing and characteristic expiratory wheezing.
    \item \textbf{Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD):} Comprising chronic bronchitis and emphysema, COPD is characterized by persistent, progressive airflow limitation. Chronic inhalation of noxious particles, most commonly tobacco smoke, induces a chronic inflammatory response in the lungs. In chronic bronchitis, this leads to goblet cell hyperplasia and excessive mucus hypersecretion in the large airways. In emphysema, inflammatory enzymes destroy the alveolar walls and elastic fibres, leading to a loss of elastic recoil. Consequently, the airways lose their structural support and collapse during expiration, causing chronic, often low-pitched wheezing (rhonchi).
    \item \textbf{Bronchiolitis:} Primarily affecting infants and children under two years of age, bronchiolitis is an acute viral infection of the lower respiratory tract, most commonly caused by Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV). The virus infects the bronchiolar epithelial cells, leading to inflammatory cell infiltration, submucosal oedema, and cellular necrosis. The debris from dead epithelial cells, combined with mucus and fibrin, forms plugs that occlude the tiny bronchioles, resulting in air trapping, atelectasis, and diffuse expiratory wheezing.
    \item \textbf{Anaphylaxis:} This is a severe, life-threatening, systemic hypersensitivity reaction. Upon re-exposure to an allergen (such as food, medication, or insect venom), widespread IgE-mediated activation of mast cells and basophils occurs. This triggers a massive systemic release of vasoactive mediators, causing profound vasodilation, laryngeal oedema, and severe bronchospasm. The resulting acute airway narrowing manifests as inspiratory stridor or expiratory wheezing, accompanied by cardiovascular collapse and urticaria.
    \item \textbf{Foreign Body Aspiration:} Accidental inhalation of a foreign object (e.g., small toys, nuts) into the tracheobronchial tree is a common cause of sudden-onset wheezing, particularly in toddlers. The foreign body physically blocks air passage, typically in the right main bronchus due to its more vertical orientation. This leads to localized, unilateral wheezing, air trapping distal to the obstruction, and localized atelectasis.
    \item \textbf{Congestive Heart Failure (Cardiac Asthma):} In patients with left-sided heart failure, the failing left ventricle cannot pump blood efficiently, leading to an increase in pulmonary venous pressure. This increased hydrostatic pressure forces fluid out of the pulmonary capillaries into the interstitial spaces and alveolar walls, resulting in pulmonary congestion. The accumulation of fluid in the peribronchial spaces (bronchial wall oedema) narrows the airways, producing a wheeze that can mimic bronchial asthma, a clinical phenomenon termed ``cardiac asthma.''
    \item \textbf{Vocal Cord Dysfunction (VCD):} Also known as inducible laryngeal obstruction (ILO), VCD is characterized by the abnormal adduction (closing) of the vocal cords during inspiration or expiration. This functional airway obstruction generates a high-pitched sound that is often mistaken for asthmatic wheezing, although it is typically heard loudest over the larynx (throat) rather than the chest.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of wheezing varies significantly depending on the underlying pathology, the severity of the airway obstruction, and the patient's general health status. Because wheezing is a symptom rather than a primary diagnosis, it is almost always accompanied by a constellation of other signs and symptoms. A detailed clinical assessment must characterize the onset, duration, triggers, and associated features of the wheezing.

Common signs and symptoms associated with wheezing include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Expiratory Wheeze:} The classic manifestation is a high-pitched, musical sound heard predominantly when the patient breathes out. In mild cases, it may only be audible with a stethoscope (subclinical wheezing). As airway obstruction worsens, the wheeze becomes audible to the naked ear (clinical wheezing).
    \item \textbf{Inspiratory Wheeze:} In cases of severe or proximal airway obstruction, the whistling sound can also be heard during inhalation. Monophonic wheezes (single-pitch sounds) typically indicate obstruction of a single large airway, whereas polyphonic wheezes (multiple pitches playing simultaneously) suggest widespread, diffuse narrowing of smaller airways, as seen in acute asthma exacerbations.
    \item \textbf{Dyspnoea (Shortness of Breath):} Patients often report a subjective feeling of air hunger, breathlessness, or difficulty catching their breath. The severity of dyspnoea usually correlates with the degree of airflow limitation.
    \item \textbf{Cough:} A persistent cough is a frequent accompaniment. A dry, hacking cough may suggest cough-variant asthma or early viral infection, whereas a productive cough with thick, discoloured sputum is characteristic of chronic bronchitis, bronchiectasis, or pneumonia.
    \item \textbf{Chest Tightness:} Many patients describe a sensation of pressure, constriction, or heaviness across their chest, which is caused by the increased work of breathing and air trapping in the lungs.
    \item \textbf{Use of Accessory Muscles:} During moderate to severe airway obstruction, the body recruits accessory muscles of respiration (such as the sternocleidomastoid, scalene, and intercostal muscles) to assist in ventilation. Visual signs include intercostal, subcostal, or suprasternal retractions (tugging of the skin between and below the ribs or at the base of the neck).
    \item \textbf{Tachypnoea and Tachycardia:} An increased breathing rate (tachypnoea) and elevated heart rate (tachycardia) are physiological responses to hypoxia, hypercapnia, and the physical exertion required to maintain ventilation.
    \item \textbf{Altered Mental Status:} In extreme cases of respiratory failure, accumulating carbon dioxide (hypercapnia) and falling oxygen levels (hypoxia) can cause agitation, confusion, lethargy, or somnolence.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because wheezing can be produced by any condition that causes airway narrowing, the differential diagnosis is extensive. It is clinically critical to differentiate between these conditions, as their treatments differ fundamentally. Misdiagnosing a cardiac or mechanical airway obstruction as bronchial asthma can lead to inappropriate treatment and clinical deterioration.

Key alternative diagnoses to consider include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bronchial Asthma:} Characterized by episodic, reversible wheezing, cough, and dyspnoea, often triggered by allergens, exercise, cold air, or viral infections. It is confirmed by showing reversible airway obstruction on spirometry.
    \item \textbf{Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD):} Typically presents in older adults with a significant smoking history. The wheezing is progressive, poorly reversible, and accompanied by chronic cough and sputum production.
    \item \textbf{Acute Bronchitis:} A self-limiting viral infection of the large airways in individuals without chronic lung disease. It presents with a prominent cough (which may last several weeks) and transient wheezing due to mucosal inflammation.
    \item \textbf{Bronchiolitis:} The leading cause of wheezing in infants under 12 months. It is distinguished by its acute onset, preceding upper respiratory symptoms (coryza, fever), and diffuse fine crackles and wheezes.
    \item \textbf{Foreign Body Aspiration:} Should be suspected in any patient, especially young children or adults with neurological impairment, who presents with sudden-onset, unexplained wheezing and coughing. The wheeze is typically localized (unilateral) and does not respond to bronchodilators.
    \item \textbf{Congestive Heart Failure (``Cardiac Asthma''):} Characterized by wheezing due to pulmonary congestion. Differentiating features include orthopnoea (shortness of breath when lying flat), paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, peripheral oedema, jugular venous distension, and bilateral basilar crackles.
    \item \textbf{Anaphylaxis:} A medical emergency presenting with sudden-onset wheezing, stridor, urticarial rash, angioedema (swelling of lips/tongue), and hypotension following exposure to a known allergen.
    \item \textbf{Tracheobronchomalacia:} A structural weakness of the trachea and bronchi where the airway walls collapse during expiration, causing a chronic, barky cough and expiratory wheezing that does not respond to medical therapy.
    \item \textbf{Pulmonary Embolism:} Occasionally, a blood clot in the lungs can trigger reflex bronchoconstriction and present with wheezing, though it is more typically characterized by sudden-onset pleuritic chest pain, dyspnoea, tachycardia, and hypoxaemia.
    \item \textbf{Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD):} Acid reflux can cause microaspiration of gastric contents into the airways, leading to chemical irritation, bronchospasm, and chronic wheezing or coughing, especially at night.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Wheezing should never be ignored, as it indicates some degree of airway compromise. While chronic, mild wheezing in a patient with a known diagnosis of asthma or COPD may be managed at home according to an established action plan, new-onset wheezing or worsening symptoms require prompt medical evaluation.

Immediate emergency medical services must be contacted---such as calling \textbf{local emergency services} in many countries, \textbf{911} in the United States, or \textbf{999} in the United Kingdom---if wheezing is accompanied by any of the following life-threatening signs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, intolerable shortness of breath or gasping for air.
    \item Cyanosis, which is a bluish or grey discolouration of the lips, tongue, face, or nail beds, indicating severe oxygen deprivation.
    \item Inability to speak in full sentences, or needing to pause for breath after every few words.
    \item Confusion, extreme drowsiness, agitation, or loss of consciousness.
    \item Significant use of accessory muscles to breathe, marked by deep chest and neck retractions.
    \item Stridor, a harsh, high-pitched squeaking sound heard on inspiration, which indicates a critical upper airway obstruction (such as from epiglottitis or foreign body inhalation).
    \item A sudden ``silent chest,'' where wheezing stops abruptly but the patient is still struggling to breathe. This is an ominous sign indicating that airflow has become too low to even generate a wheeze, representing imminent respiratory arrest.
\end{itemize}

A physician should be consulted within 24 to 48 hours if the wheezing is new, is accompanied by a high fever or thick greenish-yellow sputum, does not respond to prescribed rescue inhalers, or is associated with chest discomfort, leg swelling, or significant weight gain.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Confirming the presence of wheezing and determining its underlying cause requires a structured diagnostic approach incorporating a detailed clinical history, physical examination, and targeted investigations.

Key diagnostic steps and investigations include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical History and Physical Examination:} The clinician assesses the onset, timing (expiratory vs. inspiratory), triggers (such as exercise, cold air, allergens), and duration of the wheeze. Auscultation of the chest with a stethoscope is performed to localize the wheezing, assess air entry, and check for other adventitious sounds like crackles (suggesting fluid or infection) or stridor.
    \item \textbf{Spirometry and Pulmonary Function Tests (PFTs):} Spirometry is the gold standard for diagnosing obstructive lung diseases like asthma and COPD. It measures the volume of air an individual can exhale forcefully. A key metric is the Forced Expiratory Volume in 1 second (FEV1) and the Forced Vital Capacity (FVC). An FEV1/FVC ratio of less than 70\% indicates airflow obstruction. To diagnose asthma, a bronchodilator reversibility test is performed; an increase in FEV1 of more than 12\% and 200 mL after inhaling a short-acting beta-agonist (e.g., salbutamol) confirms reversible airway obstruction.
    \item \textbf{Peak Expiratory Flow (PEF) Monitoring:} A simple, handheld device used to measure the maximum speed of expiration. It is highly useful for serial monitoring in asthma patients to assess lung function variability and response to treatment.
    \item \textbf{Pulse Oximetry and Arterial Blood Gas (ABG):} Pulse oximetry is a non-invasive tool to measure arterial oxygen saturation (SpO2). If the SpO2 is low (less than 92\% on room air) or if the patient is in severe distress, an ABG may be drawn to precisely measure blood pH, partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2), and partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2) to rule out respiratory acidosis and failure.
    \item \textbf{Chest Radiography (CXR):} A chest X-ray is performed to identify structural abnormalities, signs of hyperinflation (common in COPD and asthma), pulmonary congestion (suggesting heart failure), focal consolidations (indicating pneumonia), or the presence of an inhaled foreign body (though many foreign objects are radiolucent and may not appear directly).
    \item \textbf{Laboratory Investigations:} Full blood count (FBC) may reveal eosinophilia (suggesting allergic asthma) or leukocytosis (indicating infection). Serum IgE and allergen-specific testing (skin prick tests or RAST) can help identify specific allergic triggers. In cases of suspected heart failure, Brain Natriuretic Peptide (BNP) or NT-proBNP levels are measured.
    \item \textbf{High-Resolution Computed Tomography (HRCT):} Indicated for chronic, unexplained wheezing to evaluate for interstitial lung disease, bronchiectasis, pulmonary embolism (via CT pulmonary angiogram), or central airway tumors.
    \item \textbf{Bronchoscopy:} Involving the insertion of a flexible camera into the airways, bronchoscopy is indicated for direct visualization of the tracheobronchial tree when foreign body aspiration, airway stenosis, or an endobronchial tumor is suspected.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of wheezing is directed primarily at treating the underlying cause, alleviating acute airway obstruction, and preventing future exacerbations. A combination of pharmacological treatments, lifestyle modifications, and, in some cases, acute respiratory support is employed.

Treatment options include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Short-Acting Beta2-Agonists (SABAs):} Often referred to as ``rescue'' or ``reliever'' inhalers, medications such as salbutamol (albuterol) and terbutaline are the first-line treatment for acute bronchospasm. They act rapidly by stimulating beta2-adrenergic receptors on airway smooth muscle cells, causing them to relax and widen the airways. They provide quick relief but do not address the underlying inflammation.
    \item \textbf{Inhaled Corticosteroids (ICS):} These are the cornerstone of long-term controller therapy for asthma. Medications like fluticasone, budesonide, and beclomethasone suppress airway inflammation, reduce bronchial hyperresponsiveness, and prevent asthma attacks. They must be used consistently, even when the patient is symptom-free.
    \item \textbf{Long-Acting Bronchodilators (LABAs and LAMAs):} Long-Acting Beta2-Agonists (e.g., salmeterol, formoterol) and Long-Acting Muscarinic Antagonists (e.g., tiotropium, umeclidinium) are used for maintenance therapy in COPD and moderate-to-severe asthma. They provide prolonged bronchodilation (typically lasting 12 to 24 hours).
    \item \textbf{Systemic Corticosteroids:} For moderate-to-severe acute exacerbations of asthma or COPD, a short course of oral (e.g., prednisolone) or intravenous (e.g., methylprednisolone) corticosteroids is prescribed to rapidly reduce severe airway inflammation.
    \item \textbf{Oxygen Therapy and Respiratory Support:} Supplementary oxygen is administered to maintain arterial oxygen saturation within target ranges (92\%--96\% for most patients, or 88\%--92\% for those at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure, such as COPD patients). In severe respiratory distress, non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV), such as BiPAP, or endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation may be required.
    \item \textbf{Antimicrobial Therapy:} Antibiotics (e.g., amoxicillin, doxycycline) or antiviral medications are indicated only when a bacterial infection (like pneumonia or bacterial exacerbation of COPD) or a specific viral pathogen (like influenza) is diagnosed. They are not indicated for uncomplicated viral bronchitis or asthma flare-ups.
    \item \textbf{Adrenaline (Epinephrine):} Administered intramuscularly immediately for wheezing caused by anaphylaxis to counteract systemic vasodilation and severe bronchospasm.
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle and Environmental Modifications:} Smoking cessation is the single most effective intervention to slow the progression of COPD and reduce wheezing. Avoiding known allergens (dust mites, pollen, pet dander), maintaining optimal indoor humidity, and avoiding exposure to occupational irritants are essential.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated or poorly managed wheezing can lead to significant physical and psychological complications. The severity of these complications depends on the underlying etiology and the frequency of acute exacerbations.

Complications of persistent airway obstruction and wheezing include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Respiratory Failure:} Severe, prolonged bronchospasm can lead to an inability to maintain adequate gas exchange. Type 1 respiratory failure (hypoxaemia) or Type 2 respiratory failure (hypercapnia and respiratory acidosis) can develop, leading to cardiac arrest if untreated.
    \item \textbf{Atelectasis and Air Trapping:} Mucus plugging and localized airway narrowing can block segments of the lung, causing the collapse of alveoli (atelectasis). Conversely, incomplete airway obstruction can act as a one-way valve, allowing air in but trapping it during expiration, leading to lung hyperinflation and barotrauma.
    \item \textbf{Pneumothorax:} Increased pressure within the alveoli due to air trapping and vigorous coughing can cause alveolar rupture, leading to the leakage of air into the pleural space and collapse of the lung.
    \item \textbf{Sleep Disturbances and Chronic Fatigue:} Wheezing and coughing often worsen at night (nocturnal asthma), disrupting sleep patterns, leading to daytime somnolence, impaired cognitive function, and reduced quality of life.
    \item \textbf{Airway Remodeling:} Chronic, uncontrolled inflammation in asthma leads to permanent structural changes in the airway walls, including subepithelial fibrosis, smooth muscle hypertrophy, and mucous gland hyperplasia. This results in fixed, irreversible airflow obstruction that no longer responds fully to bronchodilators.
    \item \textbf{Side Effects of Treatment:} Prolonged or frequent use of systemic corticosteroids can lead to significant systemic side effects, including osteoporosis, adrenal suppression, weight gain, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, cataracts, and increased susceptibility to infections.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals who experience wheezing is highly variable and depends entirely on the primary diagnosis, age of onset, adherence to treatment, and exposure to environmental triggers.

For infants and young children with viral-induced wheezing, the prognosis is generally excellent. The majority of transient wheezers outgrow the symptom by age six as their airways enlarge and their immune systems mature. However, children with atopic markers (eczema, allergic rhinitis), a family history of asthma, or sensitization to indoor allergens are at a high risk of developing persistent asthma that continues into adulthood.

For patients with bronchial asthma, the prognosis is highly favorable with modern pharmacological therapy. With appropriate use of inhaled corticosteroids and long-acting bronchodilators, most asthma patients can achieve complete symptom control, maintain normal lung function, and lead active lives without significant limitations. A small percentage of patients have severe, refractory asthma that remains difficult to control despite maximal therapy, leading to frequent hospitalizations.

In contrast, COPD is a progressive, chronic disease with a more guarded prognosis. While treatment can alleviate symptoms like wheezing, improve exercise tolerance, and reduce the frequency of exacerbations, it cannot reverse the underlying lung damage. The rate of decline in lung function is highly correlated with continued smoking; smoking cessation is the only intervention proven to slow the rate of FEV1 decline. Over time, progressive respiratory insufficiency, pulmonary hypertension, and cor pulmonale (right-sided heart failure) can develop, limiting survival.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventative strategies and diligent self-care are essential to reduce the frequency and severity of wheezing episodes, minimize the need for emergency healthcare, and improve long-term respiratory health.

Key preventative and self-care measures include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Create and Follow a Written Action Plan:} Asthma and COPD patients should work with their healthcare provider to establish a clear, written action plan. This plan details daily controller medications, how to adjust medications when symptoms like wheezing worsen, and when to seek urgent medical care.
    \item \textbf{Smoking Cessation and Avoiding Secondhand Smoke:} Eliminating exposure to tobacco smoke is crucial. Nicotine replacement therapies, counseling, and support groups should be utilized to achieve permanent cessation.
    \item \textbf{Vaccination:} Respiratory infections are major triggers for acute wheezing. Annual influenza vaccination and staying up-to-date with pneumococcal vaccinations (especially for adults over 65, or those with chronic lung conditions) and COVID-19 vaccines are highly recommended to prevent severe respiratory illness.
    \item \textbf{Allergen Mitigation:} Identify specific allergic triggers through testing and implement targeted reduction strategies. This includes using allergen-impermeable covers on pillows and mattresses, washing bedding weekly in hot water (at least 60 degrees Celsius) to kill dust mites, keeping pets out of the bedroom, and using high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters.
    \item \textbf{Proper Inhaler Technique:} Using inhalers correctly is vital to ensure medication reaches the lower airways rather than depositing in the throat. Patients should regularly demonstrate their technique to a pharmacist or doctor. Using a spacer device with metered-dose inhalers (MDIs) is highly recommended, as it improves drug delivery and reduces local side effects like oral thrush.
    \item \textbf{Regular Exercise and Healthy Weight Maintenance:} Engaging in regular, moderate physical activity strengthens respiratory muscles and improves cardiovascular efficiency. Obesity is associated with increased asthma severity and mechanical restriction of breathing; maintaining a healthy body mass index (BMI) can improve lung function.
    \item \textbf{Monitoring Lung Function:} Patients with moderate-to-severe asthma should monitor their Peak Expiratory Flow (PEF) daily or during periods of worsening symptoms to detect early declines in lung function before physical wheezing becomes apparent.
\end{itemize}
