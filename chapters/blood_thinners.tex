\chapter{Blood Thinners}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Blood thinners are medicines that reduce the blood's tendency to form clots. The term covers two main groups of drugs: anticoagulants, which slow the production of clot-forming proteins, and antiplatelet agents, which make platelets less likely to stick together. They are used to prevent and treat blood clots that can cause deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, stroke, or heart attack. Although commonly called blood thinners, these medicines do not actually make the blood thinner -- they alter how easily it clots. Treatment may be short-term or lifelong, and the choice of medicine depends on the condition being treated and the person's individual risk profile.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Conditions that blood thinners are used for are very common. Atrial fibrillation, a major reason for anticoagulation, affects millions of people worldwide and becomes more common with age. Blood clots in the legs or lungs affect roughly one in a thousand people each year, with higher rates after major surgery, prolonged immobility, and in some cancers. Heart attack and stroke are leading causes of death and disability, and antiplatelet medicines are widely prescribed after these events. Millions of people take a blood thinner on a daily basis, making appropriate use and monitoring an important public health issue.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Blood clotting is a normal response to injury, but clots can form in the wrong place or at the wrong time:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anticoagulants} (such as warfarin, rivaroxaban, apixaban, dabigatran, and heparin) block proteins in the clotting cascade, so a clot forms more slowly
    \item \textbf{Antiplatelet agents} (such as aspirin, clopidogrel, and ticagrelor) prevent platelets from clumping together at the site of a narrowed artery
    \item \textbf{Why they are needed:} atrial fibrillation allows clots to form in the heart and travel to the brain; deep vein thrombosis follows immobility or injury; narrowed arteries can clot and block blood flow to the heart or brain
    \item \textbf{Risk of treatment:} by slowing clotting, these medicines increase the risk of bleeding, which must be balanced against the risk of a clot
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
There are usually no symptoms from taking a blood thinner. The features a person should be aware of are those of the conditions the medicine prevents, and those of bleeding:

\begin{itemize}
    \item No noticeable change in daily life for most people
    \item Some medicines need regular blood tests (warfarin) while others do not (most newer anticoagulants)
    \item Possible easy bruising or prolonged bleeding from minor cuts
    \item Bleeding symptoms to watch for: nosebleeds, bleeding gums, blood in the urine or stool, or coughing up blood
    \item Internal bleeding may present as headache, dizziness, weakness, or severe abdominal pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Bleeding symptoms can have other explanations, and the need for treatment must be distinguished from other situations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Easy bruising may be normal, medication-related, or due to a bleeding disorder or low platelet count
    \item Blood in the stool may be from haemorrhoids or bowel disease rather than a medicine
    \item A clot-related event may be confused with other causes of chest pain, leg swelling, or stroke-like symptoms
    \item Not every person with atrial fibrillation or a heart condition needs a blood thinner -- the decision is individualised
    \item Some people have both a high clot risk and a high bleeding risk, requiring careful balance
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical care promptly if you take a blood thinner and notice:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding that does not stop or keeps recurring
    \item Very heavy periods, blood in the urine, or black, tarry stools
    \item Vomiting blood or material that looks like coffee grounds
    \item A severe, sudden headache or any head injury, even a minor one
    \item Unexplained weakness, dizziness, or fainting
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of a stroke: sudden weakness or numbness, drooping face, or difficulty speaking
    \item Sudden severe chest pain or difficulty breathing
    \item Any major or uncontrolled bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Before starting, and while taking, a blood thinner, several checks are made:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Medical history:} to assess clot risk and bleeding risk, and to check kidney and liver function
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, kidney and liver function, and for warfarin the INR, which measures clotting time
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} ultrasound for a suspected leg clot; CT or other scans for a suspected lung clot or stroke
    \item \textbf{Heart tests:} ECG and sometimes a longer heart monitor to confirm atrial fibrillation
    \item \textbf{Dose adjustment:} warfarin is adjusted to keep the INR within a target range; other anticoagulants are given in fixed doses adjusted for kidney function
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Choice of medicine:} depends on the condition, kidney function, age, other medicines, and personal preference
    \item \textbf{Anticoagulants:} warfarin requires regular INR monitoring and dietary consistency; newer oral anticoagulants are taken as fixed doses with less monitoring
    \item \textbf{Antiplatelets:} low-dose aspirin and drugs such as clopidogrel are often used after a heart attack, stent, or stroke
    \item \textbf{Duration:} some people take a blood thinner for a few months, others for life
    \item \textbf{Medication review:} regular checks for interactions with other drugs, supplements, and foods
    \item \textbf{Procedures:} doses are often adjusted before surgery or dental work, under the direction of the treating team
    \item \textbf{Reversal:} some bleeding emergencies can be treated with medicines or products that restore normal clotting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bleeding:} the main risk -- from minor nosebleeds and bruising to serious internal bleeding, including bleeding into the brain
    \item Bleeding in people on warfarin may follow an INR that is too high
    \item Drug interactions, especially with some antibiotics, anti-inflammatory medicines, and herbal products
    \item A clot may still occur despite treatment, particularly if doses are missed
    \item Osteoporosis with very long-term use of heparin (less common with newer agents)
    \item Kidney injury with some anticoagulants in people with pre-existing kidney problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Blood thinners are very effective when used appropriately. In atrial fibrillation, anticoagulation substantially reduces the risk of stroke, at the cost of a small increase in bleeding risk. Antiplatelet therapy after a heart attack or stroke reduces the chance of further events. Outcomes depend on taking the medicine exactly as prescribed, attending monitoring, and reporting bleeding promptly. For most people the benefit of preventing a life-threatening clot outweighs the risk of bleeding, but the balance is individual.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take your blood thinner at the same time every day, exactly as prescribed
    \item Do not skip doses, double up on a missed dose, or stop without advice
    \item Tell every doctor, dentist, and pharmacist that you take a blood thinner
    \item Check before taking over-the-counter medicines or supplements, especially aspirin and anti-inflammatory pain relievers
    \item If taking warfarin, keep vitamin K intake from food steady and attend INR checks
    \item Wear a medical alert bracelet or carry a card stating the medicine you take
    \item Use a soft toothbrush, avoid risky activities, and protect yourself from injury
\end{itemize}
