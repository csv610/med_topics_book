\chapter{Lou Gehrig's disease}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Lou Gehrig's disease, clinically known as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) or motor neurone disease (MND), is a progressive, neurodegenerative disorder characterised by the selective loss of upper motor neurones in the motor cortex and lower motor neurones in the brainstem and spinal cord. Upper motor neurones transmit signals from the brain to the spinal cord, while lower motor neurones connect the spinal cord to skeletal muscles. The degeneration of these pathways leads to progressive muscle weakness, wasting, fasciculations, and spasticity. Sensory pathways, bowel and bladder function, and extraocular eye movements are typically preserved. The disease is terminal, with death usually resulting from respiratory failure.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Lou Gehrig's disease is a rare but devastating condition with a global incidence of approximately 1.5 to 2.5 cases per 100,000 person-years, and a prevalence of 4 to 6 cases per 100,000 people. It predominantly affects individuals between 50 and 70 years of age, though it can occur in younger adults. The disease exhibits a slight male predominance, with a male-to-female ratio of about 1.5:1, which approaches 1:1 in older cohorts. Approximately 90\% of cases are classified as sporadic, occurring without a family history. The remaining 10\% are familial, showing an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. In Australia, the condition is commonly referred to as Motor Neurone Disease (MND), and about 2,000 individuals are living with the disease at any given time.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The exact cause of cell death in Lou Gehrig's disease is multifactorial, involving genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, and cellular metabolic defects.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sporadic pathogenesis:} Triggered by a complex interaction of genetic susceptibility factors and environmental influences (such as exposure to heavy metals, agricultural chemicals, or history of strenuous physical activity).
    \item \textbf{Genetic mutations (Familial ALS):} Associated with mutations in several genes, most commonly a hexanucleotide repeat expansion in the C9orf72 gene, or mutations in SOD1, TARDBP (coding for TDP-43), and FUS.
    \item \textbf{Glutamate excitotoxicity:} Dysfunction of astrocytic glutamate transporters (specifically EAAT2) leads to accumulation of the neurotransmitter glutamate in the synaptic cleft, causing overstimulation of postsynaptic receptors, excessive calcium influx, and neuronal apoptosis.
    \item \textbf{Protein aggregation:} The accumulation of misfolded protein aggregates, primarily phosphorylated TDP-43, within the cytoplasm of degenerating motor neurones, leading to cellular toxicity.
    \item \textbf{Mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress:} Impaired cellular respiration, free radical accumulation, and disrupted axonal transport mechanism lead to the breakdown of the cytoskeleton and retraction of the distal axon from the neuromuscular junction.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation is heterogeneous and varies depending on whether the symptoms begin in the limbs (limb-onset) or the bulbar muscles (bulbar-onset).

\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive asymmetric weakness in the extremities, presenting as difficulty with fine motor tasks (like buttoning a shirt or writing) or frequent tripping and foot drop.
    \item Muscle atrophy (wasting) and widespread fasciculations (involuntary muscle twitching), especially visible in the tongue and limbs.
    \item Spasticity, muscle stiffness, and hyperreflexia (hyperactive reflexes) resulting from upper motor neurone involvement.
    \item Dysarthria (slurred, slow, or nasal speech) and dysphagia (difficulty swallowing liquids or solids).
    \item Pseudobulbar affect, characterised by involuntary, emotional outbursts of laughing or crying that are inappropriate to the social context.
    \item Respiratory symptoms, including dyspnoea on exertion, orthopnoea (breathlessness when lying flat), morning headaches, and sleep disturbances due to nocturnal hypoventilation.
    \item Preservation of extraocular eye movements and sphincter control, leaving the patient with intact vision and bowel/bladder function.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the symptoms of Lou Gehrig's disease overlap with many treatable neurological and structural disorders, a thorough differential investigation is necessary.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cervical spondylotic myelopathy:} Cervical spinal cord compression that presents with a mix of upper motor neurone signs in the lower limbs and lower motor neurone signs in the upper limbs, but is distinguished by sensory deficits and spinal MRI findings.
    \item \textbf{Multifocal motor neuropathy (MMN):} A slowly progressive, asymmetric motor neuropathy characterized by conduction blocks on nerve studies, but it spares upper motor neurones and responds to intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) therapy.
    \item \textbf{Myasthenia gravis:} An autoimmune neuromuscular junction disorder causing fluctuating weakness of ocular, bulbar, and limb muscles, characterized by fatiguability, positive antibody tests, and abnormal decremental response on repetitive nerve stimulation.
    \item \textbf{Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA):} A genetic neurodegenerative disorder affecting only lower motor neurones, which usually presents earlier in life and has a much slower rate of progression.
    \item \textbf{Benign fasciculation syndrome:} A non-progressive condition presenting with isolated muscle twitching, but lacks muscle weakness, atrophy, or electromyographic denervation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should consult a general practitioner or neurologist if they experience unexplained, progressive muscle weakness, slurred speech, or difficulty swallowing.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute, severe breathing difficulty, gasping for air, or signs of respiratory failure (cyanosis).
    \item Severe choking episode where the patient is unable to clear their airway.
    \item Signs of sepsis, such as high fever, confusion, and rapid breathing, which may indicate severe aspiration pneumonia.
    \item Loss of consciousness, a seizure, or a serious fall causing injury or head trauma.
    \item Sudden inability to move or speak.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis is based on demonstrating progressive upper and lower motor neurone degeneration in multiple body regions (bulbar, cervical, thoracic, and lumbosacral) and excluding mimicking conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Electromyography (EMG):} Demonstrates active denervation (fibrillation potentials, positive sharp waves) and chronic reinnervation (large, polyphasic motor unit potentials) in muscles across multiple spinal levels.
    \item \textbf{Nerve conduction studies (NCS):} Used to evaluate for motor and sensory neuropathies, checking that sensory nerve action potentials are normal, which helps rule out MMN.
    \item \textbf{MRI of the brain and spine:} Excludes structural lesions, such as cervical cord compression, syringomyelia, or brainstem tumours.
    \item \textbf{Laboratory screening:} Full blood count, creatine kinase (often mildly elevated), thyroid studies, vitamin B12, serum protein electrophoresis, and screening for heavy metals to rule out metabolic or toxic neuropathies.
    \item \textbf{Genetic counseling and testing:} Offered to patients with a family history of Lou Gehrig's disease or frontotemporal dementia to identify known pathogenic gene mutations.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is palliative and multidisciplinary, focused on maximizing quality of life, maintaining function, and prolonging survival.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Disease-modifying pharmacotherapy:} Riluzole (an oral glutamate antagonist) is the standard treatment that has been shown to modestly extend survival by three to six months.
    \item \textbf{Respiratory care:} Non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIV/BiPAP) is introduced when respiratory muscle strength declines, which improves sleep quality, cognitive function, and survival.
    \item \textbf{Nutritional management:} Speech pathology to evaluate swallowing safety, dietetics to recommend calorie-dense, soft diets, and insertion of a percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tube when oral intake is insufficient or unsafe.
    \item \textbf{Symptomatic pharmacotherapy:} Baclofen or tizanidine for spasticity, anticholinergic medications (such as glycopyrrolate or amitriptyline) to manage sialorrhoea (excess drooling), and low-dose morphine to manage dyspnoea and pain.
    \item \textbf{Mobility and communication support:} Providing orthoses, wheelchairs, and physical therapy to preserve mobility, and using speech-generating devices or eye-gaze systems as dysarthria worsens.
    \item \textbf{Palliative care and advance care planning:} Early involvement of palliative services to establish Enduring Power of Attorney and document wishes regarding end-of-life care, mechanical ventilation, and artificial feeding.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Respiratory failure, which is the primary cause of death, due to diaphragmatic and intercostal muscle paralysis.
    \item Aspiration pneumonia, caused by the inhalation of saliva, food, or gastric contents due to bulbar muscle weakness.
    \item Severe malnutrition, dehydration, and muscle wasting (cachexia) due to progressive dysphagia and increased energy expenditure.
    \item Joint contractures, pressure injuries, and deep vein thrombosis due to chronic immobility and muscle paralysis.
    \item Psychological distress, including severe anxiety, clinical depression, and existential distress for both the patient and their family.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for Lou Gehrig's disease is poor. It is a rapidly progressive and uniformly fatal condition. The median survival time from the onset of symptoms is approximately two to five years, though there is wide clinical variability. Approximately 10\% of patients survive for ten years or longer. Bulbar-onset disease is associated with a more rapid decline and shorter survival compared to limb-onset disease. The primary cause of death is respiratory failure, often precipitated by pneumonia. Early implementation of non-invasive ventilation and multidisciplinary care is associated with prolonged survival and improved quality of life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Perform daily passive and active range-of-motion stretching exercises to prevent painful joint stiffness and muscle contractures.
    \item banking your voice using digital recording software early in the disease process, before speech becomes slurred.
    \item Eat sitting upright, using thickeners for liquids and selecting soft, moist foods to reduce the risk of choking and aspiration.
    \item Plan home modifications (such as installing ramps, grab bars, and wider doorways) early in the course of the disease.
    \item Engage with patient support networks, such as local Motor Neurone Disease associations, to access equipment libraries and counseling.
\end{itemize}
