\chapter{Dealing with Childhood (Paediatric) Cancer}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A diagnosis of cancer in a child is a life-changing event for the whole family. Paediatric cancers are rare and differ substantially from adult cancers in their types, behaviour, and treatment. With modern treatment, most children with cancer are cured, but the journey involves diagnosis, intensive treatment, and long-term follow-up. This chapter explains what to expect after a childhood cancer diagnosis, how treatment works, the emotional impact on families, and where to find support.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cancer in children is uncommon, affecting approximately 1 in 500 children before age 15. In Australia, around 750 children are diagnosed with cancer each year. The most common types are leukaemias (particularly acute lymphoblastic leukaemia), brain and spinal cord tumours, lymphomas, and neuroblastoma. Survival has improved dramatically over recent decades: five-year survival for childhood cancer in Australia now exceeds 85\%, compared with under 30\% in the 1960s. The causes of most childhood cancers are unknown, and they are not generally linked to lifestyle or environmental factors that parents can control.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} a small proportion of childhood cancers are associated with inherited gene changes, such as in retinoblastoma, Li-Fraumeni syndrome, or Down syndrome
    \item \textbf{Developmental errors:} most childhood cancers arise from errors in cell development during growth, not from environmental exposures
    \item \textbf{Chromosomal abnormalities:} specific translocations and gene rearrangements drive many leukaemias and sarcomas
    \item \textbf{Environmental exposures:} only rarely implicated, for example radiation exposure or certain infections such as Epstein-Barr virus in some lymphomas
    \item \textbf{Immune surveillance:} the body's immune system normally removes abnormal cells, and failures in this process contribute to cancer development
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent or unexplained fever, infections, and tiredness, which may suggest leukaemia
    \item Bruising or bleeding without obvious injury
    \item Bone pain, limping, or refusing to walk
    \item A lump or swelling that does not go away
    \item Headaches, vomiting, or vision changes, which can indicate a brain tumour
    \item Pallor, poor appetite, and weight loss
    \item Behavioural change or regression in skills in younger children
    \item Symptoms are often non-specific at first, which can delay diagnosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Common infections such as glandular fever, which can mimic leukaemia
    \item Iron-deficiency anaemia and other blood disorders
    \item Juvenile idiopathic arthritis, which can cause bone pain and limp
    \item Benign lumps and cysts, including lymph node swelling from infection
    \item Migraine or other neurological conditions in brain tumour presentations
    \item Non-accidental injury, which must be considered in unusual bruising or fractures
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents should see a doctor if a child has persistent unexplained symptoms such as fever, tiredness, bruising, bone pain, or a lump. Because the early symptoms of childhood cancer mimic common illnesses, repeated or persistent symptoms warrant review. Trust parental instinct: a doctor should always take concerns about a child seriously.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe difficulty breathing, seizures, or loss of consciousness
    \item Sudden severe headache with vomiting or weakness of one side of the body
    \item Signs of bleeding that will not stop or collapse
    \item Any sudden deterioration in a child known to be undergoing cancer treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count and film, which may show abnormal cells in leukaemia; biochemistry and tumour markers
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} ultrasound, X-ray, CT, or MRI to define the site and extent of the tumour
    \item \textbf{Bone marrow aspiration and biopsy:} the definitive test for leukaemia and some other cancers
    \item \textbf{Tissue biopsy:} sampling of the tumour for histology and molecular analysis
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture:} to check whether leukaemia or lymphoma cells are present in the cerebrospinal fluid
    \item \textbf{Genetic and molecular testing:} characterises the tumour and guides targeted treatment
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} children are managed in specialist paediatric oncology centres with multidisciplinary teams
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy:} the backbone of treatment for most childhood cancers, delivered in phases over many months
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} removal of solid tumours where feasible
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} used for some brain tumours, lymphomas, and other cancers, with careful planning to limit effects on growing tissues
    \item \textbf{Targeted therapy and immunotherapy:} newer treatments matched to the genetic profile of the tumour
    \item \textbf{Stem cell transplantation:} for high-risk leukaemias and some other cancers
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} management of infection, pain, nausea, and nutrition during treatment
    \item \textbf{Psychosocial support:} child life specialists, psychologists, and social workers support the child and family
    \item \textbf{Long-term follow-up:} survivors are monitored for late effects of treatment into adulthood
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Side effects of treatment, including infection, bleeding, nausea, and fatigue
    \item Late effects such as impaired growth, infertility, heart or thyroid problems, and second cancers
    \item Emotional and psychological effects on the child and siblings
    \item Financial stress on the family from treatment-related costs and time off work
    \item Learning difficulties in children who received brain radiation or certain chemotherapies
    \item The small risk of relapse or treatment-resistant disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for childhood cancer has improved enormously and continues to do so. Overall, more than eight in ten children with cancer in Australia are cured, and for some types, such as acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, survival exceeds 90\%. Outcomes depend on the cancer type, stage, and biology. Survivors need lifelong follow-up for late effects, and many live healthy, productive lives. Support services, including the childhood cancer support organisations in each state, provide practical and emotional help throughout the journey.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{For parents:} seek medical review for persistent or recurrent symptoms and trust your instincts
    \item \textbf{Support networks:} connect with the hospital social work team and cancer support organisations
    \item \textbf{Family communication:} maintain open, age-appropriate conversations with the child and siblings
    \item \textbf{Look after yourself:} parents need rest, food, and support to sustain their child through treatment
    \item \textbf{Practical planning:} discuss finances, work leave, and accommodation with hospital services
    \item \textbf{Healthy routines:} encourage sleep, gentle activity, and good nutrition where possible during treatment
\end{itemize}
