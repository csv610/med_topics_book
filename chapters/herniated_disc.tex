\chapter{Herniated Disc}
\index{Herniated Disc}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A herniated disc, also known as a slipped or prolapsed disc, occurs when the soft inner material of an intervertebral disc pushes out through a tear in its tough outer layer. The discs are the shock-absorbing cushions between the bones of the spine. When disc material bulges or leaks out, it can press on nearby nerves, causing pain, numbness, and weakness. The lower back (lumbar spine) is the most common site, followed by the neck (cervical spine). Most herniated discs improve with conservative treatment, and surgery is needed only in a minority of cases.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Herniated discs are common, affecting around 1--3\% of people each year, with the peak incidence in the 30--50 age group. Lumbar disc herniation is the most common cause of sciatica, the shooting leg pain that follows the sciatic nerve. Approximately 80--90\% of people with a lumbar herniated disc recover within weeks to months without surgery. Cervical herniations cause neck and arm symptoms and are less common. Occupations involving heavy lifting and prolonged driving are associated with increased risk.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Disc degeneration:} discs lose water and elasticity with age, making the outer ring more prone to tearing
    \item \textbf{Injury:} lifting with a twisted spine, falls, and sudden strains can tear the disc
    \item \textbf{Repetitive loading:} occupations and activities that repeatedly load the spine
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} obesity, smoking, sedentary lifestyle, and family history
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} the disc's jelly-like nucleus pulposus herniates through the annulus fibrosus and can compress or irritate nerve roots
    \item \textbf{Inflammation:} chemicals from the disc also irritate nerves, contributing to pain
    \item \textbf{Lumbar site:} commonly affects the L4/L5 and L5/S1 discs, pressing on sciatic nerve roots
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Low back pain, which may settle while leg symptoms develop
    \item Sciatica: sharp, shooting pain down the back of the leg, often below the knee
    \item Numbness, tingling, or burning in the leg or foot
    \item Muscle weakness, such as foot drop or difficulty walking on toes or heels
    \item Symptoms worse with sitting, coughing, sneezing, or straining
    \item Cervical herniation: neck pain with pain, numbness, or weakness in the arm and hand
    \item Cauda equina syndrome: bladder or bowel dysfunction with saddle numbness, a surgical emergency
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Muscle strain and ligament injury causing back pain without nerve compression
    \item Lumbar spinal stenosis, which causes pain with walking
    \item Spondylolisthesis and facet joint arthritis
    \item Sacroiliac joint dysfunction and hip disease
    \item Peripheral neuropathy, such as from diabetes
    \item Serious causes: spinal infection, tumour, fracture, and cauda equina syndrome must be excluded
    \item Imaging and neurological examination distinguish these
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for back or neck pain with leg or arm symptoms, numbness, or weakness that persists beyond a few weeks, or for pain that interferes with daily life. Urgent assessment is needed for severe or progressive neurological symptoms.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Loss of bladder or bowel control, or numbness in the saddle area (cauda equina syndrome)
    \item Rapidly progressive leg or arm weakness, including foot drop
    \item Severe back pain with fever, suggesting spinal infection
    \item Back pain after significant trauma
    \item Numbness or weakness affecting both legs
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} distribution of pain, reflexes, muscle power, and sensation localise the affected nerve root
    \item \textbf{Straight leg raise test:} reproduces leg pain in lumbar disc herniation
    \item \textbf{MRI:} the definitive imaging test, showing the disc and its effect on nerves
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} useful when MRI is unavailable or contraindicated
    \item \textbf{Nerve conduction studies:} assess nerve function when the diagnosis is unclear
    \item \textbf{Urgent MRI:} for suspected cauda equina syndrome or progressive weakness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conservative care first:} most herniated discs improve with time alone
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple analgesics, anti-inflammatories, and short-term muscle relaxants
    \item \textbf{Activity:} stay active within pain limits; bed rest is not recommended
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} exercises to strengthen the core and back, with advice on posture and lifting
    \item \textbf{Steroid injections:} epidural corticosteroid injections can reduce nerve root inflammation
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} microdiscectomy removes the herniated portion, giving faster relief in people with severe or persistent symptoms
    \item \textbf{Urgent surgery:} for cauda equina syndrome and progressive weakness
    \item \textbf{Cervical treatment:} similar principles, with surgery for persistent cord or nerve compression
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cauda equina syndrome with permanent bladder, bowel, and leg dysfunction if not treated urgently
    \item Chronic nerve root pain and permanent numbness or weakness
    \item Recurrence of disc herniation after treatment
    \item Chronic low back pain and disability
    \item Complications of surgery, including infection, bleeding, and nerve injury
    \item Opioid dependence from poorly managed pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The natural history of a herniated disc is favourable: the disc material is gradually resorbed, and 80--90\% of people recover within six to twelve weeks without surgery. Even people with significant leg pain usually improve with conservative care. Surgery speeds relief for severe symptoms, with most people satisfied and able to return to work, though outcomes are similar to conservative care in the long term. Cauda equina syndrome is the exception that demands urgent surgery to preserve function.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use safe lifting technique: lift with the legs and keep the load close
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and strong core and back muscles
    \item Stay active and avoid prolonged sitting; take regular breaks
    \item Practise good posture at work, including an ergonomic workstation
    \item Stop smoking, which accelerates disc degeneration
    \item Warm up before physical activity
    \item Stay active after an episode to prevent recurrence
    \item Seek early medical care for leg pain, numbness, or weakness
\end{itemize}
