\chapter{Cellulitis}
\index{Cellulitis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cellulitis is a bacterial infection of the deeper layers of the skin and the tissue just beneath it. It typically begins in an area where the skin barrier has been broken, such as a cut, ulcer, insect bite, or area of athlete's foot, and spreads as a rapidly enlarging patch of red, swollen, hot, and painful skin.

It most often affects the lower legs but can occur anywhere on the body. Cellulitis is different from a simple superficial infection of the surface layers (impetigo) and is more serious because, if untreated, the bacteria can spread into the bloodstream and cause a life-threatening illness.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cellulitis is a common reason for people to seek medical care, with leg infections being the most frequent form. It is more common in older adults, in males, and in people with conditions that weaken the skin or immune defences.

People with diabetes, poor circulation or swelling of the limbs (lymphoedema), previous leg surgery, or a history of cellulitis are at particular risk. Recurrent cellulitis of the same leg is a well-recognised pattern and often relates to persistent skin problems such as tinea between the toes.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Cellulitis is usually caused by bacteria that live on the skin surface entering through a break in the skin:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{\textit{Streptococcus pyogenes}:} a common cause, sometimes entering through skin breaks invisible to the naked eye
    \item \textbf{\textit{Staphylococcus aureus}:} frequent in wounds, boils, and surgical sites
    \item \textbf{MRSA:} methicillin-resistant \textit{Staphylococcus aureus}, especially after hospital contact or in certain communities
    \item \textbf{Rare causes:} other bacteria can be involved in animal bites, water exposure, or people with weakened immunity
\end{itemize}

Once inside the skin, the bacteria multiply and trigger an intense inflammatory response. The affected tissue becomes swollen, red, and warm as immune cells and fluid accumulate, and the infection can track through the lymphatics, producing red lines and swollen glands.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A rapidly expanding area of red, hot, swollen, and tender skin
    \item Pain in the affected area, often worse than the redness would suggest
    \item An ill-defined, spreading border, unlike erysipelas, which has a raised, sharp edge
    \item Fever, chills, and a feeling of being unwell
    \item Swollen, tender lymph glands near the affected area
    \item A red streak tracking up the limb (lymphangitis)
    \item Blisters or, in severe cases, dark or purple discolouration of the skin
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Several conditions can mimic cellulitis and are sometimes mislabelled as it:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Venous eczema, dermatitis, and stasis skin changes of the lower legs
    \item Deep vein thrombosis (DVT), which causes unilateral leg swelling and pain
    \item Gout or an acutely inflamed joint
    \item A ruptured or infected cyst (abscess) that needs drainage
    \item Erysipelas, a more superficial infection with a sharply defined border
    \item Allergic or insect-bite reactions
    \item Rarely, skin changes from a blood clot in a superficial vein (thrombophlebitis)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor promptly if a patch of skin becomes red, hot, swollen, or painful, especially if you have diabetes, a weakened immune system, or a fever.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rapidly spreading redness, especially involving the face or around the eyes
    \item High fever, confusion, or drowsiness
    \item Signs of sepsis: rapid breathing, rapid heart rate, mottled skin, or reduced urine output
    \item Severe pain out of proportion to the appearance of the skin
    \item Dark or black areas, or large blisters, on the infected skin
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} cellulitis is usually diagnosed on the basis of the appearance and symptoms
    \item \textbf{Marking the border:} the edge of the redness is often outlined to monitor spread
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count and inflammatory markers (CRP) in moderate or severe cases
    \item \textbf{Blood cultures:} if the person is unwell or hospitalised
    \item \textbf{Skin swabs:} only useful when there is an open wound, ulcer, or pus
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} ultrasound, CT, or MRI where an abscess, deep infection, or blood clot is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} a course of oral antibiotics is the main treatment; intravenous antibiotics are used for severe cases or people who are very unwell
    \item \textbf{Elevation:} resting and elevating the affected limb reduces swelling and pain
    \item \textbf{Pain and fever relief:} paracetamol or ibuprofen as needed
    \item \textbf{Treating the entry point:} antifungal cream for athlete's foot and regular dressing of ulcers or wounds
    \item \textbf{Drainage:} any abscess needs surgical drainage
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} review within 24--48 hours to confirm the infection is improving
    \item \textbf{Recurrent disease:} longer or repeated courses of antibiotics, and attention to skin care, may be needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Abscess formation requiring drainage
    \item Bacteraemia and sepsis, a life-threatening spread of infection to the bloodstream
    \item Permanent swelling of the limb (lymphoedema) from damage to lymphatic vessels
    \item Recurrent cellulitis in the same area
    \item Necrotising fasciitis, a rare but severe deep-tissue infection, usually accompanied by severe pain and rapid deterioration
    \item In people with diabetes, worsening of foot ulcers and risk of deeper bone infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most cases of cellulitis improve within a few days of starting antibiotics and settle fully over one to two weeks. The redness, swelling, and peeling of the top layer of skin can persist for some time after the infection has cleared. Outcomes are generally very good, but cellulitis can be dangerous when treatment is delayed, when it affects the face, or in people with weakened immunity or diabetes. Recurrence is common and can often be reduced by treating the underlying skin problem.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep skin clean and moisturised to prevent cracks and dry skin
    \item Treat athlete's foot promptly with antifungal cream
    \item Clean cuts, grazes, and insect bites with soap and water and cover them with a dressing
    \item Check feet daily if you have diabetes or reduced feeling in the legs
    \item Manage swelling of the legs with elevation, compression stockings, and regular movement
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and control blood glucose if diabetic
    \item Seek early medical advice for any skin break that becomes red or painful
\end{itemize}
