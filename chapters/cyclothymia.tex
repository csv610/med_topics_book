\chapter{Cyclothymia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cyclothymia (cyclothymic disorder) is a mood disorder characterised by many periods of mild mood elevation (hypomanic symptoms) and mild depression that alternate over at least two years. The mood swings are less severe than in bipolar I or II disorder, and do not meet the full criteria for hypomania or major depression, but they are persistent and can significantly affect work, relationships, and quality of life. Cyclothymia is a chronic condition, and some people with it later develop bipolar disorder. Treatment includes mood stabilising medicines, psychotherapy, and lifestyle management.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cyclothymia is estimated to affect around 0.4--1\% of the population, and it may be underdiagnosed because the mood swings are often seen as personality traits. It usually begins in adolescence or early adulthood and affects men and women equally. People with a family history of bipolar disorder are at increased risk. Because the symptoms are chronic and fluctuating, many people live with cyclothymia for years before it is recognised, and it is associated with increased rates of anxiety, substance use, and relationship problems.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Cyclothymia is related to bipolar disorder and has a similar underlying basis.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} a family history of bipolar disorder or cyclothymia increases risk
    \item \textbf{Brain chemistry:} imbalances in neurotransmitters that regulate mood
    \item \textbf{Brain circuits:} differences in the function of brain regions involved in emotion regulation
    \item \textbf{Environmental triggers:} stress, sleep disruption, and substance use can trigger mood episodes
    \item \textbf{Temperament:} some people have a cyclothymic temperament that sits between normal variation and illness
    \item \textbf{Chronic course:} mood instability persists across the lifespan, though it may lessen with age
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The hallmark is chronic mood instability with two phases.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High phases:} elevated mood, increased energy, reduced need for sleep, talkativeness, and inflated self-esteem
    \item \textbf{Low phases:} low mood, loss of interest, low energy, poor sleep, and reduced confidence
    \item \textbf{Fluctuations:} mood swings lasting days to weeks, with periods of normal mood in between
    \item \textbf{Impulsivity:} spending, risk-taking, or irritability in high phases
    \item \textbf{Impaired function:} difficulty sustaining work, studies, or relationships
    \item \textbf{Symptoms for at least two years:} in adults, one year in adolescents
    \item \textbf{No full episodes:} symptoms do not reach the severity of hypomania or major depression
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Mood instability has several possible explanations.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Bipolar II disorder, where hypomanic and depressive episodes are more severe
    \item Bipolar I disorder, with full manic episodes
    \item Major depressive disorder, with depressive episodes but no high phases
    \item Borderline personality disorder, with rapid mood shifts driven by interpersonal events
    \item Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, with impulsivity and mood lability
    \item Substance use or withdrawal causing mood changes
    \item Thyroid disease, which can cause mood symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a GP if you have persistent, fluctuating mood symptoms that affect your life, and ask for a referral to a psychiatrist for assessment.

\textbf{Seek urgent help if:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item You have thoughts of harming yourself or ending your life
    \item A high phase leads to dangerous behaviour, such as reckless spending, driving, or substance use
    \item Your mood symptoms suddenly become severe
    \item You are unable to cope with daily activities
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:} immediate danger of self-harm or suicide, or danger to others. For crisis support in Australia, call Lifeline on 13 11 14 or the Suicide Call Back Service on 1300 659 467.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is made clinically by a psychiatrist, using a careful history of mood over time.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical interview:} detailed history of mood episodes, including duration, frequency, and severity
    \item \textbf{Mood charting:} daily recording of mood, energy, and sleep over weeks
    \item \textbf{Collateral history:} information from family or close friends
    \item \textbf{Physical examination and blood tests:} thyroid function and other tests to exclude medical causes
    \item \textbf{Mental health assessment:} screening for other conditions and suicide risk
    \item \textbf{Substance use history:} to identify drug-related mood changes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment aims to stabilise mood and improve function.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychoeducation:} understanding the condition and its patterns
    \item \textbf{Mood stabilisers:} medicines such as lithium or lamotrigine, or in some cases an antipsychotic
    \item \textbf{Psychotherapy:} cognitive behavioural therapy and interpersonal therapy
    \item \textbf{Sleep hygiene:} regular sleep routines, as sleep disruption triggers mood swings
    \item \textbf{Stress management:} identifying and managing triggers
    \item \textbf{Avoiding substances:} reducing alcohol and drug use, which destabilise mood
    \item \textbf{Regular review:} monitoring by a psychiatrist and GP
    \item \textbf{Support:} family education and peer support
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progression to bipolar I or II disorder in some people
    \item Depression, anxiety, and substance misuse
    \item Relationship breakdown and social isolation
    \item Impaired work and study performance
    \item Financial problems from impulsive spending
    \item Increased risk of suicide
    \item The burden of chronic, unpredictable mood symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Cyclothymia is a chronic condition, and the mood instability usually continues across the lifespan, although it may become less pronounced with age. With treatment, most people learn to manage their mood swings and function well at work and in relationships. Mood stabilising medicines and psychotherapy reduce the severity and frequency of fluctuations. A significant minority of people develop full bipolar disorder, which is why ongoing psychiatric follow-up matters.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep a regular sleep schedule, as sleep disruption is a major trigger
    \item Take medicines exactly as prescribed and discuss any side effects
    \item Chart your mood to recognise early warning signs
    \item Manage stress with exercise, relaxation, and realistic routines
    \item Limit alcohol and avoid illicit drugs
    \item Maintain strong social connections and support
    \item Seek help early for worsening symptoms or any suicidal thoughts
    \item Keep follow-up appointments with your psychiatrist and GP
\end{itemize}
