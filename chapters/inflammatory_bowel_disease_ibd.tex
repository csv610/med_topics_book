\chapter{Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic inflammatory condition of the digestive tract with two main forms: Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis. Crohn's disease can affect any part of the gut from the mouth to the anus, often in patches that penetrate the full thickness of the bowel wall, while ulcerative colitis affects only the inner lining of the colon and rectum, in a continuous pattern from the rectum upwards. IBD is a lifelong relapsing and remitting illness, distinct from irritable bowel syndrome, which has no inflammation. Modern treatment can induce and maintain remission, heal the bowel, and allow most people to live normal lives, though the disease requires ongoing specialist care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Australia has one of the highest rates of IBD in the world, with around one in 250 people affected and more than 100,000 Australians living with the disease. It most commonly begins between the ages of 15 and 35, with a second smaller peak after 60. Ulcerative colitis is slightly more common than Crohn's disease, and both affect men and women at similar rates. Incidence has risen over recent decades in Australia and other Westernised countries, and is increasing in developing regions, pointing to environmental factors acting on genetic susceptibility. Around 10 to 15 per cent of people with IBD have an affected family member.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
IBD results from an abnormal immune response to the gut's normal bacterial flora in genetically susceptible people. The intestinal lining becomes the target of chronic inflammation, driven by activated immune cells and inflammatory chemicals, leading to ulceration, tissue damage, and complications such as strictures, fistulas, and abscesses. Over 200 gene variants influence risk, mostly in immune and gut-barrier functions. Environmental triggers include cigarette smoking, which doubles the risk of Crohn's disease but protects against ulcerative colitis; antibiotic use and gut microbiome changes; diet; and possibly early-life factors. In ulcerative colitis, inflammation is limited to the mucosal layer, whereas in Crohn's disease it is transmural, affecting the whole bowel wall.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent or recurring diarrhoea, which may contain blood, mucus, or pus
    \item Abdominal pain and cramping, often eased by passing stool
    \item Urgency and frequent small stools in ulcerative colitis
    \item Weight loss, poor appetite, fatigue, and fever during flares
    \item Anaemia from chronic blood loss
    \item Mouth ulcers, and in Crohn's disease, perianal fissures, fistulas, and abscesses
    \item Extraintestinal features: joint pain, eye inflammation, skin problems, and liver abnormalities
    \item In children, poor growth and delayed puberty
    \item Symptoms wax and wane in flares and remissions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of IBD overlap with many other gastrointestinal conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Irritable bowel syndrome:} functional disorder without inflammation
    \item \textbf{Infectious gastroenteritis and parasites:} acute or persistent infection
    \item \textbf{Coeliac disease:} gluten-induced small bowel damage
    \item \textbf{Diverticulitis:} inflammation of colonic pouches in older people
    \item \textbf{Microscopic colitis:} inflammation visible only on biopsy
    \item \textbf{Bowel cancer:} must be excluded, especially in older people
    \item \textbf{Ischaemic colitis:} reduced blood flow to the bowel
    \item \textbf{Tuberculosis of the gut and Behcet's disease:} rare mimics of Crohn's disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with diarrhoea lasting more than a few weeks, blood in the stool, unexplained weight loss, or persistent abdominal pain should see a doctor. People with known IBD should seek review for flares, new symptoms, medication problems, or systemic features such as fever. Regular specialist follow-up is essential even in remission to monitor the disease and screen for complications.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain with a distended, rigid abdomen, which may indicate perforation or toxic megacolon
    \item Heavy rectal bleeding with faintness or dizziness
    \item High fever, shaking, or signs of sepsis
    \item Repeated vomiting or inability to keep fluids down
    \item Sudden severe symptoms suggesting bowel obstruction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} pattern of symptoms, examination of the abdomen and perianal area
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, inflammatory markers, iron studies, and vitamin levels
    \item \textbf{Stool tests:} faecal calprotectin and cultures to exclude infection
    \item \textbf{Colonoscopy:} direct visualisation with biopsies, the definitive diagnostic test
    \item \textbf{Gastroscopy and capsule endoscopy:} assessment of the upper gut and small bowel in Crohn's disease
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} MRI or CT of the small bowel, and pelvic MRI for perianal disease
    \item \textbf{Extraintestinal assessment:} liver, eye, joint, and skin review as indicated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Aminosalicylates:} such as mesalazine, mainly for ulcerative colitis
    \item \textbf{Corticosteroids:} short courses to control flares
    \item \textbf{Immunomodulators:} azathioprine, mercaptopurine, and methotrexate for maintenance
    \item \textbf{Biological therapies:} infliximab, adalimumab, vedolizumab, and ustekinumab targeting inflammatory pathways
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} resection of diseased bowel in Crohn's disease, or proctocolectomy with ileal pouch in ulcerative colitis
    \item \textbf{Nutrition:} dietetic review, nutritional support, and exclusive enteral nutrition in children
    \item \textbf{Symptom management:} antidiarrhoeals, iron and vitamin replacement, and pain relief
    \item \textbf{Smoking cessation:} essential in Crohn's disease
    \item \textbf{Multidisciplinary care:} gastroenterologist, IBD nurse, dietitian, psychologist, and colorectal surgeon
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Strictures causing obstruction, and fistulas to other organs, in Crohn's disease
    \item Abscesses in the abdomen or perianal region
    \item Severe bleeding and toxic megacolon in ulcerative colitis
    \item Perforation of the bowel
    \item Anaemia, malnutrition, osteoporosis, and vitamin deficiencies
    \item Increased risk of colorectal cancer, requiring surveillance
    \item Complications of treatment, including infection risk
    \item Impacts on growth, fertility, pregnancy, work, and mental health
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
IBD is a lifelong condition, but the majority of people achieve remission with modern treatment and live full, productive lives. Around half of people with Crohn's disease have surgery within 10 to 15 years, but surgery is not curative and inflammation recurs. Ulcerative colitis is often well controlled medically, and colectomy cures the colonic disease when needed. Biological therapies have improved remission rates and reduced surgery and hospitalisation. Long-term surveillance for colorectal cancer and proactive management of complications improve outcomes substantially.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take maintenance medicines as prescribed, even when well
    \item Stop smoking if you have Crohn's disease
    \item Attend specialist reviews and surveillance colonoscopy as scheduled
    \item Eat a balanced diet and seek dietetic advice during flares or for strictures
    \item Stay active, sleep well, and manage stress
    \item Keep a symptom diary to identify triggers
    \item Protect bone health with calcium, vitamin D, and exercise
    \item Discuss family planning and pregnancy with the care team
    \item Connect with support organisations and mental health services
\end{itemize}
