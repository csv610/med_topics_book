\chapter{Ankle Pain}
\index{Ankle Pain}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Ankle pain is discomfort felt in or around the ankle joint, the region where the lower leg bones (tibia and fibula) meet the talus of the foot. It is a symptom rather than a disease, and the underlying cause can range from a simple sprain that settles within days to arthritis, gout, or a stress fracture that requires specific treatment.

The pain may come on suddenly after an injury or develop gradually over time, and it may be felt at the front, sides, or back of the ankle or deep within the joint. Because the ankle bears the body's weight with every step, even minor causes of pain can significantly affect walking, work, and sport. A careful history and examination usually identify the cause, and most cases respond well to simple measures.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Ankle pain is extremely common across the lifespan. Injuries to the ankle, particularly sprains, are among the most frequent reasons for emergency department attendance, and a large proportion occur during sport. Lateral ankle sprains alone are estimated to affect millions of people worldwide each year.

Chronic ankle pain becomes more common with age, often reflecting arthritis, tendon degeneration, or the late effects of previous injuries that did not fully heal. People whose work or leisure involves running, jumping, or repetitive loading of the foot are at higher risk, as are those who are overweight. A person who has sprained an ankle once is substantially more likely to injure it again, which is why proper rehabilitation matters.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The causes of ankle pain can be grouped by the mechanism involved:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ligament injury:} sprains, in which the strong bands on the sides of the ankle stretch or tear, usually when the foot rolls inwards (inversion) or outwards
    \item \textbf{Bone injury:} fractures or stress fractures, often from falls, twisting, or overuse
    \item \textbf{Tendon problems:} tendinopathy of the Achilles or peroneal tendons, typically from overuse or an abnormal gait
    \item \textbf{Arthritis:} osteoarthritis from wear and tear, rheumatoid arthritis, gout, or post-traumatic arthritis after an old injury
    \item \textbf{Joint inflammation:} gout or inflammatory arthritis causing swelling, heat, and pain within the joint
    \item \textbf{Nerve entrapment:} compression of nerves around the ankle, such as tarsal tunnel syndrome, causing burning or tingling pain
    \item \textbf{Bone death:} avascular necrosis, in which loss of blood supply damages the bone
\end{itemize}

Whatever the trigger, pain arises because injured or inflamed tissues release chemical mediators that irritate the nerve endings around the joint, producing discomfort, swelling, and stiffness.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on the cause but commonly include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain that is sharp, aching, or burning in character
    \item Swelling around the ankle, which may be mild or marked
    \item Bruising or discoloration after an injury
    \item Stiffness, especially in the morning or after a period of rest
    \item Difficulty walking, climbing stairs, or standing for long periods
    \item A feeling of instability or giving way
    \item Localised tenderness over specific points, such as the outer or inner ankle bones
    \item Redness or warmth, which can suggest gout, inflammatory arthritis, or infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Many conditions can produce ankle pain:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ankle sprain:} ligament tearing after a twist; the most common cause of acute ankle pain
    \item \textbf{Ankle fracture:} a broken bone, excluded with X-ray after significant injury
    \item \textbf{Tendinopathy:} injury to the Achilles or peroneal tendons
    \item \textbf{Osteoarthritis:} gradual wear of the joint cartilage, usually in older adults
    \item \textbf{Gout:} urate crystals in the joint causing sudden, severe pain and swelling
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory arthritis:} conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis or reactive arthritis
    \item \textbf{Sinus tarsi syndrome:} pain on the outer side of the ankle from injury to a small canal between the bones
    \item \textbf{Osteochondral defect:} damage to the cartilage and bone on the dome of the talus
    \item \textbf{Referred pain:} occasionally originating from the foot, knee, hip, or lumbar spine
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most minor ankle injuries settle with self-care, but assessment is wise if pain persists or interferes with walking. Seek care if:

\begin{itemize}
    \item You cannot bear weight on the ankle for more than a few steps
    \item There is severe swelling, deformity, or a wound near the injury
    \item Numbness, tingling, or a cold, pale, blue foot develops
    \item Pain and swelling do not improve within a few days of rest and ice
    \item The ankle is hot and red and you have a fever, which may signal infection or gout
    \item The ankle keeps giving way or feels unstable after a previous injury
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services or attend an emergency department for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item An obvious deformity or a bone protruding through the skin
    \item A cold, pale, or blue foot, or loss of feeling below the ankle
    \item Signs of major bleeding or shock after a high-energy injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A doctor will take a history of how the pain began and examine the ankle, checking for tenderness, swelling, range of movement, and stability:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{X-ray:} the key test to exclude a fracture after an injury; the Ottawa ankle rules guide whether one is needed
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} useful for assessing tendons and ligaments, and for guiding injections
    \item \textbf{MRI:} provides detailed images of cartilage, ligaments, and bone; used when the diagnosis is unclear or a joint-surface injury is suspected
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} used for complex fractures or to plan surgery
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} serum uric acid for gout and inflammatory markers for suspected arthritis
    \item \textbf{Joint aspiration:} drawing fluid from the joint with a needle to test for crystals, infection, or inflammation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on the cause:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Soft-tissue injuries:} rest, ice, compression, and elevation (RICE) in the early days, followed by gentle movement and progressive strengthening
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple analgesics such as paracetamol, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medicines (NSAIDs) where appropriate
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} exercises to restore range of motion, strength, and balance; often the most important step after an injury
    \item \textbf{Ankle support:} bracing, taping, or a walking boot for unstable or healing ankles
    \item \textbf{Footwear and orthotics:} supportive shoes or insoles to correct gait abnormalities
    \item \textbf{Treating the underlying condition:} urate-lowering therapy for gout and disease-modifying drugs for inflammatory arthritis
    \item \textbf{Injections:} corticosteroid injection into the joint or around a tendon for persistent inflammation
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} for severe fractures, torn tendons, or advanced arthritis that has not responded to other treatment, including arthroscopy, ligament reconstruction, fusion, or replacement
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chronic instability:} repeated giving way after ligament injuries
    \item \textbf{Post-traumatic arthritis:} long-term joint damage after fractures or cartilage injuries
    \item \textbf{Chronic tendinopathy:} persistent tendon pain that is slow to settle
    \item \textbf{Complex regional pain syndrome:} disproportionate pain, swelling, and skin changes after an injury
    \item \textbf{Missed fracture or stress fracture:} continuing to load a broken bone delays healing
    \item \textbf{Compartment syndrome:} rare but serious, occurring when bleeding and swelling compress nerves and blood vessels after a severe injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the cause. Most sprains and tendon injuries improve substantially within a few weeks to months with rest and rehabilitation, although some people have lingering discomfort or a tendency to re-injure the ankle. Fractures generally heal well with correct treatment. Arthritis tends to be progressive, but pain can usually be well controlled with physiotherapy, weight management, medication, and, where needed, surgery such as joint fusion or replacement. Early and appropriate treatment gives the best chance of a full recovery.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Warm up properly and use correct technique when exercising or playing sport
    \item Strengthen the muscles around the ankle and practise balance exercises to prevent sprains
    \item Wear supportive, well-fitted footwear appropriate for the activity
    \item Lose weight if overweight, to reduce the load on the ankle joint
    \item Maintain bone health with adequate calcium, vitamin D, and weight-bearing exercise
    \item Complete a rehabilitation programme after any ankle injury rather than returning to activity too soon
    \item See a physiotherapist or doctor early if pain persists, rather than living with it
    \item Use strapping or a brace during sport if you have had repeated ankle injuries
\end{itemize}
