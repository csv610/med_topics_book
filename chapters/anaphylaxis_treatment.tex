\chapter{Anaphylaxis Treatment}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Anaphylaxis treatment is the care given to a person having a severe allergic reaction, and the longer-term plan that keeps them safe afterwards. In an emergency, the priority is to stop the reaction from progressing: adrenaline is given quickly, the person is positioned to protect breathing and blood pressure, and an ambulance is called.

After the emergency, treatment shifts to identifying the trigger, avoiding it, and equipping the person with adrenaline autoinjectors and a written action plan so that future reactions are managed promptly. Treatment therefore has two equal parts: the immediate response to a reaction and the preventive strategy that reduces the risk and severity of future episodes.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Because anaphylaxis can affect anyone with an allergy, the need for treatment follows the same patterns as the condition itself. It is more common in children and young adults, and food is the leading trigger in childhood, while medicines and insect stings become relatively more important in adults.

Rates of emergency treatment for anaphylaxis have been increasing in many countries. Delay in giving adrenaline is a recognised factor in severe outcomes, so public education about when and how to treat is a key part of reducing harm.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Why treatment is needed:} in anaphylaxis, the immune system releases histamine and other chemicals that widen blood vessels, drop blood pressure, and narrow the airways
    \item \textbf{Adrenaline reverses this:} it tightens blood vessels to raise blood pressure, relaxes the muscles of the airways to ease breathing, and reduces swelling
    \item \textbf{The risk window:} symptoms can worsen quickly, and a second wave of symptoms (a biphasic reaction) may occur hours later, which is why ongoing observation is part of treatment
    \item \textbf{Individual variation:} the severity of a reaction varies between people and even between episodes in the same person, so the same treatment plan applies regardless of how mild a previous reaction was
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Symptoms that signal the need for treatment: hives, swelling of the lips or tongue, wheezing, difficulty breathing, a hoarse voice, dizziness, or collapse
    \item Symptoms that usually appear within minutes of contact with the trigger
    \item A feeling of panic or a strong sense that something is seriously wrong
    \item Improvement expected within minutes of an adrenaline injection
    \item Possible return of symptoms hours later even after initial improvement
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A severe asthma attack, which needs inhaled bronchodilators and may not respond to adrenaline alone
    \item Vasovagal fainting, which looks similar but does not respond to allergy treatment
    \item Panic or anxiety attacks causing breathlessness and a racing heart
    \item Septic shock or other causes of sudden collapse
    \item Mild allergic reactions involving only the skin, which do not need adrenaline
    \item Drug-induced angioedema, such as from ACE inhibitors, which may not respond fully to adrenaline
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anaphylaxis treatment always begins with emergency care. If symptoms such as throat swelling, breathing difficulty, or collapse occur, treatment and calling for help must happen together.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Any suspected anaphylaxis, even if adrenaline has already been given
    \item Difficulty breathing, wheezing, or swelling of the tongue or throat
    \item Faintness, collapse, or loss of consciousness
    \item Symptoms that do not improve within 5 minutes of an adrenaline dose
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Emergency assessment:} the diagnosis is clinical, based on rapid onset of symptoms and a likely trigger; treatment should not be delayed for tests
    \item \textbf{Blood tryptase:} a sample taken soon after the reaction may help confirm that anaphylaxis occurred
    \item \textbf{Follow-up allergy testing:} skin prick tests or blood antibody tests performed weeks later to identify the trigger
    \item \textbf{Specialist review:} an allergy specialist confirms the diagnosis, reviews severity, and prescribes an appropriate adrenaline autoinjector
    \item \textbf{Education check:} demonstration that the person, and their family or carers, can use the autoinjector correctly
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adrenaline autoinjector:} the first and most important treatment; injected into the outer thigh muscle and repeated after 5 minutes if needed
    \item \textbf{Positioning:} lie flat with the legs raised if feeling faint, but sit up if breathing is very difficult; never stand up suddenly
    \item \textbf{Ambulance and hospital:} all reactions require transport to hospital, where oxygen, fluids, and other medicines can be given and the person observed for several hours
    \item \textbf{Other medicines:} antihistamines and corticosteroids are used as additional treatment in hospital but do not replace adrenaline
    \item \textbf{Emergency action plan:} a written plan with contact numbers, trigger avoidance, and instructions for using adrenaline
    \item \textbf{Carry adrenaline always:} keep an autoinjector and a spare with you, and check the expiry date regularly
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Untreated or delayed anaphylaxis can lead to airway obstruction, severe low blood pressure, and in rare cases death
    \item Biphasic reactions can occur hours later even after initial recovery
    \item Adrenaline side effects such as a racing heart, tremor, and headache are common and usually short-lived
    \item Injury from fainting or a fall during the reaction
    \item Anxiety about using an autoinjector or fear of future reactions, which some people find hard to manage
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Prompt use of adrenaline greatly improves the outcome of anaphylaxis, and most people treated early recover fully without long-term harm. Hospital observation for several hours reduces the risk from delayed or returning symptoms. Most people who have had anaphylaxis can live normal lives once they understand their triggers and carry adrenaline. Repeated training and rehearsal of the action plan improve confidence and make correct use of the autoinjector more likely in a real emergency.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Carry an adrenaline autoinjector, and a spare, at all times and know exactly how to use it
    \item Rehearse your emergency action plan regularly with family, friends, school, or workplace contacts
    \item Avoid known triggers, and read food and medicine labels carefully on every occasion
    \item Seek follow-up with an allergy specialist to confirm the trigger and review your plan
    \item Tell health professionals about your allergy before any procedure, medicine, or vaccination
    \item Replace autoinjectors before they expire and store them within the recommended temperature range
\end{itemize}
