\chapter{Egg Allergy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Egg allergy is a reaction of the immune system to proteins in egg white or egg yolk. It is one of the most common food allergies in children, and reactions range from mild skin rashes and hives to potentially life-threatening anaphylaxis. Most children with egg allergy outgrow it by school age, although some continue to be allergic into adulthood. Management involves strict avoidance of egg, careful reading of food labels, and carrying an adrenaline autoinjector where the risk of severe reaction exists. Egg allergy also has implications for some vaccines, which require special advice.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Egg allergy affects approximately 1 to 2 per cent of young children, making it one of the most common food allergies in infancy. It is more common in children with other allergies, eczema, or a family history of allergy. The good news is that many children outgrow egg allergy, with a majority tolerant by the age of five to ten years. Severe reactions are less common than with peanut or tree nut allergy, but they do occur.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Egg allergy is an immunoglobulin E (IgE)-mediated reaction to proteins in eggs.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Allergens:} proteins such as ovalbumin in egg white and ovomucoid, which is the most heat- and digestion-stable
    \item \textbf{Immune response:} the immune system produces IgE antibodies to egg proteins, and on re-exposure these trigger the release of histamine and other chemicals
    \item \textbf{Reactions:} symptoms can involve the skin, gut, airways, and circulation
    \item \textbf{Heat and cooking:} some children with egg allergy can tolerate baked egg in cakes and muffins because heating changes the proteins; this must be assessed carefully and individually
    \item \textbf{Cross-reactivity:} other bird products and feathers may cause reactions in some people, though this is uncommon
    \item \textbf{Anaphylaxis:} a severe, rapid, whole-body allergic reaction that requires immediate emergency treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Reactions usually occur within minutes to a couple of hours of eating egg.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hives, redness, and itching of the skin
    \item Swelling of the lips, face, and eyes
    \item Vomiting, abdominal pain, and diarrhoea
    \item Runny nose, sneezing, and watery eyes
    \item Cough, wheezing, and difficulty breathing in severe reactions
    \item Dizziness, collapse, and loss of consciousness in anaphylaxis
    \item In infants: eczema flare-ups and feeding distress
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Not all reactions to egg are allergy.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Food intolerance, which causes digestive symptoms without immune involvement
    \item Eczema triggered by egg in children with atopic dermatitis
    \item Other food allergies, which can coexist
    \item Gastroenteritis, mistaken for a food reaction
    \item Environmental allergies, such as to feathers
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Families should seek medical assessment for any suspected food allergy, both to confirm the diagnosis and to make a management plan. Children diagnosed with egg allergy should be reviewed regularly, because tolerance can develop.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, wheezing, or throat tightness
    \item Swelling of the tongue or throat
    \item Collapse, faintness, or confusion
    \item Vomiting or severe abdominal pain with other allergy symptoms
    \item Any signs of anaphylaxis; give adrenaline if available and call for an ambulance
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is made by an allergy specialist using a combination of history and tests.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the details of the reaction, the food, and the timing
    \item \textbf{Skin prick testing:} a small amount of egg protein is placed on the skin, and a positive reaction supports the diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} specific IgE levels to egg proteins
    \item \textbf{Oral food challenge:} supervised feeding of egg in increasing amounts, the gold standard for diagnosis and for reassessing tolerance
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to an allergy specialist for testing and management
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management centres on avoidance, preparedness, and the treatment of reactions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Strict avoidance:} avoiding egg in all forms, and reading labels for terms such as albumen, ovalbumin, and lecithin
    \item \textbf{Reading labels:} understanding food labelling and asking about ingredients in restaurants
    \item \textbf{Emergency plan:} a written action plan for reactions, with clear instructions
    \item \textbf{Adrenaline autoinjector:} prescribed for children at risk of anaphylaxis, with training for families and schools
    \item \textbf{Antihistamines:} for mild reactions as directed by the plan
    \item \textbf{Baked egg introduction:} under specialist supervision, some children can safely eat baked egg, which accelerates tolerance
    \item \textbf{Education:} informing child care, school, and family members
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Anaphylaxis, which is life-threatening without prompt treatment
    \item Anxiety about eating and social situations
    \item Nutritional concerns if many foods are avoided
    \item Bullying and exclusion at school
    \item The practical burden of constant vigilance and label reading
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most children outgrow egg allergy, often by school age, and tolerance is more likely in children with milder reactions and those who can tolerate baked egg. The condition is well managed with avoidance, an emergency plan, and an autoinjector where needed. Regular specialist review tracks the development of tolerance and reassesses the need for avoidance.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Introduce egg and other foods to infants at the usual age, as recommended by current guidelines, and seek advice if there are concerns
    \item Read every food label and ask about ingredients when eating out
    \item Carry the adrenaline autoinjector at all times and check its expiry
    \item Practise using the autoinjector and make sure carers and teachers are trained
    \item Wear medical alert jewellery where recommended
    \item Keep up specialist reviews to check for tolerance
    \item Speak to a dietitian if avoidance is affecting nutrition
\end{itemize}
