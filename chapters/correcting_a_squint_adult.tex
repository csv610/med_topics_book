\chapter{Correcting a Squint (Adult)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A squint (strabismus) is a condition in which the two eyes do not point in the same direction. In adults, a squint may have persisted since childhood or developed later in life as a result of injury, illness, or neurological disease. Correcting an adult squint aims to straighten the eyes, restore binocular (two-eyed) vision where possible, and improve appearance and self-confidence. Treatment options include glasses, prism lenses, eye exercises, Botox injections, and surgery on the eye muscles. Surgery is usually performed under local or general anaesthetic as a day procedure.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Squints affect about 2--4\% of children, and many persist into adulthood if not fully corrected in childhood. Adult-onset squints are less common but are frequently caused by conditions such as diabetes, thyroid eye disease, head injury, stroke, or tumours pressing on the eye nerves. Adult squint correction has become more common as surgical techniques have improved, and both functional and cosmetic outcomes are generally good. Many adults who have lived with a squint since childhood report significant benefits to self-esteem and social confidence from surgery.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
A squint occurs when the muscles controlling the eyes are unbalanced, or when the nerves or brain centres coordinating eye movement are damaged.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Childhood strabismus:} a misalignment that has persisted because it was untreated, amblyopia (lazy eye) developed, or treatment was incomplete
    \item \textbf{Refractive error:} uncorrected long-sightedness can drive the eyes to converge
    \item \textbf{Nerve palsy:} damage to the third, fourth, or sixth cranial nerve, from diabetes, head injury, stroke, or tumours
    \item \textbf{Thyroid eye disease:} inflammation and scarring of the eye muscles, causing restrictive squint
    \item \textbf{Trauma:} injury to the eye socket or the muscles themselves
    \item \textbf{Age-related change:} decompensation of a previously controlled squint
    \item \textbf{Blindness in one eye:} loss of vision can allow the affected eye to drift
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Adults with a squint may notice the misalignment themselves or have it pointed out by others.

\begin{itemize}
    \item One eye turning inwards, outwards, upwards, or downwards
    \item Double vision (diplopia), especially with adult-onset squint
    \item A head tilt or turn adopted to avoid double vision
    \item Eye strain, fatigue, or headaches
    \item Difficulty judging depth
    \item Reduced self-confidence or avoidance of eye contact
    \item An apparent eye misalignment that does not change with time
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Not every appearance of a squint is a true squint, and underlying causes must be identified.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Pseudostrabismus: a wide bridge of the nose or skin folds making the eyes look misaligned when they are not
    \item Concomitant squint from refractive error or childhood strabismus
    \item Paralytic squint from cranial nerve palsy
    \item Restrictive squint from thyroid eye disease or trauma
    \item Nystagmus or other eye movement disorders
    \item A serious cause of new-onset squint or double vision, such as stroke, aneurysm, or brain tumour
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any adult with a new squint or new double vision should be assessed promptly, as these can indicate a neurological emergency. Long-standing squints should be assessed by an ophthalmologist to discuss treatment options.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden new double vision, especially with headache, facial weakness, or drooping eyelid
    \item Sudden weakness, numbness, or difficulty speaking (possible stroke)
    \item Sudden loss of vision
    \item Severe headache or neck stiffness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment identifies the type, cause, and correctability of the squint.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} age of onset, duration, double vision, head posture, and any underlying illness
    \item \textbf{Visual acuity:} measurement of vision in each eye, looking for amblyopia
    \item \textbf{Cover tests:} the eyes are alternately covered to reveal the direction and size of the misalignment
    \item \textbf{Prism testing:} prisms measure the angle of deviation
    \item \textbf{Ocular motility examination:} tests of eye movement in all directions
    \item \textbf{Refraction:} assessment of the need for glasses
    \item \textbf{Orthoptic assessment:} measurement of binocular function and the potential for double vision after surgery
    \item \textbf{Imaging or blood tests:} CT, MRI, or thyroid function tests where a cause such as stroke, tumour, or thyroid disease is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is individualised according to cause, symptoms, and goals.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Glasses:} correcting refractive error may control a convergent squint
    \item \textbf{Prism lenses:} prism can realign the images, abolishing double vision without surgery
    \item \textbf{Orthoptic exercises:} eye exercises can improve control of intermittent squints
    \item \textbf{Botox injection:} temporarily weakens an overacting muscle; useful for diagnosis and for some acute squints
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} eye muscles are repositioned to balance the eyes; adjustable stitches allow fine-tuning on the day
    \item \textbf{Amblyopia management:} if a lazy eye is present, treatment may improve vision before or alongside surgery
    \item \textbf{Treatment of the cause:} management of diabetes, thyroid disease, or nerve injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Double vision after surgery, which is usually temporary but can persist
    \item Over- or under-correction of the alignment
    \item The squint returning over time
    \item Redness, swelling, or a gritty feeling after surgery
    \item Infection or bleeding (rare)
    \item Damage to the eye or surrounding structures (very rare)
    \item Anaesthetic risks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Squint surgery is successful in straightening the eyes in the majority of adults, and most are pleased with both the functional and cosmetic results. Recovery is quick, with most people returning to normal activities within one to two weeks. Adult-onset squints due to nerve palsy or restrictive disease respond well once the underlying cause is stable. Double vision after surgery is common in the first weeks but usually settles; some patients need prisms or a second operation. Adults who have had a squint since childhood generally do not regain binocular depth perception, but the cosmetic improvement is often life-changing.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek early assessment of any new squint or double vision
    \item Attend all pre-operative assessments so the plan is tailored to you
    \item Manage underlying conditions such as diabetes and thyroid disease
    \item Follow the surgeon's instructions for eye drops and activity after surgery
    \item Report persistent double vision or worsening alignment promptly
    \item Discuss realistic expectations, including the possibility of needing more than one procedure
    \item Wear protective eyewear to reduce the risk of eye injury
\end{itemize}
