\chapter{Antenatal Tests}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Antenatal tests are the screening and diagnostic checks offered during pregnancy to monitor the health of the mother and the developing baby. They include routine blood and urine tests, ultrasound scans, screening for conditions such as Down syndrome, and tests for infections that could affect the pregnancy. Their aims are to detect problems early so they can be managed or treated, to provide reassurance to parents, and to support informed decision-making.

Testing is optional, and women are encouraged to discuss the purpose, timing, and implications of each test before consenting. The specific tests offered, and their timing, vary between countries and health services, but the overall structure of early booking assessment, first-trimester screening, a mid-pregnancy anatomy scan, and later glucose and blood pressure checks is common to most high-quality antenatal care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Antenatal care is one of the most effective public health interventions available, and the large majority of women in developed countries attend for regular checks. Screening tests are offered to all pregnant women regardless of their individual risk, because many complications are more common than is generally appreciated.

Around 1 in 700 babies is born with Down syndrome, and modern screening detects most of these during pregnancy. Group B streptococcus colonises roughly 1 in 5 women in late pregnancy, gestational diabetes affects about 5--10\% of pregnancies, and hypertensive disorders complicate a similar proportion. Regular antenatal testing is associated with better detection and management of these problems and with improved outcomes for mother and baby.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Antenatal testing addresses several distinct categories of risk:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infections:} infections such as rubella, syphilis, hepatitis B, HIV, and cytomegalovirus can harm the baby if acquired or reactivated during pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Blood group incompatibility:} a rhesus-negative mother carrying a rhesus-positive baby can develop antibodies that may harm future pregnancies
    \item \textbf{Chromosomal conditions:} extra or missing chromosomes, such as the extra copy of chromosome 21 in Down syndrome
    \item \textbf{Structural anomalies:} problems in the baby's organs, spine, or limbs, such as neural tube defects or heart defects
    \item \textbf{Maternal conditions:} high blood pressure, anaemia, or diabetes that may develop or worsen in pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Gestational diabetes:} rising placental hormones reduce the body's response to insulin in the later part of pregnancy, raising blood glucose
\end{itemize}

Understanding these risks allows tests to be targeted appropriately, and results guide further testing, monitoring, or treatment.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Antenatal tests are not symptoms; they are routine checks. However, certain features of the pregnancy determine which tests are offered:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A booking-in appointment early in pregnancy, when the full history is taken and the first blood tests are offered
    \item A dating and viability ultrasound scan in the first trimester
    \item Combined screening or non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) for chromosomal conditions around 11--13 weeks
    \item The morphology (detailed anatomy) scan around 18--20 weeks
    \item Glucose tolerance testing around 24--28 weeks to screen for gestational diabetes
    \item Repeat blood pressure, urine, and blood tests at intervals through the pregnancy
    \item Group B streptococcus screening in late pregnancy in some services
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
An abnormal screening result is not a diagnosis. The critical distinction is between screening tests, which estimate risk, and diagnostic tests, which confirm or exclude a condition:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Screening results:} a high- or intermediate-risk result is followed by a diagnostic test for confirmation
    \item \textbf{False positives:} screening can suggest a problem that is not actually present
    \item \textbf{False negatives:} screening can miss some cases, so a low-risk result is not a guarantee
    \item \textbf{Incidental findings:} scans occasionally reveal findings that are harmless or of uncertain significance
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Antenatal care involves scheduled visits, but some situations require earlier or urgent review:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding or spotting from the vagina at any stage of pregnancy
    \item Severe or persistent abdominal pain or cramping
    \item Reduced or absent baby movements in later pregnancy
    \item Sudden swelling of the face, hands, or feet, or a severe headache (possible pre-eclampsia)
    \item Fever, pain passing urine, or unusual vaginal discharge
    \item Leaking fluid from the vagina or regular contractions before term
    \item Any worry between appointments, however small
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heavy vaginal bleeding
    \item Severe abdominal pain or collapse
    \item Fitting or convulsions
    \item A sudden severe headache with visual changes or chest pain in pregnancy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The range of routine antenatal tests includes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} blood group and rhesus type, full blood count, blood glucose, and screening for hepatitis B, HIV, syphilis, and rubella immunity, with other infections tested where indicated
    \item \textbf{Urine tests:} checked for protein (a possible sign of pre-eclampsia) and infection at most visits
    \item \textbf{Dating scan:} an early ultrasound to confirm the dates and check the baby's development
    \item \textbf{Combined screening or NIPT:} a blood test, with or without a nuchal translucency ultrasound, to estimate the risk of Down syndrome and other chromosomal conditions
    \item \textbf{Morphology scan:} a detailed ultrasound around 18--20 weeks examining the baby's organs, spine, limbs, and placenta
    \item \textbf{Glucose tolerance test:} a fasting blood test followed by a sweetened drink, to diagnose gestational diabetes
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic tests:} chorionic villus sampling (CVS) or amniocentesis, which sample placental or amniotic fluid cells for chromosomal analysis, offered when screening suggests increased risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management depends on what the tests find:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal results:} provide reassurance; routine care and planning for the birth continue
    \item \textbf{Gestational diabetes:} dietary advice, blood glucose monitoring, and sometimes tablets or insulin
    \item \textbf{Anaemia or low iron:} iron supplementation and dietary advice
    \item \textbf{High blood pressure or pre-eclampsia:} close monitoring, medication, and planned early delivery if severe
    \item \textbf{Rhesus-negative mother:} anti-D immunoglobulin given at routine times and after any bleeding, to protect future pregnancies
    \item \textbf{Infections:} treatment of the mother and, where appropriate, the baby after birth
    \item \textbf{Chromosomal or structural conditions:} referral for specialist counselling, further scans, and planning of care during pregnancy and after birth
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} to fetal medicine or obstetric specialists for abnormal results
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anxiety:} screening can cause considerable worry, especially while waiting for results
    \item \textbf{False reassurance or false alarm:} no test is perfect, and both occur
    \item \textbf{Procedural risks:} CVS and amniocentesis carry a small risk of miscarriage
    \item \textbf{Over-investigation:} borderline results may lead to further tests and monitoring
    \item \textbf{Disclosure of unexpected findings:} such as a previously unknown infection or a condition in the baby that cannot be treated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most pregnancies proceed normally, and routine antenatal testing reassures the majority of families while identifying the minority of problems early. Gestational diabetes, when detected and managed, generally resolves after birth, although it raises the risk of type 2 diabetes in later life. Many structural and chromosomal conditions detected on screening can be planned for, and some are treatable. Early detection of pre-eclampsia and fetal growth problems allows timely intervention and improves outcomes for both mother and baby.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Book an antenatal appointment as soon as pregnancy is confirmed, ideally before 10 weeks
    \item Take folic acid and iodine supplementation as advised, ideally before conception
    \item Attend all scheduled antenatal visits and accept the recommended screening tests
    \item Ask questions about any test you do not understand, and ask for results to be explained clearly
    \item Avoid alcohol, tobacco, and recreational drugs in pregnancy
    \item Eat a balanced diet, stay active, and get enough rest
    \item Know the warning signs to report between visits, and report them promptly
    \item Keep a record of your tests and results for your birth and postnatal care
\end{itemize}
