\chapter{Immunisation Side Effects}
\index{Immunisation Side Effects}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Immunisation is one of the safest and most effective public health measures ever developed, and serious side effects from vaccines are extremely rare. Most people experience no side effects at all, or only minor ones such as a sore arm, mild fever, or tiredness for a day or two. These reactions are a sign that the immune system is responding to the vaccine and developing protection. Serious side effects, such as a severe allergic reaction, occur in about one in a million doses. This chapter explains common vaccine reactions, how they are monitored in many countries, and when to seek medical advice.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Vaccines are among the most closely monitored medicines in use. In many countries, millions of vaccine doses are administered each year through the National Immunisation Program, and the national adverse event surveillance system monitors reports of reactions. Common minor reactions such as injection-site soreness and low-grade fever are reported in around 5--20\% of vaccinations, depending on the vaccine and the person's age. Serious adverse events are reported at rates of less than one per 100,000 doses for most vaccines, and the risk of being harmed by vaccination is far smaller than the risk of complications from the diseases the vaccines prevent.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Vaccines work by exposing the immune system to components of a pathogen in a safe form, prompting the production of antibodies and memory cells without causing the disease itself. The minor symptoms that follow vaccination are caused by the immune response itself: inflammatory signals released at the injection site produce local pain, redness, and swelling, and circulating immune mediators can cause low-grade fever, headache, and fatigue. These effects are short-lived. Rare serious reactions have specific mechanisms: anaphylaxis is a severe allergic reaction to a vaccine component, and very rare events such as febrile seizures or immune-mediated conditions occur at rates too low to be detected in most clinical trials but are monitored continuously in the population.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Common, mild effects:} soreness, redness, or swelling at the injection site, lasting a day or two
    \item Low-grade fever, tiredness, headache, or muscle aches for 1--2 days
    \item In babies and young children: fussiness, poor appetite, and sleepiness for 24--48 hours
    \item Minor reactions usually start within hours of vaccination and resolve within days
    \item Rare but more significant effects: high fever, extensive swelling of the limb, or fainting
    \item Very rare serious events: severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) within minutes of vaccination, and extremely rare immune-related conditions
    \item Reactions to booster doses can be more prominent than to first doses, particularly for tetanus-containing vaccines
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Most symptoms after vaccination are caused by the vaccine itself, but other explanations should be considered.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intercurrent illness:} coincidental infection at the time of vaccination is common, especially in children
    \item \textbf{Anxiety-related reactions:} fainting and hyperventilation, common in adolescents and adults
    \item \textbf{Allergy to components:} distinguished by timing (usually within minutes) and features such as hives and breathing difficulty
    \item \textbf{Localised infection:} cellulitis at the injection site, which is different from simple vaccine-site redness
    \item \textbf{Other causes of fever or rash:} must be considered when symptoms appear several days after vaccination
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical advice if a reaction is severe, prolonged, or unusual: a fever that does not settle or is very high, extensive or painful swelling, a spreading rash, or symptoms that continue beyond a few days. Report any significant reaction to the doctor or nurse who gave the vaccine, who can arrange reporting through the national adverse event system. Always seek prompt medical assessment for any new neurological symptoms after vaccination, although these are extremely rare.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, wheezing, or swelling of the face, lips, or throat within minutes to hours of vaccination (anaphylaxis)
    \item Collapse, fainting with injury, or a seizure
    \item A rash of hives spreading quickly with dizziness or weakness
    \item Signs of a severe reaction in a baby, such as persistent inconsolable crying or limpness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical review:} careful history of the timing and nature of symptoms after vaccination
    \item \textbf{Adverse event reporting:} significant reactions are reported to the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) through the vaccine provider
    \item \textbf{Allergy assessment:} referral to an allergy specialist for suspected vaccine allergy, with testing of components if needed
    \item \textbf{Investigation of other causes:} blood tests or cultures where infection or other illness is suspected
    \item \textbf{Assessment for future doses:} specialist advice on whether further doses can be given and under what conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mild reactions:} simple measures such as paracetamol for fever or discomfort, a cool compress at the injection site, and rest
    \item \textbf{Observation:} people are usually asked to wait 15 minutes after vaccination in case of a rare immediate reaction
    \item \textbf{Treatment of anaphylaxis:} adrenaline given promptly by trained staff, followed by hospital care
    \item \textbf{Adjustment of future doses:} different vaccine formulations or spacing may be recommended after significant reactions
    \item \textbf{Avoidance only where indicated:} most people who have a minor reaction can receive all future vaccines
    \item \textbf{Ongoing monitoring:} surveillance of vaccine safety continues across the whole population
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Very rare serious allergic reactions requiring emergency treatment
    \item Febrile seizures, a rare complication of some vaccines in young children, which are distressing but almost always without lasting harm
    \item Extremely rare immune-mediated conditions linked to particular vaccines
    \item Injection-site complications such as infection or haematoma
    \item The greater and more common harm of not being vaccinated: the serious complications of vaccine-preventable diseases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The large majority of vaccine reactions are minor and resolve completely within a few days without treatment. Serious reactions are extremely rare, and the balance of benefit and risk strongly favours vaccination for nearly everyone: the diseases prevented by immunisation cause far more severe illness, disability, and death than vaccines themselves. When a rare serious reaction does occur, local reporting systems identify it quickly and inform public health decisions, maintaining the safety of the national program.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Tell the vaccinator about any allergies, previous reactions, and current illness before vaccination
    \item Remain at the clinic for 15 minutes after vaccination
    \item Use a cool compress and simple analgesia for a sore arm or mild fever
    \item Keep a record of vaccinations and any reactions
    \item Report any significant reaction to your doctor, who can notify the national surveillance system
    \item Seek advice before the next dose if you have had a notable reaction
    \item Remember that minor reactions are a normal sign the immune system is responding
\end{itemize}
