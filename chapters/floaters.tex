\chapter{Eye Floaters}
\index{Eye Floaters}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Eye floaters are small spots, threads, or cobweb-like shapes that drift across the field of vision, moving as the eye moves and continuing to float when the eye stops. They are caused by tiny clumps of gel (vitreous) inside the eye casting shadows on the retina. Floaters are extremely common and are usually harmless, becoming more frequent with age as the vitreous liquefies and shrinks. However, a sudden increase in floaters, especially with flashes of light, a shadow across the vision, or loss of vision, can signal a retinal tear or detachment, which is an eye emergency. This chapter explains what floaters are, when they matter, and what to do about them.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Floaters affect most people at some point, and their prevalence rises steadily with age: around half of people over 50 notice them, and they are almost universal in older adults. Short-sighted (myopic) people, those who have had cataract surgery, and people who have had eye injury are more likely to develop floaters. Posterior vitreous detachment, the common age-related cause, affects around half of people over 65. Retinal detachment, the serious complication that floaters can announce, affects about 1 in 10,000 people per year and requires urgent treatment to preserve vision.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vitreous gel:} the eye is filled with a clear gel called the vitreous, which is attached to the retina
    \item \textbf{Ageing:} with age, the gel liquefies and shrinks, and collagen fibres clump together, casting shadows as floaters
    \item \textbf{Posterior vitreous detachment:} the shrinking gel peels away from the retina; this is usually harmless but can tear the retina
    \item \textbf{Retinal tear and detachment:} if the gel pulls on the retina hard enough, it can tear it, allowing fluid to lift the retina off the back of the eye; this causes a sudden shower of floaters, flashes, and a shadow
    \item \textbf{Bleeding:} bleeding into the vitreous from diabetes or injury causes many new floaters
    \item \textbf{Inflammation:} uveitis can cause floaters from inflammatory cells in the gel
    \item \textbf{Other causes:} eye injury, and rarely tumours of the eye
    \item \textbf{Why they move:} floaters float in the gel and lag behind eye movements, then drift back
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Small moving spots, threads, or cobwebs, most visible against a plain bright background such as the sky or a white wall
    \item Floaters that follow eye movements and drift when the eye stops
    \item Usually harmless: present for years, stable in number, and familiar
    \item Alarm features: a sudden shower of many new floaters, like a burst of flies
    \item Flashes of light at the edge of vision, especially in a dark room
    \item A curtain or shadow spreading across the vision
    \item A sudden drop in vision
    \item These alarm features suggest retinal tear or detachment and need immediate assessment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
New or worsening floaters must be distinguished from their causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Posterior vitreous detachment, the common harmless cause
    \item Retinal tear and retinal detachment, which are emergencies
    \item Vitreous haemorrhage from diabetes, injury, or other causes
    \item Uveitis (intraocular inflammation), often with pain, redness, and light sensitivity
    \item Migraine aura, which produces visual disturbances that resolve in about an hour
    \item Optic nerve and brain causes of visual disturbance
    \item The examination by an eye specialist distinguishes these reliably
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Existing, stable floaters need no treatment, but anyone who notices a sudden increase in floaters, flashes of light, a shadow in the vision, or loss of vision should be examined urgently by an eye specialist, usually within hours.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden loss of vision or a curtain over part of the vision
    \item A sudden shower of new floaters with flashes
    \item Severe eye pain, redness, or headache with visual change
    \item Floaters and flashes after eye injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} onset, number, duration, and the presence of flashes, shadows, or vision loss
    \item \textbf{Eye examination:} vision testing, and examination of the retina with a dilated pupil using an ophthalmoscope
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound of the eye:} when the vitreous is cloudy with blood and the retina cannot be seen directly
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} sometimes an OCT scan to examine the retina in detail
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to an ophthalmologist for assessment of new or alarming floaters
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reassurance:} most floaters require no treatment and become less noticeable over time as the brain learns to ignore them
    \item \textbf{Observation:} the eye is re-examined to confirm the retina is intact, with advice to return urgently if new symptoms develop
    \item \textbf{Retinal tear:} treated promptly with laser or freezing therapy to seal the tear and prevent detachment
    \item \textbf{Retinal detachment:} surgery, either laser and gas injection or scleral buckle and vitrectomy, to reattach the retina and preserve vision
    \item \textbf{Treat the cause:} diabetes control for vitreous bleeding, and anti-inflammatory treatment for uveitis
    \item \textbf{Symptom management:} moving the eyes to shift floaters out of the line of sight
    \item \textbf{Vitrectomy:} removal of the vitreous for severe, persistent floaters is possible but is reserved for the minority in whom floaters seriously affect vision
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Retinal detachment with permanent vision loss when tears are not treated in time
    \item Reduced vision from untreated vitreous haemorrhage or uveitis
    \item Anxiety about the floaters and their meaning
    \item Complications of retinal surgery, including infection, cataract, and elevated eye pressure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most floaters are harmless and either persist unnoticed or become less bothersome with time. When floaters signal a retinal tear, prompt laser treatment prevents detachment in most cases, and even a detached retina is successfully reattached in the large majority of people when treated urgently, though the timing matters: the longer the delay, the greater the risk of permanent vision loss. Anyone with new flashes, a shower of floaters, or a shadow should be assessed the same day, because early treatment is the difference between a good and a poor visual outcome.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Have regular eye examinations, especially over 50 or with myopia, diabetes, or a family history of detachment
    \item Do not ignore a sudden increase in floaters or new flashes; seek same-day assessment
    \item Seek urgent review after any eye injury
    \item Control blood sugar and blood pressure to protect the retina
    \item Wear eye protection for sport and hazardous work
    \item Once reassured by a specialist that floaters are harmless, let the brain adapt and avoid repeatedly searching for them
    \item Return promptly if floaters change after assessment
\end{itemize}
