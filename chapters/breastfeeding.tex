\chapter{Breastfeeding}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Breastfeeding is the feeding of an infant with milk directly from the mother's breast. The World Health Organization recommends exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months of life, with continued breastfeeding alongside appropriate complementary foods for at least one to two years or for as long as mother and baby both wish. Breast milk is uniquely adapted to the human infant, providing complete nutrition in the early months along with antibodies, growth factors, and immune cells that protect against infection. Although breastfeeding is a natural biological process, it is also a learned skill that commonly requires practice, patience, and practical support. Difficulties such as painful latch, sore nipples, engorgement, and concerns about milk supply are very common but are usually correctable. Support is available from midwives, lactation consultants, general practitioners, and child and family health nurses.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Most babies receive some breast milk in the first days after birth, but rates of exclusive and continued breastfeeding fall progressively through the early months. In Australia, more than nine in ten babies are breastfed initially, yet fewer than half remain exclusively breastfed at around four months of age, and rates decline further by six months. Breastfeeding duration is generally longer among older mothers, women with more education, and those who receive skilled antenatal and postnatal support. Common reasons for stopping earlier than intended include difficulties with latch, sore nipples, perceived low milk supply, maternal illness, returning to work, and a lack of practical help. Almost all women are physically capable of breastfeeding; genuine inability to produce sufficient milk is uncommon, and most early weaning is driven by modifiable problems.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Breastfeeding problems arise from the interaction of milk production, milk removal, and the health of mother and baby.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lactation:} the hormone prolactin stimulates milk production in response to nipple and breast stimulation, while oxytocin triggers the let-down reflex that releases milk into the ducts
    \item \textbf{Supply and demand:} milk supply is driven by removal; frequent, effective feeding maintains production, whereas infrequent or inefficient emptying reduces it
    \item \textbf{Latch and positioning:} a deep, comfortable latch is essential for efficient milk transfer; a shallow or painful latch causes sore nipples and poor emptying
    \item \textbf{Engorgement:} early breast fullness from milk and tissue oedema, which can make latching difficult and set up a cycle of poor drainage
    \item \textbf{Milk stasis and mastitis:} blocked ducts and inflamed breast tissue, usually complicated by infection, develop when milk is not removed effectively
    \item \textbf{Low milk supply:} may follow infrequent feeding, poor latch, tongue-tie, maternal hormonal disorders, certain medications, or previous breast surgery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The healthy breastfeeding dyad shows a settled baby feeding on demand, six or more wet nappies in 24 hours, regular stool passage, and steady weight gain. Problems present with recognisable maternal and infant features.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sore or cracked nipples:} pain on latching that persists beyond the first moments of a feed, with visible fissures, bleeding, or crusting
    \item \textbf{Engorgement:} firm, tender, swollen breasts in the first days, sometimes with the areola too tense for a good latch
    \item \textbf{Plugged duct:} a localised, tender lump in one area of the breast that resolves with feeding
    \item \textbf{Mastitis:} a wedge-shaped area of redness, heat, and pain with fever, chills, and a generally unwell feeling
    \item \textbf{Thrush (candida):} burning nipple pain between and during feeds, with shiny, flaky, or itchy skin and sometimes white plaques in the baby's mouth
    \item \textbf{Signs of poor intake in the baby:} fewer wet or dirty nappies than expected, lethargy, poor feeding, a sunken fontanelle, and inadequate weight gain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sore nipples:} poor latch and positioning versus thrush, nipple vasospasm, eczema, or contact dermatitis from creams or breast pads
    \item \textbf{Painful, red breast:} engorgement versus plugged duct versus mastitis versus breast abscess, with inflammatory breast cancer a rare but serious alternative
    \item \textbf{Low milk supply:} perceived low supply (the baby is actually feeding well) versus true low production versus poor transfer due to tongue-tie or a weak suck
    \item \textbf{Slow infant weight gain:} inadequate milk intake versus an underlying illness or metabolic problem in the baby
    \item \textbf{Frequent, unsettled feeding:} normal cluster feeding during growth spurts versus insufficient milk transfer or an unwell baby
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek help early from a midwife, lactation consultant, or doctor if breastfeeding is persistently painful, if the baby is not feeding effectively or seems unsettled after feeds, or if there is any concern about the baby's intake or weight. Prompt attention prevents small problems from becoming entrenched.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A baby who is floppy, unusually drowsy, or difficult to wake
    \item A baby with no wet nappy for around six hours, or a marked reduction in wet or dirty nappies
    \item A baby with a sunken fontanelle, a dry mouth, or a fever
    \item High fever, chills, and a hot, painful breast suggesting severe mastitis or sepsis
    \item Any baby who appears blue, is struggling to breathe, or has a seizure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Maternal history:} feeding patterns, nappy counts, breast symptoms, nipple pain, medications, previous breast surgery, and general health
    \item \textbf{Infant history:} birth, weight trajectory, stool and urine output, and any signs of illness
    \item \textbf{Observed feed:} assessment of latch, positioning, and swallowing rhythm by a trained health professional
    \item \textbf{Serial weighing:} regular weight checks to confirm adequate growth
    \item \textbf{Breast examination:} inspection for redness, lumps, cracked nipples, or features of thrush
    \item \textbf{Infant examination:} checking the mouth for tongue-tie or thrush and assessing general condition
    \item \textbf{Targeted tests:} rarely, breast ultrasound for a suspected abscess, or microbiology swabs when infection does not respond to treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Positioning and latch:} skilled guidance from a midwife or lactation consultant on deep, comfortable attachment, and adjusting positions to drain all areas of the breast
    \item \textbf{Feeding on demand:} feeding whenever the baby shows hunger cues, commonly 8--12 times in 24 hours in the early weeks
    \item \textbf{Engorgement:} frequent feeding, expressing a little milk before latching, cool compresses between feeds, and gentle breast massage
    \item \textbf{Sore nipples:} correcting latch, varying feeding positions, using expressed milk or lanolin-based cream, and nipple shields only when recommended
    \item \textbf{Plugged duct and mastitis:} continue feeding on the affected side, warm compresses before feeds, massage towards the nipple, simple analgesia, and antibiotics for mastitis
    \item \textbf{Thrush:} antifungal treatment for both mother and baby to prevent reinfection
    \item \textbf{Low supply:} frequent effective feeding, skin-to-skin contact, expressing after feeds, and investigation of contributing causes
    \item \textbf{Expressing milk:} hand or pump expression to maintain supply when separated from the baby, or to allow another carer to feed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sore, cracked, or bleeding nipples:} very common, usually resolving with better latch and positioning
    \item \textbf{Engorgement:} temporary but can hamper feeding and predispose to blocked ducts if not managed
    \item \textbf{Mastitis:} painful breast inflammation with fever that, if untreated, may progress to a breast abscess requiring drainage
    \item \textbf{Thrush:} persistent pain that can threaten continued breastfeeding without treatment
    \item \textbf{Poor infant weight gain:} from low milk transfer or supply, requiring early recognition and intervention
    \item \textbf{Dehydration and jaundice:} consequences of insufficient milk intake in the first days of life
    \item \textbf{Recurrent blocked ducts:} more likely with infrequent, interrupted, or poorly positioned feeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most breastfeeding difficulties resolve within days to a few weeks once the underlying cause is addressed. Sore nipples and engorgement usually improve rapidly with corrected latch and positioning, and mastitis typically settles within 48--72 hours of starting antibiotics while continuing to feed. With skilled support, most women who wish to breastfeed achieve their goals, and the benefits to mother and baby are greatest when breastfeeding continues for at least the first six months, although even partial breastfeeding confers advantages. Some women stop earlier than planned despite support; this is a personal decision, and a formula-fed baby thrives with responsive, loving care.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend antenatal breastfeeding classes and learn about positioning, latch, and feeding cues before the birth
    \item Practise early and frequent skin-to-skin contact, and begin feeding as soon as possible after birth
    \item Feed on demand and avoid dummies or supplements until breastfeeding is well established
    \item Seek help early for pain or latch problems rather than struggling on
    \item Eat well, stay hydrated, and rest whenever possible
    \item Keep nipples clean and dry, change breast pads regularly, and wash hands before feeds
    \item Avoid smoking and limit alcohol; check all medications with a doctor or pharmacist for safety in breastfeeding
    \item Build a support network of family, midwives, lactation consultants, and child health nurses
\end{itemize}
