\chapter{Blood Clots}
\index{Blood Clots}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A blood clot (thrombus) is a clump of blood that forms inside a vein or artery. Clotting is a normal and life-saving process that stops bleeding after injury, but it becomes a problem when a clot forms in an intact vessel or breaks loose and travels through the bloodstream. A clot that blocks a deep vein, usually in the leg, is called a deep vein thrombosis (DVT); one that travels to the lungs is a pulmonary embolism (PE). A clot blocking an artery supplying the heart or brain causes a heart attack or stroke. Together these are known as thromboembolic disease.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Blood clots are both common and serious. Venous thromboembolism (DVT and PE together) affects roughly one in a thousand adults each year and becomes much more common with age. It is more likely during and after surgery, with prolonged immobility, in pregnancy, in cancer, and in people who smoke or are overweight. Arterial clots, in the form of heart attacks and strokes, are among the world's leading causes of death. Many people who develop a clot have no obvious risk factor, and early recognition and treatment greatly reduce deaths.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Clots tend to form when three conditions come together, a combination known as Virchow's triad:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Slow blood flow:} immobility, long-haul travel, bed rest, plaster casts, and pregnancy slow the blood, particularly in the legs
    \item \textbf{Injury to the vessel wall:} surgery, trauma, and inflammation damage the lining of veins and arteries, exposing triggers for clotting
    \item \textbf{Blood that clots too easily:} cancer, the combined oral contraceptive and hormone therapy, inherited clotting disorders, obesity, dehydration, and smoking
    \item \textbf{In arteries:} the usual trigger is a ruptured fatty plaque (atherosclerosis) on which a clot forms, causing a heart attack or stroke
    \item \textbf{In veins:} a clot may detach and travel to the lungs as a pulmonary embolism
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms depend on where the clot has formed or travelled:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Deep vein thrombosis:} swelling, pain, tenderness, warmth, and redness of one calf or thigh
    \item \textbf{Pulmonary embolism:} sudden breathlessness, chest pain that worsens on breathing, coughing up blood, and a fast heart rate
    \item \textbf{Heart attack:} crushing central chest pain, sweating, nausea, and breathlessness
    \item \textbf{Stroke:} sudden weakness or numbness of one side, facial drooping, and difficulty speaking
    \item \textbf{Superficial clots:} a tender, red, warm cord under the skin (superficial thrombophlebitis)
    \item Some clots, particularly small ones, cause no symptoms at all and are found only on scans performed for other reasons
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The clinician distinguishes blood clots from the conditions that mimic them:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A swollen calf: DVT, muscle strain or tear, a Baker's cyst, or cellulitis
    \item Chest pain and breathlessness: pulmonary embolism, pneumonia, heart attack, pleurisy, or anxiety
    \item Leg pain on walking: peripheral arterial disease, spinal stenosis, or muscle problems
    \item Sudden weakness: stroke, migraine, low blood sugar, or a seizure
    \item Chest pain: gastro-oesophageal reflux, musculoskeletal pain, or pericarditis
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek urgent medical attention for any unexplained swelling of one leg, sudden breathlessness, or new chest pain.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden breathlessness or difficulty breathing
    \item Chest pain, especially if worse on breathing, or crushing in nature and radiating to the arm or jaw
    \item Coughing up blood
    \item Sudden weakness, numbness, facial drooping, or difficulty speaking
    \item Collapse or loss of consciousness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis is made by combining clinical suspicion with targeted tests:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} assessment of risk factors such as recent surgery, long travel, pregnancy, or the combined pill
    \item \textbf{D-dimer blood test:} a negative result helps rule out a clot when combined with a low-probability clinical picture
    \item \textbf{Compression ultrasound:} the standard test for a DVT, which examines leg veins for blockage
    \item \textbf{CT pulmonary angiography:} a CT scan of the chest for a suspected pulmonary embolism
    \item \textbf{ECG and cardiac blood tests:} for a suspected heart attack
    \item \textbf{Brain imaging:} CT or MRI for a suspected stroke
    \item \textbf{Thrombophilia testing:} checking for inherited clotting disorders in selected people, particularly the young or those with recurrent clots
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment aims to stop the clot growing, prevent new clots, and reduce the risk of recurrence:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anticoagulants:} blood-thinning medicines (heparin, warfarin, or the newer direct oral anticoagulants) prevent new clots from forming
    \item \textbf{Thrombolytic drugs:} clot-dissolving agents used in severe or life-threatening clots
    \item \textbf{Compression stockings:} reduce swelling and the risk of post-thrombotic syndrome after a DVT
    \item \textbf{Procedures:} occasionally a clot is removed directly, or a filter is placed in the large vein to catch travelling clots
    \item \textbf{Treating the cause:} addressing cancer, stopping the contraceptive pill, or managing other triggers
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} pulmonary embolism and stroke are treated in hospital, sometimes in intensive care
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} anticoagulant treatment usually continues for several months, with monitoring for some medicines
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pulmonary embolism, the most dangerous complication of a leg clot and often its first sign
    \item Post-thrombotic syndrome: chronic leg swelling, pain, and skin changes after a DVT
    \item Long-term breathlessness and reduced exercise capacity after a large pulmonary embolism
    \item Bleeding as a side effect of anticoagulant treatment
    \item Heart damage and heart failure after a major heart attack
    \item Permanent disability after a stroke
    \item Recurrent clots if risk factors are not addressed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt treatment, most people with a blood clot recover well. Pulmonary embolism is fatal in a minority of cases, but survival is good when treatment begins early. After a DVT, most people improve within weeks, although some have lasting leg swelling. Anticoagulant treatment dramatically reduces the risk of recurrence, and most people stop it after a few months unless the cause persists. The long-term outlook after a heart attack or stroke depends on the extent of damage and on control of underlying risk factors.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep moving: walk regularly, and take breaks to stand and stretch during long travel or desk work
    \item Stay well hydrated
    \item Move and exercise soon after surgery or illness, as advised by your care team
    \item Wear compression stockings during long-haul travel if you are at increased risk
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and do not smoke
    \item Discuss the risks of the pill, hormone therapy, and pregnancy with your doctor if you have other risk factors
    \item Know the warning signs, and seek urgent care for a swollen leg, sudden breathlessness, or chest pain
    \item Take anticoagulants exactly as prescribed, and report any unusual bleeding
\end{itemize}
