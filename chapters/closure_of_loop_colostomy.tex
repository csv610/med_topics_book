\chapter{Closure of loop colostomy}
 
\section*{Definition and Overview}
Closure of a loop colostomy, commonly known as colostomy reversal, is a surgical procedure performed to restore intestinal continuity and close a temporary colostomy stoma. A loop colostomy is typically created during an initial abdominal surgery to divert faecal flow away from a newly formed rectal or colonic anastomosis, or an area of distal bowel inflammation, to allow it to heal safely. Once the downstream bowel has healed and is confirmed to be patent and free of leaks, the temporary colostomy is no longer needed. The reversal surgery involves mobilising the loop of colon from the surrounding abdominal wall, closing the opening in the bowel (either by primary suture repair or resection and anastomosis), and returning the colon to the abdominal cavity before repairing the abdominal wall.
 
\section*{Epidemiology}
Colostomy creation and subsequent closure are common procedures in colorectal surgery. Reversal is typically planned 3 to 6 months after the initial stoma creation, depending on the patient's recovery, nutritional status, and any ongoing adjuvant treatments like chemotherapy. The majority of patients undergoing this procedure have had initial surgeries for rectal or colon cancer, diverticulitis, inflammatory bowel disease, or trauma. Complication rates after closure of a loop colostomy are relatively high, with overall morbidity ranging from 10\% to 30\%, which requires careful patient selection and preoperative evaluation.
 
\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The indication for closing a loop colostomy is the resolution of the clinical condition that required bowel diversion:
 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Healed distal anastomosis:} confirmation that the downstream colonic or colorectal join is fully healed without leak, stricture, or active inflammation.
    \item \textbf{Anatomic patency:} ensuring there is no obstruction in the distal bowel that would prevent normal passage of stool after closure.
    \item \textbf{Physiological recovery:} the patient has recovered from their primary illness, has adequate nutritional status, and does not have active pelvic sepsis.
    \item \textbf{Surgical approach:} the stoma is dissected free from the skin, subcutaneous fat, and rectus sheath (mobilisation). The loop is then closed using sutures or surgical staples, or if the bowel ends are damaged, a segmental resection is performed followed by hand-sewn or stapled anastomosis.
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{Clinical Features}
Postoperative features after a colostomy reversal transition from immediate recovery to the return of normal bowel function:
 
\begin{itemize}
    \item Return of flatus (gas) and bowel movements, which typically occurs within 2 to 5 days after surgery
    \item Mild to moderate abdominal pain and cramping, controlled with appropriate analgesia
    \item Local wound pain, swelling, and bruising around the site of the closed stoma
    \item Temporary alterations in bowel habit, including frequent loose stools, urgency, or mild incontinence as the colon adjusts to the restoration of stool transit
    \item Low-grade fever, which is common in the early postoperative period but requires monitoring
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Postoperative issues (like delayed bowel function or pain) must be differentiated from other complications:
 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Postoperative ileus:} a common, temporary suspension of bowel motility, presenting with abdominal distension, nausea, and vomiting, but without mechanical blockage.
    \item \textbf{Mechanical bowel obstruction:} caused by early adhesions, kinking of the anastomosed bowel, or stricture at the closure site, presenting with severe crampy pain and vomiting.
    \item \textbf{Anatomotic leak:} a serious complication presenting with severe abdominal pain, high fever, tachycardia, and signs of peritonitis.
    \item \textbf{Wound infection:} characterised by worsening local pain, redness, warmth, and purulent discharge at the stoma closure site.
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients recovering from a colostomy reversal should monitor their wounds and bowel function closely.
 
\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe, or worsening abdominal pain
    \item Signs of shock: severe dizziness, confusion, rapid heart rate, or cold, clammy skin
    \item Vomiting blood or passing large amounts of fresh blood or black stools
    \item Difficulty breathing or chest pain
    \item High fever (above 38.5\textdegree{}C) combined with chills or shaking
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Preoperative assessment:} essential to check the distal bowel before surgery. This includes a contrast enema (using water-soluble contrast) or a flexible sigmoidoscopy to confirm that the downstream join is healed and patent.
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} postoperative monitoring of vital signs, abdominal tenderness, and the surgical wound for infection or fluid collection.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count (to check for elevated white blood cells suggesting infection) and electrolytes (to monitor hydration status).
    \item \textbf{CT scan of the abdomen:} if postoperative complications like an anastomotic leak, collection, or mechanical obstruction are suspected.
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Preoperative optimization:} ensuring the patient is nutritionally sound and stopping any medications that increase bleeding or infection risk where possible.
    \item \textbf{Intraoperative closure:} typically performed via a peristomal incision (local approach), avoiding a full laparotomy if possible. The bowel is closed or resected, and the skin is closed (often using a purse-string suture technique, which reduces wound infection rates).
    \item \textbf{Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS):} protocols focusing on early mobilisation, early introduction of oral fluids and diet, and minimizing intestinal spasms.
    \item \textbf{Wound care:} monitoring the incision, keeping it clean and dry, and managing dressings.
    \item \textbf{Dietary adjustments:} starting with clear fluids, progressing to a low-residue diet, and gradually reintroducing high-fibre foods as tolerated.
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Surgical site infection (SSI) at the stoma closure site, which is the most common complication
    \item Anastomotic leak or breakdown, leading to peritonitis, pelvic collection, or enterocutaneous fistula
    \item Postoperative ileus or mechanical bowel obstruction
    \item Incisional hernia at the stoma reversal site
    \item Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis after closure of a loop colostomy is generally very good, with the vast majority of patients successfully restoring bowel function and returning to their normal activities within 4 to 8 weeks. While temporary bowel changes (such as frequency and urgency) are common in the first few weeks, these symptoms typically improve over several months as the colon adapts. Long-term stoma-free status is achieved in over 90\% of cases, significantly improving quality of life.
 
\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Follow post-operative wound care instructions carefully, keeping the area clean and dry, and report any redness or discharge early
    \item Stay well hydrated by drinking plenty of water, especially if experiencing frequent loose bowel movements
    \item Consume a low-residue, soft diet initially, introducing high-fibre foods slowly and one at a time to avoid cramps or blockage
    \item Engage in light physical activity such as short walks, but avoid heavy lifting (greater than 5 kg) or strenuous exercise for 6 weeks to prevent hernia formation
    \item Take prescribed pain relief and stool softeners as directed, avoiding excessive opioid use which can cause constipation
\end{itemize}
