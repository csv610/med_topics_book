\chapter{Age-Related Macular Degeneration (AMD)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a progressive eye condition that damages the macula, the small central area of the retina responsible for sharp, detailed vision used for reading, driving, and recognising faces. It is a leading cause of vision loss in older adults in the developed world.

There are two main forms. The dry (atrophic) form is the most common, affecting roughly 85 to 90 percent of cases, and develops gradually over years as the cells beneath the macula thin and die. The wet (neovascular) form is less common but more aggressive: abnormal blood vessels grow beneath the retina and leak fluid or blood, causing rapid loss of central vision. AMD does not usually cause total blindness, because peripheral (side) vision is generally preserved, but it can seriously affect the ability to read, drive, recognise faces, and live independently.

\section*{Epidemiology}
AMD is the leading cause of vision loss in older people in developed countries. Approximately 1 in 7 people over the age of 50 has early signs of the condition, and the prevalence rises steeply with age, affecting up to about a third of people in their 80s and beyond. It is somewhat more common in women, largely because they live longer.

Smoking approximately doubles to triples the risk of AMD, and a family history of the condition increases susceptibility. High blood pressure, high cholesterol, obesity, and a diet low in leafy green vegetables and fish are additional modifiable risk factors. With ageing populations in most countries, the number of people affected by AMD is expected to continue to grow.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
AMD results from a combination of ageing, genetic susceptibility, and lifestyle factors.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Age:} the strongest risk factor, as the retina and its supporting layer wear down over decades
    \item \textbf{Drusen:} deposits of protein and lipid that accumulate beneath the macula and are the hallmark of early AMD
    \item \textbf{Oxidative stress and inflammation:} cumulative damage to the light-sensing photoreceptors and the underlying retinal pigment epithelium
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} variants in genes such as complement factor H and ARMS2 increase susceptibility, so family history matters
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} smoking, hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia, obesity, and a diet low in antioxidants and omega-3 fatty acids
    \item \textbf{Dry AMD:} progressive thinning and death of the retinal pigment epithelium causes the photoreceptors above it to waste away, producing geographic atrophy
    \item \textbf{Wet AMD:} fragile new blood vessels grow beneath the macula under the stimulus of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and leak fluid or blood, forming scar tissue and destroying central vision rapidly
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms of AMD affect central vision only and often begin gradually, particularly in the dry form.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty reading, especially in dim light, and needing brighter light than before
    \item Blurred or hazy central vision
    \item Straight lines appearing wavy or bent (metamorphopsia) -- a classic early sign
    \item A dark, empty, or grey patch (scotoma) in the centre of vision
    \item Colours appearing less vivid or washed out
    \item Difficulty recognising faces
    \item Slow progression in dry AMD, but sudden or rapid change in wet AMD
    \item Peripheral vision is usually preserved, and AMD does not cause pain or reddening of the eye
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other eye conditions can cause similar visual symptoms and must be distinguished from AMD by examination.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cataracts, which cause blurring and glare but are usually correctable with surgery
    \item Diabetic retinopathy, which causes bleeding and swelling within the retina
    \item Myopic (short-sighted) degeneration, which damages the macula in highly myopic eyes
    \item Central serous retinopathy, which causes fluid beneath the macula, mainly in younger people
    \item Retinal vein occlusion, a sudden blockage of retinal blood flow
    \item Glaucoma, which usually affects peripheral vision first
    \item Inflammation inside the eye (uveitis)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone over 50 should have regular eye examinations, even if they have no symptoms. Seek an urgent eye review, within days, if you notice any new distortion, a sudden change in vision, or a new dark patch in your sight, because wet AMD progresses quickly and early treatment gives the best chance of preserving vision.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) or seek urgent emergency eye care for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, painless loss of vision in one eye -- which can indicate wet AMD, retinal detachment, or a retinal vein occlusion
    \item The appearance of a curtain or shadow across the field of vision
    \item A sudden increase in the number of flashes or floaters
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
AMD is diagnosed by a detailed eye examination, usually performed by an optometrist or ophthalmologist.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visual acuity testing:} measuring how clearly the person can read letters on a chart at a distance
    \item \textbf{Amsler grid:} a simple grid that reveals distortion or missing areas; it can also be used at home to monitor for change
    \item \textbf{Dilated eye examination:} drops widen the pupil so the retina and macula can be inspected for drusen and pigment changes
    \item \textbf{Optical coherence tomography (OCT):} a quick, painless scan that produces a cross-section of the retina and detects fluid in wet AMD
    \item \textbf{Fluorescein angiography:} a dye is injected into a vein and photographed in the eye to identify leaking abnormal blood vessels
    \item \textbf{Genetic and other tests:} not routinely needed in clinical care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
There is no cure for AMD, but treatments can slow the dry form and, in many cases, preserve or improve vision in the wet form.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dry AMD:} no specific medical treatment exists, but monitoring, lifestyle changes, and low-vision support are important
    \item \textbf{Anti-VEGF injections:} for wet AMD, medicines such as ranibizumab, aflibercept, and bevacizumab are injected into the eye to block the growth and leaking of abnormal vessels; regular injections are usually required on an ongoing basis
    \item \textbf{Photodynamic therapy:} a light-activated treatment that is now used less commonly than anti-VEGF injections
    \item \textbf{Antioxidant supplements:} the AREDS2 formula (vitamin C, vitamin E, zinc, copper, lutein, and zeaxanthin) may slow progression in intermediate dry AMD; it should be discussed with a doctor first
    \item \textbf{Low-vision services:} magnifiers, large-print aids, reading machines, and occupational therapy help people use their remaining vision effectively
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular eye reviews and home Amsler grid testing to catch change early
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive loss of central vision, and the possibility of transition from dry to wet AMD
    \item Depression, anxiety, and social isolation related to vision loss
    \item Falls and injuries, and reduced independence in daily living
    \item Loss of the ability to drive, which affects mobility and quality of life
    \item The burden of repeated eye injections, which carry a small risk of infection, bleeding, or retinal damage
    \item Rarely, severe vision loss in both eyes, though total blindness is very unusual
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The course of AMD varies considerably between individuals. Dry AMD typically progresses slowly, often over many years, and many people retain useful reading vision for a long time. Wet AMD, without treatment, can destroy central vision within months; with prompt anti-VEGF injections, many people stabilise and some improve. Treatment is ongoing rather than one-off, and vision that has already been lost is usually permanent. Low-vision aids and rehabilitation, however, significantly improve daily function, and early detection remains the single most important factor in preserving sight.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Do not smoke:} avoid smoking and exposure to second-hand smoke
    \item \textbf{Diet:} eat a diet rich in leafy green vegetables, fruit, fish, and nuts
    \item \textbf{Health:} maintain a healthy weight and control blood pressure and cholesterol
    \item \textbf{Sun protection:} wear sunglasses and a hat in bright sunlight
    \item \textbf{Screening:} have regular eye examinations from age 50
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} test your vision at home with an Amsler grid and report any changes promptly
    \item \textbf{Supplements:} if you have intermediate AMD, discuss AREDS2 supplements with your doctor
    \item \textbf{Act early:} seek treatment without delay if you notice sudden change, because time matters in wet AMD
\end{itemize}
