\chapter{Boils}
\index{Boils}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A boil, or furuncle, is a painful, pus-filled swelling of the skin caused by infection of a hair follicle and the surrounding tissue, most commonly by the bacterium \textit{Staphylococcus aureus}. It begins as a tender red lump and enlarges over several days as pus collects, eventually developing a whitish or yellow "head" that may discharge. A carbuncle is a larger, deeper collection of boils joined under the skin, typically on the back of the neck, shoulders, or thighs, and it is associated with more marked illness. Boils can occur anywhere on the body but favour the face, neck, armpits, buttocks, and thighs. Most small boils resolve with home care, but large, facial, recurrent, or multiple boils require medical assessment and treatment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Boils are common at all ages but occur most often in adolescents and young adults, and more frequently in men than in women. They are more prevalent in hot, humid climates and in circumstances involving friction, sweating, or minor skin injury, such as tight clothing, shaving, and contact sport. Individuals who are overweight, have poorly controlled diabetes, or have skin conditions such as eczema are at increased risk, as are people with impaired immunity and those who carry \textit{Staphylococcus aureus} in the nose or on the skin. Recurrent boils affect a significant minority of people, often in families in which staphylococcal carriage is common, and the problem tends to persist unless carriage is addressed.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bacterial infection:} \textit{Staphylococcus aureus} causes the great majority of boils; the organism colonises the nose and skin of up to a third of healthy people but causes infection when it breaches the skin barrier.
    \item \textbf{Skin injury or friction:} shaving, rubbing, tight clothing, and minor cuts or insect bites create entry points for bacteria.
    \item \textbf{Sweating and occlusion:} moist, macerated skin and blocked hair follicles favour bacterial multiplication.
    \item \textbf{Reduced immunity:} diabetes, malnutrition, HIV infection, kidney disease, and immunosuppressive medicines increase susceptibility and impair clearance.
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} bacteria multiply within the hair follicle, the immune response recruits neutrophils to the site, and the resulting collection of dead cells, bacteria, and fluid forms pus. The surrounding dermis becomes inflamed and necrotic, producing a tender, fluctuant swelling.
    \item \textbf{Methicillin resistance:} a proportion of community staphylococcal isolates are methicillin-resistant (MRSA), which can complicate treatment of recurrent or severe boils.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A small, firm, tender red lump centred on a hair follicle
    \item Progressive enlargement and central pus formation over several days
    \item A whitish or yellow "head" that may rupture and drain spontaneously
    \item Throbbing pain that increases as the boil distends
    \item Surrounding erythema, warmth, and induration
    \item Mild fever, fatigue, or malaise with larger or multiple boils
    \item A carbuncle presenting as a large, deep, painful mass with several draining points and often constitutional symptoms
    \item Facial boils, particularly near the nose or upper lip, cause marked local swelling and pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Folliculitis:} superficial inflammation of hair follicles without a large pus collection.
    \item \textbf{Infective cyst:} an epidermoid or pilar cyst that has become secondarily infected and inflamed.
    \item \textbf{Hidradenitis suppurativa:} a chronic inflammatory condition producing recurrent boil-like lumps in the armpits, groin, and under the breasts.
    \item \textbf{Skin abscess:} a deeper collection of pus not necessarily related to a hair follicle.
    \item \textbf{Cellulitis:} spreading infection of the deeper skin without a discrete pus core.
    \item \textbf{Insect bite or sting reaction:} localised swelling and itch without follicular origin.
    \item \textbf{Foreign body reaction:} a localised inflammatory lump around a splinter or other retained object.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most small boils can be managed at home, but medical review is needed if the boil is very large or painful, is on the face or spine, does not come to a head within about a week, or is accompanied by fever or spreading redness. Medical assessment is also important for people with diabetes, weakened immunity, recurrent boils, or multiple boils at once, and for those who feel generally unwell.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rapidly spreading redness, red streaks, or swelling around the boil
    \item Fever with chills, or feeling very unwell
    \item A facial boil near the nose or eye causing significant swelling
    \item Severe pain that is not controlled by simple analgesia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the site and duration of the lump, previous boils, diabetes, immune problems, and affected family members.
    \item \textbf{Examination:} confirmation that the lesion is a boil, and assessment for surrounding cellulitis, fluctuance, and multiple or deeper collections.
    \item \textbf{Wound swab:} a swab of drained pus or the discharging surface for culture and antibiotic susceptibility testing, especially in recurrent or severe cases.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} blood glucose or HbA1c to screen for diabetes, and inflammatory markers if systemic infection is suspected.
    \item \textbf{Carriage screening:} nose and skin swabs to detect \textit{Staphylococcus aureus} carriage when boils are recurrent.
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} ultrasound can be used to assess a deep abscess and to determine whether pus is ready for drainage.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Warm compresses:} a warm, moist cloth applied for about 10 minutes three or four times a day encourages the boil to come to a head and drain spontaneously.
    \item \textbf{Hygiene:} keep the area clean with soap and water, cover it with a dry dressing, and wash hands after touching the site.
    \item \textbf{Do not squeeze or pierce:} squeezing pushes infection deeper into the tissue and spreads bacteria to other areas.
    \item \textbf{Analgesia:} simple pain relief such as paracetamol or ibuprofen controls discomfort.
    \item \textbf{Incision and drainage:} once pus has formed, lancing under sterile conditions is the most effective treatment; it relieves pain and speeds resolution.
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} not routinely required for simple boils, but indicated for large or multiple boils, surrounding cellulitis, facial boils, systemic symptoms, or immunocompromised patients.
    \item \textbf{Carbuncles:} need incision and drainage, antibiotics, and investigation for underlying causes.
    \item \textbf{Recurrent boils:} managed with hygiene measures, decolonisation (nasal mupirocin and antibacterial washes), and treatment of predisposing conditions.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cellulitis -- extension of infection into the deeper skin and soft tissues
    \item Abscess formation requiring surgical drainage
    \item Scarring, including permanent marks at healed sites
    \item Recurrent boils, usually from persistent nasal or skin carriage of staphylococci
    \item Bacteraemia -- spread of bacteria into the bloodstream -- which in severe cases can seed the heart, bones, or joints
    \item Cavernous sinus thrombosis, a rare but life-threatening complication of facial boils, especially those near the nose
    \item Septicaemia in immunocompromised or diabetic patients, who also heal more slowly
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most simple boils resolve within one to two weeks, often draining spontaneously once they come to a head, and surgical drainage speeds this process. Scarring is usually minimal when the boil is not squeezed or self-punctured. Serious complications are uncommon in otherwise healthy people, but facial boils, carbuncles, and boils in diabetic or immunocompromised patients behave more aggressively and warrant early treatment. People with recurrent boils may need carriage-reduction strategies, after which many improve considerably. Early medical attention for large, facial, or recurrent lesions reduces the risk of complications and speeds recovery.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wash regularly, especially after sweating and exercise, and pay attention to skin creases and areas of friction.
    \item Keep cuts, scratches, and insect bites clean and covered until healed.
    \item Avoid tight clothing, and shave carefully to avoid nicking the skin.
    \item Do not pick at or squeeze boils, pimples, or inflamed follicles.
    \item Wash hands frequently and avoid sharing towels, razors, and clothing.
    \item On medical advice, use an antiseptic body wash and nasal ointment if you are prone to recurrent boils.
    \item Maintain good blood sugar control if you have diabetes.
    \item See a doctor early if a boil is large, facial, multiple, or failing to improve.
\end{itemize}
