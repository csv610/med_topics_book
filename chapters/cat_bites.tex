\chapter{Cat Bites}
\index{Cat Bites}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A cat bite is a puncture wound caused by the teeth of a cat, usually to the hands, arms, or legs. Cat teeth are long, narrow, and sharp, so they often drive bacteria deep into the skin, tendons, joints, and bone beneath a small-looking surface wound. Because the opening on the skin is tiny and closes over quickly, infection is trapped below the surface, which is why cat bites cause serious infections far more often than their appearance suggests.

Most cat bites occur during handling, play, or attempts to restrain a frightened or injured animal, and household pets account for the majority of cases. Although the wound itself is usually minor, the risk of deep infection makes prompt cleaning and early medical review important, especially for bites over the hand or near a joint.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cat bites are common, particularly among children, people who work with animals, and households that keep cats. They are less frequent than dog bites but cause infection more often: studies estimate that one in three to one in five cat bites becomes infected, compared with roughly one in ten dog bites. Bites to the hand are the most common and carry the highest risk of complications because the hand has relatively little soft tissue and is rich in tendons, joints, and spaces where bacteria can spread.

Infections tend to develop quickly, often within 12--24 hours of the bite, which is earlier than the typical delay seen with dog bites. Children are bitten most often around the head, face, and hands, while adults are bitten most often on the hands and arms.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The normal mouth of a cat contains a wide range of bacteria, and a bite introduces these organisms deep into the tissues. The key players are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pasteurella multocida:} a bacterium carried in most cats' mouths and the most common cause of rapid, severe cat bite infections
    \item \textbf{Streptococcus and Staphylococcus species:} including Staphylococcus aureus, which can cause cellulitis and abscesses
    \item \textbf{Anaerobic bacteria:} organisms that thrive in the oxygen-poor depths of a puncture wound
    \item \textbf{Bartonella henselae:} the cause of cat scratch disease, transmitted by scratches and bites
    \item \textbf{Capnocytophaga canimorsus:} a rare but serious organism in immunocompromised people
    \item \textbf{Rabies and tetanus:} very rare in developed countries, but a consideration with unvaccinated or stray animals
\end{itemize}

Because the puncture seals quickly, bacteria are sealed inside the wound, where they multiply in the warm, moist environment. The resulting inflammation can spread along tendon sheaths and joint spaces, and severe cases can destroy tissue, damage joints, and in the worst scenario lead to bloodstream infection (sepsis).

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Small puncture wound, often appearing as a pair of tiny holes, frequently on the hand or forearm
    \item Pain that is often out of proportion to the size of the wound
    \item Redness, swelling, and warmth around the bite, developing within hours
    \item Increasing tenderness and throbbing over the first 12--24 hours
    \item Discharge of pus or fluid from the wound
    \item Swelling or pain on moving an affected finger or joint, suggesting deeper involvement
    \item Numbness or tingling if a nerve is affected
    \item Fever, chills, or feeling generally unwell in spreading infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Superficial infection (cellulitis)} limited to the skin and immediately surrounding tissues
    \item \textbf{Deep space infection:} spread into the tendon sheath, joint, or bone, which is more serious
    \item \textbf{Septic arthritis:} infection within a joint, causing severe pain on movement
    \item \textbf{Osteomyelitis:} infection of the bone beneath the bite
    \item \textbf{Tendon or nerve injury:} damage from the tooth itself even without infection
    \item \textbf{Cat scratch disease:} a related infection that typically produces swollen lymph nodes and fever days after a scratch or bite
    \item \textbf{Allergic or irritant reaction} to the injury rather than infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Clean the wound thoroughly with soap and running water immediately, apply gentle pressure to stop any bleeding, and see a doctor if the bite is deep, on the hand or over a joint, if the skin is badly torn, or if you are unsure about tetanus vaccination.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:} signs of spreading or severe infection -- spreading redness, severe pain, swelling, fever and chills, numbness, difficulty moving the hand, or if you feel generally unwell -- as well as any bite from a wild, stray, or rabid animal.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} details of the bite, including when it occurred, the animal involved, and whether it was provoked
    \item \textbf{Examination:} assessment of the wound, checking for depth, joint or tendon involvement, nerve function, and signs of infection
    \item \textbf{Tetanus status:} confirmation of up-to-date tetanus vaccination; a booster may be required
    \item \textbf{Wound culture:} samples from infected wounds can identify the bacteria and guide antibiotic choice
    \item \textbf{X-ray or ultrasound:} to look for foreign bodies such as tooth fragments, or evidence of joint or bone involvement
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} a full blood count and inflammatory markers in severe or spreading infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Wound care:} thorough cleaning with saline, removal of dead tissue, and leaving puncture wounds open rather than suturing them closed
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} most cat bites are treated with oral antibiotics (such as co-amoxiclav) because infection risk is high; intravenous antibiotics are used for severe or established infection
    \item \textbf{Prophylactic antibiotics:} often prescribed even for apparently clean bites to prevent the common Pasteurella infections
    \item \textbf{Tetanus vaccination:} a booster if it has been more than five to ten years
    \item \textbf{Rabies vaccination:} considered after bites from wild or unvaccinated animals in at-risk regions
    \item \textbf{Elevation and rest:} elevating the affected limb reduces swelling and helps control infection
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} drainage of abscesses, washout of infected joints or tendon sheaths, and occasionally removal of infected tissue
    \item \textbf{Review:} follow-up within 24--48 hours is advised because infections can develop quickly
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cellulitis, abscess, and deep soft-tissue infection
    \item Septic arthritis and permanent joint damage, particularly in the hand
    \item Osteomyelitis (bone infection), which may require prolonged antibiotics or surgery
    \item Tendon damage and loss of hand function
    \item Cat scratch disease, with fever, swollen lymph nodes, and, rarely, more widespread illness
    \item Scarring and nerve damage
    \item Rarely, bloodstream infection (sepsis), which can be life-threatening, particularly in people with weakened immune systems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most cat bites heal well with prompt cleaning and early antibiotics, and the majority of people make a full recovery. Bites to the hand carry the highest risk of complications, and delay in treatment is the main factor associated with poor outcomes. With early review, established infections usually settle with antibiotics and drainage, although severe cases can cause lasting stiffness, joint damage, or reduced hand function. Seeking care early, rather than waiting to see whether the wound becomes infected, gives the best result.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Avoid rough play and do not provoke or corner cats, especially unfamiliar or frightened animals
    \item Handle cats gently and learn to read signs of agitation, such as flattened ears, hissing, or a flicking tail
    \item Teach children not to approach or disturb eating, sleeping, or nursing animals
    \item Wash any bite or scratch immediately with soap and running water for several minutes
    \item Keep tetanus vaccination up to date
    \item See a doctor for any bite on the hand, face, or near a joint, even if it looks minor
    \item Wear protective gloves when handling stray or feral animals
\end{itemize}
