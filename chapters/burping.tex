\chapter{Burping}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Burping, also called belching or eructation, is the release of gas from the stomach through the mouth, usually with a characteristic sound. It is a normal bodily function: everyone swallows some air while eating, drinking, talking, or chewing gum, and that air must escape. Most people burp several times a day without giving it any thought.

Burping becomes a problem when it is very frequent, uncontrollable, smelly, or accompanied by pain, bloating, or a burning sensation, and in these situations it may point to an underlying digestive condition such as indigestion, reflux, or gastritis. Occasional burping needs no treatment, but frequent or troublesome burping is worth discussing with a doctor, particularly when other symptoms are present.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Burping is normal in everyone, but excessive burping is a common complaint in general practice. It is reported at all ages, from babies who swallow air while feeding and need to be burped to older adults. Frequent burping is often linked to eating habits, carbonated drinks, and anxiety-related swallowing. It is somewhat more common in people who eat quickly, drink large amounts of fizzy drinks, or chew gum regularly. Excessive belching can also be a feature of conditions such as gastro-oesophageal reflux disease and gastritis, and of a pattern of excessive air swallowing known as aerophagia.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Burping occurs when swallowed air accumulates in the stomach and upper gut and is then expelled through the mouth. Factors that increase the amount of air, or make it harder to escape, include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Aerophagia:} eating or drinking too quickly, talking while eating, chewing gum, sucking on hard sweets, and drinking through a straw
    \item \textbf{Carbonated drinks:} fizzy soft drinks, beer, and sparkling water release gas directly into the stomach
    \item \textbf{Indigestion and reflux:} acid reflux and gastritis irritate the stomach and make burping more frequent
    \item \textbf{Nervous habit:} some people repeatedly swallow air without noticing, especially when anxious or stressed
    \item \textbf{Large meals:} a very full stomach raises pressure on the gas, encouraging its release
    \item \textbf{Hiatus hernia:} part of the stomach sliding up into the chest alters pressure and increases burping
    \item \textbf{Chewing tobacco and ill-fitting dentures:} both increase saliva production and air swallowing
\end{itemize}

In most cases burping is simply the body ridding itself of swallowed air. In a minority of people, gas is produced in the gut itself by fermentation of food, although gas generated in the lower bowel is usually passed as flatus rather than burped.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal burping:} a single or occasional release of gas, often after meals or fizzy drinks, without other symptoms
    \item \textbf{Excessive burping:} very frequent, sometimes uncontrollable belching throughout the day
    \item \textbf{Bloating:} a feeling of fullness or distension in the upper abdomen
    \item \textbf{Heartburn or regurgitation:} a burning sensation behind the breastbone when reflux is the cause
    \item \textbf{Odorous burps:} gas that smells of the last meal; sulphur-like odours can follow certain foods
    \item \textbf{In babies:} bringing up wind after feeds, with excessive burping and distress sometimes seen in reflux or overfeeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Functional indigestion or aerophagia:} the most common cause, related to eating habits and nervous air swallowing
    \item \textbf{Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease and hiatus hernia:} associated with heartburn and regurgitation
    \item \textbf{Gastritis or peptic ulcer:} with burning upper abdominal pain that may be relieved by food or antacids
    \item \textbf{Food intolerance or small intestinal bacterial overgrowth:} causing excess gas and bloating
    \item \textbf{Gallbladder or pancreatic disease:} upper abdominal discomfort, typically after fatty meals
    \item \textbf{Gastric outlet obstruction:} rarely, a blockage causing fullness, early satiety, and vomiting
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People should see a doctor if burping is frequent enough to be distressing, persists despite simple measures, or is accompanied by persistent upper abdominal pain, heartburn, difficulty swallowing, unexplained weight loss, or vomiting.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe chest or upper abdominal pain with sweating or breathlessness, which may indicate a heart attack
    \item Vomiting blood or material resembling coffee grounds
    \item Inability to swallow food or fluids
    \item Severe abdominal distension with inability to pass wind or open the bowels
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} eating and drinking habits, timing of burping, associated symptoms such as heartburn or pain, and stress levels
    \item \textbf{Examination:} assessment of the abdomen for distension or tenderness
    \item \textbf{Trial of simple measures:} a diagnosis is often confirmed by seeing whether changes to eating habits improve symptoms
    \item \textbf{Gastroscopy (endoscopy):} a camera examination of the stomach to look for reflux damage, gastritis, or ulcers if these are suspected
    \item \textbf{\textit{H. pylori} testing:} breath, stool, or blood tests for the bacterium that causes gastritis and peptic ulcers
    \item \textbf{Other tests:} rarely, pH monitoring or barium studies when reflux is severe or unusual
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Eating habits:} eat slowly, chew with the mouth closed, and avoid talking while eating
    \item \textbf{Avoid triggers:} reduce fizzy drinks, chewing gum, hard sweets, and drinking through straws
    \item \textbf{Smaller meals:} eat smaller, more frequent meals rather than large ones
    \item \textbf{Treat reflux:} antacids or acid-reducing medicines as recommended by a doctor when heartburn is present
    \item \textbf{Address stress:} for a nervous air-swallowing habit, relaxation techniques and conscious attention to the habit may help
    \item \textbf{Babies:} burp after feeds, hold the infant upright, and feed in a calm, upright position
    \item \textbf{Further review:} if symptoms persist, a doctor may arrange investigations to exclude underlying disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Social embarrassment and anxiety about burping in public
    \item A bloated, uncomfortable feeling when swallowed air cannot escape easily
    \item Missed underlying problems, such as reflux or ulcer disease, if they remain untreated
    \item Rarely, complications of investigation, such as discomfort after gastroscopy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
In most people, burping settles once eating habits and triggers are addressed, and it is harmless. When reflux or gastritis is the cause, treating the underlying condition usually reduces burping. In a minority of people, excessive burping becomes a long-standing habit that is difficult to break, but it is not harmful even when frequent.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Eat and drink slowly, and chew food with the mouth closed
    \item Avoid fizzy drinks, and limit chewing gum and hard sweets
    \item Drink from a glass rather than a straw, and do not gulp drinks
    \item Avoid talking while chewing
    \item Take time over meals and eat in a relaxed setting
    \item Ensure dentures fit well
    \item Burp babies after feeds and sit them upright during feeding
    \item Seek medical advice if burping is persistent or associated with pain, weight loss, or vomiting
\end{itemize}
