\chapter{Inserting an Internal Cardioverter Defibrillator}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) is a small electronic device surgically placed under the skin that monitors the heart rhythm and delivers treatment if a life-threatening fast rhythm develops. The procedure to insert an ICD is performed by a cardiologist, usually under local anaesthesia with sedation rather than general anaesthetic. One or more thin wires (leads) are passed through a vein into the heart, and the device is placed in a pocket under the skin below the collarbone. The whole procedure typically takes one to two hours, and most people go home the same day or the next morning. A similar procedure is used for a device that also coordinates heart failure therapy (CRT-D) or for a device placed entirely under the skin (subcutaneous ICD).

\section*{Epidemiology}
ICDs are implanted in thousands of Australians each year for the prevention of sudden cardiac death. The most common indications are significantly reduced heart pumping function after a heart attack or in heart failure, and survival of a previous cardiac arrest or dangerous ventricular arrhythmia. ICD implantation is one of the most effective interventions in cardiology, reducing the risk of sudden cardiac death by up to half in appropriate patients. The procedure itself has a low complication rate in experienced centres, and most patients leave hospital within a day.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The ICD insertion procedure creates a permanent pathway for detecting and treating arrhythmias. Under imaging guidance, a vein near the collarbone is accessed and the leads are advanced into the right side of the heart, where they are positioned to sense the rhythm and, for the ventricular lead, to deliver shocks. The device is connected and placed in a subcutaneous pocket, then tested by inducing a brief ventricular fibrillation during the procedure and confirming the device terminates it. Modern devices also function as pacemakers, can deliver painless antitachycardia pacing, and transmit information to the clinic remotely.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Performed under local anaesthesia with sedation, or rarely general anaesthesia
    \item Duration of approximately 1--2 hours
    \item The device is visible as a small lump and scar below the collarbone
    \item Most people go home the same day or next day
    \item Full recovery of the surgical wound over 1--2 weeks
    \item The device is activated and programmed before discharge
    \item Regular device checks follow in clinic or remotely
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The need for an ICD, and the type of device, is determined by careful assessment before the procedure.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Standard transvenous ICD:} the usual choice, with leads inside the heart
    \item \textbf{Subcutaneous ICD (S-ICD):} for patients where leads inside the heart are risky or unsuitable, such as young people or those with previous infections
    \item \textbf{CRT-D:} an ICD combined with resynchronisation therapy for patients with heart failure and electrical conduction delay
    \item \textbf{Pacemaker without defibrillator:} for people with slow heart rhythms but no risk of dangerous fast rhythms
    \item \textbf{No device:} some people are managed with medicines or wearable defibrillators instead
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should follow their cardiologist's pre-procedure instructions, including medication adjustments and fasting. After the procedure, contact the treating team for increasing pain, swelling, redness, or discharge from the wound, or for fever. Report any new symptoms, including palpitations, dizziness, or fainting, promptly.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, difficulty breathing, or sudden severe weakness after the procedure (possible blood clot or lung complication)
    \item A device shock accompanied by feeling unwell, multiple shocks, or collapse
    \item Heavy bleeding or a rapidly expanding swelling at the wound
    \item Fever with shaking chills
    \item Sudden swelling of the arm on the side of the device
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-procedure assessment:} echocardiography, ECG, blood tests, and review of medicines
    \item \textbf{Infective screening:} swabs and antibiotics in some patients to reduce infection risk
    \item \textbf{Imaging during the procedure:} X-ray screening (fluoroscopy) to guide lead placement
    \item \textbf{Device testing:} measurement of sensing and pacing thresholds, and testing of defibrillation during the procedure
    \item \textbf{Post-procedure checks:} chest X-ray, ECG, and device interrogation before discharge
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Wound care:} keep the dressing dry for a few days and observe for signs of infection
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple analgesics for wound discomfort
    \item \textbf{Activity restrictions:} avoid lifting the arm above the shoulder and heavy lifting for several weeks
    \item \textbf{Device programming:} tailored to the patient's rhythm and lifestyle
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} device checks in clinic or remotely at scheduled intervals
    \item \textbf{Battery life:} typically 5--10 years, with planned replacement when needed
    \item \textbf{Driving restrictions:} apply for several weeks after implantation and after any shock
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding, bruising, or infection at the wound site
    \item Pneumothorax (a collapsed lung) from vein access, usually managed conservatively
    \item Damage to the heart or blood vessels, which is rare
    \item Lead dislodgement or malfunction
    \item Inappropriate shocks
    \item Blood clots in the veins
    \item Long-term risks: infection requiring device removal, and lead failure over many years
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
ICD implantation is a well-established procedure with a low complication rate and excellent outcomes in appropriate patients. More than 95\% of procedures are completed without major complications, and the device provides effective protection against sudden cardiac death for many years. Battery and lead replacements are routine procedures with similar success rates. With good follow-up, most patients live active lives with their device providing background protection they rarely notice.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend all pre-procedure appointments and follow fasting and medicine instructions
    \item Keep the wound clean and dry, and report any signs of infection
    \item Follow activity restrictions to allow the leads to settle in place
    \item Carry the device card and inform health professionals and dentists about the ICD
    \item Attend device checks and respond to remote monitoring prompts
    \item Follow driving advice and restrictions
    \item Contact the device clinic early with any concerns rather than waiting
\end{itemize}
