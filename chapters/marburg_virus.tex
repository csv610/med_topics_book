\chapter{Marburg Virus}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Marburg virus disease (MVD) is a severe, highly fatal viral haemorrhagic fever caused by the Marburg virus, a member of the family \textit{Filoviridae} and closely related to the Ebola virus. First identified in 1967 during concurrent outbreaks in Marburg and Frankfurt, Germany, and Belgrade, Yugoslavia, the virus is zoonotic, originating in sub-Saharan Africa. The natural reservoir host is the Egyptian rousette fruit bat (\textit{Rousettus aegyptiacus}). Primary transmission to humans occurs via exposure to bat excreta or secretions, typically in caves or mines. Secondary transmission is driven by direct person-to-person contact with infected blood, organs, bodily fluids, or contaminated surfaces (fomites). MVD is characterized by a rapid onset of severe systemic illness, progress to multi-organ failure, and severe haemorrhagic manifestations.

\sectionsection*{Epidemiology}
MVD is endemic to several countries in sub-Saharan Africa, with sporadic outbreaks recorded in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Angola, Uganda, Kenya, Equatorial Guinea, and Tanzania. The case fatality rate associated with Marburg virus disease is extremely high, averaging approximately 50\% but ranging from 24\% to 88\% in past outbreaks depending on the viral strain and the quality of clinical supportive care. Outbreaks are often initiated by miners, tourists, or agricultural workers entering caves inhabited by \textit{Rousettus} bats. Nosocomial transmission in healthcare settings with inadequate infection control is common and represents a major public health hazard. Due to the high risk of transmission and severity of disease, Marburg virus is classified as a category A bioterrorism agent and a high-consequence pathogen.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The Marburg virus is a filamentous, enveloped, negative-sense, single-stranded RNA virus.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral entry:} The virus attaches to host cells via its surface glycoprotein, which binds to specific receptors (such as Niemann-Pick C1) on macrophages, monocytes, and dendritic cells.
    \item \textbf{Immune evasion:} The virus suppresses early host interferon responses, enabling rapid, unchecked viral replication and systemic dissemination.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory cascade:} Infection of macrophages triggers a massive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines (a "cytokine storm"), causing widespread tissue damage.
    \item \textbf{Endothelial dysfunction:} The virus directly infects endothelial cells, leading to increased vascular permeability, capillary leak, and refractory hypovolaemic shock.
    \item \textbf{Coagulopathy:} Damage to the liver impairs production of clotting factors, while systemic inflammation triggers disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), leading to profound haemorrhage.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The incubation period ranges from 2 to 21 days. The clinical progression is rapid and typically divided into three phases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute phase (days 1--3):} Sudden onset of high fever, severe headache, extreme malaise, and debilitating muscle aches.
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal phase (days 3--5):} Severe watery diarrhoea, abdominal pain and cramping, nausea, and vomiting. A non-pruritic maculopapular rash, most prominent on the trunk, often develops within this period.
    \item \textbf{Haemorrhagic phase (days 5--7):} Spontaneous bleeding from mucous membranes, venepuncture sites, the nose, gums, and gastrointestinal tract (hematemesis and melaena).
    \item \textbf{Neurological signs:} Encephalopathy, confusion, irritability, aggression, and seizures may develop in the late stages.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
In the early stages, MVD is clinically indistinguishable from other acute tropical febrile illnesses.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ebola virus disease:} Extremely similar presentation and transmission; requires molecular testing (RT-PCR) to differentiate.
    \item \textbf{Lassa fever:} Another viral haemorrhagic fever endemic to West Africa; presents with exudative pharyngitis and is treated with ribavirin.
    \item \textbf{Severe malaria:} Caused by \textit{Plasmodium falciparum}; must be excluded immediately using blood films or rapid antigen tests.
    \item \textbf{Typhoid fever:} Bacterial infection presenting with step-ladder fever and abdominal symptoms; diagnosed via blood culture.
    \item \textbf{Meningococcemia:} Bacterial sepsis presenting with purpuric rash and shock; distinguished by blood culture or PCR.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with a history of travel to an MVD-endemic area or exposure to caves/mines inhabited by bats within the past 21 days who develops fever, muscle aches, or bleeding must immediately contact healthcare authorities and seek isolated emergency care.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Spontaneous bleeding from any site (gums, nose, or skin)
    \item Vomiting blood or blood in the stool
    \item Difficulty breathing or severe chest pain
    \item Confusion, severe lethargy, or loss of consciousness
    \item Extreme dizziness, fainting, or signs of shock (cold, clammy skin)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Due to the high biosafety risk, diagnostic testing must be conducted in specialised BSL-4 containment facilities.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR):} The primary diagnostic tool, highly sensitive for detecting viral RNA in blood during the acute phase.
    \item \textbf{ELISA testing:} Used to detect Marburg-specific IgM and IgG antibodies, useful in later stages of the disease or retrospectively.
    \item \textbf{Antigen detection assays:} Rapid antigen tests can be used in outbreak settings to quickly identify cases.
    \item \textbf{Routine laboratory findings:} Characterised by severe leucopenia, thrombocytopenia, elevated transaminases (AST higher than ALT), and signs of acute kidney injury or DIC.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
There is currently no approved specific antiviral therapy or vaccine for MVD, though monoclonal antibodies and candidate vaccines are under clinical evaluation. Treatment consists of aggressive supportive care.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Volume replacement:} Intravenous fluid resuscitation and electrolyte correction to counter losses from diarrhoea, vomiting, and capillary leak.
    \item \textbf{Haemodynamic support:} Use of vasopressors in patients with refractory shock.
    \item \textbf{Symptomatic treatment:} Administration of antipyretics (avoiding NSAIDs to reduce bleeding risk), antiemetics, and analgesics.
    \item \textbf{Blood product support:} Transfusion of packed red blood cells, fresh frozen plasma, or platelets in cases of severe haemorrhage.
    \item \textbf{Strict isolation:} Management in a designated high-level isolation unit with negative pressure and full personal protective equipment (PPE) for healthcare workers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and catastrophic multi-site haemorrhage.
    \item Hypovolaemic and distributive shock.
    \item Multi-organ failure, particularly acute kidney injury and hepatic necrosis.
    \item Post-recovery complications in survivors, including uveitis, orchitis, arthralgia, and viral persistence in immunologically privileged sites.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for Marburg virus disease is very poor, with an average mortality rate of approximately 50\%. Survival is associated with early, aggressive supportive care, maintaining hydration, and a robust host immune response. Death typically occurs between 8 and 16 days after symptom onset. Survivors face a prolonged convalescence period characterized by hair loss, muscle wasting, joint pain, and psychological sequelae.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Avoid visiting caves or mines inhabited by fruit bats in endemic regions.
    \item Wear protective gloves and masks when working in agricultural settings or handling bats in endemic areas.
    \item Implement strict barrier nursing and contact isolation precautions for all suspected patients.
    \item Practice safe and dignified burial procedures for deceased individuals to avoid contact with infected bodily fluids.
    \item Restrict sexual activity or use condoms for male survivors until semen tests negative for the virus twice.
\end{itemize}
