\chapter{Absent Periods}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Absent periods, known medically as amenorrhoea, means that a woman is not having menstrual periods. It is divided into two forms. Primary amenorrhoea is the failure of menstruation to begin by around age 15--16 years, or within three years of breast development. Secondary amenorrhoea is the cessation of periods for three months or more in a woman who previously menstruated (or six months if her periods were always irregular). The most common cause of a missed period in a woman of reproductive age is pregnancy. Beyond pregnancy, absent periods may reflect hormonal, structural, or lifestyle-related problems, and they matter even when pregnancy is not desired, because oestrogen is important for bone and cardiovascular health.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Secondary amenorrhoea affects roughly 3--5\% of women at some point in their lives. Its most common causes are polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), which affects about 1 in 10 to 1 in 8 women of reproductive age, and functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea, which develops in response to stress, low body weight, or excessive exercise. Primary amenorrhoea is much rarer, affecting approximately 1 in 300 women who have not menstruated by the age of 16. Amenorrhoea is particularly common among elite athletes and dancers, in whom energy expenditure and low body weight frequently suppress the reproductive axis.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Menstruation depends on an integrated chain of hormones. The hypothalamus and pituitary gland signal the ovaries, the ovaries produce oestrogen and progesterone, and the uterine lining builds up and then sheds. Absent periods can result from a break at any point in this chain.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} the most common cause of a missed period in reproductive-aged women
    \item \textbf{Hypothalamic or pituitary problems:} stress, weight loss, eating disorders, excessive exercise, or a benign pituitary tumour (prolactinoma) suppress the signals to the ovaries
    \item \textbf{Ovarian problems:} PCOS with infrequent ovulation, premature ovarian insufficiency, or menopause
    \item \textbf{Hormonal medications:} some contraceptives deliberately stop the cycle, and antipsychotics and other medicines can raise prolactin
    \item \textbf{Thyroid and endocrine disease:} underactive or overactive thyroid can disturb the menstrual cycle
    \item \textbf{Structural problems:} intrauterine scarring (Asherman syndrome), or outflow obstruction such as an imperforate hymen or absent cervix or vagina in primary amenorrhoea
    \item \textbf{Chronic illness:} poorly controlled diabetes, coeliac disease, and other long-term conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The presentation depends on whether the amenorrhoea is primary or secondary and on its underlying cause.

\begin{itemize}
    \item No periods at all, or a pattern of increasingly infrequent periods
    \item Features of the underlying cause, including weight loss, weight gain, or a low body mass index
    \item Acne, excess facial or body hair, or scalp hair thinning, suggesting PCOS
    \item Milky nipple discharge (galactorrhoea) from a high prolactin level
    \item Hot flushes, night sweats, and vaginal dryness, suggesting ovarian insufficiency or menopause
    \item Headaches or visual disturbance, which may indicate a pituitary tumour
    \item Difficulty conceiving
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pregnancy, including ectopic pregnancy
    \item Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)
    \item Functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea related to stress, weight, or exercise
    \item Hyperprolactinaemia from a prolactinoma or medication
    \item Thyroid disease
    \item Premature ovarian insufficiency or menopause
    \item Asherman syndrome or outflow tract obstruction
    \item Chronic illness and the effects of medications
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
A doctor should be consulted if periods have not started by age 15--16, if they have stopped for three months or more, or if a period has been missed and a home pregnancy test is positive or unclear.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal or pelvic pain with a missed period, which may indicate an ectopic pregnancy
    \item Heavy vaginal bleeding after a missed period
    \item Sudden severe headache or visual disturbance together with milky nipple discharge
    \item Fainting or collapse with pelvic pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A structured approach confirms the cause of the amenorrhoea.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} menstrual, sexual, contraceptive, exercise, weight, stress, and medication history
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test:} always performed first in reproductive-aged women
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} height and weight, signs of androgen excess, thyroid assessment, and breast and pelvic examination, particularly in primary amenorrhoea
    \item \textbf{Hormone blood tests:} follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinising hormone (LH), oestradiol, prolactin, and thyroid function; testosterone in suspected PCOS
    \item \textbf{Pelvic ultrasound:} to examine the ovaries, uterus, and endometrial lining
    \item \textbf{Brain MRI:} when a pituitary tumour is suspected
    \item \textbf{Hysteroscopy or specialised imaging:} when intrauterine scarring or outflow obstruction is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is directed at the underlying cause.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} antenatal care and appropriate planning
    \item \textbf{Functional amenorrhoea:} restoring a healthy weight, reducing excessive exercise, and managing stress, which usually brings periods back
    \item \textbf{PCOS:} lifestyle measures, sometimes metformin or hormonal treatments, and induction of a regular withdrawal bleed if the lining is overbuilding
    \item \textbf{Prolactinoma:} medicines that lower the prolactin level
    \item \textbf{Thyroid disease:} thyroid hormone replacement or specific treatment
    \item \textbf{Ovarian insufficiency or menopause:} hormone replacement therapy or combined contraception to protect bones and the cardiovascular system, unless contraindicated
    \item \textbf{Structural problems:} surgery to divide uterine adhesions or to open an outflow blockage
    \item \textbf{Fertility:} ovulation induction or assisted reproduction when pregnancy is desired
    \item \textbf{Bone protection:} adequate calcium and vitamin D, weight-bearing exercise, and bone density assessment where indicated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infertility, depending on the underlying cause
    \item Prolonged low oestrogen causes bone thinning (osteoporosis) and an increased risk of fracture
    \item Overgrowth of the uterine lining when cycles are absent but oestrogen levels remain high, as in some cases of PCOS, which can increase the risk of endometrial changes
    \item Unrecognised pregnancy or ectopic pregnancy
    \item Psychological distress, including anxiety about fertility
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the cause. Functional amenorrhoea usually reverses fully when weight, exercise, and stress are addressed. PCOS is a long-term condition, but its symptoms can be well controlled. Premature ovarian insufficiency is persistent and usually requires ongoing hormone therapy, but it can be managed effectively. Outflow obstruction and Asherman syndrome often improve with surgery. Whatever the cause, the aims of treatment include protecting bone and heart health, not merely restoring the periods.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and eat a balanced diet that provides enough energy for your level of activity
    \item Avoid extreme dieting and exercise patterns that stop periods
    \item Manage stress and get adequate sleep
    \item Take calcium and vitamin D if advised, particularly when oestrogen levels are low
    \item Use contraception if you do not wish to conceive, since the first period after a pause can occur before expected
    \item Keep up with medical follow-up, including bone density checks if recommended
    \item Seek help early if you are trying to conceive or are worried about your cycles
\end{itemize}
