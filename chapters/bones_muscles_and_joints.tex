\chapter{Bones, Muscles, and Joints}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The bones, muscles, and joints form the musculoskeletal system, the framework that supports the body, enables movement, protects vital organs, and stores minerals such as calcium. Bones provide structure and anchor points for muscles; joints allow bones to move against each other in controlled ways; muscles, working through tendons, generate the force that produces movement; and ligaments hold joints together and stabilise them. The system is remarkably durable, but it is also vulnerable to injury, inflammation, degeneration, and infection. Musculoskeletal problems are among the most common reasons for consulting a doctor, ranging from short-lived sprains and strains to chronic conditions such as osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, and inflammatory arthritis. Maintaining the health of bones, muscles, and joints through activity, good nutrition, and injury prevention benefits every part of the body.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Musculoskeletal conditions are the leading cause of disability worldwide. Back pain is the single most common musculoskeletal complaint, affecting a large proportion of adults at some point, and osteoarthritis affects over 500 million people globally, rising steeply with age and obesity. Osteoporosis and its fractures become increasingly common after 50, and rheumatoid arthritis affects about 1\% of the population. Injuries such as sprains, strains, and fractures are extremely common in sport, at work, and in falls, particularly in older people. The burden of musculoskeletal disease is growing as populations age, and it accounts for a substantial share of primary-care consultations, sick leave, and disability.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bone:} living tissue remodelled by osteoclasts and osteoblasts; its strength depends on mineralisation, protein matrix, and adequate calcium, vitamin D, and mechanical loading.
    \item \textbf{Joints:} articulations where bone ends are covered in cartilage and bathed in synovial fluid; the cartilage cushions load, and the synovium lubricates and nourishes the joint.
    \item \textbf{Muscles:} skeletal muscle shortens through the sliding of actin and myosin filaments to generate force; tendons transmit that force to bone.
    \item \textbf{Injury mechanisms:} sprains are ligament injuries from stretching, strains are muscle or tendon injuries, and fractures are breaks in bone from excessive force or weakened bone.
    \item \textbf{Degeneration:} osteoarthritis involves progressive cartilage wear, subchondral bone changes, and bony spurs, driven by ageing, joint overload, and genetics.
    \item \textbf{Inflammation:} rheumatoid arthritis and related diseases result from an autoimmune attack on the synovium, producing chronic inflammation that erodes cartilage and bone.
    \item \textbf{Metabolic effects:} declining muscle mass (sarcopenia) and bone mass with age reduce strength, balance, and resistance to injury, and increase fall and fracture risk.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Problems in the musculoskeletal system present with a limited set of symptoms, the pattern of which points to the underlying cause:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pain:} localised joint pain with osteoarthritis; bone pain with fractures, infection, or tumour; deep aching muscle pain with overuse or metabolic disease.
    \item \textbf{Stiffness:} early-morning stiffness lasting under 30 minutes suggests osteoarthritis or overuse, while prolonged morning stiffness over an hour points to inflammatory arthritis.
    \item \textbf{Swelling:} bony enlargement in osteoarthritis, boggy synovial swelling in rheumatoid arthritis, and effusions after injury.
    \item \textbf{Limited movement:} reduced range in a joint from pain, stiffness, or structural damage.
    \item \textbf{Weakness:} reduced muscle power from disuse, nerve injury, or primary muscle disease.
    \item \textbf{Deformity:} bowing, angulation, or malalignment after injury, arthritis, or metabolic bone disease.
    \item \textbf{Instability:} the sensation of the joint giving way, seen with ligament injury.
    \item \textbf{Redness and heat:} signs of acute inflammation, infection, or gout.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Injury:} fractures, sprains, and strains, distinguished by mechanism, examination, and imaging.
    \item \textbf{Osteoarthritis:} age-related, weight-bearing joint degeneration with activity-related pain and short morning stiffness.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory arthritis:} rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, and ankylosing spondylitis cause inflammatory pain, swelling, and prolonged stiffness.
    \item \textbf{Crystal arthritis:} gout and pseudogout cause acute, intensely painful joint inflammation.
    \item \textbf{Septic arthritis:} a joint infection that is a medical emergency, with severe pain, fever, and an inability to move the joint.
    \item \textbf{Bone disease:} osteoporosis, osteomalacia, Paget's disease, and bone tumours can present with pain and fracture.
    \item \textbf{Referred pain:} conditions such as hip arthritis presenting as knee pain, or spinal problems causing limb pain.
    \item \textbf{Non-musculoskeletal causes:} infection, malignancy, and polymyalgia rheumatica can produce widespread muscle and joint symptoms.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for pain that is severe, persistent, or worsening, for swelling or stiffness that limits daily activities, for joint redness and heat, for weakness or instability, or for pain that wakes you at night. Seek review if an injury has not improved within a week or two, or if you have a fever, unintentional weight loss, or a history of cancer alongside new bone or joint pain.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A suspected fracture or dislocation with severe pain, deformity, or an open wound
    \item Sudden inability to move or bear weight on a limb
    \item A hot, red, swollen joint with fever, which suggests septic arthritis
    \item Numbness, weakness, or loss of bladder or bowel control after a back injury (possible spinal cord compression)
    \item Severe chest or back pain, which can indicate a fractured vertebra or other serious problem
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the site, character, and timing of pain; stiffness patterns; mechanism of any injury; aggravating and relieving factors; and systemic symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Examination:} inspection for swelling, redness, and deformity; palpation for tenderness and warmth; assessment of range of movement, muscle power, and stability.
    \item \textbf{Plain radiographs:} first-line imaging for fractures, dislocations, and the joint space narrowing and spurs of osteoarthritis.
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} useful for tendons, bursae, and joint effusions, and to guide injections.
    \item \textbf{MRI:} the best test for ligaments, menisci, cartilage, and the bone marrow; used for sports injuries, inflammatory arthritis, and suspected osteonecrosis or infection.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} inflammatory markers, rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP antibodies, uric acid for gout, and calcium and vitamin D for bone disease.
    \item \textbf{Joint aspiration:} sampling of joint fluid to diagnose gout and to exclude septic arthritis.
    \item \textbf{Bone density scan:} for suspected osteoporosis or fragility fracture risk.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute injury:} the RICE approach -- rest, ice, compression, and elevation -- for sprains and strains, with gradual return to activity.
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} paracetamol, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and topical preparations, used for the shortest time needed.
    \item \textbf{Exercise:} the cornerstone of treatment for osteoarthritis and back pain; strengthening, aerobic, and balance programs improve pain and function more than most other treatments.
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} structured rehabilitation to restore movement, strength, and stability after injury or surgery.
    \item \textbf{Weight management:} reducing excess weight dramatically reduces load on weight-bearing joints and improves osteoarthritis symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Disease-modifying drugs:} methotrexate and biological agents for inflammatory arthritis; bisphosphonates for osteoporosis; urate-lowering therapy for gout.
    \item \textbf{Injections:} intra-articular steroid or hyaluronic acid injections for selected joint pain.
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} joint replacement, ligament reconstruction, fracture fixation, and arthroscopy are used when conservative treatment fails.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic pain, stiffness, and progressive disability
    \item Loss of independence and increased reliance on care in older people
    \item Recurrent or chronic joint instability after ligament injuries
    \item Early or advanced osteoarthritis after joint injury
    \item Fractures, and their complications of immobility, thromboembolism, and mortality
    \item Muscle wasting and sarcopenia from inactivity
    \item Falls and further injury in people with poor strength and balance
    \item Complications of treatment, including surgical infections and the gastrointestinal and renal effects of NSAIDs
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the underlying condition. Most acute injuries, sprains, and strains resolve within days to weeks with simple measures, and most people with new back pain recover within six weeks. Osteoarthritis is a lifelong condition, but symptoms can be well controlled with exercise, weight loss, and analgesia, and most people remain active; joint replacement provides excellent relief when it is needed. Inflammatory arthritis responds well to modern drug treatment, and early diagnosis greatly improves outcomes. Bone disease is treatable with targeted therapy. Across the whole musculoskeletal system, the consistent message is that appropriate activity, timely treatment, and rehabilitation are associated with far better long-term function than rest and avoidance.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Stay active with a mix of aerobic, strengthening, and balance exercises most days.
    \item Warm up before exercise and allow adequate recovery between sessions.
    \item Use good lifting technique and protect your back during daily tasks.
    \item Maintain a healthy weight to protect joints.
    \item Ensure adequate calcium, vitamin D, and protein to support bone and muscle.
    \item Wear appropriate footwear, and address hazards that increase the risk of falls.
    \item Treat acute injuries early with rest, ice, compression, and elevation, and return to activity gradually.
    \item Do not ignore persistent pain, stiffness, or swelling; early assessment prevents long-term damage.
\end{itemize}
