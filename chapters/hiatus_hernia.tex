\chapter{Hiatus Hernia}
\index{Hiatus Hernia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A hiatus hernia (also spelled hiatal hernia) is a condition in which the upper part of the stomach protrudes upwards through the diaphragm into the chest. The diaphragm is the muscle separating the chest from the abdomen, and the hiatus is the opening through which the oesophagus passes. When the stomach slides up through this opening, the normal barrier that prevents stomach acid from flowing backwards is disturbed, causing reflux symptoms in many people. Hiatus hernias are very common, especially in later life, and most are managed without surgery.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hiatus hernias are among the most common conditions found on upper gastrointestinal investigation. Their prevalence rises with age, affecting around 10--20\% of people under 40 but more than half of those over 60. They are more common in women, in people who are overweight, and in those who smoke. Most hiatus hernias are sliding hernias that produce no symptoms, and the majority of people are unaware they have one. Only a small proportion require surgical treatment.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Weakening of the diaphragm:} age-related loss of tissue strength at the hiatus
    \item \textbf{Increased abdominal pressure:} obesity, pregnancy, heavy lifting, straining, and chronic cough push the stomach upwards
    \item \textbf{Sliding hernia:} the oesophagogastric junction and part of the stomach move up into the chest; accounts for over 90\% of cases
    \item \textbf{Rolling (para-oesophageal) hernia:} part of the stomach herniates alongside the oesophagus while the junction remains below the diaphragm; carries a higher risk of complications
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} the displaced junction no longer functions as a valve, and the loss of the diaphragm's pinch effect permits acid reflux into the oesophagus
    \item \textbf{Reflux consequences:} oesophagitis, strictures, and Barrett's oesophagus
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Many people have no symptoms at all
    \item Heartburn, worse after meals, when bending, or when lying flat
    \item Regurgitation of food or acid into the mouth
    \item A sour or bitter taste and excessive belching
    \item Difficulty swallowing or the sensation of food sticking
    \item Chest pain that may mimic angina
    \item Chronic cough, hoarseness, and recurrent chest infections from aspiration
    \item Anaemia from chronic slow bleeding at the hernia site
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Gastro-oesophageal reflux without a hernia, which presents identically
    \item Angina and other cardiac causes of chest pain
    \item Oesophageal spasm, achalasia, and other swallowing disorders
    \item Peptic ulcer disease and gastritis
    \item Gallbladder disease
    \item Lung disease causing chronic cough
    \item Investigation distinguishes hiatus hernia from these conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for frequent heartburn, regurgitation, difficulty swallowing, or chest discomfort. Seek urgent review for severe pain, vomiting, or an inability to swallow or pass food.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe chest or upper abdominal pain, especially with vomiting
    \item Inability to swallow, retching, or vomiting blood
    \item Fever and severe pain (possible strangulation of a para-oesophageal hernia)
    \item Chest pain with sweating, breathlessness, or pain radiating to the arm or jaw
    \item Black, tarry stools or vomiting blood
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gastroscopy:} the standard diagnostic test, showing the position of the junction and any oesophagitis
    \item \textbf{Barium swallow:} X-ray imaging of the upper gastrointestinal tract with swallowed contrast
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} may reveal a retrocardiac air bubble in large hernias
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} for large, complex, or para-oesophageal hernias
    \item \textbf{Oesophageal manometry and pH monitoring:} assess function before surgery
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} for anaemia in chronic cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle modification:} weight loss, smaller meals, upright posture after eating, and elevation of the head of the bed
    \item \textbf{Dietary change:} avoid fatty foods, alcohol, caffeine, chocolate, and large late meals
    \item \textbf{Acid suppression:} proton pump inhibitors are the mainstay of medical treatment
    \item \textbf{Prokinetic agents:} sometimes used to improve oesophageal clearance
    \item \textbf{Observation:} asymptomatic hernias require no treatment
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} laparoscopic fundoplication for severe reflux despite medication, large para-oesophageal hernias, or complications
    \item \textbf{Emergency surgery:} for volvulus or strangulation of a para-oesophageal hernia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reflux oesophagitis with erosions, ulceration, and bleeding
    \item Oesophageal stricture causing difficulty swallowing
    \item Barrett's oesophagus with increased cancer risk
    \item Iron-deficiency anaemia from chronic blood loss
    \item Aspiration pneumonia
    \item Para-oesophageal hernia complications: gastric volvulus, obstruction, and strangulation, which are emergencies
    \item Recurrence after surgery in a minority
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For most people, a hiatus hernia is a benign condition with an excellent outlook. Symptoms are well controlled with lifestyle measures and acid suppression, and most people live without ever needing surgery. Surgery is highly effective when indicated, providing lasting relief of reflux in the majority. The rare serious complications of para-oesophageal hernias are treated successfully when recognised promptly. Barrett's oesophagus, when present, is monitored to minimise long-term cancer risk.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and avoid tight belts and clothing
    \item Eat small, regular meals and avoid eating within 2--3 hours of bed
    \item Avoid bending and heavy lifting soon after meals
    \item Elevate the head of the bed 15--20 cm for night-time symptoms
    \item Quit smoking and limit alcohol
    \item Take prescribed acid-suppressing medicines consistently
    \item Report difficulty swallowing, chest pain, or vomiting promptly
    \item Attend follow-up endoscopy as recommended
\end{itemize}
