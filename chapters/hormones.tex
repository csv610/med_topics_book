\chapter{Hormones}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hormones are chemical messengers produced by the endocrine glands and released into the bloodstream to regulate the activity of organs and tissues throughout the body. They control growth, metabolism, reproduction, mood, blood sugar, blood pressure, and many other functions. The major glands include the pituitary, thyroid, adrenal glands, pancreas, ovaries, and testes. Hormone levels are tightly regulated by feedback systems, and when these systems fail, hormone excess or deficiency causes a wide range of illnesses, from diabetes and thyroid disease to growth disorders and reproductive problems.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hormonal disorders are among the most common chronic conditions. Diabetes affects over 1.2 million Australians, thyroid disease affects around one in 20, and hormonal conditions such as PCOS, menopause, and osteoporosis affect large numbers of people. Disorders of growth, puberty, and reproduction are common reasons for specialist referral. Many hormonal disorders are underdiagnosed because their symptoms, such as fatigue, weight change, and mood disturbance, are non-specific. Routine testing and awareness have improved detection, and most hormonal disorders are highly treatable.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Feedback regulation:} the brain (hypothalamus and pituitary) controls the glands, and hormone levels feed back to adjust production
    \item \textbf{Hormone classes:} proteins (insulin, growth hormone), steroids (cortisol, oestrogen, testosterone), and amines (thyroid hormones, adrenaline)
    \item \textbf{Endocrine glands:} pituitary, thyroid, parathyroid, adrenal, pancreas, ovaries, and testes
    \item \textbf{Mechanisms of disease:} gland failure (hypothyroidism, Addison's disease), overactivity (hyperthyroidism, Cushing's), receptor problems (insulin resistance), and tumours
    \item \textbf{Hormone actions:} each hormone binds specific receptors and triggers responses in target cells
    \item \textbf{Effects of imbalance:} deficiency or excess disrupts metabolism, growth, reproduction, and homeostasis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Symptoms vary with the hormone and whether levels are high or low
    \item Fatigue, weight change, and temperature intolerance in thyroid disease
    \item Thirst, urination, and weight change in diabetes
    \item Growth and puberty changes in childhood hormone disorders
    \item Menstrual changes, infertility, and libido changes in reproductive disorders
    \item Blood pressure, heart rate, and mood changes in adrenal disorders
    \item Many symptoms are gradual and non-specific, delaying diagnosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hormonal symptoms overlap with many non-endocrine conditions, including depression and chronic fatigue
    \item Blood tests distinguish hormonal causes from others
    \item Multiple hormones may be affected together in pituitary disease
    \item Medication effects can mimic hormonal disorders
    \item Age-related changes, such as menopause and low testosterone, are distinguished from disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for persistent fatigue, unexplained weight change, excessive thirst, menstrual problems, or other symptoms that suggest a hormonal disorder. Annual health checks can detect common hormonal conditions early.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Diabetic emergencies: confusion, drowsiness, or collapse
    \item Severe headache with vision changes (possible pituitary emergency)
    \item Chest pain, palpitations, or severe breathlessness with thyroid or adrenal disease
    \item Adrenal crisis: severe vomiting, abdominal pain, and collapse in someone on steroids
    \item Severe dehydration
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hormone blood tests:} thyroid function, blood sugar and HbA1c, cortisol, reproductive hormones, and growth hormone as indicated
    \item \textbf{Stimulation and suppression tests:} assess gland function dynamically
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} ultrasound of the thyroid and ovaries, CT and MRI of the pituitary and adrenal glands
    \item \textbf{Bone density testing:} for osteoporosis risk
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} for inherited endocrine conditions
    \item \textbf{Screening:} diabetes, thyroid, and bone health screening in at-risk groups
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hormone replacement:} insulin, thyroxine, and other hormones for deficiency
    \item \textbf{Suppression:} medicines that block hormone production for overactivity
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} removal of overactive glands and hormone-secreting tumours
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} for selected pituitary and thyroid tumours
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} diet, exercise, and weight management for diabetes and metabolic conditions
    \item \textbf{Symptom management:} HRT for menopause and other targeted treatments
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular review of hormone levels and treatment
    \item \textbf{Specialist care:} endocrinologists manage complex and rare hormonal disorders
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Untreated diabetes: cardiovascular disease, kidney failure, blindness, and amputation
    \item Untreated thyroid disease: heart disease, osteoporosis, and myxoedema coma or thyroid storm
    \item Growth and development problems in children
    \item Osteoporosis and fractures from hormone deficiency
    \item Infertility and reproductive problems
    \item Adrenal crises and other life-threatening emergencies
    \item Reduced quality of life from fatigue, weight, and mood symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most hormonal disorders are highly treatable, and with correct diagnosis and management, most people live normal lives. Diabetes is controlled effectively with modern treatment, thyroid disease responds well to replacement or suppression, and growth and reproductive disorders are treated successfully in most cases. The key to good outcomes is recognition: hormonal symptoms are often treatable but overlooked. With early diagnosis, monitoring, and specialist care where needed, the prognosis for the vast majority of hormonal conditions is excellent.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a healthy weight, diet, and physical activity for metabolic health
    \item Attend routine health checks, including blood sugar and thyroid screening where appropriate
    \item Take hormone medicines exactly as prescribed and attend monitoring
    \item Report persistent fatigue, weight change, or other hormonal symptoms
    \item Protect bone health with calcium, vitamin D, and exercise
    \item Manage stress, which affects hormonal balance
    \item Pregnant women attend antenatal care, which includes hormonal screening
    \item Seek specialist advice for complex or unexplained symptoms
\end{itemize}
