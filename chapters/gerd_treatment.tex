\chapter{GORD Treatment}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
GORD (gastro-oesophageal reflux disease) treatment encompasses the range of strategies used to control reflux symptoms, heal damage to the oesophagus, and prevent complications. Treatment is individualised and usually follows a stepped approach: lifestyle modification forms the foundation, medicines that reduce or neutralise stomach acid are added when symptoms are troublesome, and surgery or endoscopic procedures are reserved for people with severe or refractory disease. The goal is to give people freedom from heartburn, regurgitation, and other symptoms while minimising the need for long-term medication. With modern treatment, most people with GORD achieve excellent control of their symptoms.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Because GORD is common, its treatment is among the most frequent reasons for medicine use in Australia and worldwide. Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are among the top-selling medicines globally, and a large proportion of the population uses them at some point. Around half of people with reflux use non-prescription antacids, while many more are prescribed PPIs by their GP. Despite effective treatments, a significant minority of people continue to have symptoms, and up to a third of those referred for specialist care do not respond fully to standard PPI therapy. Surgical treatment for reflux has declined in recent decades but remains an option for carefully selected patients.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Treatment of GORD is based on the mechanisms that cause reflux and oesophageal damage.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acid suppression:} medicines reduce the amount of acid produced by the stomach, so that refluxed fluid is less irritating
    \item \textbf{Neutralisation:} antacids and alginates neutralise acid already in the oesophagus or form a raft that blocks reflux
    \item \textbf{Motility:} some treatments aim to strengthen the lower oesophageal sphincter or speed gastric emptying
    \item \textbf{Mechanical repair:} surgery recreates a competent valve at the junction of the stomach and oesophagus
    \item \textbf{Reducing reflux triggers:} lifestyle measures lower abdominal pressure and reduce events that relax the sphincter
    \item \textbf{Mucosal protection:} barrier-forming agents coat and protect the oesophageal lining
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features of GORD that treatment addresses include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Heartburn: the burning retrosternal sensation, typically after meals and when lying down
    \item Regurgitation of sour or bitter fluid into the mouth
    \item Difficulty swallowing or a sensation of food sticking
    \item Chronic cough, hoarseness, and throat clearing
    \item Chest pain and a lump-in-the-throat sensation
    \item Sleep disturbance from nocturnal reflux
    \item Oesophagitis, stricture, and Barrett oesophagus in those with mucosal damage
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before or during treatment, other causes of symptoms should be considered, particularly when treatment fails.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cardiac disease, including angina, which can present with chest discomfort
    \item Peptic ulcer disease and functional dyspepsia
    \item Oesophageal motility disorders such as achalasia and spasm
    \item Eosinophilic oesophagitis, an allergic condition causing dysphagia
    \item Gallbladder disease
    \item Medication-related oesophagitis from pills sticking or irritating the oesophagus
    \item Rumination syndrome and other functional disorders
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with persistent reflux symptoms should be assessed by a GP to confirm the diagnosis and plan treatment. People who do not respond adequately to a standard course of acid-suppressing medicine should return for review, as should those with recurrent symptoms after treatment stops.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain with breathlessness, sweating, or pain spreading to the arm or jaw
    \item Vomiting blood or black, tarry stools
    \item Difficulty swallowing solids and liquids with weight loss
    \item Sudden severe abdominal or chest pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Treatment decisions are guided by symptom severity, response to treatment, and the presence of alarm features.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} history of typical symptoms and response to previous treatment
    \item \textbf{Gastroscopy:} recommended when symptoms are severe, persistent despite treatment, or accompanied by alarm features, and to check for oesophagitis, stricture, and Barrett oesophagus
    \item \textbf{pH monitoring:} measures actual acid exposure when the diagnosis is uncertain or symptoms persist despite treatment
    \item \textbf{Manometry:} assesses oesophageal muscle function, mainly before antireflux surgery
    \item \textbf{Review of medicines:} checking for medicines that aggravate reflux
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is stepped according to severity, and most people can be managed in primary care.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Step 1 -- Lifestyle measures:} weight loss, avoiding trigger foods, smaller meals, not lying down after eating, and elevating the head of the bed
    \item \textbf{Step 2 -- Antacids and alginates:} for mild, occasional symptoms, taken as needed
    \item \textbf{Step 3 -- Acid suppression:} H2-receptor antagonists for moderate symptoms, or a proton pump inhibitor for persistent symptoms and oesophagitis
    \item \textbf{Step 4 -- Optimising PPI use:} taking the medicine correctly (usually before breakfast), using the lowest effective dose, and trying on-demand or step-down therapy once controlled
    \item \textbf{Step 5 -- Specialist referral:} for people who do not respond to PPIs, who have alarm features, or who are being considered for surgery
    \item \textbf{Step 6 -- Surgery or endoscopy:} laparoscopic fundoplication and newer endoscopic procedures for carefully selected patients
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Treatment is generally safe, but complications can arise, including:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Side effects of PPIs, including headache, diarrhoea, and rarely low magnesium or increased risk of certain infections and fractures with long-term use
    \item Rebound acid hypersecretion when PPIs are stopped suddenly
    \item Inadequate treatment leading to oesophagitis, stricture, or Barrett oesophagus
    \item Complications of surgery, including bloating, difficulty swallowing, and gas-bloat syndrome
    \item Overuse of antacids, which can cause diarrhoea or constipation
    \item Masking of more serious conditions if investigation is delayed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Treatment of GORD is highly effective. About 80--90\% of people with reflux symptoms gain good control with a proton pump inhibitor, and oesophagitis heals in the large majority within eight weeks. Many people can step down to lower doses or take treatment only when needed. Surgery controls symptoms in around 80--90\% of carefully selected patients, though some require resumption of medicines in later years. The key to good outcomes is accurate diagnosis, appropriate use of the right medicine at the right dose, and review of people whose symptoms do not settle.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and avoid large meals, especially late at night
    \item Identify personal trigger foods and drinks and limit them
    \item Elevate the head of the bed and avoid lying down for at least two to three hours after eating
    \item Stop smoking and limit alcohol
    \item Take acid-suppressing medicines as directed, usually before a meal for best effect
    \item Do not stop a long-term PPI abruptly; step down gradually under medical advice
    \item Review your treatment with your GP at least yearly to ensure the lowest effective dose
    \item Report any alarm symptoms such as difficulty swallowing, weight loss, or bleeding promptly
\end{itemize}
