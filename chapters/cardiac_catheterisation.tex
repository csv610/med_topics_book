\chapter{Cardiac Catheterisation}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cardiac catheterisation is a procedure in which a thin, flexible tube called a catheter is passed through a blood vessel, usually in the groin or wrist, and guided into the heart. It allows doctors to measure pressures inside the heart chambers, inject contrast dye to take X-ray images of the coronary arteries (coronary angiography), and carry out treatments such as opening a blocked artery with a balloon and stent or replacing a heart valve. Most procedures are performed under local anaesthetic with light sedation, and the person remains awake throughout. It is performed in a dedicated cardiac catheter laboratory by a team of cardiologists, nurses, and radiographers.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cardiac catheterisation is one of the most common hospital procedures in the world, with millions of coronary angiograms performed each year. It is most often carried out in older adults being investigated for chest pain, heart attack, or heart failure, and its use has risen steadily with the ageing population. The procedure has also shifted partly towards treatment: many people with acute heart attacks now go straight to the catheter laboratory for urgent artery-opening treatment (primary percutaneous coronary intervention), which has substantially improved survival from heart attack over the past two decades.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The procedure is performed when the heart or its blood supply needs to be directly examined or treated:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coronary artery disease:} narrowing of the arteries by fatty plaques, which can cause angina or heart attack
    \item \textbf{Acute heart attack:} sudden blockage of a coronary artery, where rapid reopening restores blood flow and limits damage
    \item \textbf{Heart failure:} to measure pressures in the heart and assess the cause of poor pumping
    \item \textbf{Valve disease:} to assess the severity of narrowed or leaking valves and to guide repair or replacement
    \item \textbf{Congenital heart defects:} to define anatomy in adults and children with structural heart problems
    \item \textbf{Measurement of pressures and blood flow:} in the heart chambers, great vessels, and lungs, including assessment for pulmonary hypertension
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Most people are referred for cardiac catheterisation because of symptoms suggesting heart disease:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain or pressure on exertion (angina), at rest, or during a heart attack
    \item Shortness of breath, sometimes on minimal activity or at night
    \item Fatigue and reduced exercise tolerance
    \item Palpitations or episodes of faintness
    \item Abnormal findings on earlier tests, such as an ECG, exercise test, or echocardiogram
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms that prompt catheterisation can be produced by conditions other than coronary artery disease:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Gastro-oesophageal reflux or other digestive causes of chest pain
    \item Musculoskeletal chest wall pain
    \item Anxiety and panic attacks
    \item Lung problems such as pneumonia, asthma, or pulmonary embolism
    \item Aortic dissection, a tear in the wall of the large artery leaving the heart
    \item Cardiomyopathy or heart valve disease causing breathlessness without arterial blockages
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Symptoms suggesting a heart problem should be assessed promptly. Anyone with risk factors for heart disease and new or worsening chest symptoms should see a doctor without delay.
\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain or pressure that is severe, prolonged, or occurring at rest
    \item Pain spreading to the arm, jaw, neck, or back
    \item Sudden severe breathlessness, sweating, or feeling faint
    \item Collapse or loss of consciousness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Cardiac catheterisation is both a diagnostic test and a treatment, and it is supported by other investigations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coronary angiography:} contrast dye is injected into the coronary arteries to reveal narrowing or blockage
    \item \textbf{Pressure measurement:} pressures in the heart chambers and vessels are recorded to assess pumping and valve function
    \item \textbf{Left ventriculography:} dye is injected into the main pumping chamber to assess its contraction
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} small samples of heart muscle can be taken during the procedure if cardiomyopathy is suspected
    \item \textbf{Pre-procedure checks:} blood tests, ECG, kidney function, and discussion of allergies and current medications
    \item \textbf{Echocardiogram:} often performed first to assess valves and pumping without any invasive test
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The catheterisation itself may be diagnostic or therapeutic, and is followed by appropriate care:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Recovery after the test:} a dressing and pressure are applied to the entry site; the person usually rests for a few hours and may go home the same day
    \item \textbf{Angioplasty and stenting:} a balloon is inflated to open a blocked artery and a metal mesh stent is placed to keep it open
    \item \textbf{Additional procedures:} valve repair, closure of abnormal openings, or other interventions in the same setting
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} blood-thinning drugs (antiplatelet therapy) are essential after stenting to prevent clot formation
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle and risk-factor management:} stopping smoking, healthy eating, exercise, and treatment of blood pressure, cholesterol, and diabetes
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} cardiac rehabilitation and regular review to monitor the stented arteries and overall heart health
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Cardiac catheterisation is generally safe, but complications can occur:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bruising, bleeding, or a small haematoma at the catheter entry site
    \item Infection at the entry site, which is uncommon
    \item Damage to the artery used, occasionally requiring repair
    \item Contrast dye causing an allergic reaction or kidney strain, especially in people with existing kidney problems
    \item Irritation of the heart rhythm during the procedure
    \item Rarely, damage to the heart wall, a heart attack, or a stroke
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
In most people the procedure is well tolerated, with recovery measured in hours rather than days. When a heart attack is treated rapidly by opening the blocked artery, survival and salvage of heart muscle improve substantially. For people with stable angina, stenting often relieves symptoms quickly, although lifestyle measures and medication remain important for long-term benefit. Overall outcome depends on the severity of the underlying disease, the presence of other health conditions, and adherence to follow-up treatment.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Tell the team about all medicines, allergies, diabetes, and kidney problems before the procedure
    \item Follow the fasting and medication instructions exactly as given
    \item Drink plenty of fluid after the procedure unless told otherwise
    \item Keep the entry site clean and report any swelling, bleeding, or increasing pain
    \item Take prescribed antiplatelet medicines exactly as directed after stenting
    \item Control blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood sugar; stop smoking and stay physically active
    \item Attend cardiac rehabilitation and follow-up appointments
\end{itemize}
