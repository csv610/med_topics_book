\chapter{Muscle Pain}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Muscle pain, or localized myalgia, refers to sensory discomfort originating directly from muscle tissue, overlying fascia, or musculotendinous junctions. Unlike diffuse or systemic muscle aches, localized muscle pain is typically confined to specific muscle groups or anatomical regions. It is most commonly the result of mechanical trauma, acute strain, repetitive overuse, or localized ischaemia. Physically, muscle pain serves as a protective warning mechanism against tissue overload, manifesting in clinical forms such as acute muscle tears, involuntary spasms, myofascial trigger points, or compartment syndromes. Accurate clinical differentiation of muscle pain from referred joint or nerve pain is essential for selecting appropriate rehabilitative and medical treatments.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Localized muscle pain is a ubiquitous clinical presentation, particularly prevalent among athletes, manual workers, and sedentary individuals. Musculoskeletal injuries, including muscle strains, account for up to 30\% of all sports medicine consultations. High-impact and high-velocity sports (such as football, rugby, and athletics) present the highest risk of acute muscle tears. In occupational settings, localized neck, shoulder, and lower back muscle pain has a lifetime prevalence exceeding 70\%, representing a primary driver of work-related disability and healthcare expenditure.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The underlying mechanisms of localized muscle pain vary depending on whether the injury is acute, chronic, or metabolic in nature.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute muscle strain:} Structural disruption of muscle fibres resulting from excessive tensile force, typically during rapid eccentric contraction when the muscle is actively lengthening under load.
    \item \textbf{Myofascial trigger points:} Hyperirritable nodules situated within taut bands of skeletal muscle fibers. They are associated with localized ischaemia, calcium release abnormalities, and a continuous "energy crisis" at the neuromuscular junction.
    \item \textbf{Muscle spasms and cramps:} Involuntary, sustained motor unit hyperactivity resulting from dehydration, electrolyte depletion, or altered spinal reflex control secondary to muscle fatigue.
    \item \textbf{Muscle contusion:} Direct blunt trauma causing capillary rupture, tissue bleeding, and subsequent inflammatory response within the muscle belly (haematoma).
    \item \textbf{Acute compartment syndrome:} A critical state where tissue pressure within a closed osteofascial compartment exceeds capillary perfusion pressure, leading to muscle and nerve ischaemia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Clinical features are localized to the injured muscle group and reflect the extent of tissue damage and secondary inflammation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Focal tenderness:} Sharp pain elicited by palpating the affected muscle, tendon, or a distinct trigger point.
    \item \textbf{Pain on contraction or stretch:} Exacerbation of pain when the target muscle is actively contracted against resistance or passively stretched.
    \item \textbf{Loss of motor function:} Reduced range of motion and diminished strength in the affected limb or joint due to pain-induced inhibition or structural disruption.
    \item \textbf{Swelling and ecchymosis:} Visible swelling, warmth, and bruising, reflecting internal bleeding and inflammatory exudate.
    \item \textbf{Palpable defect:} A distinct gap in the muscle continuity, which is characteristic of high-grade (Grade 3) muscle ruptures.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
It is critical to distinguish muscle-derived pain from referred pain or vascular emergencies that present with localized limb pain.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tendinopathy or tendon rupture:} Pain localized strictly to the tendon or its bony insertion (e.g., Achilles tendon) rather than the muscle belly.
    \item \textbf{Deep vein thrombosis (DVT):} Presents with unilateral calf pain, swelling, and warmth, mimicking a soleus or gastrocnemius strain but requiring urgent anticoagulation.
    \item \textbf{Radiculopathy:} Compression of a spinal nerve root (e.g., L5 radiculopathy) that refers pain along a dermatome, often presenting as calf or thigh pain.
    \item \textbf{Stress fracture:} Focal bone tenderness and pain during weight-bearing that can be mistaken for deep muscle soreness.
    \item \textbf{Osteomyelitis or soft-tissue infection:} Localized limb pain accompanied by erythema, systemic fever, and elevated inflammatory markers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most minor muscle injuries respond well to self-care, but severe trauma or vascular compromise requires urgent medical review.
\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, worsening pain, tightness, numbness, and coldness in a limb following an injury, indicating possible acute compartment syndrome.
    \item Complete inability to move the affected limb or bear weight on the leg.
    \item Sudden, severe calf swelling, redness, and warmth.
    \item A visible deformity in the muscle belly accompanied by excruciating pain and swelling.
    \item Signs of shock, such as confusion, dizziness, rapid heart rate, or pale skin, following high-energy muscle trauma.
\end{itemize}
Patients should seek non-urgent medical advice if the pain fails to improve after 72 hours of self-management, if they feel a palpable gap in the muscle, or if joint instability is present.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Physical assessment is the primary diagnostic tool, but imaging studies confirm the severity and guide rehabilitation timelines.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic ultrasound:} A cost-effective, real-time imaging modality useful for detecting muscle tears, haematomas, and tendon involvement.
    \item \textbf{Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):} The most sensitive imaging modality, providing detailed anatomical detail to grade muscle strains (Grade 1: mild stretching; Grade 2: partial tear; Grade 3: complete rupture).
    \item \textbf{Plain X-ray:} Performed to rule out associated avulsion fractures, where a tendon pulls away a fragment of bone, or other skeletal injuries.
    \item \textbf{Compartment pressure measurement:} Direct needle manometry utilized in suspected compartment syndrome to measure intracompartmental pressures.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management targets pain relief, tissue healing, and structured recovery of strength and flexibility.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute conservative care (PEACE \& LOVE protocol):} Protect the injury, elevate the limb, avoid anti-inflammatory modalities in the first 48 hours to support natural healing, compress the tissue, and educate the patient; followed by active loading, vascularisation (aerobic exercise), and structured rehabilitation.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological therapies:} Short-term paracetamol or codeine during the acute phase; non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) may be used after the first 48 hours to manage persistent inflammation.
    \item \textbf{Physical therapy and rehabilitation:} Guided eccentric exercises to promote collagen alignment in the healing muscle, restoring dynamic strength and flexibility.
    \item \textbf{Dry needling and myofascial release:} Targeted physical therapies to deactivate hyperirritable trigger points and restore muscle blood flow.
    \item \textbf{Surgical repair:} Indicated for complete muscle ruptures, large haematomas requiring surgical drainage, or emergency fasciotomy in acute compartment syndrome.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Complications may arise from severe initial trauma or inadequate rehabilitation of muscle injuries.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Myositis ossificans:} Calcification and bone formation within the muscle tissue, usually occurring after a severe muscle contusion.
    \item \textbf{Recurrent muscle tears:} Increased vulnerability to repeat injuries due to premature return to sport or accumulation of dense, inelastic scar tissue.
    \item \textbf{Chronic pain and trigger points:} Persistent myofascial pain syndrome characterized by persistent localized tenderness and referred pain.
    \item \textbf{Joint contractures and atrophy:} Muscle shortening and joint stiffness resulting from prolonged splinting or disuse of the limb.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for localized muscle pain is generally highly favourable. Grade 1 strains typically resolve within 1 to 3 weeks, and Grade 2 strains recover within 4 to 8 weeks. Grade 3 complete ruptures require a prolonged course of physical therapy, and if surgical repair is performed, return to pre-injury performance levels may take 3 to 6 months. Adherence to a structured, progressive strengthening programme is the primary factor in ensuring complete structural healing and preventing reinjury.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Appropriate physical conditioning and self-care measures significantly reduce the incidence of muscle injuries.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dynamic warm-ups:} Engaging in active, movement-based warm-up routines prior to exercise to increase muscle temperature and elastic properties.
    \item \textbf{Eccentric strength training:} Integrating eccentric exercises (e.g., Nordic hamstring curls) to build muscle resilience at long lengths.
    \item \textbf{Ergonomic adaptation:} Utilizing ergonomic chairs, adjustable desks, and optimal keyboard heights to reduce postural strain on the neck and back.
    \item \textbf{Structured recovery:} Planning rest periods between high-intensity training sessions to allow for muscle repair and glycogen replenishment.
    \item \textbf{Foam rolling and self-myofascial release:} Using foam rollers or massage balls to relieve localized muscle tension and improve flexibility.
\end{itemize}
