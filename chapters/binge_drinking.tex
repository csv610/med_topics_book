\chapter{Binge Drinking}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Binge drinking is a pattern of alcohol consumption that raises the blood alcohol level to a risky degree within a short period. In Australia it is generally defined as drinking more than four standard drinks on a single occasion.

Binge drinking is distinct from alcohol dependence: people who binge drink may not drink every day and may not be dependent on alcohol. Nevertheless, every binge episode carries immediate risks of injury, poisoning, and impaired decision-making, and a recurrent pattern of binge drinking increases the risk of developing alcohol dependence and long-term physical disease.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Binge drinking is common, particularly among young adults, and is a leading preventable cause of injury and premature death.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prevalence:} roughly 1 in 4 Australians who drink exceed the single-occasion guideline at least monthly, with higher rates among young adults
    \item \textbf{Sex differences:} men are more likely to binge drink than women, although the gap has narrowed in recent decades
    \item \textbf{Age distribution:} drinking to intoxication is widespread among people aged 18 to 29, but risky patterns also occur in older adults, in whom the health consequences are greater
    \item \textbf{Burden of harm:} binge drinking contributes substantially to road crashes, falls, violence, drowning, and unplanned or unsafe sex
\end{itemize}

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Alcohol is absorbed rapidly from the stomach and small intestine into the bloodstream and quickly reaches the brain, where it depresses the central nervous system. The more alcohol consumed within a short period, the higher the peak blood alcohol concentration and the more profound the effect on the brain.

At high concentrations alcohol impairs judgment, reaction time, coordination, speech, and memory. It causes dehydration, irritates the gastric lining, and suppresses the protective reflexes of the airway, increasing the risk of vomiting and aspiration. The blood alcohol level continues to rise after drinking stops, because absorption from the gut continues, so a person may deteriorate after the last drink. Social and environmental factors strongly shape the pattern of binge drinking, including peer pressure, drinking games, the low cost and easy availability of alcohol, celebration culture, stress, and advertising.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features of binge drinking reflect acute intoxication and its after-effects.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute intoxication:} slurred speech, unsteady gait, poor coordination, and slowed reaction times
    \item \textbf{Impaired judgment:} risk-taking behaviour, aggression, and poor decisions
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal symptoms:} nausea, vomiting, and headache
    \item \textbf{Blackouts:} gaps in memory of events that occurred while intoxicated
    \item \textbf{Hangover:} headache, fatigue, nausea, thirst, and poor concentration the following day
    \item \textbf{Alcohol poisoning:} in severe cases, confusion, repeated vomiting, slow or irregular breathing, low body temperature, pale or bluish skin, and unconsciousness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When a person appears intoxicated or is found unconscious, causes other than simple intoxication must be considered.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Head injury:} a fall or assault sustained while drinking may have caused a bleed on the brain, which can mimic or accompany intoxication
    \item \textbf{Stroke or seizure:} neurological events that may be mistaken for drunkenness
    \item \textbf{Mixed drug use:} alcohol combined with other sedatives, such as benzodiazepines, opioids, or recreational drugs, can be fatal at much lower amounts
    \item \textbf{Hypoglycaemia:} low blood sugar in a person with diabetes who has been drinking
    \item \textbf{Infection or metabolic illness:} coinciding with drinking and causing confusion or collapse
    \item \textbf{Alcohol poisoning:} as distinct from ordinary intoxication, and requiring urgent medical care
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if you are unable to control how much you drink, or if binge episodes are causing injuries, blackouts, memory problems, relationship conflict, or difficulties at work or study. Seeking help is also appropriate if you are relying on alcohol to cope with stress, low mood, or anxiety.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for signs of alcohol poisoning:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The person cannot be woken, or is only barely conscious
    \item Slow, irregular, or shallow breathing (fewer than 8 breaths per minute)
    \item Cold, clammy, or bluish skin with a low body temperature
    \item Repeated vomiting, or vomiting while unconscious, which carries a choking risk
    \item Seizures or fitting
    \item Confusion that does not improve, or new confusion after an apparent recovery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} discussion of how much and how often the person drinks, using standard drinks as a measure, together with any related problems
    \item \textbf{Screening questionnaires:} tools such as the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) identify risky drinking and possible dependence
    \item \textbf{Blood alcohol measurement:} breath or blood testing confirms intoxication and its severity
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} liver function tests, blood counts, and blood sugar, particularly with repeated heavy drinking
    \item \textbf{Assessment for injury:} head injury and other trauma must be excluded in anyone found intoxicated
    \item \textbf{Mental health assessment:} screening for depression, anxiety, and other conditions that frequently accompany heavy drinking
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Emergency care:} for alcohol poisoning, protect the airway, monitor breathing, keep the person warm, and seek hospital care; never leave an unconscious person alone
    \item \textbf{Brief intervention:} short, non-judgmental conversations with a general practitioner or counsellor reduce drinking in many people
    \item \textbf{Psychological treatment:} cognitive behaviour therapy and motivational interviewing help people understand and change their drinking behaviour
    \item \textbf{Medication:} for those who develop alcohol dependence, medicines may be prescribed to reduce cravings or discourage drinking
    \item \textbf{Support groups:} organisations such as Alcoholics Anonymous and community counselling services offer ongoing support
    \item \textbf{Treatment of related problems:} management of coexisting mental health conditions, injuries, and the medical effects of alcohol
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immediate harms:} falls, road crashes, drowning, assault, and other injuries; alcohol poisoning; blackouts; unplanned or unsafe sex; and aspiration of vomit
    \item \textbf{Physical disease:} acute pancreatitis, gastritis, liver inflammation, raised blood pressure, and damage to the heart and brain over time
    \item \textbf{Mental health effects:} depression, anxiety, and worsening of pre-existing psychiatric conditions
    \item \textbf{Social consequences:} relationship breakdown, lost productivity, financial problems, and legal consequences such as driving offences
    \item \textbf{Progression to dependence:} binge drinking is a significant risk factor for the later development of alcohol dependence
    \item \textbf{Cancer risk:} alcohol is linked to cancers of the mouth, throat, liver, breast, and bowel
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people who binge drink do not become dependent on alcohol, and the harms of individual episodes are largely preventable.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Most people recover fully from single episodes of intoxication once the alcohol is cleared from the body
    \item Reducing drinking to within low-risk guidelines substantially lowers the risk of injury and long-term disease
    \item Those who develop alcohol dependence can improve substantially with treatment and ongoing support
    \item Repeated binge drinking over many years carries a growing risk of liver, heart, and brain damage
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Follow the Australian guideline of drinking no more than 10 standard drinks per week and no more than 4 standard drinks on any single day
    \item Learn how many standard drinks are in your usual drinks, because many contain more alcohol than people expect
    \item Pace yourself: drink slowly, alternate alcoholic drinks with water or soft drinks, and eat before and while drinking
    \item Avoid drinking games and rounds, which push drinking faster than intended
    \item Arrange safe transport home before you start drinking, and never drive after drinking
    \item If you are trying to cut down, tell friends and family and ask for their support
    \item Seek help early from a doctor or a counselling service if you find it hard to control your drinking
\end{itemize}
