\chapter{Illicit Drug Use}
\index{Illicit Drug Use}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Illicit drug use refers to the use of drugs that are illegal to possess, use, or supply in many countries, including cannabis, methamphetamine (ice), cocaine, heroin, ecstasy (MDMA), and hallucinogens such as LSD. It also covers the misuse of prescription medicines, including opioids, benzodiazepines, and stimulants. People use illicit drugs for many reasons: to feel pleasure, to cope with stress or trauma, to fit in socially, or because of dependence. While some people use drugs occasionally without significant harm, illicit drug use carries serious risks to physical and mental health, and dependence can develop quickly with certain substances. A compassionate, non-judgemental approach from health services helps people seek care, reduce harm, and recover.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Illicit drug use is common in many countries. National surveys show that around two in five people have used an illicit drug at some point in their lives, and roughly one in six has used one in the past year. Cannabis is by far the most widely used, followed by pharmaceuticals used for non-medical purposes, cocaine, ecstasy, and methamphetamine. The health, social, and economic costs of illicit drug use in many countries are estimated at many billions of dollars each year, including healthcare costs, lost productivity, crime, and the toll on families. Rates of use are generally higher in young adults and are influenced by social disadvantage, trauma, and mental illness.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Drug dependence arises from the interaction of the drug with the brain's reward system, individual vulnerability, and the social environment. Most illicit drugs increase dopamine signalling in the brain's reward pathways, producing feelings of pleasure and reinforcement that drive repeated use. With repeated exposure, the brain adapts, so that the same dose produces less effect (tolerance) and stopping produces withdrawal symptoms, both of which fuel continued use. Genetics, childhood adversity, trauma, mental illness, and social factors all influence who develops dependence. Different drugs have different mechanisms: opioids activate specific receptors to relieve pain and produce euphoria, stimulants flood the brain with dopamine, and cannabis acts on the endocannabinoid system.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The effects of illicit drugs depend on the drug, the dose, and the person, but some patterns are common.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intoxication:} euphoria, altered perception, drowsiness, or agitation depending on the substance
    \item \textbf{Physical signs:} dilated or pinpoint pupils, raised or lowered heart rate and blood pressure, sweating, and tremor
    \item \textbf{Withdrawal:} cravings, anxiety, agitation, sweating, nausea, and disturbed sleep when the drug wears off
    \item \textbf{Behavioural changes:} mood swings, irritability, secrecy, financial problems, and withdrawal from family and friends
    \item \textbf{Declining function:} reduced performance at work or school, neglect of responsibilities and self-care
    \item \textbf{Mental health effects:} depression, anxiety, paranoia, and drug-induced psychosis
    \item \textbf{Physical health effects:} weight loss, infections, and complications of the route of use, such as injecting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Drug-related presentations can be mistaken for other conditions, and underlying problems may be driving drug use.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychiatric disorders:} bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, and severe anxiety may present like intoxication or be complicated by drug use
    \item \textbf{Medical conditions:} head injury, seizures, infections, and metabolic disturbances can mimic drug intoxication
    \item \textbf{Adverse medicine reactions:} from prescribed drugs, including corticosteroids and stimulants
    \item \textbf{Alcohol use disorder:} commonly coexists with illicit drug use
    \item \textbf{Neurodegenerative and other organic disorders:} in older people presenting with confusion or behaviour change
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who is concerned about their own or a family member's drug use should seek help; early engagement improves outcomes. GPs, drug and alcohol services, and helplines provide confidential assessment and referral. People using drugs should also be reviewed for blood-borne viruses, mental health problems, and other health effects. Seek urgent help for a panic reaction, severe agitation, or unusual behaviour after drug use.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unconsciousness, unresponsiveness, or difficulty breathing after drug use
    \item Seizures or convulsions
    \item Chest pain, palpitations, or signs of stroke
    \item Very high temperature, agitation, and confusion
    \item Severe aggression, paranoia, or violence
    \item Self-harm or thoughts of suicide
    \item Suspected overdose, including with opioids, where breathing may become dangerously slow
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical interview:} a respectful, non-judgemental history of all substances used, amounts, routes, and consequences
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} vital signs, pupil size, injection sites, and signs of acute intoxication or withdrawal
    \item \textbf{Urine and blood drug screens:} confirm recent use of particular substances when clinically indicated
    \item \textbf{Mental health assessment:} screening for depression, anxiety, psychosis, and suicide risk
    \item \textbf{Blood-borne virus testing:} HIV, hepatitis B and C for people who inject drugs
    \item \textbf{Assessment of dependence and motivation:} structured tools help plan treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Brief intervention:} for people with lower-level use, brief counselling can increase awareness and support change
    \item \textbf{Withdrawal management:} medically supervised detoxification, especially for alcohol, opioids, and benzodiazepines
    \item \textbf{Pharmacotherapy:} evidence-based medicines such as methadone or buprenorphine for opioid dependence
    \item \textbf{Psychological therapy:} cognitive behavioural therapy and related approaches are the core of treatment
    \item \textbf{Residential rehabilitation:} structured live-in programs for people with severe dependence
    \item \textbf{Harm reduction:} needle and syringe programs, naloxone for opioid overdose, and education about safer use
    \item \textbf{Treatment of co-occurring conditions:} mental health care and treatment of hepatitis C and other infections
    \item \textbf{Family involvement and peer support:} improve engagement and long-term outcomes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Overdose and death, particularly with opioids, stimulants, and drug combinations
    \item Dependence and withdrawal, with intense physical and psychological distress
    \item Drug-induced psychosis, depression, anxiety, and suicide
    \item Infections from injecting: HIV, hepatitis B and C, skin abscesses, and heart valve infections
    \item Accidents, violence, and injury while intoxicated
    \item Cardiovascular and neurological damage with stimulant use
    \item Financial hardship, unemployment, homelessness, and family breakdown
    \item Harms to unborn children when drugs are used in pregnancy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Recovery from drug dependence is a process that often involves several attempts and relapses, but long-term outcomes with treatment are good. People who engage with treatment for a sufficient duration, address co-occurring mental health problems, and have social support achieve stable recovery at rates comparable to other chronic conditions. Dependence on some drugs, such as opioids, is increasingly managed as a long-term condition with maintenance pharmacotherapy that allows people to live stable and productive lives. Early intervention and reducing stigma are the most powerful ways to improve outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Talk openly with young people about drugs and their effects, and model healthy coping
    \item Seek help early rather than waiting for a crisis
    \item If using drugs, practise harm reduction: avoid using alone, know the risks, and keep naloxone available for opioids
    \item Never mix drugs, especially with alcohol or sedatives
    \item Use needle and syringe programs if injecting, and never share equipment
    \item Get tested for blood-borne viruses and seek treatment if positive
    \item Access helplines such as the National Alcohol and Other Drug Hotline on 1800 250 015
    \item Support friends and family without judgement and encourage professional help
\end{itemize}
