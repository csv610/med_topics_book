\chapter{Fibromyalgia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Fibromyalgia is a chronic condition characterised by widespread musculoskeletal pain, fatigue, sleep disturbance, and problems with memory and concentration, sometimes called "fibro fog". It is not an inflammatory or autoimmune disease, and standard tests for those conditions are normal; instead, the condition is understood as a problem with how the brain and nervous system process pain signals, amplifying them so that normal sensations are experienced as pain. Fibromyalgia is a real and disabling condition, and its cause involves a combination of genetic, physical, and psychological factors. Although there is no cure, most people improve substantially with the right combination of treatments. This chapter explains the condition, how it is diagnosed, and how it is managed.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Fibromyalgia affects around 2--5\% of the population, making it one of the most common chronic pain conditions, and it is roughly three times more common in women than in men. It can begin at any age, including childhood, and is more common in people with other chronic pain and fatigue conditions such as irritable bowel syndrome, chronic fatigue syndrome, and rheumatoid arthritis. Diagnosis is often delayed for years because symptoms overlap with many other conditions and there is no diagnostic test. The condition causes significant disability and reduced quality of life, yet most people respond to treatment, and outcomes have improved with modern approaches.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Central sensitisation:} the brain and spinal cord amplify pain signals, so that stimuli that are not normally painful are experienced as pain
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} fibromyalgia runs in families, and several genes influencing pain processing have been identified
    \item \textbf{Triggers:} onset is often linked to a physical or emotional stressor, including infections, surgery, injury, trauma, or chronic stress, though it can also develop gradually without a clear trigger
    \item \textbf{Neurotransmitter changes:} altered levels of substances that transmit and dampen pain, including serotonin, noradrenaline, and substance P
    \item \textbf{Sleep disturbance:} poor sleep is both a symptom and a driver, since sleep deprivation amplifies pain
    \item \textbf{Coexisting conditions:} irritable bowel syndrome, headaches, temporomandibular disorders, and anxiety or depression commonly coexist
    \item \textbf{No tissue damage:} joints, muscles, and organs appear normal on examination and investigation; the problem is in pain processing, not in the tissues
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Widespread pain on both sides of the body, above and below the waist, lasting at least 3 months
    \item Fatigue that is profound and not relieved by sleep
    \item Unrefreshing sleep, insomnia, and waking tired
    \item Cognitive difficulties: poor concentration, memory lapses, and mental "fog"
    \item Tender points and generalised tenderness to touch
    \item Morning stiffness, headaches, and muscle aches
    \item Symptoms of irritable bowel, bladder symptoms, and sensitivity to light, noise, and temperature
    \item Fluctuating course, with good and bad days, and flares triggered by stress, poor sleep, or overactivity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because fibromyalgia has no specific test, other conditions must be considered and excluded.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hypothyroidism, which causes pain, fatigue, and cognitive symptoms
    \item Rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, and other inflammatory diseases
    \item Polymyalgia rheumatica, particularly in older adults
    \item Chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS), with overlapping fatigue but less prominent pain
    \item Depression and anxiety, which can cause pain and fatigue and often coexist
    \item Chronic infections and malignancy in appropriate contexts
    \item Neurological conditions causing pain, such as peripheral neuropathy
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with chronic widespread pain and fatigue should see a doctor for assessment. Early diagnosis, even though it may take time, allows treatment to begin and reduces the anxiety of unexplained symptoms.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe pain, weakness, or numbness that is new or different from the usual symptoms
    \item Chest pain or breathlessness
    \item Suicidal thoughts, which are more common in people with chronic pain and should always be taken seriously
    \item Any emergency symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical diagnosis:} fibromyalgia is diagnosed from the history of widespread pain, fatigue, sleep problems, and cognitive symptoms, using accepted criteria; examination shows tenderness but no inflammation
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} used to exclude other conditions, including full blood count, thyroid function, inflammatory markers, and vitamin D
    \item \textbf{Rarely other tests:} imaging and further investigations are only performed when specific conditions are suspected
    \item \textbf{Assessment of coexisting conditions:} screening for irritable bowel syndrome, headaches, and mood disorders
    \item \textbf{The diagnosis is positive:} once other conditions are excluded, the diagnosis is made on the pattern of symptoms, and it is a valid and useful label
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Exercise:} the most effective treatment; gradual, regular activity such as walking, swimming, or gentle strength work improves pain, fatigue, and mood
    \item \textbf{Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT):} helps reframe pain-related thoughts and build coping strategies
    \item \textbf{Pacing:} balancing activity and rest to avoid the boom-and-bust cycle of overdoing it and crashing
    \item \textbf{Sleep improvement:} consistent sleep routines and treatment of sleep problems
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} certain antidepressants (amitriptyline, duloxetine), pregabalin, and some pain medicines reduce symptoms in some people; simple analgesics and NSAIDs generally help little
    \item \textbf{Stress management:} mindfulness, relaxation, and psychological support
    \item \textbf{Manual therapies:} physiotherapy, massage, and acupuncture help some people as part of a broader program
    \item \textbf{Self-management programs:} structured group courses improve outcomes and are available through many health services
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reduced ability to work, study, and participate in family life
    \item Depression and anxiety, which affect a large proportion of people
    \item Sleep deprivation and its effects on health
    \item Weight gain from inactivity
    \item Social isolation and relationship strain
    \item Dependence on strong painkillers, which are generally ineffective and harmful in fibromyalgia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Fibromyalgia is a chronic condition, but it is not progressive in the sense of damaging tissues, and most people improve significantly with treatment. Studies show that regular exercise, CBT, and pacing produce meaningful reductions in pain and fatigue, and many people return to work and active lives. The course fluctuates, with flares and remissions, and symptoms rarely disappear completely. The strongest predictors of a good outcome are early diagnosis, an engaged patient, and a comprehensive, active treatment plan rather than a passive reliance on medicines.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Start a gentle, graded exercise program and build up gradually
    \item Establish a regular sleep routine and protect sleep time
    \item Learn pacing: plan activity, take breaks, and avoid overdoing it on good days
    \item Practise relaxation and mindfulness regularly, not only during flares
    \item Eat a balanced diet and avoid alcohol and excess caffeine
    \item Seek psychological support for the emotional burden of chronic pain
    \item Join a support group or self-management program
    \item Work with a doctor to review medicines regularly and avoid strong opioids
\end{itemize}
