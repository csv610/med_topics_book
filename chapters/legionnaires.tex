\chapter{Legionnaires}
\index{Legionnaires}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Legionnaires' disease, also known as legionellosis, is a severe and potentially life-threatening form of atypical pneumonia caused by Gram-negative, aerobic, rod-shaped bacteria belonging to the genus \textit{Legionella}, most commonly \textit{Legionella pneumophila}. The infection is contracted through the inhalation of aerosolised water droplets or, less commonly, through the aspiration of water contaminated with the bacteria. Legionnaires' disease does not spread via person-to-person transmission. The disease is characterised by severe pulmonary consolidation accompanied by systemic symptoms, including gastrointestinal distress and neurological dysfunction. It represents a significant public health concern due to its capacity to cause outbreaks linked to poorly maintained artificial water systems.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Legionnaires' disease occurs worldwide, both sporadically and as outbreaks. In many countries, it is a nationally notifiable condition, with several hundred cases reported annually. The disease predominantly affects middle-aged and older adults, with a higher incidence in males than females (ratio of approximately 2:1). Major risk factors include cigarette smoking, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes mellitus, renal or hepatic impairment, and immunocompromised states (such as those receiving corticosteroid therapy or post-transplantation immunosuppression). Outbreaks are frequently associated with complex water systems, such as air-conditioning cooling towers, evaporative condensers, spa pools, misting machines, and dental water lines. In many countries, a distinct pattern exists where a significant proportion of cases are caused by \textit{Legionella longbeachae}, which is contracted through exposure to commercial potting mixes, compost, and garden soils.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The pathogenesis of Legionnaires' disease involves the inhalation of the bacteria and its subsequent survival and replication inside host cells.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Legionella pneumophila:} The species responsible for approximately 85\% to 90\% of Legionnaires' cases globally, particularly serogroup 1. It thrives in warm water environments between 20\textdegree C and 50\textdegree C.
    \item \textbf{Legionella longbeachae:} A species commonly found in soil and compost, accounting for nearly half of the cases in many countries and New Zealand.
    \item \textbf{Intracellular replication:} Upon inhalation into the alveoli, the bacteria are phagocytosed by alveolar macrophages. Instead of being destroyed, the bacteria inhibit phagosome-lysosome fusion and replicate within the specialized vacuole.
    \item \textbf{Cell lysis and inflammatory response:} Once the vacuole is filled, the host cell lyses, releasing bacteria to infect adjacent cells. This triggers a robust host inflammatory response, leading to recruitment of neutrophils and macrophages, causing extensive alveolar exudate and lobar consolidation.
    \item \textbf{Environmental colonization:} The bacteria survive in natural and artificial aquatic environments by living within biofilms or replicating inside free-living protozoa (such as amoebae), which shields them from standard chlorination and thermal disinfection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The incubation period for Legionnaires' disease is typically two to ten days, though it can extend up to two weeks. The onset is usually abrupt, presenting with a prodromal phase of flu-like symptoms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item High fever (frequently exceeding 39\textdegree C to 40\textdegree C) accompanied by rigors and profuse sweating.
    \item Cough, which is initially non-productive but may later become productive of mucoid or purulent sputum.
    \item Dyspnoea (shortness of breath) and pleuritic chest pain.
    \item Gastrointestinal symptoms, particularly watery diarrhoea, abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting, which often precede the respiratory symptoms.
    \item Neurological signs, including confusion, delirium, headache, lethargy, and ataxia, which can be disproportionately severe relative to the pulmonary features.
    \item Myalgia (muscle pain), arthralgia (joint pain), and profound malaise.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Legionnaires' disease must be differentiated from other causes of community-acquired and hospital-acquired pneumonia, as well as non-infectious pulmonary conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Streptococcus pneumoniae pneumonia:} The most common cause of bacterial pneumonia, presenting with rapid onset of cough, rusty sputum, and lobar consolidation, responding well to beta-lactam antibiotics.
    \item \textbf{Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia:} A common cause of atypical pneumonia, particularly in younger cohorts, presenting with a gradual onset of dry cough, headache, and systemic symptoms, but lacking the prominent gastrointestinal and neurological features of Legionnaires'.
    \item \textbf{Psittacosis:} An atypical pneumonia caused by \textit{Chlamydia psittaci}, linked to contact with infected birds, presenting with fever, severe headache, dry cough, and splenomegaly.
    \item \textbf{Q fever:} Caused by \textit{Coxiella burnetii}, associated with exposure to farm animals, presenting with high fever, atypical pneumonia, severe headache, and hepatitis.
    \item \textbf{Viral pneumonias (Influenza, COVID-19, RSV):} Viral infections presenting with coryza, cough, fever, and respiratory distress, confirmed by molecular PCR testing.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Immediate medical attention should be sought if a person develops symptoms of pneumonia, particularly if they have recently handled potting mix or been in areas associated with cooling tower outbreaks.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe shortness of breath, rapid breathing, or gasping for air.
    \item Bluish discolouration of the lips, tongue, or face (cyanosis), indicating low oxygen levels.
    \item Sudden onset of severe confusion, disorientation, or inability to wake up.
    \item Persistent chest pain or pressure, especially when breathing or coughing.
    \item Signs of shock, such as cold, clammy skin, a weak or rapid pulse, and severe dizziness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Confirming the diagnosis of Legionnaires' disease requires specific laboratory tests, as it cannot be distinguished from other pneumonias based on clinical features or chest X-rays alone.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urinary antigen test:} A rapid and widely used test that detects the \textit{Legionella pneumophila} serogroup 1 antigen. It is highly specific but will not detect other serogroups or \textit{Legionella longbeachae}.
    \item \textbf{Sputum or bronchoalveolar lavage culture:} Culturing the organism on buffered charcoal yeast extract (BCYE) agar is the diagnostic gold standard. It allows for the detection of all \textit{Legionella} species and serogroups, though it takes three to five days.
    \item \textbf{Polymerase chain reaction (PCR):} High-sensitivity molecular assays performed on sputum, lower respiratory tract secretions, or throat swabs to rapidly detect \textit{Legionella} DNA.
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} Typically demonstrates patchy, segmental, or lobar consolidation that can progress rapidly to bilateral involvement or pleural effusions.
    \item \textbf{Blood biochemistry:} Often reveals characteristic laboratory abnormalities, notably severe hyponatraemia (low sodium levels), elevated inflammatory markers (CRP and ESR), elevated creatine kinase (CK), and mild transaminitis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management involves prompt administration of targeted antimicrobial therapy alongside supportive care. Beta-lactam antibiotics are ineffective because the bacteria are intracellular.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Targeted antibiotic therapy:} Macrolides (such as azithromycin or clarithromycin) or respiratory fluoroquinolones (such as levofloxacin or ciprofloxacin) are the treatments of choice. Therapy typically lasts 7 to 14 days, but may be extended in immunocompromised patients.
    \item \textbf{Oxygen therapy and ventilatory support:} Administered to maintain adequate oxygen saturation. In cases of severe respiratory failure, non-invasive positive pressure ventilation or mechanical ventilation in an intensive care unit is required.
    \item \textbf{Intravenous fluid resuscitation:} Used to correct dehydration, manage electrolyte disturbances (such as hyponatraemia), and maintain haemodynamic stability.
    \item \textbf{Symptomatic therapies:} Antipyretics (such as paracetamol) to manage fever and discomfort, and chest physiotherapy to assist with clearance of secretions.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), a life-threatening inflammatory injury to the lungs causing severe hypoxia.
    \item Septic shock, characterised by persistent hypotension and poor organ perfusion despite fluid resuscitation.
    \item Acute kidney injury (AKI) or acute renal failure, which may occur due to hypotension, sepsis, or direct rhabdomyolysis-induced tubular damage.
    \item Multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), involving hepatic, renal, haematological, and cardiovascular systems.
    \item Neurological sequelae, including long-term cognitive impairment, persistent ataxia, or peripheral neuropathies in severe cases.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of Legionnaires' disease depends heavily on the patient's baseline health status, age, and how quickly appropriate antibiotic treatment is initiated. In previously healthy individuals who receive prompt treatment, the prognosis is generally good, although complete recovery and resolution of pulmonary changes on X-ray can take several weeks to months. The mortality rate is approximately 5\% to 10\% for community-acquired cases. However, in patients who acquire the infection in a hospital environment (nosocomial) or those who are severely immunocompromised, the mortality rate can exceed 30\% to 50\%, particularly if targeted therapy is delayed.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wear a P2 or N95 face mask and protective gloves when opening bags of potting mix, compost, or soil to prevent inhalation of bacterial dust.
    \item Wet potting mix or soil slightly with a gentle spray of water before handling to suppress dust formation.
    \item Wash hands thoroughly with soap and water after gardening or handling soil products.
    \item Regularly clean, maintain, and chemically treat cooling towers, evaporative air coolers, and public spa pools according to local public health regulations.
    \item Avoid smoking, as it impairs the mucociliary escalator and phagocytic function of alveolar macrophages, increasing susceptibility to infection.
    \item Flush hot and cold water outlets (such as showers and taps) in unused or recently vacant properties for several minutes before use.
\end{itemize}
