\section*{Definition and Overview}
Colposcopy is a procedure that allows a doctor to examine the cervix, vagina, and vulva closely using a colposcope -- a special magnifying instrument with a bright light. It is usually performed after an abnormal cervical screening (Pap) test or a positive HPV test, to look for areas of abnormal cells and decide whether they need treatment. Colposcopy does not mean that cancer is present; most women who have the procedure turn out to have mild or no significant abnormality. During the procedure the doctor may take small samples of tissue (biopsies) from areas that look abnormal so they can be examined under a microscope.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Colposcopy is one of the most common gynaecological procedures. Most women invited to colposcopy have abnormal cervical screening results, which are found in a small percentage of screening tests. Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer in women worldwide, but organised screening and HPV vaccination have greatly reduced the number of abnormal results and cancers in countries with these programs. Women are usually referred for colposcopy between the ages of 20 and 65, the years covered by screening programs, though the procedure may also be used to investigate bleeding, warts, or unusual symptoms at any age.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Colposcopy itself is a diagnostic examination rather than a disease, but it is carried out because of conditions affecting the cervix and lower genital tract:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Persistent HPV infection:} human papillomavirus, the cause of virtually all cervical pre-cancer and cancer, is detected on screening; most infections clear on their own, but persistent infection with high-risk types can cause cell changes
    \item \textbf{Cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN):} pre-cancerous changes in the cells lining the cervix, graded CIN 1, 2, or 3 according to how much of the lining is affected
    \item \textbf{Abnormal screening cytology:} cells reported as low- or high-grade abnormalities that need assessment
    \item \textbf{Symptoms:} unusual bleeding after sex, between periods, or after menopause, and visible genital warts or other growths
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} assessment after treatment for cervical abnormality to check the area has healed and no abnormal cells remain
\end{itemize}

When abnormal cells are found, a small area of the cervical lining is examined and sampled so the severity can be confirmed under the microscope.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Colposcopy is performed to investigate or confirm certain findings, and the woman usually has no symptoms. The features that lead to the referral include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item An abnormal result on a cervical screening test or a positive high-risk HPV test
    \item Cells seen on screening that are described as low-grade, high-grade, or atypical
    \item Bleeding after intercourse, between periods, or after the menopause
    \item Unusual vaginal discharge or growths on the cervix or vulva
    \item A cervix that looks abnormal or bleeds on contact during a routine examination
    \item A need to check the result of previous treatment to the cervix
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Abnormal screening results and symptoms may reflect a range of conditions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inflammation (cervicitis):} infection or irritation causing the smear to appear abnormal
    \item \textbf{Infections:} vaginal or cervical infection (thrush, chlamydia, gonorrhoea) causing discharge or bleeding
    \item \textbf{HPV-related change:} low-grade changes that usually clear without treatment
    \item \textbf{CIN (pre-cancer):} true pre-cancerous change requiring treatment at grades 2 and 3
    \item \textbf{Invasive cervical cancer:} rare on screening but the main reason the procedure is done
    \item \textbf{Endometrial or vaginal pathology:} polyps, fibroids, or abnormalities higher in the genital tract that cause similar bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor before your colposcopy appointment if you develop new symptoms or are worried about the procedure:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Unusual bleeding after sex, between periods, or after the menopause
    \item A change in vaginal discharge, especially with an unpleasant odour or itching
    \item Pelvic or abdominal pain
    \item Any doubt about your appointment, medicines, or instructions
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 or seek urgent care if, after the procedure, you develop:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heavy bleeding that soaks a pad within an hour or does not settle
    \item Severe pelvic pain that is getting worse
    \item Fever or a feeling of being unwell after a biopsy
    \item Foul-smelling discharge after the procedure (possible infection)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The colposcopy procedure itself is the main investigation, and it includes several steps:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Preparation:} the woman lies on her back with legs in supports, and a speculum is inserted to open the vagina so the cervix can be seen
    \item \textbf{Colposcope examination:} the magnified cervix is inspected under a bright light; a weak vinegar solution (acetic acid) is applied, which makes abnormal cells appear white
    \item \textbf{Schiller's test:} an iodine solution may be painted on the cervix; abnormal tissue fails to stain normally
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} small samples are taken from abnormal-looking areas; a local anaesthetic may be used, and there may be some cramping and light bleeding afterwards
    \item \textbf{Histology:} the biopsy samples are examined under a microscope to grade any cell changes and exclude cancer
    \item \textbf{Decision:} based on the appearance and biopsy results, the woman is either reassured, treated, or brought back for follow-up
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is based on the colposcopy and biopsy findings:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No abnormality:} return to routine screening, usually within 12 months or at the normal interval
    \item \textbf{Low-grade change (CIN 1):} observation with repeat colposcopy, as most of these changes regress on their own
    \item \textbf{High-grade change (CIN 2--3):} treatment to remove the abnormal area, most commonly LLETZ (a loop of fine wire that excises the affected tissue) or rarely a cone biopsy
    \item \textbf{Treatment methods:} LLETZ under local anaesthetic, or cold coagulation and laser therapy for surface destruction of small abnormalities
    \item \textbf{Invasive cancer:} referral to a specialist cancer centre for staging and treatment
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} repeat HPV and screening tests are arranged because women who have been treated remain at slightly higher risk
    \item \textbf{Support:} explanation, reassurance, and counselling where anxiety about the result is significant
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Colposcopy is generally very safe, but the examination and its treatment carry some risks:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Mild bleeding and a watery, dark discharge for a few days after biopsy or LLETZ
    \item Infection of the treated area, causing pain, fever, or smelly discharge
    \item Heavier bleeding, uncommon but possible after treatment, requiring further attention
    \item Rarely, narrowing of the cervical opening (stenosis), which may affect later periods or fertility checks
    \item Some treatments slightly increase the risk of early labour or cervical weakness in a future pregnancy; this is discussed before treatment
    \item The emotional impact of an abnormal result and the possibility of cancer, even when the outcome is reassuring
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For most women, colposcopy provides reassurance. Low-grade changes clear without treatment in a large proportion of cases within a year or two. High-grade changes (CIN 2--3) are treated effectively in the great majority of women, and treatment prevents most of these from ever becoming cancer. Regular follow-up after treatment is important, because the risk of later abnormality is slightly higher than in women who never had changes. When invasive cancer is found, the outlook depends mainly on the stage at diagnosis, which is another reason timely colposcopy after an abnormal result matters.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend cervical screening when invited, and follow up abnormal results promptly rather than delaying
    \item Have the HPV vaccine (ideally before sexual activity begins), which prevents most cervical pre-cancer and cancer
    \item Stop smoking, as smoking increases the chance that an HPV infection will progress to abnormal cells
    \item Use condoms to reduce the risk of new HPV infection
    \item If you have had treatment, attend all scheduled follow-up appointments
    \item After a biopsy or treatment, avoid tampons, swimming, and intercourse for the time advised, usually one to two weeks
    \item Seek help early for unusual bleeding or discharge rather than waiting for the next screening round
\end{itemize}
