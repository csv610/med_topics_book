\chapter{First Aid}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
First aid is the immediate help given to a person who is injured or suddenly unwell, before professional medical help arrives. Its goals are to preserve life, prevent the condition from worsening, promote recovery, and provide reassurance. First aid covers a wide range of situations, from minor cuts and burns to unconsciousness, choking, heart attacks, and severe bleeding. Effective first aid depends on a small set of simple, remembered skills: checking for danger, calling for help, managing the airway and breathing, controlling bleeding, and placing an unconscious person in the recovery position. Everyone can learn these skills, and doing so genuinely saves lives. This chapter outlines the core principles of first aid and the key responses for common emergencies.

\section*{Epidemiology}
First aid is relevant to everyone: most Australians will face a situation requiring first aid at some point, and the majority of cardiac arrests, choking episodes, and severe injuries happen in the home or community, not in hospitals. Bystander CPR more than doubles the chance of survival after cardiac arrest, yet it is performed in only a minority of out-of-hospital arrests. Similarly, first aid for choking, severe bleeding, and burns measurably improves outcomes. Programs such as Australian Red Cross and St John Ambulance train hundreds of thousands of people each year, and workplace first aid requirements ensure many Australians hold current certificates.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
First aid works by addressing the immediate threats to life.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Airway:} an obstructed airway prevents breathing; in an unconscious person, the tongue can fall back and block the throat
    \item \textbf{Breathing:} cardiac arrest stops breathing and circulation; without oxygen, the brain is damaged within minutes
    \item \textbf{Circulation:} severe bleeding depletes the blood volume, leading to shock and cardiac arrest if not controlled
    \item \textbf{The chain of survival:} early recognition, early CPR, early defibrillation, and early advanced care give the best outcome in cardiac arrest
    \item \textbf{The recovery position:} side-lying keeps the airway open and lets vomit or fluid drain from an unconscious person who is breathing
    \item \textbf{Choking:} a foreign body blocks the airway; back blows and abdominal thrusts expel it by raising pressure in the airway
    \item \textbf{Burns:} cooling stops the burn progressing into deeper tissue
    \item \textbf{The first minutes:} the time before ambulance arrival is when first aid matters most, and simple actions change survival
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
First aid responses follow a standard sequence.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Check for danger to yourself, the casualty, and bystanders before acting
    \item Check the casualty's response: call their name, gently squeeze their shoulders; if no response, call 000
    \item Check airway, breathing, and circulation, opening the airway by tilting the head back
    \item If not breathing normally: start CPR, 30 compressions to 2 breaths, at a rate of 100--120 compressions per minute, and use an AED if available
    \item If breathing normally: place in the recovery position and monitor
    \item Control severe bleeding with firm, direct pressure and a dressing
    \item Cool burns with running water for 20 minutes
    \item Provide reassurance and keep the person warm while waiting for help
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Different emergencies require different first aid, and assessment guides the response.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unconsciousness: determine whether breathing normally (recovery position) or not (CPR)
    \item Chest pain: treat as a possible heart attack; aspirin if advised and available, rest, and call 000
    \item Choking: mild (coughing, breathing) versus severe (unable to breathe, blue, silent), which needs back blows and abdominal thrusts
    \item Severe bleeding versus minor bleeding, which needs only cleaning and a dressing
    \item Burns: assess size and depth to decide between home care and emergency care
    \item Seizures versus fainting, which are managed differently
    \item The key skill is sorting the emergency from the non-emergency and acting accordingly
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Call 000 for any life-threatening emergency: unconsciousness, not breathing, severe chest pain, severe bleeding, choking, or any situation where the person's condition is rapidly deteriorating. For less urgent problems, see a doctor or pharmacist the same day or as advised.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item An unconscious or unresponsive person
    \item A person not breathing or breathing abnormally
    \item Chest pain, especially with sweating, breathlessness, or nausea
    \item Severe bleeding that soaks dressings or does not stop with pressure
    \item Choking that is severe or not relieved
    \item Fitting (seizure) lasting more than 5 minutes
    \item Drowning, serious burns, head injury, or poisoning
    \item Any situation in which you are worried the person may be dying
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Danger check:} ensure the scene is safe before approaching
    \item \textbf{Response check:} voice and touch to establish consciousness
    \item \textbf{Airway and breathing check:} look, listen, and feel for breathing, and assess the chest rising
    \item \textbf{Circulation check:} control bleeding and assess signs of shock
    \item \textbf{Continuous monitoring:} reassess the breathing of an unconscious person regularly, as it can stop at any time
    \item \textbf{Handover:} give the ambulance officers a clear account of what happened, what was done, and any medicines the person takes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CPR:} 30 compressions to 2 breaths for adults, children, and infants (with adjustments for infants); push hard and fast in the centre of the chest; use an AED as soon as it is available
    \item \textbf{Recovery position:} roll an unconscious, breathing person onto their side, tilt the head back, and monitor breathing
    \item \textbf{Bleeding control:} apply firm, direct pressure with a clean dressing; do not remove soaked dressings, add more on top; elevate the limb where possible
    \item \textbf{Choking:} encourage coughing; if severe, give up to 5 back blows then up to 5 abdominal thrusts, alternating
    \item \textbf{Burns:} cool with running water for 20 minutes, cover with cling film or a clean dressing, and do not burst blisters or apply creams
    \item \textbf{Seizures:} protect the head, move objects away, time the seizure, and place in the recovery position afterwards
    \item \textbf{Poisoning:} do not induce vomiting; call the Poisons Information Centre (13 11 26) and follow advice
    \item \textbf{Reassurance:} stay calm, keep the person warm and still, and communicate what is happening
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Failure to act because of fear of doing it wrong, when even imperfect first aid helps
    \item Worsening of injury or illness when first aid is delayed
    \item Brain damage and death when CPR is not started promptly in cardiac arrest
    \item Infection in wounds and burns that are not properly cleaned
    \item Hypothermia in casualties exposed to cold while waiting for help
    \item Injury to the first aider when the scene is not checked for danger
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
First aid saves lives. Bystander CPR doubles or triples survival from cardiac arrest, immediate pressure stops most fatal bleeding, and prompt cooling markedly improves burn outcomes. Even basic first aid, such as putting an unconscious person in the recovery position and calling for help, prevents many avoidable deaths. The skills are simple, take a few hours to learn, and are quickly refreshed. People who have done a first aid course are calmer and more effective in emergencies, and their presence at the scene is one of the strongest predictors of a good outcome.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Complete a first aid course and update it every few years
    \item Keep a first aid kit at home, in the car, and at work, and know what is in it
    \item Learn the current CPR guidelines, including hands-only CPR
    \item Teach children the basics of calling for help and their home address
    \item Rehearse responses to the most likely emergencies in your setting
    \item Keep the Poisons Information number (13 11 26) and emergency numbers visible
    \item After giving first aid, seek professional review for anything beyond the most minor injury
    \item Look after your own safety and wellbeing during and after an emergency
\end{itemize}
