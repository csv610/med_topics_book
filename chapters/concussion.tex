\section*{Definition and Overview}
Concussion is a mild traumatic brain injury caused by a blow to the head, or by a force that shakes the head and brain, such as a fall, a collision in sport, or a motor vehicle accident. The brain moves rapidly inside the skull, stretching nerve fibres and temporarily disrupting normal brain function. Concussion is considered "mild" because it usually does not cause structural damage visible on standard brain scans, but it still requires proper care because symptoms can be significant and repeated concussions are dangerous. It is one of the most common types of head injury, especially in sport.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Concussion is common across all ages. It occurs frequently in contact and collision sports such as rugby, Australian football, soccer, and boxing, and is also common in falls, road traffic crashes, and assaults. Children, adolescents, and older adults are at higher risk from falls. Many concussions go unreported, especially in sport, because symptoms are subtle or the person does not want to leave the game. Most people recover within a few weeks, but a significant minority have symptoms that persist.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} the head is struck directly, or the body is hit so that the head whips forward and back, causing the brain to strike the inside of the skull
    \item \textbf{Axonal stretching:} the force stretches and damages nerve fibres (axons), slowing the passage of signals within the brain
    \item \textbf{Metabolic disruption:} injured brain cells require extra energy at a time when blood flow to the brain is reduced, leaving the brain vulnerable
    \item \textbf{Chemical imbalance:} shifts in ions and neurotransmitters temporarily disturb normal brain activity
    \item \textbf{Recovery phase:} symptoms reflect the brain trying to restore its normal balance; doing too much too soon can slow this process
    \item \textbf{Repeated injury:} sustaining a second concussion while still recovering carries serious risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms usually appear soon after the injury, though some are delayed:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Headache, a feeling of pressure, or a dull ache in the head
    \item Dizziness, balance problems, or feeling unsteady
    \item Confusion, feeling "in a fog", or difficulty concentrating
    \item Memory problems, such as forgetting what happened just before or after the injury
    \item Nausea or vomiting (usually once or twice, not persistent)
    \item Blurred or double vision, and sensitivity to light or noise
    \item Slowed reaction time and clumsiness
    \item Irritability, sadness, or unusual emotional reactions
    \item Sleep disturbance: sleeping much more, much less, or having trouble getting to sleep
    \item Brief loss of consciousness (occurring in a minority of cases)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item More serious brain injury, such as bleeding around or inside the brain (intracranial haemorrhage)
    \item Skull fracture
    \item Injury to the neck or cervical spine, which often accompanies head injury
    \item Vestibular problems causing dizziness, such as inner ear disturbance
    \item Migraine, which can be triggered by head injury
    \item Intoxication with alcohol or drugs, which can mimic concussion
    \item Hypoglycaemia, dehydration, or seizure, in the right circumstances
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any suspected concussion should be assessed by a doctor, and a person who has been knocked out, or who has confusion lasting more than a few minutes, should be seen promptly.

\textbf{Call 000 for red-flag symptoms suggesting a more serious brain injury:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Worsening headache or a headache that will not settle
    \item Repeated vomiting
    \item A seizure or fit
    \item Weakness, numbness, or loss of movement in an arm or leg
    \item Slurred speech, unequal pupils, or difficulty staying awake
    \item Confusion or drowsiness that gets worse
    \item Loss of consciousness, especially lasting more than a minute or two
    \item Unusual behaviour or agitation
\end{itemize}
Babies, older adults, and anyone taking blood-thinning medication should be assessed very promptly after any significant blow to the head.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} details of how the injury happened, whether there was loss of consciousness, and any previous concussions
    \item \textbf{Neurological examination:} checking pupils, reflexes, coordination, balance, and strength
    \item \textbf{Cognitive screening:} brief tests of memory, concentration, and orientation (e.g.\ the SCAT tool in sport)
    \item \textbf{CT scan of the head:} only when red flags or risk factors are present (such as older age, anticoagulant use, or a dangerous mechanism of injury), to exclude bleeding
    \item \textbf{Observation:} many people are observed at home by a responsible adult for the first 24--48 hours
    \item \textbf{Specialist review:} referral if symptoms persist, for assessment of vestibular, vision, or cognitive problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Relative rest:} 24--48 hours of physical and cognitive rest (limited screens, reading, and schoolwork), without being confined to bed
    \item \textbf{Graded return to activity:} a stepwise return to normal daily activities, then to study and exercise, increasing activity only while symptoms remain controlled
    \item \textbf{Symptom management:} paracetamol as needed for headache, and simple measures such as rest in a quiet, dim room
    \item \textbf{Graduated return to sport:} a formal staged protocol, with each stage requiring symptoms to have settled before progressing
    \item \textbf{School and work adjustments:} temporary reductions in workload, extra time, or breaks during the recovery period
    \item \textbf{Avoid:} alcohol, recreational drugs, and any activity that risks another blow to the head during recovery
    \item \textbf{Specialist rehabilitation:} vestibular or vision therapy, and psychological support, for people with persistent symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Post-concussion syndrome: symptoms such as headache, dizziness, and poor concentration lasting longer than a few weeks
    \item Second impact syndrome: a rare but dangerous swelling of the brain if a second concussion occurs before the first has healed
    \item Cumulative effects of repeated concussions, including lasting cognitive or mood problems
    \item Depression and anxiety after concussion
    \item Longer recovery in people with a history of previous concussions, migraine, or mental health conditions
    \item Increased risk of further injury if the person returns to sport too early
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people -- around 8 in 10 -- recover fully within 2 weeks of a concussion, although children and adolescents may take a little longer. Recovery is faster when the person rests early and returns gradually to activity. A minority continue to have symptoms for weeks or months and benefit from specialist assessment. The vast majority of people who recover fully have no lasting brain damage, and returning to normal life and sport is safe once cleared by a doctor or qualified health professional.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wear an appropriately fitted helmet for cycling, motorcycling, horse riding, skiing, and contact or collision sports
    \item Follow the rules of the game and avoid dangerous tackling or heading techniques, especially in children
    \item Use seatbelts and appropriate child restraints in vehicles
    \item Reduce fall risk at home, particularly for older adults and young children
    \item Never return to sport, work, or driving until medically cleared
    \item Do not take part in contact sport for a time after any concussion, in line with current guidelines
    \item Strengthen the neck and practise safe landing and falling techniques where relevant to your sport
\end{itemize}
