\chapter{Dental Erosion}
\index{Dental Erosion}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dental erosion is the loss of tooth enamel caused by acid attacking the teeth, without the involvement of bacteria. Unlike decay, which is caused by acids produced from sugar by plaque bacteria, erosion is caused directly by acidic foods and drinks or by stomach acid reaching the teeth. The enamel progressively wears away, making teeth thinner, sensitive, and more prone to damage. Dental erosion is increasingly common, particularly in young people, and is largely preventable. This chapter covers the causes, signs, and management of dental erosion.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Dental erosion is common and increasing, especially among children, teenagers, and young adults. Studies in many countries and other countries find signs of erosion in a significant proportion of adolescents and adults. The rise is linked to the growing consumption of acidic drinks such as soft drinks, sports drinks, fruit juices, and energy drinks, as well as diets high in acidic foods. Gastro-oesophageal reflux and eating disorders are important causes in affected adults. Erosion is more common in people who sip acidic drinks over long periods or swish drinks around the mouth.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acidic drinks:} soft drinks, sports drinks, energy drinks, fruit juice, cordial, and sparkling water with added acid dissolve enamel
    \item \textbf{Acidic foods:} citrus fruits, vinegar, and some other foods contribute
    \item \textbf{Stomach acid:} gastro-oesophageal reflux disease and frequent vomiting from eating disorders or pregnancy expose teeth to strong acid
    \item \textbf{Habits:} swishing, holding, or sipping acidic drinks slowly increases exposure
    \item \textbf{Saliva:} reduced saliva (dry mouth) removes protection against acid, increasing erosion risk
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} acid dissolves the mineral of enamel; without repair, the softened surface wears away with brushing and chewing
    \item \textbf{Age:} erosion accumulates over a lifetime
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Teeth appear thinner, shorter, or transparent, especially at the edges of the front teeth
    \item Cupping or hollowing of the chewing surfaces of back teeth
    \item Smooth, shiny, or yellowed appearance as enamel thins and dentine shows through
    \item Sensitivity to hot, cold, sweet, or sour foods and drinks
    \item Rounded or notched edges on the incisors
    \item Increased risk of chipping, fracture, and decay
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Tooth decay, which is caused by bacterial acid from sugar and appears as brown or white spots and cavities
    \item Tooth wear from brushing too hard (abrasion) or from grinding and clenching (attrition)
    \item Fluorosis, which causes white or brown enamel markings
    \item Developmental enamel defects such as hypoplasia
    \item Ageing changes, which cause gradual enamel wear
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who notices their teeth thinning, becoming sensitive, or changing appearance should see a dentist. People with reflux, vomiting, or eating disorders should discuss dental protection with their doctor and dentist, as early intervention prevents serious damage.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe tooth pain or a tooth that has fractured
    \item Serious mouth trauma
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dental examination:} the pattern of wear helps distinguish erosion from abrasion and attrition
    \item \textbf{Diet history:} review of acidic foods and drinks, including frequency and habits
    \item \textbf{Medical history:} enquiry about reflux, vomiting, and medications causing dry mouth
    \item \textbf{Photographic records:} monitoring of erosion over time
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a gastroenterologist for reflux or to other specialists where indicated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary change:} reduce the frequency of acidic drinks and foods, and drink them quickly with meals rather than sipping
    \item \textbf{Rinsing:} rinse with plain water or fluoride mouthwash after acidic drinks; do not brush immediately after
    \item \textbf{Delay brushing:} wait at least 30--60 minutes after acid exposure before brushing, to avoid brushing softened enamel
    \item \textbf{Fluoride:} high-fluoride toothpaste and professional fluoride applications strengthen enamel
    \item \textbf{Treating the cause:} management of reflux with medical treatment, and support for eating disorders
    \item \textbf{Saliva support:} sugar-free gum, adequate hydration, and treatment of dry mouth
    \item \textbf{Restorative care:} fillings, veneers, or crowns for advanced erosion
    \item \textbf{Desensitising products:} toothpastes and treatments for sensitivity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe enamel loss requiring complex restoration
    \item Tooth sensitivity that affects eating and drinking
    \item Increased risk of decay and fractures
    \item Shortening of teeth and changes in appearance
    \item Exposure of dentine and pulp, causing pain and possibly root canal treatment
    \item In advanced cases, tooth loss
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Dental erosion is progressive but can be halted completely by removing the cause and adopting protective habits. With early recognition, diet modification, fluoride use, and management of reflux or other causes, further enamel loss stops and teeth can be protected for life. Advanced erosion can be restored successfully, although restoration is more complex and costly than prevention. The outlook is excellent with consistent preventive care.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Limit acidic drinks and foods, and avoid sipping them over long periods
    \item Use a straw for acidic drinks to reduce contact with teeth
    \item Drink water, milk, or tap water with fluoride instead of acidic beverages
    \item Wait 30--60 minutes after acid before brushing
    \item Use a soft toothbrush and gentle technique
    \item Use high-fluoride toothpaste and consider fluoride mouthwash
    \item Chew sugar-free gum to stimulate saliva
    \item See a dentist regularly so erosion is caught early
\end{itemize}
