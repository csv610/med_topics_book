\section*{Definition and Overview}
Eeg is a clinical investigation used to diagnose, monitor, or screen for disease. Investigations may involve analysis of body samples (blood, urine, tissue), imaging of internal structures, or measurement of physiological function. The choice of test depends on the clinical question: is a disease present? How severe is it? Is treatment working? Is a healthy person at risk?

\section*{Epidemiology}
Diagnostic and screening tests are among the most frequently performed medical interventions. Billions of pathology tests and imaging studies are ordered globally each year. Overuse and inappropriate testing contribute to unnecessary costs, false-positive results, and patient anxiety. Evidence-based guidelines aim to optimise test use. Screening programs (cervical, breast, bowel) have demonstrably reduced cancer mortality in eligible populations.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic testing:} performed when a patient has symptoms, to identify or exclude a condition
    \item \textbf{Screening:} offered to asymptomatic individuals at risk to detect disease at an early, treatable stage
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} repeated to track disease progression or treatment response
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative assessment:} to evaluate fitness for surgery and anaesthesia
    \item \textbf{Test performance:} characterised by sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, and likelihood ratios
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Most tests cause minimal discomfort
    \item Blood tests: brief sting and possible small bruise at the puncture site
    \item Imaging: some scans require contrast injection, fasting, or lying still in an enclosed space
    \item Procedural tests (e.g.\ biopsy, endoscopy): may require sedation or local anaesthetic
    \item Anxiety about results is common and should be acknowledged
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Interpretation of test results must always be integrated with the clinical picture. Consider:
\begin{itemize}
    \item False positives: abnormal result in the absence of disease
    \item False negatives: normal result despite disease being present
    \item Borderline results: may require repeat testing or specialist interpretation
    \item Pre-analytical errors: wrong sample, wrong tube, or delayed transport
    \item Analytical errors: laboratory or equipment malfunction
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item If you have unexplained symptoms, ask your doctor whether a test is appropriate
    \item If you feel faint, unwell, or have a reaction during a test, tell staff immediately
    \item If results are abnormal, follow up to discuss next steps
    \item Never ignore a recommendation for follow-up after an abnormal screening result
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Test selection:} based on the clinical question, pre-test probability, and test characteristics
    \item \textbf{Preparation:} some tests require fasting, a full bladder, or medication adjustment
    \item \textbf{Sample analysis:} performed in accredited laboratories using standardised methods
    \item \textbf{Imaging interpretation:} by a radiologist, with results communicated to the requesting clinician
    \item \textbf{Result interpretation:} always in clinical context -- a test result alone is not a diagnosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A test itself is not a treatment -- results guide management
    \item Normal results may be reassuring or may require further investigation if clinical suspicion remains high
    \item Abnormal results trigger further evaluation, specialist referral, or treatment initiation
    \item Discussion of results with the patient is essential, including implications and next steps
    \item Incidental findings (unexpected abnormalities) require judgement about further investigation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding, bruising, or infection at puncture or biopsy sites
    \item Allergic reaction to contrast dye (e.g.\ iodinated contrast in CT)
    \item Radiation exposure -- cumulative dose from repeated imaging (especially CT)
    \item False-positive results leading to unnecessary further testing, anxiety, and overdiagnosis
    \item False-negative results providing false reassurance and delayed diagnosis
    \item Incidental findings causing psychological distress and cascade of investigations
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most tests are safe and well tolerated. The clinical value of a test lies not in the result itself but in how it changes management decisions. A test that does not alter management should be questioned. Screening programs with strong evidence (cervical, breast, bowel) reduce disease-specific mortality. Over-investigation can cause harm through false positives, radiation, and patient anxiety.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Participate in recommended screening programs for your age, sex, and risk factors
    \item Ask your doctor what a test involves, its risks, and how to prepare
    \item Inform the doctor of allergies, pregnancy, or previous problems with tests
    \item Keep a record of your results and follow-up plans
    \item Ask for an explanation of results in plain language
    \item Do not request tests that are not clinically indicated
\end{itemize}
