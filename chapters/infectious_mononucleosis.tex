\chapter{Infectious Mononucleosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Infectious mononucleosis, commonly called glandular fever, is an infection usually caused by the Epstein-Barr virus (EBV), a member of the herpes family. It most often affects adolescents and young adults and is characterised by fever, severe sore throat, swollen lymph glands, and fatigue. The illness typically resolves over several weeks, but tiredness can persist for months. EBV is extremely common and, once infected, people carry the virus for life, usually without further illness. There is no specific antiviral treatment, so care focuses on symptom relief, rest, hydration, and recognising and managing the uncommon complications.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Epstein-Barr virus infects most people at some stage of life. In many parts of the world, infection occurs in early childhood, when it is usually mild or symptomless. In Australia and other higher-income countries, infection is often delayed until adolescence and young adulthood, when it produces glandular fever in around half of those infected. It is most common between the ages of 15 and 25, with no strong sex difference. The virus is transmitted through saliva, which is why it is called the kissing disease, and it spreads easily among young people living closely together. Around nine in ten adults worldwide have antibodies to EBV.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
EBV is spread by contact with infected saliva, including kissing, sharing drinks or utensils, and droplet exposure. The virus infects cells lining the throat and then B lymphocytes, the immune cells that produce antibodies. The body mounts a strong immune response, particularly of T cells, which produces the swollen glands, enlarged spleen and liver, and many of the symptoms. The immune response also generates the atypical lymphocytes seen on blood films, and antibodies to the virus and to other antigens, some of which are used in diagnosis. After the acute illness, EBV remains dormant in B cells for life, kept in check by the immune system.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever, often high and lasting one to two weeks
    \item Severe sore throat, sometimes with exudate on the tonsils, resembling tonsillitis
    \item Swollen lymph glands in the neck, armpits, and groin
    \item Severe fatigue and malaise, often the most disabling symptom
    \item Enlarged spleen and liver, and sometimes mild jaundice
    \item Headache, muscle aches, and loss of appetite
    \item A rash, especially if ampicillin or amoxicillin antibiotics are taken
    \item Symptoms develop gradually over days, and fatigue may last for weeks to months
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Glandular fever mimics several other causes of fever, sore throat, and fatigue.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Streptococcal tonsillitis:} bacterial throat infection with similar features
    \item \textbf{Other viral infections:} cytomegalovirus, HIV seroconversion, adenovirus, and influenza
    \item \textbf{Toxoplasmosis:} a parasite causing similar glandular illness
    \item \textbf{Acute HIV infection:} fever and glandular swelling within weeks of exposure
    \item \textbf{Leukaemia and lymphoma:} persistent glandular swelling and fatigue with other features
    \item \textbf{Chronic fatigue:} in the months after glandular fever, distinguished by persistence and disability
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with a severe sore throat, fever, swollen glands, and fatigue should be assessed to confirm the diagnosis and exclude tonsillitis or other illness. Review is needed for worsening symptoms, an enlarging or very painful neck gland, difficulty swallowing or breathing, or jaundice. Rest and simple measures manage most cases, but complications require medical care.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing or severe difficulty swallowing
    \item Sudden severe abdominal pain, which may indicate a ruptured spleen
    \item Confusion, severe headache, or a seizure
    \item Severe dehydration from inability to drink
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} the triad of fever, sore throat, and swollen glands in a young person
    \item \textbf{Monospot test:} a rapid antibody test, though it can be negative early in the illness
    \item \textbf{EBV serology:} specific antibodies that confirm recent infection
    \item \textbf{Full blood count and blood film:} atypical lymphocytes and low counts support the diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Liver function tests:} mild elevations of liver enzymes are common
    \item \textbf{Throat swab:} to exclude streptococcal infection, which would change management
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} ultrasound to assess the spleen in severe cases or before return to contact sport
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rest:} especially during the acute phase, with gradual return to activity
    \item \textbf{Hydration and nutrition:} fluids, soft foods, and throat lozenges or gargles
    \item \textbf{Fever and pain relief:} paracetamol or ibuprofen
    \item \textbf{Avoiding antibiotics:} they do not treat EBV, and ampicillin and amoxicillin cause a rash
    \item \textbf{Avoiding contact sport:} for at least four to six weeks, because of the risk of splenic rupture
    \item \textbf{Monitoring for complications:} airway obstruction, splenic rupture, and severe fatigue
    \item \textbf{Supporting recovery:} gradual return to school, work, and exercise as energy allows
    \item \textbf{Treating complications:} steroids in severe throat swelling or other specific measures as needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rupture of the enlarged spleen, a rare but life-threatening emergency, usually from trauma
    \item Airway obstruction from severe tonsillar swelling
    \item Anaemia, low platelets, and other blood abnormalities
    \item Hepatitis and jaundice, usually mild
    \item Neurological complications: meningitis, encephalitis, and Guillain-Barré syndrome, all rare
    \item Prolonged fatigue and post-viral fatigue syndrome
    \item Chronic active EBV infection in rare immunocompromised cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Infectious mononucleosis is self-limiting in almost all people, with fever and throat symptoms usually resolving within two to three weeks. Fatigue may persist for several weeks, and a minority of people report tiredness for months. Complications are uncommon and mostly occur in the first few weeks. After recovery, the virus persists silently for life, and recurrence as a symptomatic illness is rare. Return to sport should await resolution of splenic enlargement to avoid the rare but catastrophic complication of splenic rupture.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rest during the acute phase and return to activity gradually
    \item Drink plenty of fluids and eat soft, nourishing foods
    \item Use paracetamol or ibuprofen for fever and throat pain
    \item Gargle with salt water and use throat lozenges for comfort
    \item Avoid alcohol while the liver is recovering
    \item Avoid kissing and sharing drinks or utensils while unwell
    \item Avoid contact sport and heavy lifting for at least four to six weeks
    \item Do not take antibiotics unless prescribed for a confirmed bacterial infection
    \item Seek prompt review for new severe symptoms, especially abdominal pain
\end{itemize}
