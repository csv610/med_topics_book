\chapter{Hormonal Contraceptives and Periods}
\index{Hormonal Contraceptives and Periods}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hormonal contraceptives are medicines that prevent pregnancy using synthetic versions of the hormones oestrogen and progesterone. They work mainly by stopping ovulation and thickening cervical mucus. Beyond preventing pregnancy, hormonal contraceptives have important effects on periods: they commonly make periods lighter, more regular, and less painful, and many women use them specifically for these benefits. The combined pill, progestogen-only pill, contraceptive implant, injection, and hormonal intrauterine device differ in their effects on bleeding patterns. Choosing the right method involves balancing contraceptive needs with period-related goals and side effects.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hormonal contraceptives are among the most widely used medicines in many countries. The combined oral contraceptive pill is the most popular method among younger women, while the contraceptive implant and hormonal intrauterine device (IUD) are increasingly used. Around half of sexually active women of reproductive age use some form of contraception, and a significant proportion choose hormonal methods partly to manage heavy, painful, or irregular periods. The levonorgestrel IUD is now a recognised treatment for heavy menstrual bleeding.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Combined contraceptives:} oestrogen and progestogen stop ovulation, thin the uterine lining, and thicken cervical mucus
    \item \textbf{Progestogen-only methods:} mainly thicken mucus and thin the lining, with variable effects on ovulation
    \item \textbf{Effects on periods:} the uterine lining is thinner, so withdrawal bleeds are lighter and less painful
    \item \textbf{Continuous use:} skipping the inactive pills avoids withdrawal bleeds altogether
    \item \textbf{IUDs:} local progestogen thins the lining, often causing light, infrequent, or absent periods
    \item \textbf{The implant and injection:} can cause irregular bleeding, especially in the first months
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} hormonal suppression of ovulation and endometrial growth underlies both contraception and period changes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Lighter, shorter, and more regular periods with most combined methods
    \item Reduced period pain and premenstrual symptoms
    \item Withdrawal bleeds on the combined pill, which are not true periods
    \item Light or absent periods with the hormonal IUD and continuous pill use
    \item Irregular, unpredictable bleeding with the implant and injection, particularly early on
    \item Breakthrough bleeding can occur in the first months of any method
    \item Period-related benefits continue while the method is used
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding changes on contraceptives are normal in the first months but must be distinguished from pregnancy
    \item Breakthrough bleeding may indicate missed pills, interactions, or a different method may suit better
    \item Absent periods may be due to contraception or to other causes such as pregnancy, stress, or hormonal conditions
    \item New or persistent bleeding problems warrant review, including exclusion of other causes
    \item The method, dose, and duration guide whether bleeding patterns are expected
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor before starting any contraceptive to choose the right method, and for review if bleeding patterns are unacceptable, if side effects occur, or if pregnancy is suspected.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe pelvic pain or very heavy bleeding
    \item Severe calf pain or swelling, or sudden chest pain or breathlessness while on the combined pill (possible blood clot)
    \item Severe headache with visual disturbance or one-sided weakness
    \item Fainting or collapse
    \item Signs of pelvic infection with fever
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} period pattern, contraceptive use, and any symptoms
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure:} checked before starting combined hormonal methods
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test:} where bleeding changes suggest pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Assessment of risk factors:} smoking, age, migraine, and blood clot history for combined methods
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} for suspected hormonal or bleeding disorders
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} if bleeding problems suggest uterine causes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Choosing a method:} individualised to contraceptive needs, period goals, and medical suitability
    \item \textbf{Combined pill:} regular, light, predictable withdrawal bleeds; continuous use for fewer or no bleeds
    \item \textbf{Hormonal IUD:} lightens periods progressively and is a treatment for heavy bleeding
    \item \textbf{Implant and injection:} effective but with unpredictable bleeding in many users
    \item \textbf{Troubleshooting bleeding:} give methods time to settle, then consider dose changes or a different method
    \item \textbf{Pain management:} many methods reduce period pain
    \item \textbf{Switching methods:} with medical guidance if bleeding patterns are unacceptable
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Irregular or prolonged bleeding with progestogen-only methods
    \item Side effects: nausea, breast tenderness, mood changes, and headaches
    \item Small increases in blood clot risk with combined methods, mainly in smokers and older women
    \item Absent periods, which may cause anxiety even when expected
    \item Return of period symptoms when the method is stopped
    \item Interactions with some medicines reduce contraceptive effectiveness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most women achieve satisfactory period control with hormonal contraceptives, and the majority who try more than one method find one that suits them. The hormonal IUD is particularly effective for heavy periods, reducing blood loss by up to 90\%. Period changes are reversible: cycles return after stopping. With informed choice and medical review, hormonal contraceptives provide reliable contraception and period benefits for the majority of women.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discuss period concerns and contraceptive options with your doctor
    \item Give a new method 3--6 months to settle before changing
    \item Keep a record of bleeding patterns to share with your doctor
    \item Take the combined pill consistently and be aware of interactions
    \item Do not stop the pill without alternative contraception unless pregnancy is desired
    \item Attend regular reviews and cervical screening as recommended
    \item Report severe pain, heavy bleeding, or possible pregnancy promptly
\end{itemize}
