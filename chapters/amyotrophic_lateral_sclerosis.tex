\chapter{Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), known as motor neurone disease (MND) in many countries, is a progressive neurological disorder in which the nerve cells (motor neurones) that control voluntary muscles gradually stop working and die. Messages from the brain and spinal cord can no longer reach the muscles, so the person develops progressive weakness, wasting, and eventually paralysis. The condition typically affects the muscles of the arms, legs, face, and chest, while sensation, thinking, and the special senses usually remain intact.

There is currently no cure, but a coordinated multidisciplinary approach can slow progression, treat symptoms, and maintain quality of life for as long as possible. ALS is named for the combination of findings it produces: the wasting of muscle (amyotrophy) and the hardening or scarring of the lateral tracts of the spinal cord (lateral sclerosis).

\section*{Epidemiology}
ALS is relatively rare, affecting roughly 1 to 2 people per 100,000 each year, with a lifetime risk of approximately 1 in 400. It usually begins between the ages of 50 and 70, although younger adults and occasionally older people can be affected. Men are affected slightly more often than women, and the condition affects people of all ethnic backgrounds, with similar rates across most populations worldwide.

Around 5 to 10\% of cases run in families (familial ALS), while the remainder occur sporadically with no clear family history. Rates are increasing slightly in many countries, a change attributed partly to the ageing of populations and partly to better recognition of the condition.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
In ALS, motor neurones in the brain and spinal cord die for reasons that are not yet fully understood. A combination of genetic susceptibility and cellular mechanisms is thought to be involved:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic changes:} mutations in several genes, including C9orf72, SOD1, TARDBP, and FUS, cause familial ALS, and some of the same genes contribute to sporadic cases
    \item \textbf{Protein clumping:} abnormal proteins accumulate inside nerve cells and damage them
    \item \textbf{Excitotoxicity:} excessive stimulation of neurones by the chemical messenger glutamate, which becomes toxic in high concentrations
    \item \textbf{Oxidative stress and neuroinflammation:} damage to cells by reactive molecules and chronic inflammation within the nervous system
    \item \textbf{Mitochondrial dysfunction:} impaired energy production inside the nerve cell
    \item \textbf{Axonal transport failure:} disruption of the movement of materials along the long nerve processes
\end{itemize}
As motor neurones die, muscles lose their nerve supply, weaken, waste away, and become stiff (spastic). Eventually the muscles needed for swallowing, speaking, and breathing are affected, and this determines the major threats to life.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The early symptoms vary from person to person but typically begin in one region and spread, and often include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Limb onset:} progressive weakness in one hand or arm, such as dropping objects or difficulty doing up buttons; weakness of one leg, causing a foot to drag or difficulty climbing stairs
    \item \textbf{Muscle signs:} visible wasting (thinning) of muscles and twitching under the skin (fasciculations)
    \item \textbf{Spasticity:} stiffness, tightness, and cramping of muscles, often with brisk reflexes
    \item \textbf{Bulbar onset:} slurred speech, difficulty swallowing, or choking, and weakness of the neck muscles causing the head to droop
    \item \textbf{Respiratory onset:} shortness of breath with activity, poor sleep, and morning headaches as breathing muscles weaken
    \item \textbf{Preserved functions:} sensation, vision, hearing, and bowel and bladder control are generally retained, though some people develop changes in memory, thinking, or mood
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Many conditions can cause similar symptoms, and distinguishing them is essential before a diagnosis of ALS is made:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cervical or lumbar spine disease compressing the spinal cord or nerve roots
    \item Other motor neurone disorders, such as primary lateral sclerosis or progressive muscular atrophy
    \item Peripheral neuropathies, including those caused by diabetes, alcohol, or vitamin deficiency
    \item Multifocal motor neuropathy, an immune-mediated condition affecting motor nerves
    \item Muscle diseases such as polymyositis or inclusion body myositis
    \item Myasthenia gravis, which causes fluctuating, fatigable weakness
    \item Thyroid disease, heavy-metal exposure, or metabolic disorders
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if you have progressive weakness, muscle wasting, twitching, or difficulty with speech or swallowing that is getting worse. Ask for a specialist neurological referral if symptoms persist or spread. Seek urgent medical attention if breathing is becoming more difficult, you are choking on food, fluids, or saliva, or a chest infection develops with fever and cough.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe breathlessness or inability to breathe
    \item Loss of consciousness or a person who stops responding
    \item Choking that cannot be cleared, with a blue or pale face
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
There is no single test for ALS; the diagnosis is made on clinical grounds and supported by investigations that exclude other causes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the site of onset, rate of progression, family history, and the absence of sensory symptoms
    \item \textbf{Neurological examination:} assessment of strength, wasting, reflexes, tone, coordination, and speech in many muscle groups, looking for upper and lower motor neurone signs in several regions
    \item \textbf{Electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction studies:} electrical tests showing denervation in muscles and excluding a primary nerve or muscle disorder
    \item \textbf{Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):} scans of the brain and cervical spine to exclude structural lesions such as tumours, stroke, or spinal cord compression
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} to exclude thyroid disease, vitamin deficiencies, inflammatory and autoimmune disorders, and infection
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture (spinal tap):} occasionally performed to examine the cerebrospinal fluid when inflammatory causes are suspected
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} offered to people with a family history of ALS and increasingly in sporadic cases where relevant
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
ALS is managed by a multidisciplinary team that includes a neurologist, respiratory and palliative care physicians, speech and swallowing therapists, dietitians, physiotherapists, and occupational therapists. Approaches include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} riluzole, which modestly slows progression, and agents for stiffness, cramps, excess saliva, and mood disturbance
    \item \textbf{Breathing support:} regular measurement of breathing strength; non-invasive ventilation at night and, later, for longer periods, which improves survival and quality of life
    \item \textbf{Nutrition:} dietitian advice, fortified foods, and thickened fluids for swallowing problems; a feeding tube (PEG or RIG) when eating and drinking become unsafe
    \item \textbf{Communication aids:} speech therapy and electronic or computer-based devices as speech deteriorates
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy and occupational therapy:} to maintain mobility, prevent contractures, and adapt the home for safety and independence
    \item \textbf{Equipment:} walking aids, wheelchairs, and devices for daily tasks
    \item \textbf{Psychological and palliative support:} counselling for the person and family, with early palliative care involvement to manage symptoms, coordinate care, and plan ahead
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Aspiration pneumonia from food or saliva entering the airway
    \item Respiratory failure from progressive weakness of the breathing muscles, the most common cause of death
    \item Malnutrition and significant weight loss from swallowing difficulties
    \item Loss of speech, requiring alternative and augmentative communication
    \item Pressure sores, joint contractures, and deep vein thrombosis from immobility
    \item Falls and injuries related to limb weakness
    \item Depression, anxiety, and significant carer burden
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
ALS is a progressive condition for which there is currently no cure. On average, people live around 2 to 5 years from the onset of symptoms, although a minority live much longer, sometimes for more than a decade. Survival is generally shorter when symptoms begin with speech or swallowing problems, and when breathing is affected early. Treatment with riluzole, non-invasive ventilation, and good nutritional care have modestly extended survival and improved quality of life. The course varies greatly between individuals, and periods of relative stability occur, so doctors are cautious about predicting any individual's outlook. Advance care planning with the team helps many people and families feel more in control.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
There is no known way to prevent ALS, because its causes are largely unknown. For people living with the condition, self-care focuses on maintaining wellbeing, safety, and comfort:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend regular multidisciplinary clinic appointments and keep the team informed of new symptoms
    \item Report swallowing or breathing problems early, rather than waiting for them to become severe
    \item Maintain a nutrient-rich diet with dietitian advice, and use supplements if recommended
    \item Remain as active as is comfortable, with therapist guidance to avoid overexertion and falls
    \item Use adaptive equipment early to reduce fatigue and conserve energy
    \item Discuss breathing, feeding, and emergency plans with the care team and family
    \item Seek emotional support for yourself and for carers; support groups and counselling can be very helpful
    \item Make a care plan, including a named emergency contact, advance care preferences, and preferred place of care
\end{itemize}
