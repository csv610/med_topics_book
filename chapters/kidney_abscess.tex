\chapter{Kidney Abscess}
\index{Kidney Abscess}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A kidney abscess, also called a renal abscess, is a collection of pus within the kidney, usually caused by a bacterial infection that has spread from the urinary tract or bloodstream. It is an uncommon but serious complication of kidney infection (pyelonephritis), occurring when infection is not fully treated, or in people with diabetes, kidney stones, or weakened immunity. A kidney abscess causes fever, flank pain, and sometimes a mass, and it requires antibiotics and often drainage. With prompt treatment the outlook is good, but without it the abscess can rupture, cause sepsis, or permanently damage the kidney.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Kidney abscesses are uncommon, affecting a small proportion of people with urinary tract and kidney infections. They are more common in people with diabetes, kidney stones, structural abnormalities of the urinary tract, and weakened immunity, and in women, who are more prone to urinary infections. The risk rises with age and with prolonged or recurrent infections. The responsible bacteria are usually the same as those causing urinary infections, especially Escherichia coli and, in some cases, Staphylococcus aureus from bloodstream spread. Improved diagnosis with CT scanning has increased detection of abscesses that were once found only at surgery or autopsy.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
A kidney abscess develops when bacteria reach the kidney and multiply within its tissue, and the body's immune response walls off the infection with a collection of pus. This usually follows an ascending infection from the bladder and ureter (pyelonephritis) that is not cleared, or spread of bacteria through the blood from another site, such as the skin or heart valves. Obstruction from kidney stones, diabetes, and immune suppression all allow infection to establish and persist. The abscess destroys kidney tissue, and if it enlarges, it can rupture into the surrounding tissues, the urinary tract, or the bloodstream, causing sepsis. Some abscesses form between the kidney and its capsule, called perinephric abscesses.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever, chills, and sweats
    \item Flank or back pain, which may be severe and constant
    \item Tenderness over the affected kidney
    \item A palpable mass in the flank in some cases
    \item Pain or burning with urination and urinary frequency
    \item Nausea, vomiting, and feeling generally unwell
    \item Weight loss and fatigue with chronic abscesses
    \item Failure of a kidney infection to improve with antibiotics
    \item Symptoms may develop gradually over days to weeks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of fever and flank pain must be distinguished.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Kidney infection (pyelonephritis):} infection without a pus collection
    \item \textbf{Kidney stones:} colicky pain, often without high fever
    \item \textbf{Bowel conditions:} appendicitis, diverticulitis, and other abdominal infections
    \item \textbf{Gallbladder and liver abscess:} right upper abdominal infection
    \item \textbf{Kidney cancer:} a mass and blood in the urine
    \item \textbf{Musculoskeletal pain:} back strain and disc disease
    \item \textbf{Sepsis from other sources:} other collections and infections
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with a urinary or kidney infection that is not improving, or with fever and flank pain, should be assessed, especially people with diabetes, stones, or immune suppression. Persistent fever, severe pain, or signs of sepsis require urgent care, and a kidney abscess should be considered in any patient whose pyelonephritis does not respond to antibiotics.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item High fever with shaking, confusion, or feeling very unwell, which may indicate sepsis
    \item Severe flank or abdominal pain
    \item Difficulty breathing or collapse
    \item Vomiting with an inability to keep fluids down
    \item Blood in the urine with pain and fever
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} fever, pain, and risk factors
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, inflammatory markers, kidney function, and blood cultures
    \item \textbf{Urine tests:} microscopy, culture, and sensitivity
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} the best imaging test for kidney abscesses
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} useful for detection and guided drainage
    \item \textbf{MRI:} in selected cases
    \item \textbf{Assessment of complications:} imaging for rupture and spread
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} intravenous antibiotics, then oral, guided by cultures, often for several weeks
    \item \textbf{Drainage:} ultrasound- or CT-guided drainage of the pus, or surgical drainage when needed
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} fluids, pain relief, and monitoring
    \item \textbf{Treatment of the underlying cause:} kidney stones, obstruction, and diabetes control
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} rarely, removal of part or all of the kidney for severe disease
    \item \textbf{Follow-up imaging:} confirming resolution of the abscess
    \item \textbf{Management of sepsis:} intensive care for severe cases
    \item \textbf{Prevention of recurrence:} treating urine infections promptly and managing risk factors
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sepsis and septic shock
    \item Rupture of the abscess into surrounding tissues
    \item Permanent kidney damage and loss of function
    \item Spread of infection to the blood and other organs
    \item Recurrent abscess formation
    \item Complications of drainage and surgery
    \item Prolonged illness and hospitalisation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt antibiotics and drainage, most people with a kidney abscess recover fully, and kidney function is preserved in the majority. Outcomes are worst when treatment is delayed, in people with diabetes or immune suppression, and when sepsis develops, but modern care has greatly reduced mortality. Follow-up imaging confirms healing, and managing the underlying causes prevents recurrence.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Treat urinary tract infections promptly and completely
    \item Complete the full course of antibiotics
    \item Manage diabetes and other risk factors well
    \item See a doctor if a kidney infection is not improving
    \item Seek urgent care for high fever, severe pain, or feeling very unwell
    \item Drink plenty of fluids
    \item Attend follow-up imaging and reviews
    \item Report any return of symptoms promptly
\end{itemize}
