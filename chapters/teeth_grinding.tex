\chapter{Teeth grinding}
\index{Teeth grinding}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Educational reference; always seek professional advice for individual care.}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Teeth grinding, known medically as bruxism, is the involuntary clenching, grinding, or gnashing of the teeth. It may occur during sleep (sleep bruxism) or while awake (awake bruxism). Sleep bruxism is classified as a sleep-related movement disorder; awake bruxism is more often linked to stress, concentration, or habit. Many people grind their teeth without knowing it until a partner hears the sound at night, a dentist notices wear, or jaw pain and headaches appear. Mild bruxism may need only monitoring and simple self-care. Persistent or severe grinding can wear enamel, crack teeth, strain the jaw joints and muscles, disturb sleep, and contribute to temporomandibular joint (TMJ) problems. In many countries, dentists and GPs commonly encounter bruxism in adults under pressure at work or study, in people with sleep disorders such as obstructive sleep apnoea, and in children, where grinding is often temporary. This chapter explains causes, recognition, when to seek care, and evidence-based ways to protect the teeth and ease jaw strain.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bruxism is common across the lifespan. Sleep bruxism is estimated to affect roughly eight to fifteen per cent of adults and a higher proportion of children, many of whom outgrow it. Awake clenching is even more frequent and is often under-reported because people do not notice daytime jaw tension. Prevalence is higher in people with anxiety, high stress, stimulant use (including caffeine and some medicines), tobacco use, and sleep-disordered breathing. Shift workers, students in exam periods, and people with untreated sleep apnoea are over-represented in dental and sleep clinic samples. There is no strong sex difference overall, though presentation and help-seeking patterns may differ. Family history of bruxism is reported more often than chance, suggesting a heritable component to sleep-related grinding. Most local cases are managed in general dental practice; specialist referral to oral medicine, orofacial pain, or sleep medicine is reserved for severe wear, refractory pain, or suspected sleep apnoea.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Bruxism is multifactorial. It is not simply a dental misalignment problem, although bite factors can influence local stress on teeth.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Central nervous system drive:} sleep bruxism is linked to brief arousals and rhythmic masticatory muscle activity during light sleep, under brainstem and autonomic control rather than conscious will
    \item \textbf{Psychosocial stress and anxiety:} awake clenching often tracks workload, worry, anger suppression, and hypervigilance; muscle tension in the masseter and temporalis becomes habitual
    \item \textbf{Sleep disorders:} obstructive sleep apnoea and snoring are associated with sleep bruxism; grinding may partly reflect airway-protective arousals
    \item \textbf{Medicines and substances:} selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), some antipsychotics, amphetamines, ecstasy, high caffeine, alcohol, and tobacco can increase bruxism risk
    \item \textbf{Genetic and developmental factors:} family clustering and higher rates in some childhood developmental profiles support a biological predisposition
    \item \textbf{Occlusal and dental factors:} high restorations, missing teeth, or unstable bite can concentrate force but are rarely the sole cause
    \item \textbf{Neurological conditions:} dystonia, Parkinson disease, and some movement disorders can produce secondary bruxism or jaw clenching
\end{itemize}

Repeated high bite forces abrade enamel, microfracture dentine, overload the periodontal ligament, and fatigue the TMJ disc and joint capsule, producing pain, clicking, and limited opening in some people.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Audible grinding at night:} partners or parents often report a scraping or clicking sound during sleep
    \item \textbf{Morning jaw stiffness or pain:} aching masseter or temporalis muscles on waking, sometimes with limited mouth opening
    \item \textbf{Tension-type headaches:} especially temples or forehead, worse in the morning or after long concentration
    \item \textbf{Tooth wear facets:} flattened, shiny biting surfaces; chipped edges; increased sensitivity to cold or sweet
    \item \textbf{Tooth fractures or failed restorations:} cracked cusps, broken fillings, or loosened crowns under repeated load
    \item \textbf{Indentations on the tongue or cheek:} scalloped tongue borders or linea alba inside the cheek from chronic pressure
    \item \textbf{TMJ symptoms:} clicking, locking, ear-region pain, or pain on chewing
    \item \textbf{Daytime awareness of clenching:} jaw held tight during driving, screen work, or stress, often without grinding noise
    \item \textbf{Disrupted sleep or partner sleep disturbance:} from noise or from associated apnoea arousals
\end{itemize}

Children may grind loudly without pain; parents should still mention it at dental visits so wear and airway issues can be checked.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Temporomandibular joint disorders without bruxism:} joint inflammation, disc displacement, or arthritis can cause similar pain without tooth wear
    \item \textbf{Dental infection or cracked tooth syndrome:} localised pain on biting may mimic bruxism-related sensitivity
    \item \textbf{Sinusitis or ear disease:} referred pain to the jaw and temple
    \item \textbf{Migraine and other primary headaches:} may coexist with or be mistaken for bruxism-related tension pain
    \item \textbf{Trigeminal neuralgia or neuropathic facial pain:} sharp, electric pain unlike dull muscle ache
    \item \textbf{Medication-related jaw dystonia:} sustained abnormal jaw posture rather than rhythmic grinding
    \item \textbf{Gastro-oesophageal reflux:} acid wear thins enamel differently from mechanical grinding facets and often affects palatal surfaces of upper teeth
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a dentist if you notice wear, chipped teeth, morning jaw pain, or if a partner reports grinding. See a GP if headaches, sleepiness, loud snoring, or witnessed breathing pauses suggest sleep apnoea or if medicines may be contributing.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe facial swelling, difficulty breathing or swallowing, or signs of serious allergic reaction after dental treatment
    \item Uncontrolled bleeding after oral surgery
    \item Sudden neurological symptoms (face droop, arm weakness, speech difficulty) that must not be attributed to jaw pain alone
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is mainly clinical, based on history, partner report, and dental examination.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dental examination:} wear patterns, fractures, mobility, muscle tenderness, jaw range of motion, and occlusal contacts
    \item \textbf{History of sleep and substances:} snoring, apnoeas, caffeine, alcohol, SSRIs, stimulants, and stress load
    \item \textbf{Imaging when indicated:} dental radiographs for cracks or periapical disease; rarely CBCT or MRI for complex TMJ pathology
    \item \textbf{Sleep study:} polysomnography if obstructive sleep apnoea is suspected or if grinding is severe and unexplained
    \item \textbf{Specialist assessment:} oral medicine or orofacial pain clinics for chronic pain; ENT or sleep physician for airway issues
\end{itemize}

Home wearable devices and apps may suggest activity but do not replace clinical assessment.

\section*{Management}
Management aims to reduce damage, relieve pain, and address drivers such as stress and sleep apnoea. There is no single cure that permanently stops all grinding.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Occlusal splints (night guards):} custom hard or dual-laminate splints worn at night protect teeth from wear and can reduce muscle load; boil-and-bite shop guards are a short-term compromise but fit poorly for many people
    \item \textbf{Behavioural strategies for awake bruxism:} habit reversal, jaw resting posture (teeth apart, tongue relaxed), scheduled relaxation, and workplace ergonomics
    \item \textbf{Stress and psychological care:} cognitive behavioural approaches, mindfulness, and treatment of anxiety or depression when relevant
    \item \textbf{Sleep apnoea treatment:} continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) or mandibular advancement devices when indicated may reduce associated sleep bruxism
    \item \textbf{Medication review:} discuss with the prescriber whether an SSRI or other drug could be switched or adjusted if bruxism began after starting it
    \item \textbf{Short-term medicines:} muscle relaxants or short courses of analgesics for acute flare-ups; long-term sedative use is not first-line
    \item \textbf{Botulinum toxin:} specialist option for severe masseter hypertrophy or refractory pain in selected adults
    \item \textbf{Dental restoration:} rebuilds worn or fractured teeth once forces are controlled with a splint and stable plan
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} jaw exercises, manual therapy, and posture work for TMJ-related muscle pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Irreversible tooth wear and sensitivity:} loss of enamel and dentine exposing pulp risk over years
    \item \textbf{Tooth fracture and restoration failure:} cracked teeth, lost crowns, implant overload
    \item \textbf{Periodontal strain:} in susceptible people, excess force may worsen mobility where bone support is already reduced
    \item \textbf{Chronic orofacial pain and TMJ dysfunction:} persistent myofascial pain, limited opening, joint degeneration
    \item \textbf{Sleep disruption and daytime fatigue:} for the person and household members
    \item \textbf{Aesthetic and functional bite collapse:} shortened lower face appearance and reduced chewing efficiency in severe long-standing cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Many children improve spontaneously. Adults often need ongoing protection rather than a one-off cure. With a well-fitting night guard, attention to stress and sleep, and timely dental repair, most people preserve function and comfort. Outcomes are poorer when sleep apnoea, heavy substance use, or untreated anxiety continue, or when extensive restorative work is done without controlling parafunction. Regular dental review allows early detection of new wear.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep teeth slightly apart at rest; lips closed, tongue resting gently on the palate
    \item Reduce evening caffeine and alcohol; avoid nicotine close to bedtime
    \item Use a custom night guard as advised and bring it to dental visits for adjustment
    \item Manage stress with exercise, adequate sleep, and professional psychological support when needed
    \item Treat snoring and suspected sleep apnoea rather than ignoring partner reports
    \item Avoid chewing gum excessively and biting pens or nails, which train jaw muscles to stay active
    \item Attend routine dental checks so wear is documented and addressed early
    \item Review long-term medicines with your GP or psychiatrist if grinding started after a prescription change
\end{itemize}
