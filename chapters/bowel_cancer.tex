\chapter{Bowel Cancer}
\index{Bowel Cancer}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bowel cancer, also called colorectal cancer, is a malignant tumour that arises from the lining of the large bowel (colon) or the rectum. Most bowel cancers develop over many years from benign growths called polyps, particularly adenomatous polyps, which acquire genetic changes that allow the cells to grow uncontrollably. Because of this slow progression from polyp to cancer, bowel cancer is among the most preventable and, when detected early, most curable of the common cancers. It can spread through the bowel wall, to nearby lymph nodes, and eventually to the liver, lungs, and other organs. The terms colon cancer and rectal cancer describe the site of origin, and treatment and outlook differ slightly between them.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bowel cancer is one of the most common cancers worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer death in many countries. Roughly one in 22 men and one in 26 women develop the disease during their lifetime. Risk rises sharply with age, with most cases diagnosed in people over 50, and it is slightly more common in men. Rates are higher in developed countries and in populations adopting Western dietary patterns. Notably, the incidence of bowel cancer in adults under 50 has been rising over recent decades, for reasons that are not yet fully understood. About 5\% of cases are linked to inherited conditions such as Lynch syndrome, and a further proportion are associated with a strong family history.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Age:} the strongest single risk factor; most cases occur after the age of 50.
    \item \textbf{Adenomatous polyps:} the precursor of most bowel cancers; only a minority of polyps progress, over a period of roughly 10 years, to invasive cancer.
    \item \textbf{Inherited conditions:} Lynch syndrome, familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP), and other rarer syndromes cause a much younger age of onset and higher risk.
    \item \textbf{Family history:} a first-degree relative with bowel cancer approximately doubles the risk.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory bowel disease:} long-standing ulcerative colitis and Crohn's colitis increase risk in proportion to extent and duration of inflammation.
    \item \textbf{Diet:} high consumption of red and processed meat is associated with increased risk, while dietary fibre, vegetables, and fruit appear protective.
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} obesity, physical inactivity, smoking, and heavy alcohol use all raise risk.
    \item \textbf{Molecular pathway:} progression from polyp to cancer typically involves serial mutations in genes such as \textit{APC}, \textit{KRAS}, \textit{TP53}, and defects in mismatch-repair genes, which drive uncontrolled growth, invasion, and metastasis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Early bowel cancer is often silent, which is why screening is so important. When symptoms do occur, they include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A change in bowel habit lasting more than a few weeks -- looser stools, constipation, or going to the toilet more often
    \item Blood in the stool or bleeding from the bottom
    \item Abdominal pain, cramping, or a feeling of fullness
    \item A sensation that the bowel does not empty completely
    \item Unexplained weight loss and reduced appetite
    \item Tiredness and anaemia from slow, invisible blood loss
    \item A palpable abdominal lump, more often found by a doctor
    \item Persistent urgency, or an urgent need to pass stool that is difficult to control
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of bowel cancer overlap with many more common, less serious conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Haemorrhoids (piles):} a very common cause of bright-red bleeding that does not cause weight loss or change in bowel habit.
    \item \textbf{Anal fissure:} a painful tear at the anal margin causing bleeding with defecation.
    \item \textbf{Irritable bowel syndrome:} altered bowel habit and pain without bleeding or structural disease.
    \item \textbf{Diverticular disease:} outpouchings of the colon wall that cause pain, bleeding, and change in habit.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory bowel disease:} Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis cause diarrhoea, bleeding, and pain.
    \item \textbf{Benign polyps:} non-cancerous growths that can bleed but have not become malignant.
    \item \textbf{Anal cancer:} a separate malignancy of the anal canal that can bleed and cause pain.
    \item \textbf{Medication effects:} iron tablets, antacids, and other drugs can darken stool and mimic bleeding.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor promptly -- without waiting for a routine check-up -- if you have blood in the stool, a change in bowel habit lasting more than a few weeks, unexplained weight loss, persistent abdominal pain, or tiredness with low iron levels. People over 50, those with a family history, and those with inflammatory bowel disease should be particularly alert. Do not assume bleeding is haemorrhoids until it has been assessed.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain with vomiting and an inability to open the bowels (possible bowel obstruction)
    \item Sudden heavy bleeding from the bottom with light-headedness, faintness, or collapse
    \item Severe abdominal pain with fever, which may indicate a perforated bowel
    \item Severe dehydration from prolonged vomiting or diarrhoea
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} assessment of symptoms, family history, and risk factors, including a digital rectal examination.
    \item \textbf{Faecal occult blood test (FOBT):} the screening test, detecting invisible blood in stool samples; a positive result requires further investigation.
    \item \textbf{Colonoscopy:} the key diagnostic test, allowing inspection of the whole large bowel and removal of polyps; suspicious lesions are biopsied.
    \item \textbf{Sigmoidoscopy:} examination of the lower bowel and rectum, useful for distal tumours.
    \item \textbf{CT colonography (virtual colonoscopy):} an alternative whole-bowel imaging test for people who cannot undergo colonoscopy.
    \item \textbf{Staging scans:} CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis, and MRI of the rectum, to determine whether the cancer has spread.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count for anaemia, liver and renal function, and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) as a tumour marker.
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} for mismatch-repair genes in people with suggestive features or a family history of Lynch syndrome.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is planned by a multidisciplinary team and depends on the stage, site, and genetics of the cancer:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} removal of the segment of bowel containing the tumour, with the joining of the healthy ends, is the main treatment for localised disease.
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy:} given after surgery when the cancer has spread to lymph nodes, and in advanced disease to shrink and control tumours.
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} used mainly for rectal cancer, before or after surgery, to reduce the risk of local recurrence.
    \item \textbf{Targeted therapy and immunotherapy:} drugs directed at specific molecular features of the tumour, used particularly in metastatic disease.
    \item \textbf{Stenting:} a tube placed across an obstructing tumour can relieve blockage without surgery in selected patients.
    \item \textbf{Palliative care:} symptom management and quality-of-life support for advanced disease.
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} surveillance colonoscopy, imaging, and blood tests for several years to detect recurrence or new polyps early.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bowel obstruction, when the tumour blocks the passage of stool and requires emergency treatment
    \item Perforation of the bowel, causing peritonitis and septicaemia
    \item Severe or chronic bleeding leading to anaemia
    \item Spread to the liver, lungs, peritoneum, or other organs
    \item Treatment side effects, including fatigue, nausea, peripheral neuropathy from chemotherapy, and bowel frequency after surgery
    \item The need for a stoma (colostomy or ileostomy), which may be temporary or permanent
    \item Psychological distress, body image concerns, and reduced quality of life
    \item Venous thromboembolism, a recognised risk in people with active cancer
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for bowel cancer depends strongly on the stage at diagnosis. When the cancer is confined to the bowel wall, more than 90\% of people survive at least five years and most are cured. Survival falls progressively once the cancer has reached lymph nodes, and it is much lower with distant spread, although modern chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and surgery for liver metastases are steadily improving these figures. Because most bowel cancers develop slowly from polyps, the National Bowel Cancer Screening Program and removal of polyps at colonoscopy can prevent the disease entirely or catch it at a curable stage. Even after a diagnosis, early-stage bowel cancer is associated with an excellent long-term outlook.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take part in the bowel-cancer screening programme offered where you live; eligibility, test type, and interval vary by country.
    \item Eat a high-fibre diet rich in vegetables, fruit, legumes, and whole grains.
    \item Limit red meat to modest amounts and avoid processed meat where possible.
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and be physically active most days.
    \item Limit alcohol and do not smoke.
    \item See a doctor promptly for any persistent change in bowel habit, bleeding, or unexplained weight loss.
    \item If you have a family history or an inherited syndrome, discuss earlier or more frequent screening.
    \item Attend surveillance colonoscopy if you have had polyps removed, and take iron or other treatments for anaemia as advised.
\end{itemize}
