\chapter{Breast Cancer}
\index{Breast Cancer}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Breast cancer is a disease in which cells in the breast grow out of control and form a tumour. It almost always begins in the cells lining the \textbf{milk ducts} (ductal carcinoma) or in the \textbf{milk-producing lobules} (lobular carcinoma). When the abnormal cells remain confined within the duct or lobule, the disease is called \textbf{carcinoma in situ} -- a non-invasive, pre-invasive condition. When the cells have broken through into the surrounding breast tissue, it is termed \textbf{invasive} breast cancer, and the cancer may then spread through lymphatic channels to the lymph nodes under the arm and, over time, through the bloodstream to distant organs such as bone, liver, lung, or brain. Breast cancer can also occur in men, although it is far less common. Because invasive breast cancer is often curable when detected early, the emphasis across all health systems is on screening, early diagnosis, and multidisciplinary treatment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in women worldwide and a leading cause of cancer death in women, with more than 2 million new cases diagnosed globally each year. Approximately 1 in 8 women will develop breast cancer at some point in their lifetime. Risk rises steeply with age, and most cases are diagnosed in women over 50. Breast cancer in men is uncommon, accounting for around 1\% of all cases. Survival has improved substantially over recent decades because of earlier diagnosis through screening mammography and better systemic treatments; in high-income countries, the five-year relative survival now exceeds 90\%, and most women diagnosed today survive the disease.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Breast cancer develops when a breast cell accumulates genetic mutations that allow it to divide uncontrollably, evade cell death, and invade surrounding tissue. Oestrogen and progesterone drive the growth of the majority of cancers, which carry receptors for these hormones, and the HER2 growth-signalling pathway is overactive in a further 15--20\%. The cancer may then spread via lymphatics to axillary nodes and via the bloodstream to distant organs.

Risk factors include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Age:} risk increases with age, particularly after menopause
    \item \textbf{Hormonal factors:} early menarche, late menopause, nulliparity, a first child after age 30, and use of combined hormone replacement therapy
    \item \textbf{Family history:} close relatives with breast or ovarian cancer raise the risk
    \item \textbf{Inherited gene faults:} mutations in genes such as \textit{BRCA1} and \textit{BRCA2}, and other less common genes
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} overweight and obesity after menopause, alcohol consumption, and physical inactivity
    \item \textbf{Dense breast tissue:} visible on mammography
    \item \textbf{Prior chest radiation:} particularly treatment before the age of 30, and certain benign breast conditions that carry increased risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A new lump or thickening in the breast or armpit
    \item A change in the size or shape of the breast
    \item Nipple changes, such as inversion, crusting, scaling, or a new discharge
    \item Dimpling or puckering of the skin over the breast, sometimes described as orange-peel skin
    \item Redness, ulceration, or a persistent rash on the skin of the breast
    \item Persistent, localised breast pain or tenderness, although pain alone is an uncommon presentation
\end{itemize}

Many early breast cancers cause no symptoms at all and are detected only by screening mammography, which is why routine screening participation is so important.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fibroadenoma, a benign solid lump most common in younger women
    \item Breast cyst, a fluid-filled sac
    \item Mastitis or breast abscess, including inflammatory presentations
    \item Fat necrosis, damage to breast fat often following injury
    \item Fibrocystic breast change
    \item Duct ectasia, with or without infection
    \item Phyllodes tumour, a rare tumour that is usually benign
    \item Gynaecomastia, benign breast enlargement in men
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor promptly if you notice any new or unusual change in your breast or armpit, even if it does not hurt. Most breast changes are not cancer, but they can only be distinguished by assessment, and a delay in diagnosis can affect the outcome. Do not wait to see whether symptoms go away.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item High fever or chills while receiving chemotherapy or soon after treatment -- possible neutropenic sepsis
    \item Difficulty breathing or chest pain
    \item Sudden, severe back pain or new leg weakness or numbness -- possible spinal cord compression from metastatic disease
    \item Confusion or severe drowsiness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis follows the \textbf{triple test} -- clinical examination, imaging, and biopsy:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} the doctor examines the breast, armpits, and neck for lumps and enlarged lymph nodes
    \item \textbf{Mammography:} X-ray imaging of the breasts, the standard screening and diagnostic test
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} distinguishes solid lumps from cysts and guides needle biopsy
    \item \textbf{MRI:} used in selected cases, such as high-risk women, lobular cancer, or complex findings
    \item \textbf{Core needle biopsy:} samples of the lump are examined under the microscope to confirm the diagnosis, determine the type and grade, and assess hormone receptors and HER2 status
    \item \textbf{Staging tests:} CT, bone scan, or PET-CT where spread is suspected
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} offered to selected women based on age, family history, and tumour features, and discussed by the multidisciplinary team
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is planned by a multidisciplinary team and depends on the type and stage of the cancer, its hormone receptor and HER2 status, and the person's age, fitness, and preferences:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} breast-conserving surgery (lumpectomy or wide local excision) or mastectomy, together with assessment of the axillary lymph nodes
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} radiation to the remaining breast tissue or chest wall after surgery, and to the axilla in selected cases
    \item \textbf{Hormone therapy:} tamoxifen or aromatase inhibitors for hormone-receptor-positive cancers, often taken for several years
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy:} cytotoxic medicines given before or after surgery, or as the main treatment for advanced disease
    \item \textbf{Targeted therapy:} drugs such as trastuzumab for HER2-positive cancers, and CDK4/6 inhibitors and other agents for advanced disease
    \item \textbf{Breast reconstruction:} rebuilding the breast shape at the time of mastectomy or later
    \item \textbf{Surveillance:} regular clinical review, with imaging and further tests to detect recurrence
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} physiotherapy, psychological support, lymphoedema services, and management of treatment side effects
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Metastatic spread to bone, liver, lung, or brain
    \item Recurrence of the cancer, which can occur many years after initial treatment
    \item Lymphoedema, persistent swelling of the arm after lymph node surgery or radiotherapy
    \item Treatment side effects, including fatigue, nausea, hot flushes, joint pains, infertility after some chemotherapy, and long-term effects on the heart and bones
    \item Emotional and psychological distress, including anxiety, depression, and body-image concerns
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for breast cancer has improved dramatically. Most cancers detected early, while still confined to the breast, are curable, and the majority of women diagnosed today survive at least five years, with most living much longer. Prognosis depends on the size and stage of the cancer, its grade and subtype, whether it is hormone-receptor or HER2 positive, and the response to treatment. Even when breast cancer has spread, modern systemic therapy can often control the disease for many years, allowing many women to live active and fulfilling lives with advanced disease. Genetic and molecular profiling now allows treatment to be tailored precisely to each individual tumour.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend breast screening (mammography) as recommended for your age group
    \item Be breast aware: know the normal look and feel of your breasts and report any changes promptly
    \item Maintain a healthy weight, particularly after menopause
    \item Be physically active and limit alcohol consumption
    \item Breastfeed where possible, which is associated with a lower risk
    \item Discuss the risks and benefits of hormone replacement therapy with your doctor
    \item If you have a strong family history, ask about genetic testing and risk-reducing options
    \item Follow your treatment and follow-up plan closely and attend scheduled reviews
\end{itemize}
