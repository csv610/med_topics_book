\chapter{Domestic Violence}
\index{Domestic Violence}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Domestic violence, also called family and domestic violence, is abusive behaviour used by one person to control another within an intimate or family relationship. It includes physical, sexual, emotional, psychological, and financial abuse, and it can affect people of any gender, age, background, or sexuality. Domestic violence is a serious health and social issue with profound effects on physical and mental health, and it is a leading cause of homelessness and death in many countries. No one deserves violence, help is available, and there are services and legal protections to support people experiencing it.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Domestic violence is widespread: around one in four women and one in thirteen men have experienced intimate partner violence, and women are far more likely to be killed by a partner than by anyone else. On average, one woman is killed by a current or former partner every week in many countries. Children who witness domestic violence are harmed too, and violence rates are higher among some groups, including Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women and people with disability. Domestic violence is under-reported, and many victims do not seek help.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physical abuse:} hitting, slapping, pushing, and other physical harm
    \item \textbf{Sexual abuse:} forced or coerced sexual activity
    \item \textbf{Emotional and psychological abuse:} threats, intimidation, humiliation, and control
    \item \textbf{Financial abuse:} controlling money, preventing work, or taking finances
    \item \textbf{Social abuse:} isolating the victim from family and friends
    \item \textbf{Digital abuse:} monitoring, controlling, or harassing through technology
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} abuse is about power and control, and it escalates over time
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Injuries inconsistent with the explanation given
    \item Frequent presentations for injuries, anxiety, depression, or stress-related illness
    \item Signs of control: the partner attending all appointments, monitoring phone calls, or answering for the person
    \item Fear of the partner, reluctance to speak, and low self-esteem
    \item Isolation from family and friends
    \item Financial hardship with no explanation
    \item Children showing fear, anxiety, or behaviour problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Domestic violence versus other causes of injuries, which must be considered
    \item Psychological symptoms from abuse versus other causes of depression and anxiety
    \item Child abuse and elder abuse, which may coexist with domestic violence
    \item Self-harm versus inflicted injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Health professionals can provide support, document injuries, and connect people with services. Anyone experiencing domestic violence should know that help is available, including 1800RESPECT, police, and domestic violence services. Health care is a safe place to disclose violence.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Immediate danger of physical harm or assault
    \item A person has been hurt and needs urgent medical care
    \item Threats with weapons or threats to life
    \item Concerns for the safety of children
    \item Suicidal thoughts or severe distress
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Support lines:} 1800RESPECT (1800 737 732), the Men's Referral Service (1300 766 491), and a local youth crisis service (1800 551 800).

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asking about violence:} health professionals routinely and sensitively ask about safety and abuse
    \item \textbf{Assessment of injuries:} documentation and treatment of physical harm
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} immediate safety, lethality risk, and the presence of weapons
    \item \textbf{Safety planning:} a plan for escape and support
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to specialist domestic violence services and legal advice
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Safety first:} the priority is the safety of the victim and any children
    \item \textbf{Safety planning:} a personalised plan covering escape routes, documents, money, and phones
    \item \textbf{Specialist services:} 1800RESPECT, state domestic violence services, and refuges
    \item \textbf{Legal protection:} intervention orders (apprehended violence orders) and family law advice
    \item \textbf{Policing:} reporting violence to police when safe to do so
    \item \textbf{Health care:} treatment of injuries and psychological support
    \item \textbf{Support for children:} assessment and support for children affected by violence
    \item \textbf{Perpetrator programs:} behaviour-change programs for people who use violence
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Physical injury, disability, and death
    \item Depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress, and suicide
    \item Pregnancy complications and harm to unborn children
    \item Homelessness and financial devastation
    \item Long-term effects on children's development and mental health
    \item Social isolation and loss of support networks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Leaving a violent relationship is a process that takes time and often involves multiple attempts. With safety planning, specialist support, and legal protection, many people successfully leave violence and rebuild their lives. Recovery from the physical and psychological effects is possible with appropriate support. Early recognition, sensitive responses from health professionals, and access to services all improve outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Know the signs of domestic violence and the services available
    \item Keep important documents, money, medicines, and keys accessible
    \item Have a safety plan, including where to go and who to call
    \item Memorise or save the 1800RESPECT number and emergency contacts
    \item Talk to a trusted person, GP, or support service
    \item Protect children and seek help for their wellbeing
    \item Remember that the violence is never the victim's fault
    \item Encourage respectful relationships and healthy conflict resolution in the community
\end{itemize}
