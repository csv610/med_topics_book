\chapter{Altitude Sickness}
\index{Altitude Sickness}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Altitude sickness is the collective term for a group of illnesses that can occur when a person ascends to high altitude faster than the body can adapt. As altitude increases, the partial pressure of oxygen in the air falls, so the blood takes up less oxygen. The common and usually benign form is acute mountain sickness (AMS), characterised by headache, fatigue, and disturbed sleep. The two severe forms are high-altitude pulmonary oedema (HAPE), in which fluid accumulates in the lungs, and high-altitude cerebral oedema (HACE), in which fluid accumulates in the brain; both are medical emergencies requiring urgent descent. Altitude illness is largely preventable with gradual ascent, is treated most reliably by descent, and can be recognised early by paying attention to symptoms.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Altitude illness can affect anyone, regardless of age, fitness, or prior experience. Risk rises with increasing altitude and with the speed of ascent, and sleeping altitude is more important than the height reached during the day. Above approximately 2,500 metres, around one-quarter to one-half of trekkers develop symptoms of AMS. HAPE and HACE are much rarer, affecting roughly one to a few percent of people who sleep above 4,000 metres. Individuals who have had altitude illness before are more susceptible, as are those who ascend rapidly, climb higher despite early symptoms, or have pre-existing medical conditions affecting the heart or lungs.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Altitude illness results from the body's response to reduced inspired oxygen at height:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lower oxygen delivery:} falling barometric pressure at altitude reduces the amount of oxygen that reaches the blood and tissues.
    \item \textbf{Rapid ascent:} climbing too quickly gives the body insufficient time to acclimatise through increased breathing, haemoglobin production, and other adaptations.
    \item \textbf{Sleeping altitude:} the height at which a person sleeps overnight has a greater influence on illness than the day's peak altitude.
    \item \textbf{Individual susceptibility:} some people are prone to altitude illness despite slow, sensible climbing, while others tolerate great heights easily.
    \item \textbf{Severe forms:} hypoxia increases pressure in the pulmonary capillaries and raises vascular permeability, allowing fluid to leak into the lungs (HAPE) or to accumulate in the brain with swelling (HACE).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on the form and severity of altitude illness:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute mountain sickness:} headache, fatigue, dizziness, loss of appetite, nausea, and poor sleep, usually beginning 6 to 12 hours after arrival at altitude.
    \item \textbf{Worsening AMS:} vomiting, a headache unrelieved by simple analgesia, and unsteadiness signal impending danger.
    \item \textbf{HAPE:} breathlessness at rest, a dry cough progressing to productive cough, rattly breathing, chest tightness, fatigue, and blue lips or fingers.
    \item \textbf{HACE:} confusion, clumsiness, severe drowsiness, and an inability to walk in a straight line, typically following AMS or HAPE.
\end{itemize}

The cardinal rule is that any symptom of altitude illness at rest, or any neurological change, is a warning that ascent must stop and descent must begin.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Many symptoms at altitude have other explanations that must be considered before blaming altitude:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Dehydration, exhaustion, and sleep deprivation.
    \item The effects of alcohol or a hangover.
    \item Viral infection, gastroenteritis, or a chest cold.
    \item Hypothermia, especially with confusion or sluggishness.
    \item Migraine and other primary headache disorders.
    \item Pneumonia or other chest infections, which can mimic HAPE.
\end{itemize}

When in doubt, altitude illness is the working diagnosis, because the consequences of missing it are serious.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical help, or descend, if symptoms of AMS do not improve within a day despite rest, fluids, and simple analgesia, and never continue climbing with symptoms.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services, and activate mountain rescue where available, for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Breathlessness at rest or frothy sputum, suggesting HAPE.
    \item Confusion, severe unsteadiness, or drowsiness, suggesting HACE.
    \item An inability to walk in a straight line or to behave normally.
    \item Worsening symptoms despite rest and treatment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is made clinically on history and examination; investigations are used mainly to exclude other conditions or to confirm complications in hospital:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} altitude gained, the rate of ascent, sleeping altitude, and the onset and pattern of symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Examination:} temperature, respiratory rate, pulse oximetry, and chest auscultation for crackles.
    \item \textbf{Oxygen saturation:} particularly low readings suggest more serious illness, although values vary with altitude.
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} may confirm fluid in the lungs in HAPE.
    \item \textbf{Brain imaging:} CT scanning is used in hospital to exclude other causes of confusion or coma.
    \item \textbf{Key diagnostic test:} clear improvement with descent confirms the diagnosis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The guiding principle is simple: do not ascend further, and descend if symptoms are severe or worsening:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stop ascending:} rest at the current altitude and do not climb higher until fully recovered.
    \item \textbf{Descend:} descent is the definitive treatment; a drop of 500 to 1,000 metres usually relieves symptoms, and anyone with HAPE or HACE must descend urgently.
    \item \textbf{Rest and fluids:} with simple analgesia such as paracetamol for headache.
    \item \textbf{Acetazolamide:} speeds acclimatisation and is used to treat and to prevent AMS.
    \item \textbf{Dexamethasone:} a corticosteroid used for severe AMS and for HACE.
    \item \textbf{Oxygen:} helpful in severe illness and during descent.
    \item \textbf{Portable hyperbaric bag:} simulates descent and buys time when immediate evacuation is impossible.
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} required for HAPE and HACE, with oxygen, nifedipine or phosphodiesterase inhibitors for HAPE, and close monitoring.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated altitude illness can progress rapidly:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Progression of AMS to life-threatening HACE or HAPE.
    \item Respiratory failure from pulmonary oedema.
    \item Coma and death from cerebral oedema.
    \item Frostbite, hypothermia, and falls during delayed or hazardous evacuation.
    \item Permanent neurological injury in some survivors of severe HACE.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people with AMS recover fully within one to two days of rest or descent. With prompt descent, oxygen, and appropriate drug treatment, most people with HAPE or HACE recover, although severe cases can be fatal if evacuation is delayed. Untreated HACE can leave permanent brain damage. Anyone who has had altitude illness is at higher risk on later trips and should plan ascents more conservatively.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Altitude illness is largely preventable with sensible planning:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Ascend gradually: above 3,000 metres, raise sleeping altitude by no more than about 500 metres per day.
    \item Build in rest days and avoid the pattern of climbing high and sleeping low being reversed.
    \item Consider preventive acetazolamide for forced rapid ascents, under medical guidance.
    \item Avoid alcohol and sleeping tablets at altitude, which depress breathing.
    \item Drink plenty of fluids and eat regularly.
    \item Learn the warning signs of HAPE and HACE before travelling.
    \item Never continue ascending with symptoms of altitude illness.
\end{itemize}
