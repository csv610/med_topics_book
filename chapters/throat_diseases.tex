\noindent\textit{Educational reference; always seek professional advice for individual care.}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Throat diseases are disorders of the pharynx, tonsils, larynx (voice box), and adjacent structures that cause pain, swallowing difficulty, voice change, breathing problems, or a lump sensation. They range from short-lived viral pharyngitis to bacterial tonsillitis, glandular fever, reflux-related irritation, chronic tonsillar disease, peritonsillar abscess, epiglottitis, laryngitis, and head and neck cancers. Because the throat is a shared pathway for air and food and is rich in lymphoid tissue, infections are common, but not every sore throat needs antibiotics. Red-flag features---drooling, stridor, trismus, unilateral swelling, progressive hoarseness, or unexplained weight loss---change urgency dramatically. This chapter surveys major throat conditions for the public and primary care context in many countries, emphasising safe triage and evidence-based care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Sore throat is one of the commonest reasons for GP visits and self-care. Most acute cases are viral and peak in colder months and in school-aged children. Streptococcal pharyngitis matters particularly in children and adolescents and, in local Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities and other at-risk groups, connects to acute rheumatic fever prevention strategies. Glandular fever (Epstein--Barr virus) clusters in teenagers and young adults. Smoking, heavy alcohol use, and human papillomavirus (HPV) shape laryngeal and oropharyngeal cancer epidemiology. Gastro-oesophageal reflux and allergy contribute heavily to chronic throat clearing and globus. Epiglottitis became rarer in children after Haemophilus influenzae type b vaccination but still occurs, including in adults.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral infection:} rhinovirus, coronavirus, adenovirus, influenza, parainfluenza, and others inflame mucosa and lymphoid tissue
    \item \textbf{Bacterial infection:} Streptococcus pyogenes (group A strep) is the main antibiotic-relevant pathogen; Fusobacterium and mixed anaerobes feature in some deep neck infections
    \item \textbf{EBV and other systemic viruses:} intense pharyngitis, tonsillar exudate, and lymphadenopathy
    \item \textbf{Abscess formation:} peritonsillar (quinsy), retropharyngeal, and parapharyngeal collections follow inadequately contained infection
    \item \textbf{Reflux and irritants:} acid, smoke, and dry air inflame laryngopharynx
    \item \textbf{Allergy and post-nasal drip:} chronic throat irritation and cough
    \item \textbf{Neoplasia:} squamous cell carcinoma and other tumours present with persistent hoarseness, pain, ulcer, or neck mass
    \item \textbf{Neuromuscular and functional disorders:} vocal cord palsy, muscle tension dysphonia, and globus pharyngeus
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sore throat and odynophagia:} pain at rest or on swallowing
    \item \textbf{Fever and malaise:} systemic infective features
    \item \textbf{Tonsillar enlargement and exudate:} viral or bacterial; not specific alone
    \item \textbf{Cervical lymphadenopathy:} tender nodes in acute infection; firm persistent nodes need cancer exclusion
    \item \textbf{Hoarseness or voice loss:} laryngitis or cord pathology
    \item \textbf{Stridor or stertor:} airway warning signs
    \item \textbf{Trismus and hot-potato voice:} suggest quinsy or deep space infection
    \item \textbf{Globus and chronic clearing:} often reflux or muscle tension rather than infection
    \item \textbf{Referred otalgia:} throat pain felt in the ear, important in cancer pathways
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral versus streptococcal pharyngitis:} Centor/FeverPAIN-type features help but are imperfect; testing strategies vary by age and rheumatic fever risk
    \item \textbf{Glandular fever versus strep:} overlap is common; amoxicillin rash risk in EBV
    \item \textbf{Quinsy versus severe tonsillitis:} asymmetry, trismus, uvular deviation
    \item \textbf{Epiglottitis versus croup:} age, vaccination, drooling, and posture differ
    \item \textbf{Reflux laryngitis versus asthma cough versus ACE-inhibitor cough:} history and medicine review
    \item \textbf{Thyroid and cervical spine referred pain:} less common
    \item \textbf{Oropharyngeal cancer versus chronic tonsil asymmetry:} smoking, alcohol, HPV risk, and duration matter
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a GP for sore throat lasting more than a few days, high fever, inability to cope with fluids, rash, or recurrent tonsillitis. Seek same-day care for severe unilateral pain, muffled voice, or suspected glandular fever with marked swelling.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, stridor, drooling, or inability to swallow saliva
    \item Severe neck swelling, spreading redness, or signs of sepsis
    \item Sudden severe allergic swelling of tongue or throat
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} oropharynx, neck, ears, and respiratory status; avoid distressing throat exam if epiglottitis is likely---prioritise airway
    \item \textbf{Throat swab / rapid antigen / PCR:} for group A strep when results will change management
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} FBC, EBV serology, CRP when systemic illness or mononucleosis suspected
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} lateral neck X-ray rarely; CT for deep neck infection; ultrasound for selected neck masses
    \item \textbf{Flexible nasendoscopy:} ENT assessment of larynx and pharynx for chronic symptoms or red flags
    \item \textbf{Biopsy and oncology pathway:} for suspicious ulcers or masses
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} fluids, rest, simple analgesia (paracetamol or ibuprofen if suitable), humidification, and salt-water gargles for comfort
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics when indicated:} for proven or high-probability strep in appropriate patients; complete the course; follow local rheumatic fever prevention guidelines in high-risk groups
    \item \textbf{Corticosteroids:} sometimes used in severe swelling under medical advice
    \item \textbf{Abscess drainage:} needle aspiration or incision for quinsy; hospital care
    \item \textbf{Tonsillectomy:} considered for recurrent acute tonsillitis meeting criteria, obstructive sleep issues, or complications---shared decision with ENT
    \item \textbf{Reflux and allergy treatment:} lifestyle measures, medicines, and trigger control for chronic laryngopharynx irritation
    \item \textbf{Voice care:} rest, hydration, and speech pathology for dysphonia
    \item \textbf{Cancer treatment:} surgery, radiotherapy, and systemic therapy via multidisciplinary head and neck teams
    \item \textbf{Vaccination:} Hib, influenza, COVID-19 as recommended; HPV vaccine reduces some oropharyngeal cancer risk long-term
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Peritonsillar and deep neck abscess:} airway and mediastinal risk
    \item \textbf{Acute rheumatic fever and post-strep glomerulonephritis:} critical in high-risk populations
    \item \textbf{Airway obstruction:} epiglottitis, severe tonsillar hypertrophy, bilateral cord palsy
    \item \textbf{Dehydration:} especially in children refusing fluids
    \item \textbf{Chronic tonsillar disease and sleep apnoea:} quality-of-life and cardiopulmonary effects
    \item \textbf{Missed malignancy:} delayed diagnosis when symptoms are attributed only to infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most acute throat infections resolve within a week. Bacterial complications are uncommon with timely care. Recurrent tonsillitis may improve with time or surgery in selected patients. Chronic throat symptoms often improve when reflux, smoking, and voice strain are addressed. Cancer prognosis depends on stage and type; early ENT review for persistent hoarseness or ulcer improves odds.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wash hands and avoid sharing drink bottles during infective seasons
    \item Do not smoke; limit alcohol; complete HPV vaccination schedules for eligible people
    \item Stay up to date with influenza and other recommended vaccines
    \item Manage reflux: smaller meals, head-of-bed elevation, and reduced late-night eating
    \item Use voice sensibly; seek help for persistent hoarseness beyond two to three weeks
    \item Take antibiotics only when prescribed and as directed
    \item In high-risk communities, follow local sore throat assessment protocols aimed at rheumatic fever prevention
\end{itemize}
