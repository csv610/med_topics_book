\chapter{Lactose Intolerance}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Lactose intolerance is a common digestive condition characterised by the inability to digest lactose, the primary disaccharide sugar found in milk and other dairy products. This condition arises from a deficiency of lactase, an enzyme produced by the brush border microvilli of the enterocytes in the small intestine. Lactase is responsible for hydrolysing lactose into its absorbable monosaccharide components, glucose and galactose. When lactase levels are insufficient, undigested lactose passes into the colon, where it undergoes osmotic draw and bacterial fermentation, resulting in symptoms such as abdominal pain, bloating, flatulence, and diarrhoea. Lactose intolerance is distinct from cow's milk protein allergy, which is an immune-mediated reaction to milk proteins rather than a non-immunological metabolic malabsorption.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The prevalence of lactose intolerance varies widely across global populations, heavily influenced by genetic lineage and ancestral dairy farming practices. In northern European populations, lactase persistence (the continued production of lactase into adulthood) is highly prevalent, with lactose intolerance affecting less than 5\% of the population. Conversely, in East Asian populations, the prevalence of lactose intolerance exceeds 90\%. In Australia, the condition is highly prevalent among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, as well as individuals of Asian, African, Middle Eastern, and Mediterranean descent, where prevalence rates range from 70\% to 90\%. In the broader Australian population of European descent, it affects approximately 5\% to 20\% of individuals. The condition typically manifests in childhood or adolescence as natural lactase levels decline, though secondary lactose intolerance can occur at any age following bowel injury.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Lactose intolerance is classified into primary, secondary, congenital, and developmental forms, each with distinct underlying mechanisms of lactase insufficiency.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary lactase deficiency:} The most common form, caused by a genetically programmed down-regulation of lactase gene expression (LCT gene) after weaning, known as lactase non-persistence.
    \item \textbf{Secondary lactase deficiency:} A transient state resulting from mucosal damage to the small intestine. Common triggers include infectious gastroenteritis (for example, rotavirus or Giardia), coeliac disease, Crohn's disease, small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO), and chemotherapy or pelvic radiation.
    \item \textbf{Congenital lactase deficiency:} A rare autosomal recessive disorder where infants are born with complete absence or minimal activity of the lactase enzyme, preventing them from digesting breast milk or standard formula.
    \item \textbf{Developmental lactase deficiency:} A temporary deficiency observed in premature infants born before 34 weeks of gestation, due to the late developmental maturation of lactase activity in the fetal gut.
    \item \textbf{Osmotic draw and fermentation:} Undigested lactose remains in the intestinal lumen, exerting an osmotic gradient that draws water and electrolytes into the bowel, causing liquid stools. Upon reaching the colon, anaerobic bacteria ferment the lactose, producing gases (hydrogen, carbon dioxide, and methane) and short-chain fatty acids, leading to bloating, flatulence, and abdominal pain.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of lactose intolerance is characterised by gastrointestinal distress that typically onset between 30 minutes and two hours following the ingestion of lactose-containing food or drink.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Abdominal cramps and pain, usually colicky and located around the umbilicus or in the lower abdomen.
    \item Bloating and abdominal distension due to gas accumulation.
    \item Increased flatulence as a direct byproduct of colonic bacterial fermentation.
    \item Osmotic diarrhoea, characterised by loose, watery, and sometimes acidic stools that can cause perianal irritation.
    \item Borborygmi, or loud rumbling and gurgling noises from the abdomen.
    \item Nausea and, less frequently, vomiting.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the gastrointestinal symptoms of lactose intolerance are non-specific, it is essential to distinguish it from other functional and structural disorders of the gastrointestinal tract.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS):} A functional bowel disorder characterised by recurrent abdominal pain, bloating, and altered bowel habits, but symptoms are not specifically linked to lactose ingestion and are often triggered by multiple FODMAPs.
    \item \textbf{Cow's milk protein allergy (CMPA):} An IgE or non-IgE mediated immune reaction to milk proteins (such as casein or whey). CMPA presents with immunological signs like urticaria, angioedema, atopic eczema, or respiratory distress, and is common in infants, unlike primary lactose intolerance.
    \item \textbf{Coeliac disease:} An autoimmune enteropathy triggered by dietary gluten, resulting in chronic mucosal damage and malabsorption, distinguished by positive serology (anti-tTG IgA) and characteristic histological findings on duodenal biopsy.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD):} Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis, which present with systemic features such as weight loss, fever, nocturnal diarrhoea, hematochezia, and elevated inflammatory markers (such as faecal calprotectin).
    \item \textbf{Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO):} An excessive bacterial population in the small intestine that causes premature fermentation of carbohydrates, producing similar symptoms of gas and bloating, diagnosed via glucose or lactulose breath tests.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should seek medical evaluation if they experience persistent gastrointestinal symptoms that do not respond to dietary adjustments, or if they develop features suggestive of a more serious underlying pathology.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, sudden, or localised abdominal pain that may indicate an acute abdomen or bowel obstruction.
    \item Persistent vomiting with an inability to tolerate fluids, leading to severe dehydration (characterised by confusion, lethargy, oliguria, or hypotension).
    \item Passage of blood in the stool or black, tarry stools (melaena).
    \item High fever accompanied by chills, rigors, or signs of systemic infection.
    \item Severe chest pain or difficulty breathing.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of lactose intolerance is often established clinically, but objective testing can support the diagnosis and help exclude other conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary elimination and reintroduction:} A pragmatic approach involving a strict lactose-free diet for two weeks, followed by a formal challenge with lactose. Resolution of symptoms during elimination and recurrence during reintroduction strongly supports the diagnosis.
    \item \textbf{Hydrogen breath test (HBT):} The non-invasive diagnostic standard. The patient ingests a standardised dose of lactose (usually 25 to 50 grams), and breath hydrogen levels are measured at regular intervals. A rise of 20 parts per million (ppm) or more above baseline indicates lactose malabsorption.
    \item \textbf{Stool acidity test:} Useful in infants where breath testing is impractical. A stool pH of less than 5.5 indicates carbohydrate malabsorption, as bacterial fermentation produces lactic acid.
    \item \textbf{Lactose tolerance test:} A blood-based test where blood glucose is measured after lactose ingestion. A failure of blood glucose to rise by more than 1.1 mmol/L (20 mg/dL) indicates poor absorption of glucose and galactose, though this test is largely superseded by HBT.
    \item \textbf{Duodenal biopsy:} Obtained via upper endoscopy to evaluate disaccharidase activity or to rule out mucosal causes of secondary lactase deficiency, such as coeliac disease.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management focuses on symptom control through dietary modification, enzyme replacement, and maintaining nutritional adequacy.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary restriction:} Limiting the intake of high-lactose foods (such as fresh milk, soft cheeses, and ice cream) based on individual tolerance. Many patients can tolerate up to 12 grams of lactose (approximately one cup of milk) when consumed with other foods.
    \item \textbf{Lactase enzyme supplements:} Over-the-counter tablets or drops containing lactase (derived from fungi or yeasts) taken immediately before consuming dairy products to assist in digestion.
    \item \textbf{Lactose-free and alternative dairy products:} Substituting regular dairy with lactose-free cow's milk (which contains pre-hydrolysed glucose and galactose) or plant-based alternatives (such as soy, almond, or oat milk) that are fortified with calcium.
    \item \textbf{Calcium and Vitamin D supplementation:} Recommended for individuals who strictly avoid dairy products to mitigate the risk of bone mineral density depletion.
    \item \textbf{Probiotic supplementation:} Some strains of probiotics, such as Lactobacillus acidophilus, produce lactase and may help reduce symptoms by altering colonic microflora.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Osteopenia and osteoporosis, resulting from a chronic lack of dietary calcium and vitamin D intake due to strict dairy avoidance.
    \item Nutritional deficiencies, particularly in children, leading to poor weight gain or failure to thrive if dairy is restricted without suitable nutritional substitutes.
    \item Psychological distress, anxiety around meals, and social isolation due to the fear of experiencing acute gastrointestinal symptoms in public.
    \item Diagnostic delay of serious underlying conditions, such as coeliac disease or IBD, if symptoms are falsely attributed solely to primary lactose intolerance.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals with lactose intolerance is excellent. The condition is non-fatal and does not cause permanent damage to the gastrointestinal mucosa. Most individuals can achieve complete symptom resolution through simple dietary adjustments and the use of lactase enzyme replacements. Secondary lactose intolerance typically resolves within two to eight weeks once the primary mucosal injury (such as from infectious gastroenteritis) has healed, allowing the enterocytes to regenerate and resume normal lactase production.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Gradually introduce small amounts of lactose into the diet to determine individual tolerance thresholds.
    \item Consume dairy products alongside other foods (especially those high in fat or protein) to slow gastric emptying and improve digestion.
    \item Read ingredient labels carefully to identify hidden sources of lactose, such as milk solids, whey, curds, or non-fat milk powder in processed foods.
    \item Select aged hard cheeses (such as cheddar, parmesan, or Swiss) and traditional yoghurts, which naturally contain very low levels of lactose.
    \item Ensure adequate calcium intake by incorporating non-dairy calcium sources, such as leafy green vegetables, canned sardines with bones, tofu, and calcium-fortified foods.
\end{itemize}
