\chapter{Loose stools (diarrhoea)}
\index{Loose stools (diarrhoea)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Loose stools or diarrhoea is defined as the passage of three or more loose, watery, or unformed bowel movements within a 24-hour period, or a stool frequency that is significantly higher than normal for the individual. Diarrhoea is classified based on duration: acute diarrhoea lasts for less than 14 days; persistent diarrhoea lasts between 14 and 30 days; and chronic diarrhoea continues for more than 30 days. It is a common symptom representing physiological disruptions in gastrointestinal fluid secretion, absorption, or transit. While most acute episodes are self-limiting and resolve with minimal intervention, diarrhoea can become life-threatening—particularly in infants, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals—due to rapid dehydration, hypovolaemia, and severe electrolyte imbalances.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Diarrhoea is one of the most common reasons for primary care consultations and hospitalisations worldwide. Globally, acute diarrhoeal disease is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, causing approximately 1.5 million deaths annually, predominantly among children under five years of age in developing countries. In many countries, gastrointestinal infections leading to acute diarrhoea are highly prevalent, with millions of cases occurring each year, often related to foodborne pathogens or viral outbreaks in childcare and aged-care facilities. Chronic diarrhoea affects approximately 4\% to 5\% of the adult population in high-income nations. The primary drivers of chronic diarrhoea in these populations include irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and microscopic colitis, the latter being increasingly diagnosed in older females.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Diarrhoea results from altered fluid and electrolyte transport across the intestinal mucosa, categorized as secretory, osmotic, inflammatory, or hypermotile.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infectious pathogens:} Viruses (such as norovirus, rotavirus, and adenovirus) are the most common causes of acute gastroenteritis. Bacterial pathogens (including \textit{Campylobacter jejuni}, \textit{Salmonella enterica}, \textit{Shigella} species, and toxin-producing \textit{Escherichia coli}) typically cause more severe, invasive, or inflammatory diarrhoea. Parasites (such as \textit{Giardia duodenalis} and \textit{Cryptosporidium} species) are common causes of persistent or waterborne diarrhoea.
    \item \textbf{Medication side effects:} Broad-spectrum antibiotics disturb the gut microbiota, leading to mild dysbiosis or predisposing patients to opportunistic infections like \textit{Clostridioides difficile}. Other medications, including metformin, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), and magnesium-containing antacids, can also cause diarrhoea.
    \item \textbf{Malabsorptive disorders:} Coeliac disease (an autoimmune reaction to gluten causing enteropathy), pancreatic exocrine insufficiency, and lactose or fructose intolerance lead to unabsorbed solutes in the bowel lumen, drawing water in and causing osmotic diarrhoea.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory conditions:} Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, and microscopic colitis disrupt the mucosal barrier, impairing absorption and causing inflammatory exudation of blood, mucus, and proteins.
    \item \textbf{Functional bowel disorders:} Irritable bowel syndrome with diarrhoea predominance (IBS-D) is characterised by visceral hypersensitivity and accelerated colonic transit in the absence of structural pathology.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The presentation of loose stools varies according to the underlying cause, ranging from mild watery output to severe systemic illness.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Altered stool consistency and frequency:} Stools are loose, mushy, or watery, and are often accompanied by urgency, faecal incontinence, and tenesmus.
    \item \textbf{Abdominal discomfort:} Cramping, bloating, and generalised abdominal pain, which may be relieved or worsened by defecation.
    \item \textbf{Systemic infectious symptoms:} Fever, chills, myalgia, nausea, and vomiting, pointing to a viral or bacterial gastroenteritis.
    \item \textbf{Signs of dehydration:} Thirst, dry mucous membranes, decreased skin turgor, dark urine, oliguria, tachycardia, orthostatic hypotension, and dizziness.
    \item \textbf{Alarm features (red flags):} Haematochezia (blood in the stool), melaena (black, tarry stools), nocturnal diarrhoea that awakens the patient from sleep, unexplained weight loss, and persistent high fever.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
In patients presenting with loose stools, it is crucial to differentiate self-limiting infectious processes from chronic, systemic, or neoplastic conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute infectious gastroenteritis vs. food poisoning:} Infectious gastroenteritis involves active pathogen replication in the gut, whereas food poisoning (e.g., from \textit{Staphylococcus aureus} enterotoxin) results from pre-formed toxins, causing rapid-onset vomiting and diarrhoea that resolves within 24 hours.
    \item \textbf{Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS-D) vs. inflammatory bowel disease (IBD):} IBS-D lacks markers of inflammation, system symptoms, or bleeding, whereas IBD (Crohn's or ulcerative colitis) presents with mucosal inflammation, elevated faecal calprotectin, and potential systemic signs.
    \item \textbf{Coeliac disease vs. non-coeliac gluten sensitivity:} Coeliac disease is characterised by positive serological markers and characteristic duodenal villous atrophy, which are absent in non-coeliac gluten sensitivity.
    \item \textbf{Microscopic colitis:} A condition diagnosed only via colonic biopsies, characterized by normal endoscopic appearances but microscopic mucosal inflammation, typically presenting as chronic, watery diarrhoea in middle-aged or older adults.
    \item \textbf{Colorectal cancer:} Must be excluded in any patient over 50 years of age presenting with new-onset, persistent loose stools or rectally bleeding.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most cases of acute diarrhoea resolve within a few days. However, patients should seek medical advice if they experience prolonged symptoms or warning signs.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe dehydration characterised by confusion, lethargy, extreme weakness, or loss of consciousness.
    \item Signs of shock, such as cold, clammy skin, rapid shallow breathing, and a weak, rapid pulse.
    \item Intolerable, severe abdominal pain that is constant and localized.
    \item Passing large quantities of blood or black, tarry stools.
    \item Inability to retain any oral fluids due to intractable vomiting.
\end{itemize}
\textit{Note: Seek GP advice if diarrhoea lasts more than 48 hours in children or the elderly, or more than four weeks in adults, or if it is accompanied by high fever or unexplained weight loss.}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
For acute, uncomplicated diarrhoea, investigations are rarely required. For chronic or severe presentations, diagnostic testing is essential.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stool multiplex PCR:} Rapidly detects common viral, bacterial, and parasitic pathogens (e.g., norovirus, \textit{Campylobacter}, \textit{Salmonella}, \textit{Shigella}, \textit{Giardia}).
    \item \textbf{Stool culture and microscopy:} Indicated to identify specific bacterial strains, assess antibiotic sensitivities, and screen for ova and parasites (O\&P).
    \item \textbf{Faecal calprotectin:} A sensitive biomarker of intestinal inflammation, used to differentiate between inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and functional disorders like IBS.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} Full blood count (to check for anaemia or leukocytosis), urea, electrolytes, and creatinine (to monitor kidney function and hydration), C-reactive protein (CRP), and coeliac serology (tTG IgA and total IgA).
    \item \textbf{Colonoscopy with biopsy:} The gold standard for investigating chronic diarrhoea, allowing direct visualisation of the mucosa and biopsy collection to diagnose IBD, microscopic colitis, or malignancy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The primary objective of management is the prevention or correction of dehydration, followed by addressing the underlying cause.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rehydration therapy:} The cornerstone of treatment. Oral Rehydration Solutions (ORS) containing specific ratios of glucose and electrolytes should be used to exploit active sodium-glucose co-transport in the gut. Intravenous fluids (such as Normal Saline or Hartmann's solution) are required for severe dehydration or when oral intake is impossible.
    \item \textbf{Antidiarrhoeal agents:} Loperamide (an opioid receptor agonist that slows intestinal transit) can be used for symptomatic relief in mild-to-moderate watery diarrhoea. It is strictly contraindicated in cases of bloody diarrhoea, high fever, or suspected \textit{Clostridioides difficile} infection due to the risk of precipitating toxic megacolon.
    \item \textbf{Antibiotic therapy:} Avoided in most cases of acute gastroenteritis as it is self-limiting and can prolong carrier states (e.g., in \textit{Salmonella}). Antibiotics (e.g., azithromycin or ciprofloxacin) are reserved for severe, invasive bacterial infections, high-risk travellers' diarrhoea, or immunocompromised patients.
    \item \textbf{Dietary modification:} Avoid lactose-containing foods, caffeine, alcohol, high-fat foods, and artificial sweeteners, which can worsen diarrhoea. Encourage small, frequent meals consisting of easily digestible starches (such as rice, oats, boiled potatoes, and crackers) as tolerated.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Unmanaged or severe diarrhoea can lead to rapid clinical deterioration and long-term sequelae.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Severe dehydration and hypovolaemic shock:} Critical volume depletion leading to organ hypoperfusion and cardiovascular collapse.
    \item \textbf{Electrolyte disturbances:} Hypokalaemia (low potassium) and hyponatraemia (low sodium), which can cause cardiac arrhythmias, muscle weakness, confusion, and seizures.
    \item \textbf{Acute kidney injury (AKI):} Prerenal AKI resulting from profound dehydration and reduced renal blood flow.
    \item \textbf{Malnutrition and cachexia:} Chronic malabsorption of macronutrients and micronutrients, leading to weight loss, muscle wasting, and vitamin deficiencies.
    \item \textbf{Post-infectious syndromes:} Temporary lactose intolerance, post-infectious irritable bowel syndrome (PI-IBS), or reactive arthritis following bacterial gastroenteritis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for acute diarrhoea is excellent, with the vast majority of cases resolving spontaneously within three to seven days without long-term consequences. In cases of chronic diarrhoea, the prognosis depends entirely on the underlying diagnosis. Coeliac disease carries a good prognosis with strict adherence to a gluten-free diet. Inflammatory bowel disease requires lifelong monitoring and maintenance medical therapy, but most patients achieve long-term remission. Chronic diarrhoea secondary to functional bowel disorders is managed with dietary adjustments, stress management, and symptomatic therapy.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventative strategies focus on hygiene, safe food preparation, and vaccination.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hand hygiene:} Wash hands thoroughly with soap and running water for at least 20 seconds, particularly after using the toilet, changing nappies, and before preparing or eating food. Alcohol-based hand sanitisers are useful but less effective against certain pathogens like norovirus and \textit{Clostridioides difficile}.
    \item \textbf{Safe food hygiene:} Keep raw and cooked foods separate, cook meat (especially poultry) to safe internal temperatures, store food below 5 degrees Celsius, and avoid consuming unpasteurised milk or juices.
    \item \textbf{Travel precautions:} When travelling in areas with poor sanitation, drink only bottled, boiled, or chemically treated water, avoid ice, and eat only food that has been freshly cooked and served hot.
    \item \textbf{Vaccination:} Ensure infants receive the rotavirus vaccine as part of the National Immunisation Program to prevent severe childhood diarrhoeal illness.
\end{itemize}
