\chapter{Watery stools (diarrhoea)}
\index{Watery stools (diarrhoea)}
\section*{Definition and Overview}
Diarrhoea means passing loose or watery stools more often than is normal for the individual. Acute diarrhoea usually lasts less than 14 days; persistent diarrhoea lasts 14--29 days and chronic diarrhoea lasts 4 weeks or longer. The principal immediate risk is dehydration and loss of electrolytes.
\section*{Epidemiology}
Acute diarrhoea is commonly caused by viral gastroenteritis, contaminated food or water, or medication effects. Risk of serious dehydration is higher in infants, older adults, pregnant people, and people with significant chronic disease or immune suppression.
\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Causes include infections, antibiotics and other medicines, food intolerance, inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease, malabsorption, endocrine disease, and functional bowel disorders. Fluid loss results from reduced absorption, increased secretion, or rapid intestinal transit.
\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms may include cramps, urgency, nausea, vomiting, fever, bloating, and dehydration. Blood or mucus, severe pain, persistent fever, weight loss, nocturnal symptoms, or symptoms after travel or antibiotics require closer assessment.
\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Consider infectious gastroenteritis, food poisoning, medication-associated diarrhoea including *Clostridioides difficile*, inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease, irritable bowel syndrome, microscopic colitis, pancreatic or bile-acid malabsorption, endocrine disease, and colorectal disease.
\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Urgent assessment is needed for confusion, fainting, very little urine, inability to keep fluids down, severe abdominal pain, bloody or black stools, high fever, signs of sepsis, or dehydration in a young child or frail adult. Seek medical advice for diarrhoea lasting more than a few days, recurrent episodes, recent antibiotics or hospitalisation, or significant immune suppression.
\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
History and examination usually guide assessment. Stool testing is reserved for selected cases, such as blood, fever, severe illness, prolonged symptoms, outbreaks, travel, or immune suppression. Blood tests and imaging are guided by severity, duration, and suspected cause.
\section*{Management}
Use frequent small amounts of oral rehydration solution, continue breastfeeding and usual tolerated food, and avoid dehydration-promoting alcohol. Antibiotics are not routinely needed and can be harmful; use them only when a clinician identifies an indication. Antidiarrhoeal medicines are unsuitable in some children and in people with bloody diarrhoea or high fever. Severe dehydration may require intravenous fluids.
\section*{Complications}
Complications include dehydration, electrolyte disturbance, acute kidney injury, malnutrition, haemolytic uraemic syndrome with certain infections, and post-infectious bowel symptoms.
\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most uncomplicated infectious episodes settle within several days. Persistent or recurrent diarrhoea needs investigation because treatment and prognosis depend on the cause.
\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Wash hands, use safe water, cook food thoroughly, avoid preparing food for others while infectious, clean contaminated surfaces, and follow local advice about exclusion from childcare or food work.
