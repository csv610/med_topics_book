\chapter{Heart Surgery}
\index{Heart Surgery}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Heart surgery is a range of operations performed on the heart and its major vessels to treat conditions that cannot be managed adequately with medication or minimally invasive procedures. It includes coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), valve repair and replacement, surgery for congenital heart defects, surgery on the aorta, and implantation of devices such as ventricular assist devices. Some heart surgery is elective and planned, while emergency surgery is required for conditions such as aortic dissection and complications of heart attacks. Modern heart surgery is safe and effective, with most patients returning to a full, active life.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Heart surgery is common in many countries, with thousands of procedures performed each year. Coronary artery bypass grafting is the most frequently performed major heart operation, although angioplasty and stenting have reduced the need for bypass in some patients. Valve surgery is increasingly common as the population ages and degenerative valve disease becomes more prevalent. Around half of all valve operations now involve repair rather than replacement where possible. Mortality rates for elective bypass surgery are under 2\%, and outcomes continue to improve with advances in technique and perioperative care.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coronary artery disease:} atherosclerotic blockages in the arteries supplying the heart muscle, treated with bypass grafts
    \item \textbf{Valvular heart disease:} stenotic or leaking valves repaired or replaced
    \item \textbf{Aortic disease:} aneurysms and dissection of the aorta repaired with grafts
    \item \textbf{Congenital defects:} holes, malformed valves, and abnormal connections corrected in children and adults
    \item \textbf{End-stage heart failure:} treated with mechanical support devices and transplantation
    \item \textbf{Procedures:} most operations use cardiopulmonary bypass, which temporarily takes over the heart and lung function while the surgeon works on a still, bloodless heart
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Surgery is recommended when symptoms such as angina, breathlessness, or fainting cannot be controlled by medication
    \item Bypass surgery is indicated for severe coronary disease, particularly when the left main artery or multiple vessels are affected
    \item Valve surgery is indicated for severe stenosis or regurgitation, especially with symptoms or declining heart function
    \item Emergency surgery treats aortic dissection, ruptured aneurysms, and complications of heart attack
    \item Congenital defects are repaired to prevent heart failure, growth problems, and long-term complications
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Not all coronary disease needs surgery: angioplasty with stenting and medical therapy are alternatives selected by severity, anatomy, and patient factors
    \item Not all valve disease needs surgery: the timing depends on severity, symptoms, and valve repair potential
    \item Some arrhythmias and structural problems are treated by catheter procedures rather than open surgery
    \item Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) offers a less invasive option for many patients with aortic stenosis
    \item The decision to operate is individualised through a multidisciplinary heart team
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients with known heart disease should report any new or worsening chest pain, breathlessness, or fainting, and those awaiting surgery should follow pre-admission instructions carefully.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, pressure, or breathlessness before a planned operation
    \item Sudden severe chest or back pain, suggesting aortic dissection
    \item Fainting, collapse, or palpitations with dizziness
    \item Emergency symptoms after recent heart surgery, such as severe pain, heavy bleeding from the wound, or sudden breathlessness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coronary angiography:} maps the coronary arteries and their blockages
    \item \textbf{Echocardiogram:} assesses valve structure and function, chamber sizes, and pumping strength
    \item \textbf{Cardiac CT and MRI:} detailed anatomical imaging for planning
    \item \textbf{Preoperative assessment:} blood tests, ECG, chest X-ray, and assessment of kidney, lung, and general fitness
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} scores estimate operative risk to inform shared decision-making
    \item \textbf{Informed consent:} the surgeon explains the procedure, its benefits, risks, and alternatives
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coronary artery bypass grafting:} arteries or veins are used to bypass blocked vessels
    \item \textbf{Valve repair or replacement:} repairs are preferred where feasible; replacements use mechanical or tissue valves
    \item \textbf{Aortic surgery:} replacement of diseased sections of the aorta
    \item \textbf{Congenital surgery:} closure of defects and correction of malformations
    \item \textbf{Cardiopulmonary bypass:} the heart is stopped and blood is oxygenated by a machine during most operations
    \item \textbf{Minimally invasive approaches:} smaller incisions for selected operations, with faster recovery
    \item \textbf{Postoperative care:} intensive care, pain management, early mobilisation, and cardiac rehabilitation
    \item \textbf{Secondary prevention:} medications, lifestyle change, and risk factor control after surgery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding and the need for reoperation
    \item Infection of the wound, chest, or blood, including sternal wound infection
    \item Stroke and neurological complications
    \item Arrhythmias, particularly atrial fibrillation after bypass surgery
    \item Kidney injury and heart failure in the early postoperative period
    \item Long-term risks depend on the procedure: mechanical valves require lifelong blood-thinning treatment
    \item Graft or valve failure over many years, requiring repeat procedures in some patients
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Outcomes after heart surgery are excellent in suitable patients. Bypass surgery relieves angina in most people and improves survival in those with extensive coronary disease. Valve repair and replacement restore quality of life, and repair of congenital defects in childhood offers a normal life expectancy for many conditions. The recovery period typically involves several weeks of reduced activity and a structured cardiac rehabilitation program. Long-term results depend on adherence to medication, risk factor control, and ongoing cardiac follow-up.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Control blood pressure, cholesterol, and diabetes to slow disease progression
    \item Quit smoking and maintain a heart-healthy diet and weight
    \item Take prescribed medicines exactly as directed, including blood thinners and heart medications
    \item Complete cardiac rehabilitation and progress activity gradually after surgery
    \item Attend regular follow-up with your cardiologist and surgeon
    \item Practise good wound care and report any sign of infection
    \item Ask your health care team about driving, work, and activity restrictions during recovery
\end{itemize}
