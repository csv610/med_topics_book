\chapter{Marijuana}
\index{Marijuana}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Marijuana, commonly known as cannabis, is a psychoactive preparation derived from the \textit{Cannabis sativa} or \textit{Cannabis indica} plants. The plant contains over 100 cannabinoids, the most prominent being delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC, the primary psychoactive compound responsible for the "high") and cannabidiol (CBD, a non-psychoactive compound with therapeutic properties). Marijuana is the most widely used illicit substance globally and in many countries. It is classified as a Schedule 9 controlled substance in many countries, though pharmaceutical-grade cannabis preparations (medicinal cannabis) are legally available under strict prescription guidelines (Schedule 8 or 4) for specific indications like chronic pain, muscle spasticity in multiple sclerosis, and refractory epilepsy. Recreational marijuana use primarily affects the central nervous system, producing relaxation, altered sensory perception, and cognitive impairments.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cannabis is the most commonly consumed illicit drug in many countries. According to national drug strategy surveys, approximately 35--38\% of people aged 14 and over have used cannabis in their lifetime, with about 11\% reporting use in the past year. Use is most common among young adults aged 18 to 29 years, with a higher prevalence in males than females. Presentations to emergency departments and general practices for cannabis-related concerns (such as cyclic vomiting, cannabis-induced psychosis, or withdrawal symptoms) have increased, coinciding with rising concentrations of THC in modern cultivated cannabis products.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The biological effects of marijuana are mediated through its interaction with the endogenous cannabinoid system:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cannabinoid receptor binding:} THC acts as a partial agonist at G-protein coupled cannabinoid receptors, primarily CB1 (abundant in the central nervous system, including the hippocampus, cerebellum, and basal ganglia) and CB2 (associated with the immune system).
    \item \textbf{Neurotransmitter modulation:} activation of CB1 receptors inhibits the release of neurotransmitters such as GABA and glutamate, altering cognitive function, memory, coordination, and reward pathways.
    \item \textbf{Psychological effects:} the stimulation of dopamine release in the mesolimbic reward system mediates the euphoric properties of THC.
    \item \textbf{Therapeutic mechanism of CBD:} CBD has low affinity for CB1/CB2 receptors but acts as an allosteric modulator, exerting anti-inflammatory, analgesic, and anticonvulsant effects while reducing the psychotropic effects of THC.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features vary based on route of administration, dosage, and chronicity of use:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute intoxication: feelings of euphoria, relaxation, heightened sensory perception, increased sociability, and increased appetite ("the munchies")
    \item Physical features of intoxication: conjunctival injection (red eyes), tachycardia, dry mouth (xerostomia), and mild pupillary dilation
    \item Acute adverse effects: anxiety, panic attacks, paranoia, impaired short-term memory, slowed reaction times, and motor incoordination
    \item Chronic use features: chronic cough and bronchitis (if smoked), cognitive deficits (impaired attention and learning), and amotivational syndrome
    \item Cannabis Hyperemesis Syndrome (CHS): characterized by cyclic episodes of severe nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain, typically relieved temporarily by hot showers or baths
    \item Withdrawal features: irritability, anger, sleep disturbances, vivid dreams, decreased appetite, weight loss, restlessness, and mild physical symptoms (sweating, tremors) peaking 2--4 days after cessation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Synthetic cannabinoid toxicity ("Kronic", "Spice"):} produces a much more severe sympathomimetic and psychotic presentation, including severe agitation, hallucinations, seizures, and cardiotoxicity, compared to natural cannabis.
    \item \textbf{Alcohol or sedative intoxication:} presents with ataxia and slurred speech, but lacks the tachycardia and red eyes characteristic of cannabis.
    \item \textbf{Primary psychotic disorders:} presenting with delusions or hallucinations, distinguished by long-term clinical history and absence of a positive toxicology screen.
    \item \textbf{Cyclic vomiting syndrome:} presents with recurrent vomiting episodes, but lacks the specific association with chronic daily cannabis use and the compulsive hot-bathing behavior seen in CHS.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical care if experiencing severe anxiety, chest pain, or intractable vomiting after cannabis use, or if experiencing withdrawal symptoms when attempting to quit.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, sudden chest pain or rapid, irregular heartbeats
    \item Seizures or loss of consciousness
    \item Acute, severe psychiatric distress: drug-induced psychosis with severe paranoia, delusions, or hallucinations
    \item Thoughts of self-harm or suicide
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical history and physical examination:} assessing frequency of use, route of administration, and presence of physical signs like red eyes and tachycardia.
    \item \textbf{Urine drug screen:} detects 11-nor-delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol-9-carboxylic acid (THC-COOH), the main metabolite. It can remain positive for up to 3--5 days in occasional users and up to 30 days or more in chronic daily users.
    \item \textbf{Blood testing:} to exclude other drug exposures, check electrolytes (in CHS patients with severe vomiting), and rule out other causes of tachycardia.
    \item \textbf{ECG:} indicated in patients presenting with palpitations or chest pain.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is primarily supportive and symptomatic:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute intoxication:} supportive care in a calm, quiet environment. Oral benzodiazepines (e.g., diazepam) are highly effective for managing severe anxiety, panic, or agitation.
    \item \textbf{Cannabis Hyperemesis Syndrome (CHS):} cessation of all cannabis use is the only definitive cure. Acute episodes are treated with intravenous fluids, antiemetics, and topical capsaicin cream applied to the abdomen.
    \item \textbf{Withdrawal management:} supportive counseling, cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), and brief symptomatic treatment for insomnia or anxiety (e.g., melatonin or short-term diazepam).
    \item \textbf{Long-term rehabilitation:} motivation enhancement therapy (MET), CBT, and support groups (such as Narcotics Anonymous) to address cannabis use disorder.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cannabis use disorder (addiction) and severe withdrawal symptoms
    \item Cognitive impairment, particularly affecting memory, attention, and learning, especially when daily use starts in adolescence
    \item Increased risk of developing chronic psychotic disorders (e.g., schizophrenia) in genetically vulnerable individuals
    \item Chronic respiratory illnesses (bronchitis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) if smoked
    \item Severe dehydration and electrolyte imbalances from CHS
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for acute cannabis intoxication and withdrawal is excellent, with symptoms resolving fully without permanent physical harm. For individuals with cannabis use disorder, outcomes vary; recovery is highly achievable with structured psychological interventions and strong social support. For patients with CHS, the prognosis is excellent if they maintain complete abstinence from cannabis, as symptoms typically resolve within days to weeks of cessation.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Abstinence from non-medicinal cannabis use is the most effective way to prevent associated health risks
    \item If using medicinal cannabis, take it strictly under the supervision of a prescribing doctor
    \item Avoid driving or operating machinery while under the influence of cannabis, as it impairs motor skills and reaction times
    \item Seek professional help early if you experience difficulties controlling your cannabis use, or if it is affecting your mental health or daily functioning
    \item Discuss any family history of mental health disorders (particularly psychosis) with your doctor before using cannabis
\end{itemize}
