\chapter{Mental illness}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Mental illness (also referred to as mental health disorders) encompasses a broad spectrum of clinically significant disturbances in an individual's cognition, emotional regulation, or behaviour. These conditions are characterised by alterations in thinking, mood, or behaviour associated with personal distress and impaired personal, social, or occupational functioning. Mental illnesses are not simply temporary responses to life stressors, but are recognised medical conditions arising from complex interactions between genetic, biological, environmental, and psychosocial factors. Examples include mood disorders (e.g. major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder), anxiety disorders, psychotic disorders (e.g. schizophrenia), eating disorders, and personality disorders. Clinically, mental illness is diagnosed using standardised criteria such as the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) or the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11). Effective management involves multidisciplinary care combining pharmacological, psychological, and social interventions, aiming to support recovery and improve overall quality of life.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Mental illness represents a leading contributor to the global burden of disease, affecting hundreds of millions of people worldwide. In Australia, the National Study of Mental Health and Wellbeing indicates that approximately one in five Australians (20\%) aged 16--85 experience a mental disorder in any given 12-month period, and nearly half (45\%) will experience a mental illness at some point in their lifetime. Anxiety disorders are the most prevalent class of mental illness in Australia, followed by affective (mood) disorders and substance use disorders. The onset of mental illness typically occurs early, with 75\% of disorders emerging before the age of 25. Vulnerable populations, including Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, LGBTQIA+ individuals, and those living in rural or remote areas, experience disproportionately higher rates of psychological distress and barriers to accessing mental healthcare services.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The development of mental illness is explained by the biopsychosocial model, involving a combination of predisposing, precipitating, and perpetuating factors.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurochemical dysregulation:} Alterations in the synthesis, release, or receptor binding of neurotransmitters such as serotonin, dopamine, noradrenaline, and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) in the brain.
    \item \textbf{Genetic predisposition:} Polygenic hereditary factors that influence brain development, temperament, and susceptibility to environmental stressors.
    \item \textbf{Structural and functional brain changes:} Alterations in specific brain regions, such as hyperactivity of the amygdala (in anxiety) or volume loss in the hippocampus (in chronic depression).
    \item \textbf{Early life adversity:} Childhood trauma, abuse, neglect, or unstable home environments that permanently alter the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to chronic stress dysregulation.
    \item \textbf{Environmental and social stressors:} Chronic physical illness, poverty, social isolation, relationship breakdown, or substance abuse that precipitate or exacerbate mental health conditions.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features of mental illness vary widely across diagnostic categories, affecting multiple domains of human experience.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Affective disturbances:} Persistent sadness, loss of pleasure in activities (anhedonia), extreme mood swings, or inappropriate emotional responses.
    \item \textbf{Cognitive changes:} Difficulty concentrating, memory impairment, racing thoughts, or intrusive, obsessive worries.
    \item \textbf{Perceptual alterations:} Hallucinations (hearing, seeing, or feeling things that are not present) and delusions (fixed, false beliefs), characteristic of psychotic disorders.
    \item \textbf{Behavioural alterations:} Social withdrawal, avoidance of specific situations, impulsivity, self-harming behaviours, or changes in eating and sleeping patterns.
    \item \textbf{Somatic symptoms:} Physical complaints such as chronic fatigue, unexplained aches and pains, digestive issues, or palpitations, often associated with severe anxiety or depression.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
It is essential to differentiate mental illness from physical health conditions and substance-induced states that can mimic psychiatric symptoms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Endocrine disorders:} Thyroid diseases (e.g. hypothyroidism mimicking major depression, or hyperthyroidism mimicking generalised anxiety disorder) and adrenal disorders (e.g. Cushing's disease).
    \item \textbf{Neurological conditions:} Early-stage dementia, delirium, Parkinson's disease, or temporal lobe epilepsy presenting with cognitive deficits, mood shifts, or hallucinations.
    \item \textbf{Substance-induced disorders:} Psychiatric symptoms caused by acute intoxication or withdrawal from alcohol, cannabis, stimulants (such as amphetamines), or prescription medications.
    \item \textbf{Nutritional deficiencies:} Vitamin B12 or folate deficiency and severe anaemia presenting with chronic fatigue, low mood, and cognitive decline.
    \item \textbf{Systemic infectious diseases:} Neurosyphilis, Lyme disease, or chronic viral infections presenting with neuropsychiatric symptoms.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Individuals experiencing persistent changes in mood, thinking, or behaviour that cause distress or interfere with daily life should consult a general practitioner or mental health professional. Immediate emergency services or crisis helplines must be contacted if there is an imminent risk of self-harm or harm to others.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Suicidal crisis:} Imminent risk of self-harm or suicide, or if the individual has taken steps to end their life.
    \item \textbf{Acute psychosis:} Severe hallucinations or delusions that cause the individual to be unable to care for themselves or place them in danger.
    \item \textbf{Aggressive or violent behaviour:} Loss of behavioural control due to acute psychiatric distress, posing an immediate threat to safety.
    \item \textbf{Severe drug overdose:} Intentional ingestion of toxic doses of substances or medications.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis involves a comprehensive psychiatric evaluation, physical examination, and laboratory tests to exclude organic causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychiatric interview:} Detailed assessment of symptoms, history of present illness, psychiatric history, developmental history, and family history.
    \item \textbf{Mental State Examination (MSE):} Structured clinical observation evaluating appearance, behaviour, speech, mood, affect, thought content, perception, and cognition.
    \item \textbf{Standardised rating scales:} Diagnostic questionnaires such as the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) for depression, or the GAD-7 for anxiety.
    \item \textbf{Routine laboratory screening:} Full blood count, thyroid function tests, electrolytes, renal and liver function tests, vitamin B12 levels, and urine toxicology to rule out physical causes.
    \item \textbf{Brain imaging (CT or MRI):} Indicated if there are new-onset focal neurological signs, atypical presentations, or late-onset psychiatric symptoms in older adults.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is multidisciplinary, individualised, and focused on functional recovery, integrating psychological, pharmacological, and social therapies.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychotherapy:} Evidence-based psychological treatments, such as Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT), Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT), or Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT).
    \item \textbf{Pharmacotherapy:} Use of psychotropic medications, including antidepressants (e.g. SSRIs), anxiolytics, mood stabilisers (e.g. lithium), or antipsychotics, matching the specific disorder.
    \item \textbf{Psychoeducation and self-management:} Teaching patients and families about the illness, warning signs of relapse, and healthy coping mechanisms.
    \item \textbf{Social and lifestyle support:} Promoting regular exercise, healthy sleep hygiene, vocational rehabilitation, and engagement with support groups.
    \item \textbf{Crisis intervention and hospitalisation:} Short-term inpatient psychiatric care for acute stabilisation when safety cannot be maintained in the community.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Suicide and self-harm:} The most serious complication, with mental illness being the single largest risk factor for completed suicide.
    \item \textbf{Substance abuse:} Self-medication of distress leading to secondary alcohol or drug dependence (dual diagnosis).
    \item \textbf{Physical health decline:} Increased risk of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and obesity due to poor self-care, smoking, and side effects of psychiatric medications.
    \item \textbf{Social and occupational dysfunction:} Relationship breakdown, loss of employment, financial instability, and homelessness.
    \item \textbf{Stigma and discrimination:} Social isolation and delay in seeking treatment due to societal misconceptions about mental illness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals with mental illness is variable but generally optimistic with early intervention and comprehensive treatment. Many people experience significant symptom reduction and maintain functional recovery, leading fulfilling lives in the community. Some disorders, such as acute depressive episodes, may resolve completely, while others (like schizophrenia or bipolar disorder) are chronic conditions requiring long-term, ongoing management. Prognosis is enhanced by early access to mental health services, a strong social support network, adherence to treatment plans, and active engagement in self-care. Conversely, untreated mental illness is associated with chronic disability, poorer physical health, and higher mortality rates.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cultivate strong social networks:} Building and maintaining supportive relationships with family, friends, and community groups.
    \item \textbf{Engage in regular physical activity:} Exercising regularly to boost mood and reduce stress hormones.
    \item \textbf{Practice mindfulness and relaxation:} Utilising techniques like meditation, yoga, or deep-breathing exercises to manage stress.
    \item \textbf{Establish healthy sleep habits:} Prioritising 7--9 hours of quality sleep per night and maintaining a consistent sleep schedule.
    \item \textbf{Limit alcohol and drug use:} Avoiding substances that can disrupt neurochemistry and exacerbate mental health symptoms.
\end{itemize}
