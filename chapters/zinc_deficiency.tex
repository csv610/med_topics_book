\chapter{Zinc deficiency}
\index{Zinc deficiency}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Zinc is an essential trace element that plays a fundamental role in human biology, second only to iron in its abundance and physiological significance among the transition metals. Unlike other essential minerals, such as iron, which is stored in ferritin, or calcium, which is stored in the bone matrix, the human body does not possess a specialized storage system for zinc. Consequently, a continuous, daily dietary supply of zinc is required to maintain cellular homeostasis. Zinc deficiency is defined as a physiological state in which the dietary intake or systemic bioavailability of zinc is insufficient to meet the body's metabolic demands, leading to impaired cellular function, growth retardation, and immune dysfunction.

At the molecular level, zinc is a versatile micronutrient that serves catalytic, structural, and regulatory functions. It is a cofactor for more than 300 enzymes, stabilizes the tertiary structure of thousands of proteins (such as zinc finger transcription factors), and acts as an intracellular signaling molecule. Therefore, any disruption in zinc homeostasis has widespread systemic consequences, affecting the integumentary, gastrointestinal, immune, reproductive, and central nervous systems.

The recognition of zinc as an essential nutrient for humans is relatively recent, dating back to the early 1960s. Studies conducted by Prasad and colleagues in Egypt and Iran identified zinc deficiency as the underlying cause of a syndrome characterized by dwarfism, hypogonadism, hepatosplenomegaly, and severe anemia in young men. Since then, zinc deficiency has been recognized as a major global public health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where access to diverse, nutrient-rich diets is limited. It is a significant driver of childhood morbidity and mortality, primarily due to its role in compromising the immune response and increasing susceptibility to infectious diseases.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The global epidemiology of zinc deficiency is closely tied to socioeconomic factors, dietary patterns, and soil quality. It is estimated that approximately 17\%--20\% of the global population is at risk of inadequate zinc intake. However, this burden is not distributed evenly. In developing regions, including Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and parts of Central and South America, the prevalence of zinc deficiency can exceed 30\%--50\%. This is largely due to diets that rely heavily on staple crops (like rice, wheat, and maize) which are low in zinc and high in phytates, compounds that inhibit zinc absorption.

In low-income nations, zinc deficiency is a leading risk factor for childhood mortality, contributing to an estimated 450,000 deaths annually among children under five years of age. It is directly linked to increased susceptibility to, and severity of, diarrhea, pneumonia, and malaria. The demographic groups at the highest risk include infants, young children, pregnant and lactating women, and the elderly. Infants and children have high growth velocities, which translate into elevated zinc requirements per unit of body weight. Pregnant women require additional zinc to support fetal organogenesis and skeletal development, while lactating women lose significant amounts of zinc through breast milk.

In high-income countries, severe zinc deficiency is rare, occurring in less than 1\% of the population. However, mild to moderate zinc deficiency is increasingly recognized in specific clinical subpopulations. These include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Patients with malabsorption syndromes (such as celiac disease, Crohn's disease, and ulcerative colitis).
    \item Individuals with chronic alcohol use disorder, which impairs zinc absorption and increases urinary excretion.
    \item The elderly, among whom poor dentition, social isolation, and chronic illnesses often lead to inadequate dietary intake.
    \item Patients receiving long-term total parenteral nutrition (TPN) without adequate trace element supplementation.
    \item Strict vegans and vegetarians, whose diets may contain adequate total zinc but have low bioavailability due to the absence of animal proteins and high intake of phytates.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The aetiology of zinc deficiency can be broadly categorized into inadequate dietary intake, impaired absorption, increased physiological demands, excessive losses, and impaired metabolic utilization.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inadequate Dietary Intake}: This is the most common cause globally. Animal products, including red meat, poultry, and shellfish (particularly oysters), are the richest and most bioavailable sources of zinc. Plant-based diets, while containing zinc in whole grains, legumes, and nuts, also contain high levels of phytic acid (myo-inositol 1,2,3,4,5,6-hexakisphosphate). Phytic acid binds to zinc in the acidic environment of the stomach, forming insoluble, non-absorbable complexes in the small intestine.
    \item \textbf{Impaired Gastrointestinal Absorption}: Conditions that damage the mucosal lining of the duodenum and jejunum—the primary sites of zinc absorption—severely impair zinc uptake. These include celiac disease, Crohn's disease, tropical sprue, short bowel syndrome, and surgical resections. Bariatric surgeries, such as Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, also significantly reduce the absorptive surface area and alter luminal pH, leading to deficiency. Additionally, a rare autosomal recessive genetic disorder known as \textbf{Acrodermatitis Enteropathica} is caused by mutations in the \textit{SLC39A4} gene, which encodes the ZIP4 transporter protein, resulting in a near-total failure of intestinal zinc absorption.
    \item \textbf{Increased Physiological Demands}: Periods of rapid tissue growth significantly increase the requirement for zinc. This is observed in infants, young children, and adolescents during pubertal growth spurts. Pregnant women require additional zinc for fetal development, and lactating women must secrete zinc into breast milk (averaging 1--2~mg/day), which can deplete maternal stores if not matched by increased intake.
    \item \textbf{Excessive Pathological Losses}: Chronic diarrhea causes significant loss of both dietary and endogenous zinc (which is secreted in pancreatic and intestinal juices), creating a compounding cycle of depletion. Severe cutaneous burns lead to large losses of zinc through wound exudates. Chronic kidney disease, poorly controlled diabetes mellitus (due to glucose-induced osmotic diuresis), and sickle cell disease are associated with hyperzincuria (excessive urinary zinc excretion). Prolonged use of chelating agents, such as penicillamine, also increases renal zinc clearance.
    \item \textbf{Impaired Metabolic Utilization}: In patients with liver cirrhosis, zinc metabolism is altered, leading to decreased levels of albumin, which is the primary carrier protein for zinc in the circulation (carrying approximately 60\% of plasma zinc). Systemic inflammatory states and infections induce the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as interleukin-6), which stimulate the synthesis of metallothionein in the liver. This causes a rapid redistribution of zinc from the plasma into liver tissues, resulting in transient hypozincemia.
\end{itemize}

The pathophysiology of zinc deficiency is complex and systemic, stemming from the element's diverse biochemical roles. Zinc is a catalytic cofactor for enzymes representing all six major classes, including carbonic anhydrase (essential for carbon dioxide transport and acid-base regulation), alkaline phosphatase (involved in bone mineralization), and RNA and DNA polymerases (essential for nucleic acid synthesis and cell division). Consequently, zinc depletion halts cell division and protein synthesis, explaining the characteristic growth stunting and delayed wound healing observed in deficient individuals.

Furthermore, zinc is critical for the structural integrity of zinc finger proteins, which represent the largest family of eukaryotic transcription factors. These proteins bind to specific DNA sequences to regulate gene expression. In the absence of zinc, transcription of genes regulating cellular differentiation, growth, and apoptosis is impaired.

The immune system is particularly sensitive to zinc status. Zinc is required for the development and function of T-lymphocytes, neutrophils, macrophages, and natural killer cells. Zinc deficiency leads to thymic atrophy, resulting in decreased helper T-cell (Th1) activity, reduced production of interleukin-2 and interferon-gamma, and a shift toward a helper T-cell type 2 (Th2) response. This compromises cell-mediated immunity, leaving the host highly vulnerable to intracellular pathogens. Additionally, zinc acts as an antioxidant by stabilizing cell membranes against lipid peroxidation and serving as a cofactor for copper-zinc superoxide dismutase, an enzyme that neutralizes reactive oxygen species.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of zinc deficiency spans a spectrum from subtle, subclinical manifestations to severe, life-threatening symptoms. The severity of the clinical features depends on the degree and duration of the deficiency.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dermatological Manifestations}: Skin lesions are a classic and highly visible feature of zinc deficiency. In severe cases, patients develop a distinctive dermatitis characterized by symmetrical, erythematous, scaly, and vesiculobullous plaques. These lesions are primarily located in periorificial areas (around the mouth, nose, eyes, and perineum) and acral areas (hands, feet, elbows, and knees). The skin is prone to secondary bacterial or fungal infections. Alopecia (hair loss) is common, ranging from thinning hair to the complete loss of scalp, eyebrow, and body hair. Nail changes include the development of Beau's lines (transverse depressions) and leukonychia (white spots).
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal Dysfunction}: Chronic, watery diarrhea is a major clinical feature, particularly in infants and young children. Zinc deficiency impairs the regeneration of the intestinal mucosa, leading to villous atrophy, decreased brush-border enzyme activity, and increased mucosal permeability. This malabsorptive state exacerbates the deficiency by preventing the absorption of other nutrients and increasing endogenous zinc loss.
    \item \textbf{Growth Retardation and Sexual Maturity}: In children and adolescents, chronic zinc deficiency results in growth failure and stunting. Skeletal growth is halted due to impaired protein synthesis and decreased activity of insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1). Sexual development is significantly delayed. In males, hypogonadism presents as testicular atrophy, oligospermia, decreased spermatogenesis, and delayed development of secondary sexual characteristics, primarily because zinc is essential for testosterone synthesis and androgen receptor function.
    \item \textbf{Sensory and Neurological Impairments}: Dysgeusia (distortion of the sense of taste) and hyposmia (decreased sense of smell) are early signs of zinc depletion. This is because gustin, a major protein in saliva that promotes the growth and maintenance of taste buds, is a zinc-dependent metalloprotein. Neuropsychiatric symptoms include cognitive impairment, poor concentration, learning difficulties, emotional lability, irritability, and depression. In severe cases, night blindness (nyctalopia) can develop because zinc is a required cofactor for retinol dehydrogenase, the enzyme that converts retinol (Vitamin A) to retinal in the retina.
    \item \textbf{Impaired Wound Healing and Host Defense}: Minor cuts, abrasions, and surgical wounds take significantly longer to heal due to impaired fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis. Patients experience recurrent, prolonged infections of the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts, reflecting a state of general immunodeficiency.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Due to the diverse and multi-systemic manifestations of zinc deficiency, several other nutritional, infectious, and inflammatory conditions must be considered in the differential diagnosis.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Essential Fatty Acid Deficiency}: This condition presents with periorificial and acral dermatitis and alopecia that closely resemble the cutaneous signs of zinc deficiency. It can be differentiated by obtaining a detailed nutritional history (such as long-term fat-free parenteral nutrition) and measuring plasma fatty acid profiles, specifically looking for an elevated triene-to-tetraene ratio.
    \item \textbf{Cutaneous Candidiasis}: Perineal and periorificial skin lesions in zinc deficiency can be mistaken for candidiasis. While secondary candidal infection of zinc deficiency plaques is common, primary candidiasis can be distinguished by the presence of satellite pustules, intense pruritus, and the identification of budding yeast cells and pseudohyphae on potassium hydroxide (KOH) wet mounts or fungal cultures.
    \item \textbf{Atopic Dermatitis}: A common chronic inflammatory skin condition that presents with pruritic, erythematous plaques. Unlike zinc deficiency, atopic dermatitis typically spares the periorificial regions, is associated with a personal or family history of atopy (such as asthma or allergic rhinitis), and is often characterized by elevated serum immunoglobulin E (IgE) levels and eosinophilia.
    \item \textbf{Necrolytic Migratory Erythema (NME)}: This is a rare cutaneous manifestation of a glucagon-secreting pancreatic tumor (glucagonoma). The skin lesions of NME are histologically and clinically similar to those of severe zinc deficiency, featuring erythematous, blistering, and crusted plaques in periorificial and intertriginous areas. It is differentiated by marked elevations in plasma glucagon levels, the presence of a pancreatic mass on abdominal imaging, weight loss, and diabetes mellitus.
    \item \textbf{Pellagra (Niacin Deficiency)}: Pellagra is characterized by the classic triad of dermatitis, diarrhea, and dementia. The dermatitis of pellagra is typically photosensitive and confined to sun-exposed areas of the skin, such as the face, neck (Casal's necklace), and dorsum of the hands. In contrast, zinc deficiency lesions are acral and periorificial, without photosensitivity.
    \item \textbf{Riboflavin (Vitamin B2) and Pyridoxine (Vitamin B6) Deficiencies}: These deficiencies cause angular cheilitis, glossitis, and seborrheic dermatitis. They are differentiated from zinc deficiency by the absence of severe watery diarrhea, alopecia, and hypogonadism, and can be confirmed by specific enzyme activity assays (e.g., erythrocyte glutathione reductase activity for riboflavin status).
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
While mild zinc deficiency can be managed through dietary changes and routine medical consultation, moderate to severe deficiency requires prompt professional evaluation to prevent systemic complications.

Medical care should be sought if a patient experiences:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic, unexplained skin rashes that do not respond to standard topical treatments, particularly if located around the mouth, eyes, or groin.
    \item Persistent hair loss or thinning that cannot be attributed to male-pattern baldness or other known factors.
    \item A noticeable and prolonged loss or distortion of the sense of taste or smell.
    \item Delayed growth or lack of sexual development in children and adolescents.
    \item Non-healing or slow-healing wounds and ulcers.
\end{itemize}

Prompt medical attention is necessary if an individual (especially an infant or young child) develops persistent watery diarrhea that leads to signs of dehydration, such as dry mouth, crying without tears, sunken eyes, or decreased urination.

Emergency services must be contacted immediately (e.g., calling \textbf{local emergency services} in many countries, \textbf{911} in the United States, or \textbf{999} in the United Kingdom) if the patient exhibits signs of life-threatening complications, including:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of sepsis or severe systemic infection (e.g., high fever or abnormally low body temperature, rapid heart rate, fast breathing, confusion, extreme lethargy, or pale, blotchy skin).
    \item Severe dehydration with confusion, inability to stand, or loss of consciousness.
    \item Acute respiratory distress (difficulty breathing, chest retractions, or blue-tinted lips).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosing zinc deficiency requires a high index of clinical suspicion, a thorough dietary and medical history, and a detailed physical examination, supplemented by targeted laboratory investigations.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical Assessment}: The clinician should evaluate the patient's dietary habits, looking for risk factors such as strict veganism or high intake of unrefined grains containing phytates. A history of gastrointestinal diseases, bariatric surgery, or chronic alcohol use should be noted. Physical examination should focus on identifying characteristic skin lesions, alopecia, nail changes, and assessing growth velocity and pubertal development.
    \item \textbf{Serum or Plasma Zinc Concentration}: This is the most widely used laboratory test. Blood samples must be collected under strict guidelines to avoid contamination, as zinc is present in many environmental sources and laboratory equipment. Specimens should be collected in the morning after an overnight fast, using trace-element-free tubes. The normal reference range for plasma zinc is typically 70--120~$\mu$g/dL (10.7--18.4~$\mu$mol/L). However, serum zinc has significant limitations:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item It represents less than 0.1\% of total body zinc, making it insensitive to mild or marginal tissue depletion.
            \item It undergoes diurnal variation, with levels peaking in the morning and declining throughout the day.
            \item It is an acute-phase reactant. During infection, inflammation, or physical stress, pro-inflammatory cytokines cause zinc to shift from the plasma into the liver, resulting in low plasma zinc levels that may not reflect true nutritional deficiency.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) Activity}: Alkaline phosphatase is a zinc-dependent enzyme. In states of zinc deficiency, its activity often declines. A useful diagnostic approach, especially in ambiguous cases, is to measure ALP activity before and after a brief trial of zinc supplementation. A significant rise in ALP activity (e.g., greater than 15\%--20\%) following zinc administration suggests pre-existing zinc deficiency.
    \item \textbf{Urinary Zinc Excretion}: Measured via a 24-hour urine collection. In patients with dietary zinc deficiency, the kidneys conserve zinc, resulting in low urinary zinc excretion (typically less than 150~$\mu$g/24 hours in adults). Conversely, elevated urinary zinc in the presence of low serum zinc indicates renal wasting, which can occur in diabetes mellitus, kidney disease, or during therapy with certain chelating drugs.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory Markers (CRP)}: C-reactive protein (CRP) should be measured concurrently with plasma zinc. If CRP is elevated, it indicates an active inflammatory state, meaning that a low plasma zinc level must be interpreted with caution, as it may be due to the acute-phase response rather than nutritional depletion.
    \item \textbf{Genetic Testing}: In infants presenting with classic symptoms of acrodermatitis enteropathica shortly after weaning from breast milk, genetic sequencing of the \textit{SLC39A4} gene should be performed to identify homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of zinc deficiency involves addressing the underlying cause, modifying the diet to increase zinc intake and bioavailability, and administering appropriate zinc supplements.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary Interventions}: Patients should be encouraged to consume foods high in bioavailable zinc. Animal proteins, such as beef, lamb, pork, poultry, and seafood (especially oysters), are highly recommended. For individuals on plant-based diets, good sources include pumpkin seeds, sesame seeds, cashews, almonds, lentils, and chickpeas. To improve the bioavailability of zinc from plant sources, patients should be educated on food preparation techniques that reduce phytic acid content. These include soaking grains and legumes in water before cooking, sprouting (germination), and using yeast-fermented (leavened) bread products.
    \item \textbf{Oral Zinc Supplementation}:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Mild to Moderate Deficiency}: Adults are typically treated with 20--50~mg/day of elemental zinc. Children are treated with 10--20~mg/day. Zinc is available in various salt forms, including zinc sulfate, zinc gluconate, and zinc acetate. For reference, a standard 220~mg tablet of zinc sulfate heptahydrate provides approximately 50~mg of elemental zinc.
            \item \textbf{Severe Deficiency and Acrodermatitis Enteropathica}: High-dose oral supplementation is required, typically 1--3~mg of elemental zinc per kilogram of body weight per day. Patients with the genetic disorder acrodermatitis enteropathica require lifelong zinc supplementation.
            \item \textbf{Pediatric Diarrhea}: The World Health Organization (WHO) and UNICEF recommend that children with acute diarrhea in high-risk areas receive 20~mg of elemental zinc daily for 10--14 days (10~mg/day for infants under 6 months) alongside oral rehydration salts. This short course reduces the duration and severity of the illness and prevents subsequent diarrheal episodes for up to three months.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intravenous Supplementation}: Patients who cannot tolerate oral intake, such as those on total parenteral nutrition (TPN) or individuals with severe malabsorption syndromes, require intravenous zinc. The standard daily dose in TPN is 2.5--5~mg of elemental zinc for adults, with higher doses required for patients with excessive fluid losses from enterocutaneous fistulas or severe diarrhea.
    \item \textbf{Monitoring and Copper Balance}: Zinc and copper compete for the same absorption pathways in the small intestine. Long-term supplementation with high doses of zinc (typically greater than 50~mg/day of elemental zinc for more than a few weeks) induces the intestinal synthesis of metallothionein, which binds copper and prevents its absorption. Therefore, patients on long-term zinc therapy must be monitored for secondary copper deficiency. Serum copper and ceruloplasmin levels should be checked regularly, and if copper deficiency develops, the zinc dose should be reduced or copper supplementation should be introduced (usually 1--2~mg/day, taken at a different time from the zinc supplement).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated zinc deficiency can lead to severe, long-term health complications, while improper or excessive treatment can introduce distinct clinical risks.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stunting and Cognitive Impairment}: In children, chronic zinc deficiency results in permanent failure to reach genetic growth potential. It can also cause irreversible deficits in cognitive development, learning capacity, and memory, limiting educational and economic opportunities in adulthood.
    \item \textbf{Severe Immunodeficiency}: The profound suppression of cell-mediated immunity increases the risk of severe, life-threatening opportunistic infections. In developing regions, this translates to high mortality rates from childhood pneumonia, malaria, and diarrheal diseases.
    \item \textbf{Reproductive and Obstetric Complications}: In males, untreated hypogonadism can lead to permanent infertility. In pregnant women, severe zinc deficiency is associated with spontaneous abortions, intrauterine growth restriction, low birth weight, and an increased incidence of congenital malformations, particularly neural tube defects. It also increases the risk of maternal complications such as pre-eclampsia, prolonged labor, and post-partum hemorrhage.
    \item \textbf{Induced Copper Deficiency (Treatment-Related)}: This is a major complication of chronic, high-dose zinc supplementation. The resulting copper deficiency causes microcytic anemia and neutropenia (which can lead to recurrent infections). It can also cause a progressive, irreversible demyelinating neurological condition known as zinc-induced copper deficiency myeloneuropathy, which presents with sensory ataxia, spastic gait, and peripheral neuropathy.
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal Toxicity}: Acute high doses of oral zinc supplements often cause gastrointestinal irritation. Symptoms include nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps, and diarrhea. Taking supplements with food can reduce these symptoms, though it may slightly decrease zinc absorption.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals with zinc deficiency is generally excellent, provided the condition is diagnosed early and treated appropriately.

For patients with nutritional zinc deficiency, clinical improvement is often rapid once supplementation is initiated. Diarrhea and neuropsychiatric symptoms (such as irritability) typically begin to resolve within 24--72 hours. Cutaneous lesions show signs of healing within 2--5 days, and a complete resolution of dermatitis and hair regrowth is usually achieved within a few weeks. The senses of taste and smell generally return to normal within one to two months.

Children who experience growth stunting due to zinc deficiency often demonstrate rapid "catch-up" growth after their zinc status is normalized. However, if the deficiency was severe and prolonged during critical developmental windows, some deficits in stature and cognitive development may not be fully reversible.

For patients with hereditary acrodermatitis enteropathica, the prognosis was historically poor, with most infants dying from severe malnutrition and opportunistic infections. Today, with lifelong oral zinc supplementation, these patients have a normal life expectancy, complete resolution of symptoms, and a normal quality of life.

In cases where zinc deficiency is secondary to an underlying medical condition (such as celiac disease, Crohn's disease, or chronic kidney disease), the long-term prognosis depends on the successful management of the primary disorder. Supplementation will correct the deficiency, but ongoing monitoring is required to prevent recurrence.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventing zinc deficiency relies on maintaining a balanced diet, utilizing proper food preparation techniques, and targeting supplementation to high-risk groups.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary Diversification}: The most sustainable way to prevent zinc deficiency is to consume a diet that includes a variety of nutrient-rich foods. Incorporating animal-based proteins (such as red meat, poultry, fish, and dairy) ensures adequate intake of highly bioavailable zinc. For vegetarians and vegans, incorporating zinc-rich plant foods like pumpkin seeds, hemp seeds, lentils, beans, cashews, and fortified cereals is essential.
    \item \textbf{Enhancing Zinc Bioavailability}: Because phytates in plant foods inhibit zinc absorption, specific food preparation methods should be used to reduce phytate levels. Soaking dried beans, grains, and seeds in water for several hours before cooking helps activate endogenous phytases, which break down phytic acid. Allowing seeds and grains to sprout (germination) or ferment (such as using sourdough yeast fermentation for bread) also significantly increases zinc bioavailability.
    \item \textbf{Considerations for Vegetarians and Vegans}: Vegans and vegetarians should aim for a daily zinc intake that is approximately 50\% higher than the Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) for omnivores (the RDA for adult men is 11~mg/day and for adult women is 8~mg/day). This compensates for the lower absorption rate of zinc from plant-based diets.
    \item \textbf{Targeted Preventive Supplementation}: In areas with a high prevalence of zinc deficiency, public health programs that provide low-dose zinc supplements to pregnant women, infants, and young children have been successful in reducing the incidence of diarrhea, respiratory infections, and growth stunting.
    \item \textbf{Avoiding Excessive Intake}: Individuals using over-the-counter zinc supplements (such as zinc lozenges for the common cold) should be careful not to exceed the Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) of 40~mg/day of elemental zinc for adults. Self-supplementation at high doses should be avoided unless recommended and monitored by a healthcare professional.
\end{itemize}
