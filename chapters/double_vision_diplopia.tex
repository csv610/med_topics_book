\chapter{Double Vision (Diplopia)}
\index{Double Vision (Diplopia)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Double vision, also called diplopia, is seeing two images of a single object. It can affect one eye (monocular diplopia) or both eyes (binocular diplopia), and distinguishing between the two is the first step in finding the cause. Monocular double vision usually results from problems in the eye itself, such as cataract or dry eyes, while binocular double vision, which disappears when one eye is closed, is usually caused by misalignment of the eyes from problems with the muscles, nerves, or brain. Binocular double vision can indicate serious conditions such as stroke or neurological disease and requires urgent assessment. This chapter explains the causes and management of double vision.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Double vision is a common symptom that prompts visits to GPs, optometrists, ophthalmologists, and emergency departments. It is more common in older people, in whom stroke, diabetes, and nerve problems are more frequent causes. Most causes of double vision are benign and treatable, but a minority indicate serious neurological disease. Sudden-onset double vision in an older person or someone with risk factors warrants urgent assessment.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Monocular diplopia:} double vision in one eye, from cataract, dry eyes, astigmatism, or a problem with the cornea or lens
    \item \textbf{Binocular diplopia:} the two eyes are misaligned, so each sends a different image to the brain
    \item \textbf{Muscle problems:} weakness or restriction of the eye muscles
    \item \textbf{Nerve problems:} damage to the cranial nerves that control eye movement, from diabetes, head injury, or aneurysm
    \item \textbf{Neurological disease:} stroke, multiple sclerosis, and tumours affecting the eye movement pathways
    \item \textbf{Systemic disease:} thyroid eye disease, myasthenia gravis, and other conditions
    \item \textbf{Convergence insufficiency:} the eyes struggle to work together for close work
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Two overlapping or separate images of one object
    \item Monocular: still double with one eye closed; binocular: double only with both eyes open
    \item Double vision that is worse in certain directions of gaze
    \item Head tilt or turning to compensate for the double vision
    \item Squinting, eye pain, bulging eyes, or drooping eyelids
    \item Associated symptoms: headache, weakness, numbness, or difficulty speaking in serious causes
    \item Onset can be sudden (stroke, nerve palsy) or gradual (thyroid disease, cataract)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Monocular versus binocular diplopia, which have different causes
    \item Cranial nerve palsies (third, fourth, and sixth nerve) from different causes
    \item Stroke and transient ischaemic attack
    \item Thyroid eye disease and myasthenia gravis
    \item Multiple sclerosis and other neurological conditions
    \item Cataract and other eye conditions
    \item Convergence insufficiency in children and young adults
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with new double vision should see a doctor promptly, especially if it is sudden, persistent, or associated with other symptoms. An optometrist or ophthalmologist can assess the eyes, and a doctor can investigate neurological causes.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden double vision with weakness, facial droop, or difficulty speaking (possible stroke)
    \item Double vision after a head injury
    \item Double vision with severe headache, vomiting, or a drooping eyelid (possible aneurysm)
    \item Double vision with difficulty breathing or swallowing
    \item Sudden vision change with eye pain and redness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} which eye, when it started, and what makes it worse
    \item \textbf{Eye examination:} vision, eye movements, and alignment testing
    \item \textbf{Cover test:} distinguishes monocular from binocular diplopia
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} glucose, thyroid function, and tests for myasthenia
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT or MRI of the brain and orbits for neurological causes
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} to an ophthalmologist or neurologist
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Treating the cause:} cataract surgery, dry eye treatment, glasses, or treatment of the underlying condition
    \item \textbf{Prisms:} prisms in glasses can correct some double vision
    \item \textbf{Patching:} covering one eye relieves double vision while the cause is treated
    \item \textbf{Orthoptic exercises:} for convergence insufficiency and muscle balance problems
    \item \textbf{Urgent treatment:} for stroke, myasthenia, or other serious causes
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} for persistent misalignment when other treatments are insufficient
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} monitoring of recovery and function
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty driving, reading, and walking
    \item Falls and accidents from impaired vision
    \item Head tilt causing neck strain
    \item Missed serious conditions such as stroke or aneurysm
    \item Psychological distress from persistent double vision
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the cause. Many causes of double vision resolve spontaneously or with treatment: nerve palsies from diabetes often recover over months, convergence insufficiency responds well to exercises, and cataract surgery cures monocular double vision. Serious causes such as stroke require urgent treatment, and double vision improves as the underlying condition is managed. With accurate diagnosis, most people achieve good control of their double vision.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item See a doctor promptly for new or sudden double vision
    \item Cover one eye temporarily to relieve symptoms while seeking care
    \item Manage diabetes and blood pressure to reduce nerve and stroke risk
    \item Wear prescribed glasses and use prisms as advised
    \item Do orthoptic exercises as directed
    \item Avoid driving until double vision is resolved
    \item Attend follow-up appointments
\end{itemize}
