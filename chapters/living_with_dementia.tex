\chapter{Living with dementia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Living with dementia encompasses the ongoing experience, adaptation, and care required by individuals diagnosed with progressive cognitive decline, as well as the support systems needed by their families and carers. Dementia is not a single disease but an umbrella term for a collection of symptoms caused by various underlying neurodegenerative disorders, most commonly Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia, dementia with Lewy bodies, and frontotemporal dementia. It is characterised by a progressive decline in memory, language, executive function, and reasoning, severe enough to interfere with daily activities. Managing the condition involves moving from a curative focus to a supportive, multidisciplinary approach aimed at maximizing independence, safety, and quality of life while preserving the individual's dignity.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Dementia is a major public health challenge globally and in Australia. It is estimated that more than 400,000 Australians are currently living with dementia, a figure projected to double by 2050 due to an ageing population. Dementia is the second leading cause of death for all Australians and the leading cause of death among Australian women. It predominantly affects individuals over the age of 65, with the prevalence rising exponentially from approximately 1 in 10 people in this age bracket to 3 in 10 for those over 85. However, younger-onset dementia (affecting individuals under the age of 65) is also recognized, presenting unique social and economic challenges. The burden of care is largely borne by family members, with hundreds of thousands of informal carers providing complex, long-term support.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The underlying pathobiology of living with dementia depends on the specific neurodegenerative or vascular disease process driving the cognitive decline.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurodegenerative proteinopathies:} Progressive loss of neurons and synapses associated with abnormal protein aggregation, such as extracellular amyloid-beta plaques and intracellular hyperphosphorylated tau tangles in Alzheimer's disease.
    \item \textbf{Cerebrovascular damage:} Multiple small infarcts, microbleeds, or large strokes that disrupt white matter tracts and cortical connections, resulting in vascular dementia.
    \item \textbf{Lewy body pathology:} Intraneuronal accumulation of alpha-synuclein protein aggregates (Lewy bodies) in the cerebral cortex and brainstem, causing dementia with Lewy bodies and Parkinson's disease dementia.
    \item \textbf{Frontotemporal lobar degeneration:} Selective degeneration of the frontal and temporal lobes associated with tau or TDP-43 protein accumulation, causing frontotemporal dementia.
    \item \textbf{Synaptic dysfunction and atrophy:} Chronic progressive damage leads to widespread cerebral atrophy, neurotransmitter deficits (particularly acetylcholine), and loss of functional brain networks, manifesting as progressive cognitive and functional impairment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features evolve as dementia progresses through mild, moderate, and severe stages, affecting multiple domains of functioning.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Memory impairment, particularly short-term memory loss, leading to repeating questions, misplacing items, and difficulty learning new information.
    \item Executive dysfunction, including difficulty with planning, organising, problem-solving, and managing complex tasks such as financial affairs.
    \item Language and communication difficulties (aphasia), characterised by word-finding difficulties, repeating phrases, or struggling to follow conversations.
    \item Disorientation to time and place, which can lead to wandering or getting lost in familiar surroundings.
    \item Behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD), such as depression, anxiety, agitation, apathy, delusions, hallucinations, and sleep-wake cycle disturbances.
    \item Apraxia and agnosia, resulting in the inability to perform purposeful physical tasks (like dressing) or recognize common objects and faces despite intact sensory function.
    \item Loss of functional independence, progressing from difficulty with complex chores (instrumental activities of daily living) to requiring total assistance with eating, bathing, and mobility.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When assessing an individual living with cognitive changes, it is essential to exclude other conditions that can mimic or worsen the clinical presentation of dementia.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Delirium:} An acute, fluctuating state of confusion and inattention, usually triggered by an underlying medical illness, urinary tract infection (UTI), dehydration, or medication toxicity. It is potentially reversible and represents a medical emergency.
    \item \textbf{Depression (pseudodementia):} Depression in older adults can present with cognitive complaints, psychomotor slowing, apathy, and poor performance on memory tests, which may resolve with appropriate antidepressant therapy or psychological interventions.
    \item \textbf{Normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH):} A treatable neurological condition characterised by the triad of progressive cognitive decline, a magnetic (wide-based, shuffling) gait, and urinary incontinence.
    \item \textbf{Metabolic and endocrine disorders:} Severe vitamin B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism, hyponatraemia, hypercalcaemia, or chronic renal/hepatic impairment, which must be identified and corrected via blood screening.
    \item \textbf{Sensory deficits:} Undetected or unmanaged severe hearing loss or visual impairment can exacerbate confusion, social withdrawal, and poor performance on cognitive screens.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Carers and individuals should consult a general practitioner if they notice new, progressive memory issues or changes in personality.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, acute changes in mental state, alertness, or confusion, which may indicate delirium or an acute medical illness.
    \item Signs of a stroke: sudden weakness or numbness on one side of the body, facial drooping, difficulty speaking, or sudden loss of balance.
    \item Severe, unmanageable agitation, aggression, or psychotic symptoms that pose an immediate risk of harm to the individual or others.
    \item Life-threatening injury resulting from a fall or accident.
    \item Loss of consciousness, a seizure, or an accidental medication overdose.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A comprehensive assessment is required to diagnose dementia, determine its subtype, and formulate a care plan.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cognitive and neuropsychological testing:} Standardised tests such as the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), or detailed neuropsychological profiling to map cognitive deficits.
    \item \textbf{Neuroimaging:} A brain CT or MRI to detect structural changes, vascular lesions, patterns of localized lobar atrophy, or to rule out subdural haematomas and brain tumours.
    \item \textbf{Screening blood tests:} Full blood count, electrolytes, thyroid function, vitamin B12, folate, and calcium levels to exclude metabolic causes of cognitive decline.
    \item \textbf{Functional and occupational therapy assessment:} Evaluation of the home environment and the individual's ability to safely carry out activities of daily living (ADLs).
    \item \textbf{Collateral history:} Detailed interviews with family members or close contacts to trace the onset, progression, and impact of cognitive and behavioural changes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is multidimensional and changes over time, prioritizing non-pharmacological interventions, environmental modifications, and support for carers.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological management:} Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine) for mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's disease or Lewy body dementia, and NMDA receptor antagonists (memantine) for moderate-to-severe stages to support cognitive function.
    \item \textbf{Environmental modification:} Designing a safe, navigation-friendly living space by improving lighting, installing grab rails, removing trip hazards (like loose rugs), and using clear signage or memory prompts.
    \item \textbf{Management of BPSD:} Implementing non-pharmacological strategies (such as music therapy, sensory stimulation, structured daily routines, and gentle physical exercise) as first-line measures for agitation and sleep issues.
    \item \textbf{Support and training for carers:} Providing education, access to support groups, and respite care to help family carers manage stress, prevent burnout, and learn effective communication techniques.
    \item \textbf{Advance care planning:} Assisting the individual to document their future healthcare preferences (Enduring Power of Attorney, Guardianship, and Advance Care Directives) early in the course of the disease while they retain decision-making capacity.
    \item \textbf{Allied health involvement:} Engaging occupational therapists, speech pathologists (for swallowing or communication difficulties), physiotherapists (for balance and mobility), and social workers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Recurrent falls, fractures, and head injuries due to impaired balance, spatial disorientation, and visual-spatial agnosia.
    \item Malnutrition, weight loss, and dehydration from forgetting to eat or drink, loss of appetite, or difficulty swallowing (dysphagia) in advanced stages.
    \item Aspiration pneumonia, which is a leading cause of death in late-stage dementia due to impaired airway protective reflexes.
    \item Carer burnout, characterized by high rates of clinical depression, anxiety, physical exhaustion, and financial strain among family carers.
    \item Vulnerability to elder abuse, financial exploitation, and medication non-compliance due to impaired judgement and memory loss.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Dementia is a progressive, life-limiting condition for which there is currently no cure. The clinical course and rate of decline vary widely, with survival times post-diagnosis ranging from three to twelve years or longer, depending on the age of onset, underlying cause, and co-existing medical conditions. Over time, the individual will experience a gradual loss of all cognitive and physical capacities, transitioning from mild memory impairment to requiring total, 24-hour nursing care. Early diagnosis, active management of symptoms, and robust social support are crucial in optimizing quality of life and prolonging functional independence.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Establish a consistent, predictable daily routine to minimize confusion, anxiety, and the risk of sundowning.
    \item Use simple memory aids, such as large-print calendars, daily planners, whiteboards, and pre-packaged medication organisers (Webster-paks).
    \item Adapt the home to reduce safety risks: store hazardous chemicals securely, install auto-shutoff devices on stoves, and ensure paths are clear and well-lit.
    \item Engage in regular, gentle physical activity, such as walking, to maintain muscle strength, mobility, and cardiovascular health.
    \item Stay socially connected and mentally active through participation in community groups, reading, or structured cognitive stimulation.
    \item Access support organisations, counselling, and dementia helplines early to prepare for future changes.
\end{itemize}
