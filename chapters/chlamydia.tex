\section*{Definition and Overview}
Chlamydia is a sexually transmitted infection (STI) caused by the bacterium \textit{Chlamydia trachomatis}. It is one of the most common bacterial STIs worldwide and can infect the cervix, urethra, rectum, throat, and eyes. A large proportion of infections cause no symptoms at all, which means the infection can be passed on unknowingly and go undetected for long periods. Untreated chlamydia can spread through the reproductive tract and cause serious long-term complications, particularly in women. With testing and prompt antibiotic treatment, chlamydia is easily cured.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Chlamydia is the most commonly reported bacterial STI in many high-income countries, including Australia. It is most often diagnosed in sexually active young people, with the highest rates in men and women under the age of 25. Rates are generally higher in women than men, in part because women are screened more often and are more likely to be tested during routine gynaecological care. Factors associated with higher risk include having a new or multiple sexual partners, inconsistent condom use, and previous STIs. Because most infections are asymptomatic, the reported number of cases is likely an underestimate of the true burden.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Chlamydia is caused by the bacterium \textit{Chlamydia trachomatis}, which is transmitted during vaginal, anal, or oral sex with an infected partner, and can also pass from a mother to her baby during childbirth. The bacterium is an obligate intracellular organism, meaning it can only grow inside human cells.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infection:} the bacteria invade the cells lining the genital, urinary, and sometimes rectal or throat tissues
    \item \textbf{Inflammation:} the body's immune response causes inflammation, swelling, and discharge at the site of infection
    \item \textbf{Tissue damage:} chronic or repeated infection can scar the fallopian tubes and other reproductive structures
    \item \textbf{Re-infection:} having chlamydia does not provide lasting immunity, so people can be infected again after treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Many people with chlamydia have no symptoms at all -- it is estimated that up to three-quarters of women and half of men with the infection do not notice anything wrong.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{In women:} unusual vaginal discharge, bleeding between periods or after sex, pain with urination, lower abdominal or pelvic pain
    \item \textbf{In men:} discharge from the penis, pain or burning when urinating, pain or swelling in the testicles
    \item \textbf{Rectal infection:} discharge, pain, bleeding, or discomfort from the rectum
    \item \textbf{Throat infection:} usually no symptoms, occasionally a sore throat
    \item \textbf{Eye infection:} redness, discharge, and irritation of the eye (conjunctivitis)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of chlamydia overlap with several other conditions, so testing is needed to tell them apart.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Gonorrhoea -- another common bacterial STI with very similar symptoms
    \item Trichomoniasis -- a parasitic STI causing discharge and itching
    \item Mycoplasma genitalium and ureaplasma infections
    \item Urinary tract infection or cystitis
    \item Pelvic inflammatory disease from other causes
    \item Non-specific vaginitis or bacterial vaginosis
    \item Genital herpes (when ulcers are present)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who is sexually active should consider STI testing, especially after unprotected sex or a change of partner. See a doctor or sexual health clinic promptly if you have any discharge, pain with urination, or unusual pelvic or testicular pain.

\textbf{Call 000 or attend an emergency department for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal or pelvic pain, especially with fever
    \item Sudden severe pain on one side of the lower abdomen
    \item Fever, chills, or vomiting in a pregnant woman with suspected infection
    \item Signs of a severe pelvic infection (fever with pelvic tenderness)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Chlamydia is diagnosed with a simple swab or urine test, and testing is quick, painless, and usually free through sexual health services.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT):} the standard test, using a urine sample, or a vaginal, cervical, rectal, or throat swab
    \item \textbf{History:} details of symptoms and sexual history help guide which sites to test
    \item \textbf{Co-testing:} testing for gonorrhoea at the same time, because the two infections often occur together
    \item \textbf{Other STI screening:} HIV, syphilis, and hepatitis B and C may be offered as part of the same visit
    \item \textbf{Follow-up testing:} a repeat test about 3 months after treatment to check for re-infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Chlamydia is treated with a short course of antibiotics, usually a single dose or a 7-day course, prescribed by a doctor or sexual health service.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} doxycycline or azithromycin are the usual choices; it is essential to finish the whole course
    \item \textbf{Abstinence:} avoid sex for 7 days after treatment (and until partners have been treated) to avoid re-infection
    \item \textbf{Partner notification:} current and recent sexual partners should be tested and treated to stop the infection spreading
    \item \textbf{Treatment of partners:} in some settings "patient-delivered partner therapy" is arranged by the clinician
    \item \textbf{Retesting:} a test 3 months later is recommended because re-infection is common
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} safe antibiotic options exist for pregnant women; treatment is important to protect both mother and baby
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
If chlamydia is left untreated, the infection can spread and cause lasting damage.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID):} infection spreading to the uterus and fallopian tubes
    \item \textbf{Infertility:} scarring of the fallopian tubes can make it difficult or impossible to conceive
    \item \textbf{Ectopic pregnancy:} a pregnancy that implants in the fallopian tube, which is a medical emergency
    \item \textbf{Chronic pelvic pain}
    \item \textbf{Epididymitis:} painful inflammation of the tube that carries sperm in men
    \item \textbf{Reactive arthritis:} joint pain and swelling that can follow infection
    \item \textbf{Infection in a newborn:} conjunctivitis or pneumonia in babies born to infected mothers
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt treatment, chlamydia is completely curable and most people recover fully without lasting problems. Untreated infection may persist for months and can cause permanent damage to the reproductive organs, so the outlook depends on early diagnosis. Because many infections cause no symptoms, routine testing after any episode of unprotected sex is the best way to protect long-term fertility. People who have had chlamydia once are at risk of catching it again, so repeat testing and consistent safe-sex practices are important.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use condoms consistently and correctly for vaginal, anal, and oral sex
    \item Get tested regularly, especially if you have a new partner or more than one partner
    \item Ask new partners about their STI testing status
    \item Have a follow-up test 3 months after treatment to check for re-infection
    \item Avoid sex until treatment is completed and partners are treated
    \item If pregnant, ask for chlamydia screening as part of antenatal care
    \item Report symptoms early -- early testing prevents complications
\end{itemize}
