\chapter{Albinism}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Albinism is a group of inherited conditions in which the body produces little or none of the pigment melanin, which normally colours the skin, hair, and eyes. It is caused by changes (mutations) in genes involved in melanin production and transport.

The two main types are oculocutaneous albinism, which affects the skin, hair, and eyes, and ocular albinism, which affects mainly the eyes. People with albinism have very pale skin and hair, reduced vision, and increased sensitivity to light. Albinism is present from birth, does not worsen over time, and does not affect intelligence or lifespan. The main health challenges are lifelong sun protection and ongoing eye care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Albinism affects roughly 1 in 17,000 to 1 in 20,000 people worldwide and occurs in all ethnic groups. Oculocutaneous albinism type 2 is the most common form and is seen more often in people of African descent, while type 1 is more common in people of European descent.

Because albinism is inherited in a recessive pattern, it can appear in a family with no previous history of the condition. Both sexes are affected equally in oculocutaneous albinism, whereas ocular albinism is X-linked and therefore affects boys predominantly.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Albinism is caused by mutations in genes that control the production and transport of melanin.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{OCA1:} caused by changes in the \textit{TYR} gene, which codes for the enzyme tyrosinase needed for melanin production
    \item \textbf{OCA2:} caused by changes in the \textit{OCA2} gene, which affects melanin production in pigment cells
    \item \textbf{Other forms:} caused by changes in genes such as \textit{TYRP1} and \textit{SLC45A2} (OCA4)
    \item \textbf{Ocular albinism:} usually inherited in an X-linked pattern, so it affects boys predominantly
    \item \textbf{Syndromic forms:} albinism can occur as part of syndromes such as Hermansky-Pudlak and Chediak-Higashi, which add bleeding or immune problems
\end{itemize}

In albinism the pigment cells (melanocytes) are present but cannot produce normal amounts of melanin. The lack of pigment in the skin and hair is obvious, but the eye is also affected: without melanin the light-sensitive retina develops abnormally, and the nerve fibres running from the eyes to the brain follow abnormal pathways, producing reduced vision, nystagmus, and light sensitivity.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Very pale skin, hair, and eye colour, although some forms retain a degree of pigment
    \item Light sensitivity (photophobia) and squinting in bright light
    \item Reduced visual acuity, which in many people is at the level of legal blindness
    \item Nystagmus -- rapid, involuntary movements of the eyes
    \item Strabismus (turned or crossed eyes) and difficulty with depth perception
    \item Short-sightedness, long-sightedness, or astigmatism
    \item A tendency to sunburn quickly, with little or no tanning
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vitiligo, which causes patchy loss of pigment later in life rather than pale skin from birth
    \item Other conditions with pale skin or pigment anomalies, such as Waardenburg syndrome or phenylketonuria
    \item Other causes of nystagmus or poor vision in childhood, such as optic nerve hypoplasia
    \item Ocular albinism, which can resemble other eye conditions before the skin features are recognised
    \item Normal very fair skin in people without a pigment disorder
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if a child has very pale skin together with nystagmus, poor vision, or light sensitivity, so that the condition can be properly assessed. Regular eye examinations are needed throughout life. Seek urgent care for any new mole, sore, or skin change that does not heal, or for any features suggesting skin cancer.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe sunburn with extensive blistering, fever, or dizziness
    \item Signs of heat exhaustion or heat stroke, such as confusion, fainting, or vomiting in hot weather
    \item Sudden changes in vision or severe eye pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} the combination of pale skin and hair with the characteristic eye features usually makes the diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Eye examination:} assessment of vision, eye movements, and the back of the eye by an ophthalmologist
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} blood tests to identify the specific gene change and confirm the type of albinism
    \item \textbf{Hair bulb test:} in some centres, examining a plucked hair to measure melanin production
    \item \textbf{Family history:} information about other affected family members to clarify the inheritance pattern
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
There is no cure, but the main problems -- vision and skin cancer risk -- can be well managed.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sun protection:} high-SPF sunscreen, protective clothing, hats, and sunglasses used daily and reapplied
    \item \textbf{Skin checks:} regular examination of the skin by a doctor or dermatologist to detect skin cancers early
    \item \textbf{Vision correction:} glasses or contact lenses for short-sightedness, long-sightedness, and astigmatism
    \item \textbf{Low-vision aids:} magnifiers, large-print materials, and assistive technology for reading and study
    \item \textbf{Eye muscle surgery:} for strabismus, which can improve eye alignment and sometimes head posture
    \item \textbf{Educational support:} seating near the front of the classroom, adapted materials, and learning support as needed
    \item \textbf{Genetic counselling:} information for families about inheritance and the risk to future children
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Skin cancer, including melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers, due to increased sun sensitivity
    \item Visual impairment affecting education, driving, and daily activities
    \item Severe sunburn and heat-related illness
    \item Social and psychological effects, including stigma, bullying, and low confidence
    \item In syndromic forms, bleeding problems (Hermansky-Pudlak syndrome) or immune problems (Chediak-Higashi syndrome), depending on the genetic type
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
People with albinism have a normal lifespan and normal intelligence. Vision is reduced but usually stable and does not deteriorate significantly beyond normal age-related change. With good sun protection, the risk of skin cancer can be substantially reduced, and most people with albinism lead full, active lives. The main ongoing challenges are dedicated eye care and lifelong sun protection.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sun protection:} use high-SPF sunscreen every day, reapply it regularly, and cover exposed skin with clothing and hats
    \item \textbf{Glasses:} wear sunglasses that block ultraviolet light
    \item \textbf{Skin surveillance:} have regular skin checks by a doctor, and report any new or changing mole
    \item \textbf{Eye care:} attend regular eye examinations and update glasses as needed
    \item \textbf{Reading aids:} use good lighting and magnifying aids for reading and close work
    \item \textbf{Genetic advice:} for families, genetic counselling can clarify the risk to siblings and future children
\end{itemize}
