\chapter{Cerebral Aneurysm}
\index{Cerebral Aneurysm}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A cerebral aneurysm is a balloon-like bulge or weak spot in the wall of an artery inside the brain. The vessel wall thins and swells under the pressure of blood flow, creating an outpouching that may remain stable for years or, in some cases, rupture and bleed into the space surrounding the brain.

Most cerebral aneurysms cause no symptoms and are found incidentally on brain scans done for other reasons. The main danger is rupture, which produces a subarachnoid haemorrhage -- a life-threatening bleed that requires emergency medical care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cerebral aneurysms are found in roughly 2--3\% of the general population, making them relatively common, although most never rupture. They occur at any age but are most often diagnosed in people between the ages of 40 and 60, and they are slightly more common in women.

Most cases are sporadic, but a small proportion run in families, and people with a first-degree relative who has had a ruptured aneurysm are at increased risk. Rupture is uncommon overall, but the consequences are severe: subarachnoid haemorrhage affects about 1 in 10,000 people per year and often strikes without warning.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The cause of most cerebral aneurysms is not fully understood, but several factors are known to weaken artery walls:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High blood pressure:} sustained hypertension puts stress on vessel walls
    \item \textbf{Smoking:} a strong and modifiable risk factor for both formation and rupture
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} family history, and rare conditions such as polycystic kidney disease and connective tissue disorders (such as Marfan syndrome)
    \item \textbf{Advancing age:} wear and tear on the vessel wall over time
    \item \textbf{Branch points:} aneurysms most often form where arteries divide, where blood flow is turbulent
    \item \textbf{Head injury or infection:} uncommon causes of damage to the artery wall
\end{itemize}

The arterial wall at the aneurysm is thinner and less elastic than normal. If it stretches beyond its limits, it can tear, allowing blood to escape under arterial pressure into the space around the brain.

\section*{Clinical Features}
An unruptured aneurysm often causes no symptoms. When it does, symptoms depend on its size and position:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Headache, sometimes localised to one side or behind one eye
    \item Pain above or behind the eye, or a dilated pupil
    \item Double vision or a drooping eyelid, from pressure on nearby nerves
    \item Numbness, weakness, or difficulty speaking
\end{itemize}

Rupture produces the classic sudden severe headache:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A sudden, explosive "thunderclap" headache, often described as the worst headache of life
    \item Nausea and vomiting
    \item Neck stiffness and sensitivity to light
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or loss of consciousness
    \item Seizures or collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Migraine and other primary headache disorders
    \item Sudden severe headache from other causes, including benign sexual headache or cough headache
    \item Meningitis and encephalitis, which can cause headache, fever, and neck stiffness
    \item Intracerebral haemorrhage or ischaemic stroke
    \item Brain tumour or other space-occupying lesions
    \item Cervical artery dissection, which can mimic the pain of aneurysm rupture
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek urgent medical assessment for any new or severe headache, or for visual problems, weakness, or confusion.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The sudden onset of the worst headache of your life, especially with vomiting or neck stiffness
    \item Sudden weakness, numbness, or difficulty speaking
    \item Loss of consciousness, a seizure, or a sudden collapse
    \item A dilated pupil or sudden double vision
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CT scan of the head:} the first test for a suspected bleed, done urgently
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture:} may be performed if the CT is normal but a bleed is still suspected; it detects blood or its breakdown products in the spinal fluid
    \item \textbf{CT angiography or MR angiography:} detailed imaging of the brain's blood vessels to locate the aneurysm
    \item \textbf{Cerebral angiography:} catheter-based imaging of the vessels, used to plan treatment
    \item \textbf{Incidental findings:} aneurysms are sometimes found on scans done for unrelated reasons, when the risk of rupture is assessed individually
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management aims to prevent rupture or to limit damage once rupture has occurred:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Emergency care for rupture:} close monitoring in a neurosurgical unit, pain control, and treatment to control blood pressure and reduce the risk of further bleeding
    \item \textbf{Coiling (endovascular):} thin catheters are passed into the aneurysm and small coils are inserted to block blood flow into it
    \item \textbf{Clipping (surgery):} a small metal clip is placed across the neck of the aneurysm during open surgery
    \item \textbf{Surveillance:} small unruptured aneurysms are sometimes monitored with repeated scans rather than treated
    \item \textbf{Risk-factor control:} stopping smoking and treating high blood pressure reduce rupture risk
    \item \textbf{Follow-up imaging:} to check that a treated aneurysm remains closed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vasospasm: spasm of nearby brain arteries days after a bleed, which can cause stroke
    \item Hydrocephalus: build-up of cerebrospinal fluid after a bleed, causing pressure on the brain
    \item Re-bleeding of the aneurysm, which is most dangerous in the first days
    \item Stroke and permanent neurological damage, including weakness, speech, and memory problems
    \item Seizures and epilepsy
    \item Complications of treatment, such as infection, bleeding, or vessel injury
    \item Long-term cognitive and psychological effects, including fatigue, anxiety, and depression
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Outcomes depend strongly on whether the aneurysm has ruptured and on how quickly treatment begins. Unruptured aneurysms that are small and carefully monitored often cause no trouble for many years. Rupture is serious: a proportion of people die before reaching hospital, and of the survivors, many recover well but some have lasting disability. Recovery can continue for months, and rehabilitation after a bleed plays an important role. Prompt emergency treatment greatly improves the chances of a good outcome.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Do not smoke, and avoid exposure to second-hand smoke
    \item Keep blood pressure under control with regular checks and prescribed treatment
    \item Maintain a healthy weight, exercise regularly, and limit alcohol
    \item Avoid heavy straining where possible, including during bowel movements
    \item If you have a family history, discuss screening with your doctor
    \item Seek immediate help for a sudden severe headache
    \item Follow up with recommended scans if you have a known aneurysm
\end{itemize}
