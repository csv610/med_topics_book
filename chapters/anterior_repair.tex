\chapter{Anterior Repair}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Anterior repair is an operation to correct prolapse of the front wall of the vagina, the condition in which the bladder bulges downwards into the vaginal passage (a cystocele). The procedure is performed through an incision in the front of the vaginal wall, tightening the connective tissues and muscles that support the bladder and the anterior vagina.

It is most often carried out in women whose pelvic floor supports have become weak, causing symptoms such as a sensation of a bulge, pressure, or urinary problems. Anterior repair may be combined with surgery to other parts of the pelvic floor, with a procedure for stress urinary incontinence, or with a hysterectomy, depending on the findings.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Pelvic organ prolapse is very common: roughly half of all women have some degree of prolapse, although many have no symptoms. Anterior wall prolapse (cystocele) is the most frequent type. The condition becomes more common with age and after childbirth, and the lifetime risk of surgery for prolapse or urinary incontinence is significant, estimated at around 1 in 8 women in some studies.

Most anterior repairs are performed in women over the age of 50, but younger women who have had difficult or multiple vaginal deliveries can also be affected. Rates of prolapse surgery vary between countries, reflecting differences in delivery practices, healthcare access, and surgical approaches.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The pelvic floor is a hammock of muscles and connective tissue that supports the bladder, womb, and bowel. Anterior repair is needed when these supports fail:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Childbirth:} vaginal delivery, especially with large babies, prolonged labour, or assisted delivery, stretches and damages the pelvic floor muscles and fascia
    \item \textbf{Ageing and menopause:} falling oestrogen levels reduce the strength and elasticity of the tissues
    \item \textbf{Chronic straining:} from constipation, heavy lifting, or a chronic cough increases pressure on the pelvic floor
    \item \textbf{Obesity:} excess weight raises the intra-abdominal pressure borne by the pelvic supports
    \item \textbf{Hysterectomy:} removal of the womb can weaken the supporting structures around it
    \item \textbf{Genetics and connective tissue weakness:} some women have inherently weaker tissues, and a family history increases the risk
\end{itemize}

When the connective tissue between the bladder and the front of the vagina stretches or tears, the bladder sags into the vagina. Anterior repair repositions the bladder and strengthens the overlying tissues to restore support.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms vary with the degree of prolapse:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A feeling of heaviness, pressure, or fullness in the vagina
    \item A bulge or lump that can sometimes be felt at the entrance to the vagina
    \item Symptoms that are worse after standing, lifting, or at the end of the day, and better when lying down
    \item Urinary symptoms: difficulty emptying the bladder, a slow or weak stream, leaking urine, or needing to strain to void
    \item The need to push the bulge back inside to pass urine comfortably
    \item Occasional discomfort during intercourse
    \item Backache or lower abdominal discomfort
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions can cause similar symptoms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Other types of prolapse:} posterior wall prolapse (rectocele) or prolapse of the womb (uterine prolapse), which frequently occur together with anterior prolapse
    \item \textbf{Urinary stress incontinence:} leaking urine without a significant prolapse
    \item \textbf{Vaginal or cervical growths:} benign or malignant masses that can be felt as a bulge
    \item \textbf{Bowel problems:} constipation or faecal loading that mimics the feeling of pelvic pressure
    \item \textbf{Urinary tract infection:} causing urgency and poor emptying
    \item \textbf{Pelvic masses:} such as fibroids or ovarian cysts pressing on the bladder
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if you notice a persistent bulge, pressure, or heaviness in the vagina, or difficulty emptying your bladder. These symptoms are not simply a normal part of ageing, and they respond well to treatment.

\textbf{Seek urgent care if:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item You are completely unable to pass urine (urinary retention)
    \item The prolapse becomes painful, swollen, or cannot be pushed back
    \item There is heavy bleeding or a new lump that is enlarging
    \item You develop a fever, which could signal infection
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Inability to pass urine at all, with lower abdominal pain
    \item Severe or heavy vaginal bleeding
    \item Collapse or severe pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is made on examination, with further tests to plan surgery:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} a careful account of the symptoms, childbirth history, and their impact on daily life
    \item \textbf{Pelvic examination:} the clinician assesses the prolapse with the woman lying down and often standing, grading its severity
    \item \textbf{Urine tests:} to check for infection, or to measure residual urine after voiding
    \item \textbf{Urodynamic testing:} specialised tests of bladder function in some women, particularly when urinary symptoms are prominent
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} to check the kidneys and bladder emptying in selected cases
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative assessment:} blood tests, review of general health, and discussion of anaesthetic options
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Anterior repair is usually considered when symptoms are bothersome and conservative measures have not helped:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conservative options first:} pelvic floor muscle training, vaginal oestrogen for postmenopausal women, weight loss, and treatment of constipation
    \item \textbf{Vaginal pessary:} a supportive device inserted into the vagina that many women try before deciding on surgery
    \item \textbf{Anterior repair surgery:} an incision in the front of the vaginal wall to tighten the underlying tissues and restore support
    \item \textbf{Native tissue versus mesh:} most repairs now use the woman's own tissues; synthetic mesh is used only rarely and in specialist centres because of safety concerns
    \item \textbf{Combined procedures:} repair of other prolapses, a bladder sling for stress incontinence, or hysterectomy if needed
    \item \textbf{Anaesthesia:} usually a general or spinal anaesthetic, with a short hospital stay
    \item \textbf{After surgery:} rest, avoidance of heavy lifting and constipation, and gradual return to normal activity over several weeks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Recurrence:} the prolapse can return over time, and repeat surgery is needed in a minority of women
    \item \textbf{Bleeding or infection:} as with any operation, including urinary tract infection after catheter use
    \item \textbf{Damage to nearby organs:} very rarely, injury to the bladder or ureters during surgery
    \item \textbf{Urinary symptoms:} some women develop difficulty emptying or new leakage after surgery
    \item \textbf{Painful intercourse:} scar tissue can cause discomfort, although this usually settles
    \item \textbf{Blood clot in the leg or lung:} a small risk after surgery, reduced by early mobilisation
    \item \textbf{Mesh-related complications:} where mesh is used, a risk of erosion, pain, or infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most women who have an anterior repair are satisfied with the result and report significant improvement in their bulge, pressure, and bladder symptoms. The operation is effective in the short and medium term, but prolapse can recur with time, and a minority of women need further surgery.

Recovery typically takes around six weeks, with restrictions on heavy lifting and intercourse during that period. Outcomes are better when the pelvic floor is protected afterwards through a healthy weight, regular pelvic floor exercises, and avoidance of chronic straining.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Practise pelvic floor muscle exercises regularly, especially after childbirth
    \item Prevent and treat constipation with plenty of fluids, fibre, and physical activity
    \item Avoid chronic heavy lifting, and use proper technique when lifting
    \item Maintain a healthy weight
    \item Treat a chronic cough, such as from smoking or asthma
    \item For postmenopausal women, consider vaginal oestrogen on medical advice
    \item After surgery, follow the surgeon's advice on rest, lifting, and activity, and attend follow-up appointments
    \item Report any new bulge, pain, or bladder problem promptly
\end{itemize}
