\chapter{Hallucination}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A hallucination is the experience of sensing something that is not actually there, in the absence of any external stimulus. Hallucinations can affect any of the senses, but hearing voices (auditory hallucinations) is the most common type, followed by visual, tactile, olfactory, and gustatory hallucinations. They occur in a range of conditions, including psychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia, neurological conditions such as Parkinson disease and dementia, the effects of drugs and alcohol, sleep deprivation, and severe illness. Hallucinations can be distressing for the person and their family. Treatment addresses the underlying cause and, where appropriate, includes antipsychotic medicines and psychological support.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hallucinations are more common than often assumed. Hypnagogic and hypnopompic hallucinations (on falling asleep and waking) occur in up to 30% of the population and are harmless. Hallucinations occur in about 70% of people with schizophrenia, in a significant proportion of people with Parkinson disease and dementia (especially Lewy body dementia), and in many people who misuse alcohol and drugs. Visual hallucinations are common in older people with poor vision (Charles Bonnet syndrome). Around 1 in 10 older hospitalised patients experiences delirium with hallucinations.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Hallucinations arise from disturbances in the brain regions that process sensory information.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychiatric causes:} schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, and severe mood disorders, in which the brain generates false perceptions, often auditory
    \item \textbf{Neurological causes:} Parkinson disease, dementia (especially Lewy body and Alzheimer), epilepsy, migraine, and stroke
    \item \textbf{Substance use:} alcohol withdrawal (delirium tremens), hallucinogens, stimulants, cannabis, and many other drugs
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} some antiparkinsonian, corticosteroid, and other medicines can cause hallucinations
    \item \textbf{Delirium:} acute confusion from infection, surgery, or severe illness
    \item \textbf{Sleep-related:} hypnagogic and hypnopompic hallucinations, and narcolepsy
    \item \textbf{Sensory deprivation:} poor vision or hearing can cause the brain to generate images or sounds (Charles Bonnet syndrome)
    \item \textbf{Mechanisms:} overactivity or disinhibition of sensory cortex, and altered dopamine and other neurotransmitter signalling
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features depend on the type and cause of the hallucination.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Hearing voices, which may comment, command, or converse
    \item Seeing things that are not there, from simple shapes to complex scenes
    \item Feeling sensations on the skin without a cause (tactile)
    \item Smelling or tasting things that are not present (olfactory and gustatory)
    \item Hallucinations that occur only when falling asleep or waking
    \item Hallucinations that the person recognises are not real, or that they believe are real (with delusions)
    \item Accompanying symptoms of the underlying condition, such as paranoia, confusion, or movement problems
    \item Distress, agitation, and fear, especially with threatening hallucinations
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Hallucinations must be distinguished from other perceptual experiences.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Illusions: misperceptions of real stimuli (seeing a coat as a person)
    \item Delusions: fixed false beliefs without sensory experience
    \item Pseudohallucinations: perceptions recognised as coming from the mind
    \item Dream-like states and intrusive thoughts
    \item Pseudo-hallucinations in dissociative disorders
    \item Normal sleep phenomena
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if hallucinations occur, especially if they are new, persistent, or distressing, or if they are accompanied by other symptoms. Family members should seek help for a person who is experiencing hallucinations.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hallucinations with confusion, agitation, or a threat of harm to self or others
    \item Hallucinations during alcohol or drug withdrawal, which can be dangerous
    \item Hallucinations with a high fever or severe illness (delirium)
    \item Any situation in which the person is unsafe
\end{itemize}
For immediate support, Lifeline (13 11 14) and Beyond Blue (1300 22 4636) are available.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis identifies the underlying cause of the hallucinations.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical interview:} detailed history of the hallucinations, other symptoms, medicines, and substance use
    \item \textbf{Mental state examination:} assessment of perception, thought, and mood
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} including neurological examination
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} to exclude metabolic, infectious, and toxic causes
    \item \textbf{Brain imaging:} CT or MRI if a neurological cause is suspected
    \item \textbf{EEG:} for suspected epilepsy
    \item \textbf{Cognitive assessment:} for dementia
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a psychiatrist, neurologist, or geriatrician as appropriate
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment targets the underlying cause and the hallucinations themselves.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Treating the cause:} antipsychotic medicines for schizophrenia, management of Parkinson disease and dementia, and treatment of delirium
    \item \textbf{Substance withdrawal:} medical management of alcohol and drug withdrawal
    \item \textbf{Stopping culprit medicines:} under medical supervision
    \item \textbf{Antipsychotic medicines:} reduce hallucinations across many causes
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} therapy to help people cope with persistent hallucinations, including cognitive behavioural therapy
    \item \textbf{Reassurance:} for benign types such as hypnagogic hallucinations and Charles Bonnet syndrome
    \item \textbf{Environment:} adequate light, familiarity, and orientation for people with delirium or dementia
    \item \textbf{Support for families:} education and support for carers
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Distress, fear, and anxiety
    \item Behavioural disturbance, including agitation and, rarely, harm to self or others (especially with command hallucinations)
    \item Social withdrawal and stigma
    \item Worsening of the underlying condition if untreated
    \item Misdiagnosis leading to delayed treatment
    \item Falls and injuries in older people with dementia-related hallucinations
    \item Effects on family and caregivers
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis depends on the cause. Hallucinations from delirium, drugs, and fever resolve when the cause is treated. Sleep-related hallucinations are harmless and lifelong but benign. Hallucinations in schizophrenia are usually well controlled with antipsychotic medicines, though they may not disappear entirely. Visual hallucinations in dementia and Parkinson disease are managed with adjustment of medicines and specialist care. With appropriate treatment, most people experience significant improvement in their hallucinations and quality of life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek early treatment for mental health and neurological conditions
    \item Take medicines as prescribed and report any hallucinations that develop after starting a new medicine
    \item Limit alcohol and avoid illicit drugs
    \item Maintain good sleep habits
    \item For older people, keep the environment calm and well lit, and maintain social connection
    \item If you experience hallucinations, tell your doctor rather than hiding them
    \item Keep a diary of when hallucinations occur
    \item Seek support for the emotional impact on yourself or your family
    \item In an emergency or crisis, call 000 or a crisis line
\end{itemize}
