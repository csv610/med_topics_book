\chapter{Aphasia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Aphasia is an acquired disorder of language caused by damage to the parts of the brain that produce and understand speech. It affects the ability to speak, understand what others say, read, or write, and the pattern of difficulty depends on which language areas of the brain are injured. Aphasia is not a problem with intelligence -- the person's thinking and reasoning are often completely unaffected -- and it is not a psychiatric condition.

It is most commonly caused by stroke, but it can also follow head injury, brain tumours, infections, and some progressive brain diseases. The severity ranges from mild word-finding difficulty to a near-total inability to communicate, and it can be profoundly isolating for the person and their family.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Aphasia affects a substantial minority of stroke survivors: estimates suggest that roughly one in three to four people who have a stroke experience it in the early stages, with many recovering substantially over the following months. Because stroke becomes more common with age, aphasia is most often seen in older adults, but it can affect anyone, including children, after injury or illness.

It also occurs after head trauma, brain tumours, brain infections, and in progressive conditions such as primary progressive aphasia. Language is processed predominantly in the left hemisphere, so aphasia is far more common after left-sided brain damage and is frequently accompanied by weakness of the right side of the body.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Language is processed mainly in the left hemisphere of the brain. \textbf{Broca's area}, in the frontal lobe, is important for producing speech; \textbf{Wernicke's area}, in the temporal lobe, is important for understanding language; and a band of fibres, the arcuate fasciculus, connects them. Damage to these regions, or to the pathways between them, causes aphasia:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stroke:} the commonest cause; a blocked artery or a bleed destroys brain tissue within the language network
    \item \textbf{Head injury:} trauma can bruise, shear, or destroy language areas
    \item \textbf{Brain tumour:} a growth can compress or infiltrate language tissue
    \item \textbf{Brain infection:} encephalitis or a brain abscess can damage the language cortex
    \item \textbf{Progressive disease:} primary progressive aphasia, a form of dementia, slowly and progressively damages language regions
    \item \textbf{Temporary causes:} transient aphasia can occur during a migraine aura or a transient ischaemic attack (mini-stroke)
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} the specific deficit reflects which language function -- expression, comprehension, or the link between them -- is served by the damaged area
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms depend on which language functions are damaged:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Expressive (Broca's) aphasia:} slow, effortful, telegraphic speech made of short phrases, with relatively preserved understanding
    \item \textbf{Receptive (Wernicke's) aphasia:} fluent but nonsensical or meaningless speech, with severe difficulty understanding others
    \item \textbf{Global aphasia:} severe impairment of both producing and understanding language
    \item \textbf{Anomic aphasia:} prominent word-finding difficulty with vague, empty, or circumlocutory speech
    \item Difficulty reading (alexia) or writing (agraphia)
    \item Frustration, distress, irritability, or withdrawal when communication repeatedly fails
    \item In stroke, aphasia is often accompanied by right-sided weakness, because the same hemisphere of the brain controls both language and the right side of the body
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dysarthria:} slurred or unclear speech from weak, paralysed, or poorly coordinated speech muscles, with language itself intact
    \item \textbf{Apraxia of speech:} difficulty planning and sequencing the movements needed for speech, rather than a language disorder
    \item \textbf{Confusion or delirium:} impaired attention and consciousness, rather than a primary language problem
    \item \textbf{Dementia and other cognitive disorders:} generalised decline that may include language as one component
    \item \textbf{Psychiatric conditions:} mutism, catatonia, or conversion disorders
    \item \textbf{Hearing impairment:} which can masquerade as poor comprehension
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
A sudden change in speech, understanding, reading, or writing is a medical emergency, because it may be a stroke. Even if the difficulty lasts only a few minutes, it still requires urgent assessment, as it can be a warning of a transient ischaemic attack. Call 000 immediately -- do not wait to see whether the symptoms pass.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden difficulty speaking, finding words, or understanding speech
    \item Face drooping on one side, or weakness or numbness of one arm or leg
    \item Sudden confusion, dizziness, or loss of balance or coordination
    \item A severe headache accompanied by any of these symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} a doctor and speech--language pathologist assess all language skills, including naming, repetition, comprehension, reading, and writing
    \item \textbf{Brain imaging:} CT or MRI to identify the cause, its location, and its extent -- such as a stroke, tumour, or abscess
    \item \textbf{Formal language testing:} structured standardised tools that measure the type and severity of aphasia and guide therapy
    \item \textbf{Stroke work-up:} blood tests, cardiac monitoring, and imaging of the neck and brain vessels to find the cause of a stroke and prevent a recurrence
    \item \textbf{Hearing assessment:} to exclude hearing loss as a contributing factor
    \item \textbf{Mood and cognitive screening:} to identify depression, anxiety, and associated cognitive problems that complicate recovery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Speech--language therapy:} the main treatment; regular, intensive, aphasia-specific therapy helps retrain language skills and develop practical communication strategies
    \item \textbf{Treatment of the cause:} urgent stroke care, including clot-dissolving or clot-removal treatment where appropriate, limits the brain damage
    \item \textbf{Communication aids:} picture boards, writing tools, gesture, and tablet applications that support conversation
    \item \textbf{Group therapy:} communication groups give the person a chance to practise language in a supportive, structured setting
    \item \textbf{Family training:} teaching carers effective communication techniques -- speaking clearly, allowing time, using gestures, and confirming understanding
    \item \textbf{Mood support:} treatment of the depression and anxiety that commonly accompany aphasia, through counselling, therapy, or medication
    \item \textbf{In primary progressive aphasia:} a supportive, multidisciplinary approach focused on maintaining communication, safety, and quality of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Social isolation and loss of relationships because communication is difficult and tiring
    \item Depression and anxiety, which are common and often under-recognised
    \item Difficulty returning to work, driving, or managing finances and medications
    \item Reduced independence in daily activities
    \item Frustration and behavioural changes, including anger or withdrawal
    \item Strain on family and caregivers, who carry the burden of communication
    \item Increased risk of falls and hospitalisation if rehabilitation and support are inadequate
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook varies widely. Much of the recovery takes place in the first few months after a stroke, as swelling settles and the brain begins to reorganise, but improvement can continue for years, particularly with regular therapy. Some people regain most of their language function; others live with significant long-term difficulties and learn to communicate in new ways.

The factors that influence outcome include the size and site of the brain injury, age, general health, and the intensity and timing of therapy. Motivation, family support, and the availability of speech--language services all matter. In primary progressive aphasia, language declines gradually over time, and care focuses on maintaining communication and quality of life for as long as possible.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Prevent stroke -- the main cause -- by controlling blood pressure, not smoking, managing diabetes and cholesterol, and staying physically active
    \item Protect the head from injury with seatbelts, helmets, and fall-prevention measures
    \item After aphasia begins, use clear, slow speech with the affected person, and give plenty of time to respond
    \item Use gestures, pictures, and writing to support conversation, and keep background noise low
    \item Encourage daily practice and attend speech therapy regularly
    \item Maintain social contact and seek support groups for both the person and their family
    \item Seek help early for depression, frustration, or family strain
\end{itemize}
