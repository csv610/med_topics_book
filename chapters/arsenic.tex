\chapter{Arsenic}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Arsenic is a naturally occurring metalloid element present in the earth's crust, water, and soil. It exists in inorganic forms, which are found in water and soil, and organic forms, which predominate in seafood. Inorganic arsenic is a potent toxin and a recognised human carcinogen, and chronic exposure through contaminated drinking water is a major global public health problem. Historically, arsenic compounds were used as poisons, pesticides, pigments, and even medicines. This chapter discusses arsenic as a health hazard from environmental and occupational exposure rather than as a deliberate poison.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Millions of people worldwide are exposed to arsenic through groundwater used for drinking, particularly in parts of Bangladesh, India, China, the United States, and South America where the element occurs naturally in aquifer sediments. Rice and rice products concentrate arsenic from soil and irrigation water, and some seafood contains high levels of organic arsenic. Occupational exposure affects workers in mining, smelting, and industries that use arsenic in wood preservatives, glass, and pesticides. Both acute poisoning and chronic low-level exposure are clinically important, but chronic exposure through drinking water is by far the more common problem and affects entire communities.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Inorganic arsenic enters the body mainly by ingestion and inhalation and is absorbed into the bloodstream, where it interferes with cellular energy production and repair. It binds to and inhibits essential enzymes, generates reactive oxygen species, and causes DNA damage. These effects account for its toxicity to many organs and its classification as a group 1 human carcinogen.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Contaminated drinking water:} natural arsenic in groundwater is the commonest source of chronic exposure worldwide
    \item \textbf{Food:} rice and rice-based products, some seafood, and crops grown in contaminated soil
    \item \textbf{Occupational exposure:} mining, smelting, and the manufacture of pesticides, wood preservatives, and glass
    \item \textbf{Industrial products:} pressure-treated timber and some older pigments and paints
    \item \textbf{Traditional medicines:} some unregulated herbal or folk remedies contain arsenic compounds
    \item \textbf{Burning of treated wood:} releases arsenic into smoke and ash, which can be inhaled
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} inhibition of mitochondrial enzymes, oxidative stress, and DNA damage underlie the acute toxicity and long-term carcinogenicity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Acute exposure to a large dose of arsenic produces a rapid, severe illness, whereas chronic low-level exposure causes damage that accumulates over years.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute effects:} nausea, vomiting, severe abdominal pain, and watery or bloody diarrhoea
    \item \textbf{Acute severe effects:} low blood pressure, rapid heartbeat, confusion, seizures, and kidney failure
    \item \textbf{Skin changes:} dark, mottled pigmentation and thickening of the palms and soles (hyperkeratosis)
    \item \textbf{Mees' lines:} transverse white bands across the nails that appear weeks after significant exposure
    \item \textbf{Peripheral neuropathy:} tingling, numbness, and weakness, usually in a glove-and-stocking distribution
    \item \textbf{Anaemia:} fatigue and pallor from impaired red blood cell production
    \item \textbf{Long-term effects:} increased risk of skin, bladder, and lung cancer, together with cardiovascular disease, hypertension, and diabetes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The clinical effects of arsenic, particularly chronic exposure, can easily be attributed to other conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Other heavy metal poisoning, such as lead, mercury, or thallium
    \item Vitamin B12 deficiency or diabetes causing peripheral neuropathy
    \item Other causes of skin pigmentation and hyperkeratosis, including chronic sun damage
    \item Gastroenteritis or food poisoning in acute presentations
    \item Anaemia of other cause, such as iron deficiency
    \item Peripheral vascular disease from smoking or diabetes
    \item Chronic liver disease from alcohol, viruses, or other toxins
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical advice if you develop symptoms that could relate to arsenic exposure, or if you work with or live near a known source of arsenic. Testing of private drinking water wells is worthwhile in areas where arsenic is known to occur naturally.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Suspected deliberate or accidental ingestion of a large amount of arsenic
    \item Severe vomiting, bloody diarrhoea, or signs of shock such as fainting or confusion
    \item Seizures or difficulty breathing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis depends on a careful history of possible exposure combined with laboratory testing to confirm the presence of arsenic.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Exposure history:} water source, dietary habits, occupation, hobbies, and use of traditional remedies
    \item \textbf{Urine arsenic test:} the principal test for recent exposure, ideally on a 24-hour collection; levels distinguish inorganic arsenic from the less toxic organic seafood forms
    \item \textbf{Blood arsenic:} useful in the hours shortly after acute exposure
    \item \textbf{Hair and nail analysis:} can indicate exposure occurring over preceding months
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, liver function, renal function, and blood glucose
    \item \textbf{Nerve conduction studies:} for people with symptoms of peripheral neuropathy
    \item \textbf{Environmental testing:} analysis of well water, soil, or workplace samples to confirm the source
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The most important intervention is to stop further exposure. Beyond this, treatment is supportive and depends on the severity of illness.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Removal from exposure:} changing the water source, diet, workplace, or product responsible for exposure
    \item \textbf{Acute poisoning:} hospital care with intravenous fluids, supportive monitoring, and, in severe cases, chelating agents that bind and remove arsenic
    \item \textbf{Symptom management:} treatment of skin, neurological, and haematological problems as they arise
    \item \textbf{Skin surveillance:} regular skin examination for people with arsenical skin changes, because of the markedly increased cancer risk
    \item \textbf{Cancer screening:} ongoing monitoring for skin, bladder, and lung cancer after significant exposure
    \item \textbf{Community measures:} installation of water filters, provision of alternative water sources, or use of treated or bottled water in affected areas
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} repeat urine testing to confirm that levels fall after removal from the source
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cancers of the skin, bladder, lung, liver, and kidney
    \item Permanent peripheral nerve damage with weakness and numbness
    \item Circulatory disease, including peripheral vascular disease and gangrene
    \item Hypertension, diabetes, and liver damage
    \item Anaemia and impaired immune function
    \item Effects on children exposed during development, including reduced intelligence and impaired growth
    \item Death from severe acute poisoning without prompt treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Severe acute arsenic poisoning can be fatal without prompt hospital treatment, but with supportive care and chelation many patients recover. Chronic exposure causes damage that accumulates over years; if exposure stops early, some effects, such as skin changes and anaemia, may improve, but the elevated risk of cancer persists for decades. Outcomes are better where contaminated water supplies are replaced early and affected communities are monitored for the long-term consequences of past exposure.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Test private drinking water wells for arsenic, especially in areas where it is known to occur
    \item Use an alternative or treated water source if arsenic is detected
    \item Limit intake of rice from regions with high arsenic in soil and water, and rinse rice before cooking
    \item Wear protective equipment and follow safety rules when working with arsenic-containing products
    \item Avoid burning pressure-treated wood, and wash hands after handling it
    \item Avoid unregulated herbal or traditional remedies of unknown composition
    \item If exposed, keep up with skin checks, cancer screening, and medical follow-up
\end{itemize}
