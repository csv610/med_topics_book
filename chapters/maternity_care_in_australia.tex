\chapter{Maternity care in Australia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Maternity care in Australia refers to the comprehensive health services, clinical interventions, and emotional support provided to women throughout preconception, pregnancy (antenatal period), childbirth (intrapartum period), and the first six weeks following birth (postnatal period). The Australian maternity care system offers several models of care aimed at ensuring the safety and wellbeing of both mother and neonate. These models include public hospital clinics, midwifery-led services (such as Midwifery Group Practice), private obstetric care, shared care with general practitioners (GPs), and home birth programmes. The system prioritises woman-centred care, evidence-based practice, and collaborative clinical pathways to manage both low-risk and high-risk pregnancies.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Approximately 300,000 babies are born in Australia each year. The vast majority of births (around 98\%) occur in hospital settings, with the public sector accommodating approximately 70\% of all births and the private sector managing the remaining 30\%. Maternal mortality in Australia is rare, with rates consistently among the lowest in the world, averaging between 5 to 7 maternal deaths per 100,000 women who give birth. However, significant health disparities persist. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women, women living in rural or remote areas, and those from lower socioeconomic backgrounds experience higher rates of maternal morbidity, stillbirth, preterm birth, and low birth weight.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Maternity care is structured around clinical pathways designed to support the normal physiological adaptations of pregnancy and detect pathophysiological changes early:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antenatal care schedules:} A structured schedule of 8 to 10 clinical visits for uncomplicated pregnancies, or more frequent visits for high-risk cases, to monitor gestational milestones and fetal growth.
    \item \textbf{Midwifery Group Practice (MGP):} A continuity-of-care model where a primary midwife or small team provides care throughout pregnancy, labour, birth, and the postnatal period, which has been shown to reduce interventions and improve maternal satisfaction.
    \item \textbf{General Practitioner shared care:} A collaborative model where a credentialed community GP manages routine antenatal care, and coordinates with local public hospital obstetric units for birth and specialised reviews.
    \item \textbf{Specialist obstetric care:} Consultant-led care for women with pre-existing medical conditions, pregnancy-induced complications, or complex obstetric histories (e.g., multiple gestations).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Clinical encounters in maternity care involve monitoring key maternal and fetal indicators to assess the progression of pregnancy and labour:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Maternal physiological signs:} Tracking changes in blood pressure, checking for proteinuria (pre-eclampsia screening), and monitoring gestational weight gain.
    \item \textbf{Symphysis-fundal height (SFH):} Measuring the distance from the pubic symphysis to the top of the uterine fundus from 20 weeks onwards to screen for fetal growth restrictions or macrosomia.
    \item \textbf{Fetal movement monitoring:} Assessing the frequency and character of fetal movements from approximately 24 to 28 weeks of gestation as an indicator of fetal wellbeing.
    \item \textbf{Labour progression parameters:} Monitoring uterine contraction frequency and intensity, cervical dilatation, effacement, and fetal station during labour.
    \item \textbf{Postnatal clinical parameters:} Assessing uterine involution (fundal height and tone), checking lochia (post-birth vaginal discharge), monitoring perineal or caesarean wound healing, and screening for postnatal psychological distress.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Maternity care requires distinguishing between normal discomforts of pregnancy and serious gestational complications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gestational hypertension vs. pre-eclampsia:} Differentiating isolated high blood pressure developing after 20 weeks from pre-eclampsia, which is a multi-system syndrome involving proteinuria, thrombocytopenia, or renal/hepatic impairment.
    \item \textbf{Pre-labour rupture of membranes (PROM) vs. physiological discharge:} Confirming amniotic fluid leakage via sterile speculum examination and biochemical testing, and ruling out urinary incontinence or vaginal candidiasis.
    \item \textbf{Postnatal blues vs. postnatal depression:} Differentiating transient, self-limiting emotional sensitivity in the first 3 to 10 days post-delivery (baby blues) from clinical postnatal depression, which persists beyond 2 weeks and requires intervention.
    \item \textbf{Antepartum haemorrhage causes:} Distinguishing life-threatening conditions such as placental abruption or placenta praevia from benign cervical ectropion or a "bloody show" associated with early labour.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Pregnant women should contact their midwife, GP, or hospital maternity unit immediately if they experience any warning signs or feel concerned about their baby's health.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Severe vaginal bleeding:} Heavy bright red bleeding at any stage of pregnancy or postpartum.
    \item \textbf{Signs of severe pre-eclampsia or eclampsia:} Sudden onset of a severe headache, visual disturbances (flashing lights, blurriness), severe pain under the ribs, rapid swelling of the face/hands, or seizures.
    \item \textbf{Reduced or altered fetal movements:} A sudden decrease, change, or cessation of the baby's movements.
    \item \textbf{Severe, persistent abdominal pain:} Sharp, constant uterine pain, which may indicate placental abruption or uterine rupture.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Routine and targeted diagnostic tests are offered throughout pregnancy to screen for maternal and fetal anomalies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antenatal blood screening:} First-trimester testing for blood group, rhesus (Rh) antibody status, full blood count, rubella immunity, syphilis, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and HIV.
    \item \textbf{Aneuploidy screening:} Offering non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) or combined first-trimester screening (ultrasound and serum markers) to assess the risk of chromosomal abnormalities.
    \item \textbf{Fetal morphology ultrasound:} A detailed scan performed at 18 to 20 weeks of gestation to evaluate fetal anatomy, placental position, and amniotic fluid volume.
    \item \textbf{Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT):} A 2-hour fasting blood test performed at 24 to 28 weeks to screen for gestational diabetes mellitus.
    \item \textbf{Group B Streptococcus (GBS) screening:} A vaginal-rectal swab collected at 35 to 37 weeks to identify women who require intrapartum intravenous antibiotic prophylaxis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management in maternity care involves promoting healthy physiological processes, administering preventative treatments, and intervening when complications arise:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Routine antenatal management:} Prescribing folic acid and iodine supplements, administering anti-D immunoglobulin to Rh-negative women, and providing pertussis and influenza immunisations.
    \item \textbf{Intrapartum care:} Facilitating labour support, providing pharmacologic pain management (e.g., nitrous oxide, epidural) and non-pharmacologic options, and performing assisted deliveries (forceps, vacuum) or caesarean sections when indicated.
    \item \textbf{Active management of the third stage:} Administering a prophylactic uterotonic (typically intramuscular oxytocin) immediately after delivery of the infant to prevent postpartum haemorrhage.
    \item \textbf{Postnatal and neonatal management:} Conducting newborn examinations, administering vitamin K and hepatitis B vaccinations to the neonate, providing lactation support, and performing the newborn bloodspot screening.
    \item \textbf{Mental health screening:} Routine screening using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) during the antenatal and postnatal periods to identify women requiring psychosocial support.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Pregnancy and childbirth carry risks of acute and chronic complications for both mother and child:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Postpartum haemorrhage (PPH):} Bleeding exceeding 500 mL after a vaginal birth or 1000 mL after a caesarean section, which is a major cause of maternal morbidity.
    \item \textbf{Pre-eclampsia and eclampsia:} Gestational hypertension with multi-system involvement that can lead to maternal stroke, organ failure, fetal growth restriction, or life-threatening maternal seizures.
    \item \textbf{Gestational diabetes mellitus:} Impaired glucose tolerance leading to fetal macrosomia, shoulder dystocia during birth, neonatal hypoglycaemia, and long-term risk of type 2 diabetes.
    \item \textbf{Preterm birth:} Birth before 37 completed weeks of gestation, which is the primary cause of neonatal morbidity and long-term developmental delays.
    \item \textbf{Perinatal mental health disorders:} Clinical depression, anxiety, or postpartum psychosis (a rare but severe psychiatric emergency).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for women and infants receiving maternity care in Australia is excellent, with the vast majority experiencing normal, healthy outcomes. Modern clinical guidelines, state-of-the-art neonatal intensive care units (NICUs), and rapid obstetric emergency response teams ensure high safety standards. However, ongoing national initiatives focus on reducing the higher rates of adverse perinatal outcomes in regional areas and indigenous populations by improving access to culturally safe, local midwifery care and specialist support.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Active self-care and lifestyle optimization before and during pregnancy significantly improve maternal and infant outcomes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Preconception planning:} Optimising weight, ensuring rubella immunity, stopping smoking and alcohol intake, and managing chronic conditions (e.g., hypertension, diabetes) before conceiving.
    \item \textbf{Dietary and supplement guidelines:} Consuming a diet rich in iron and calcium, avoiding foods high in listeria risk (such as soft cheeses, cold deli meats, and raw seafood), and taking recommended supplements.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic floor exercises:} Daily pelvic floor muscle training to reduce the risk of urinary and faecal incontinence during pregnancy and after birth.
    \item \textbf{SIDS and safe sleeping education:} Learning safe infant sleeping guidelines (sleeping baby on their back, keeping head and face uncovered, and keeping environment smoke-free) prior to delivery.
\end{itemize}
