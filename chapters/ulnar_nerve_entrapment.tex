\chapter{Ulnar nerve entrapment}
\index{Ulnar nerve entrapment}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Ulnar nerve entrapment is a common compressive neuropathy characterized by the mechanical compression, traction, or friction of the ulnar nerve along its anatomical pathway from the neck down to the hand. The ulnar nerve is one of the three primary nerves providing sensory and motor innervation to the upper extremity, originating from the C8 and T1 nerve roots, which merge to form the lower trunk of the brachial plexus, progress through the medial cord, and travel down the medial aspect of the arm. Throughout its course, the ulnar nerve passes through several tight anatomical spaces, making it highly susceptible to entrapment. The most prevalent site of entrapment is at the elbow within the cubital tunnel, a clinical condition known as cubital tunnel syndrome. This is the second most common compression neuropathy of the upper extremity, surpassed only by carpal tunnel syndrome of the median nerve. A secondary, less frequent site of entrapment occurs at the wrist within Guyon's canal (also known as the ulnar tunnel), presenting as Guyon's canal syndrome.

The ulnar nerve is responsible for the sensory innervation of the fifth digit (little finger) and the medial (ulnar) half of the fourth digit (ring finger), as well as the corresponding palmar and dorsal aspects of the ulnar side of the hand. Motor-wise, it innervates crucial muscles in the forearm and hand, including the flexor carpi ulnaris, the medial half of the flexor digitorum profundus, the hypothenar muscles (abductor digiti minimi, flexor digiti minimi, opponens digiti minimi), the palmar and dorsal interossei, the third and fourth lumbricals, the adductor pollicis, and the deep head of the flexor pollicis brevis. Entrapment leads to a spectrum of symptoms ranging from mild paresthesia to severe muscle wasting, hand weakness, and permanent loss of fine motor coordination. In advanced stages, the lack of innervation to the intrinsic hand muscles leads to an imbalance between the extrinsic and intrinsic muscles, resulting in a characteristic structural deformity known as the ``claw hand'' or intrinsic-minus hand. Because of its potential to cause severe and irreversible functional disability of the dominant hand, ulnar nerve entrapment is a significant clinical entity requiring thorough understanding, early recognition, and structured management.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cubital tunnel syndrome represents the vast majority of cases of ulnar nerve entrapment. Epidemiological studies estimate the incidence of cubital tunnel syndrome to be approximately 21 to 30 cases per 100,000 person-years. The condition exhibits a marked gender predilection, affecting males approximately twice as frequently as females (a ratio of roughly 2:1). This disparity is attributed to multiple factors, including differences in elbow anatomy (such as a smaller medial epicondyle or a more prominent tubercle of the coronoid process in males), occupational exposures, higher muscle mass in the upper limbs, and a higher prevalence of repetitive mechanical stress.

The peak incidence of ulnar nerve entrapment occurs in individuals aged between 40 and 60 years. However, it can present in younger demographics, particularly athletes involved in throwing sports (such as baseball pitchers or javelin throwers) or individuals with specific anatomical anomalies. Occupational factors play a significant role in its epidemiology. Workers whose daily tasks require repetitive elbow flexion and extension, prolonged static elbow flexion, or direct pressure on the ulnar nerve (e.g., computer operators, truck drivers, assembly line workers, and musicians) display a significantly elevated prevalence. Guyon's canal syndrome is much rarer, accounting for less than 10\% of all ulnar nerve compression cases. It is most frequently diagnosed in long-distance cyclists due to prolonged compression of the wrist against the handlebars (referred to as ``cyclist's palsy''), manual workers using vibrating hand tools, and patients with local space-occupying lesions.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The aetiology of ulnar nerve entrapment is multifactorial, involving anatomical vulnerability, mechanical factors, direct trauma, and systemic conditions. The pathophysiology centers around mechanical compression, nerve traction during joint movement, and chronic friction, all of which culminate in localized ischaemia, focal demyelination, and potentially axonal degeneration if the compression remains unresolved.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Anatomical Vulnerability at the Elbow:} The cubital tunnel is a fibro-osseous space bounded medially by the medial epicondyle, laterally by the olecranon process, and superiorly by the cubital tunnel retinaculum, also known as Osborne's ligament. The nerve can be compressed by several structures along this path: the arcade of Struthers (a thin aponeurotic band extending from the medial intermuscular septum to the medial head of the triceps), the medial intermuscular septum itself, the deep flexor carpi ulnaris aponeurosis, or the superficial fascia connecting the two heads of the flexor carpi ulnaris (Osborne's ligament).
\item \textbf{Repetitive Flexion and Traction:} During elbow flexion, the ulnar nerve is subjected to significant traction. The distance between the medial epicondyle and the olecranon increases, stretching the nerve by up to 4.7\% to 8.0\% of its length. Concurrently, the volume of the cubital tunnel decreases by approximately 55\%, leading to a threefold increase in intraneural pressure. This physiological change is exacerbated in individuals who maintain prolonged elbow flexion during sleep or work.
\item \textbf{Direct Pressure and Chronic Friction:} Leaning on the elbows (such as at a desk or on an armrest) applies direct external pressure onto the ulnar nerve as it runs superficially behind the medial epicondyle. Additionally, in some individuals, the ulnar nerve is hypermobile and subluxates or dislocates anteriorly over the medial epicondyle during elbow flexion, causing chronic friction, mechanical microtrauma, and reactive perineural fibrosis.
\item \textbf{Space-Occupying Lesions:} Tumors, ganglion cysts, lipomas, osteophytes (bone spurs due to osteoarthritis), rheumatoid pannus, or heterotopic ossification can directly compress the ulnar nerve. Ganglion cysts are particularly common causes of compression at the wrist within Guyon's canal.
\item \textbf{Post-traumatic Deformities:} Fractures or dislocations of the elbow can lead to immediate nerve injury or delayed compression. A classic example is ``tardy ulnar palsy,'' where a childhood lateral condylar fracture leads to a cubitus valgus deformity, slowly stretching and compressing the ulnar nerve over decades.
\item \textbf{Systemic and Metabolic Predispositions:} Systemic conditions can compromise the nerve's resilience to mechanical stress or impair its microvascular supply. These include diabetes mellitus, chronic renal failure, hypothyroidism, alcoholism, and amyloidosis. Additionally, generalized peripheral neuropathies render the nerve more susceptible to compression at standard anatomical bottlenecks (double crush syndrome).
\end{itemize}

At a microscopic level, chronic compression restricts blood flow within the vasa nervorum, the capillary network supplying the nerve. The resulting localized ischaemia impairs axonal transport and damages the myelin sheath, leading to focal demyelination. If the compressive force is severe or prolonged, the axons themselves undergo degeneration (Wallerian degeneration), leading to irreversible muscle atrophy and permanent sensory loss.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of ulnar nerve entrapment depends on the site and severity of compression. Patients typically report a combination of sensory disturbances and motor weakness, which may be unilateral or bilateral.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Sensory Disturbances:} The earliest and most common symptom is intermittent numbness, tingling, or paresthesia in the little finger and the ulnar half of the ring finger. These symptoms are frequently worse at night, often due to sleeping with bent elbows, or during activities involving prolonged elbow flexion (e.g., talking on a telephone, typing, or driving). As the condition progresses, the sensory deficit becomes constant and can lead to complete anesthesia. In cubital tunnel syndrome, patients may also experience decreased sensation on the dorsomedial aspect of the hand due to involvement of the dorsal cutaneous branch of the ulnar nerve. If the compression is localized to Guyon's canal at the wrist, this dorsal cutaneous branch (which exits the nerve 5 centimeters proximal to the wrist) is spared, resulting in normal sensation on the back of the hand.
\item \textbf{Motor Deficits and Weakness:} Patients often complain of hand clumsiness, difficulty opening jars, dropping objects, and a weakened grip or pinch strength. This is due to weakness of the intrinsic hand muscles. Patients may notice difficulty coordinating fine motor tasks, such as typing, playing an instrument, or buttoning a shirt.
\item \textbf{Physical Examination Signs:}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tinel's Sign at the Elbow:} Elicited by lightly tapping over the ulnar nerve within the cubital tunnel. A positive response consists of electric, tingling sensations radiating down the forearm into the little and ring fingers.
  \item \textbf{Elbow Flexion Test:} The patient holds the elbow in full flexion with the wrist in extension for 60 seconds. The test is positive if paresthesia or numbness in the ulnar nerve distribution is reproduced or exacerbated.
  \item \textbf{Froment's Sign:} Evaluates the adductor pollicis muscle. The patient is asked to grasp a piece of paper between the thumb and index finger while the examiner attempts to pull it away. Due to ulnar nerve weakness, the patient cannot adduct the thumb and instead flexes the interphalangeal joint of the thumb, utilizing the median-nerve-innervated flexor pollicis longus to maintain a grip.
  \item \textbf{Jeanne's Sign:} Associated with a positive Froment's sign; it is characterized by simultaneous hyperextension of the thumb metacarpophalangeal joint during the pinch grip test.
  \item \textbf{Wartenberg's Sign:} Elicited when the patient is asked to adduct all fingers. The little finger remains abducted (drifted outwards) due to weakness of the third palmar interosseous muscle, while the extensor digiti minimi (innervated by the radial nerve) acts unopposed.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Advanced Structural Changes:} In chronic and severe cases, visible muscle wasting (atrophy) develops. This is most prominent in the first dorsal web space (between the thumb and index finger) and the hypothenar eminence. Severe intrinsic muscle weakness leads to the classic ``ulnar claw hand'' (main en griffe), where the ring and little fingers are hyperextended at the metacarpophalangeal joints and flexed at the proximal and distal interphalangeal joints.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the symptoms of ulnar nerve entrapment can overlap with other neurological and musculoskeletal conditions, a meticulous differential diagnosis is essential.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C8--T1 Cervical Radiculopathy:} Compression of the C8 or T1 spinal nerve roots at the cervical spine (due to cervical disc herniation or foraminal stenosis) can mimic ulnar neuropathy. However, cervical radiculopathy typically presents with neck pain, radiating pain down the medial arm and forearm, sensory loss that extends proximal to the wrist into the medial forearm, and motor weakness in non-ulnar muscles that share C8--T1 innervation (such as the median-innervated abductor pollicis brevis).
\item \textbf{Thoracic Outlet Syndrome (TOS):} Compression of the lower trunk of the brachial plexus as it passes through the thoracic outlet (between the anterior and middle scalene muscles, or between the clavicle and first rib). TOS can cause pain, numbness, and tingling along the medial side of the arm, forearm, and hand, similar to ulnar nerve compression. It is distinguished by positional symptoms (worsened by arm elevation), vascular changes (such as coldness or pallor), and positive provocative tests like Adson's maneuver or the Roos test.
\item \textbf{Carpal Tunnel Syndrome (CTS):} While CTS involves the median nerve and causes sensory symptoms in the thumb, index, middle, and radial half of the ring finger, patients sometimes present with vague hand pain that can be confused with ulnar neuropathy. Phalen's test and Tinel's sign at the wrist will point toward median nerve compression, and electrodiagnostic testing can clearly differentiate the two.
\item \textbf{Guyon's Canal Syndrome:} If a patient presents with ulnar nerve symptoms, it is vital to distinguish between compression at the elbow and compression at the wrist. Sensation on the dorsum of the hand is preserved in Guyon's canal syndrome. Additionally, Tinel's sign will be positive at the wrist (volar carpal ligament) rather than the elbow.
\item \textbf{Motor Neuron Disease (MND) / Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS):} MND can present with isolated hand weakness and intrinsic muscle atrophy. However, MND is characterized by a complete absence of sensory deficits, the presence of fasciculations in other muscle groups, and upper motor neuron signs (such as hyperreflexia and spasticity).
\item \textbf{Medial Epicondylitis (Golfer's Elbow):} This condition involves inflammation or microtearing of the common flexor tendon at the medial epicondyle. It causes localized medial elbow pain and tenderness that worsens with resisted wrist flexion and pronation. While it does not cause numbness or tingling, it frequently co-exists with cubital tunnel syndrome because of their anatomical proximity.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should seek medical evaluation if they experience persistent, worsening, or recurrent numbness and tingling in the hand, particularly if these symptoms begin to interfere with daily activities, sleep, or work productivity. Prompt clinical consultation is highly recommended if there is any noticeable weakness in the hand, such as a tendency to drop objects, difficulty with tasks requiring fine motor control (buttoning shirts, writing, using keys), or if there is visible flattening or muscle wasting in the spaces between the hand bones or in the palm.

Immediate emergency medical services must be accessed (e.g., calling \textbf{local emergency services} in many countries, \textbf{911} in the United States, or \textbf{999} in the United Kingdom) if the onset of hand weakness or numbness is sudden and accompanied by any of the following symptoms:
\begin{itemize}
\item Chest pain, pressure, or a squeezing sensation in the chest.
\item Radiating pain to the jaw, neck, back, or down one or both arms.
\item Shortness of breath, dizziness, cold sweats, or nausea.
\item Sudden weakness or numbness affecting one entire side of the body.
\item Sudden difficulty speaking, slurred speech, confusion, facial drooping, or visual disturbances.
\end{itemize}
These acute symptoms may indicate a life-threatening medical emergency, such as a myocardial infarction (heart attack) or an acute cerebrovascular accident (stroke), rather than a localized peripheral nerve compression.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A definitive diagnosis of ulnar nerve entrapment is established through a combination of detailed clinical history, physical examination, and objective diagnostic investigations.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Clinical History and Physical Examination:} The initial step involves documenting the onset, duration, and triggers of symptoms. The physical examination includes testing the sensory function of the fourth and fifth digits (using light touch and two-point discrimination) and checking for muscle weakness in ulnar-innervated muscles (such as the first dorsal interosseous and adductor pollicis). Provocative maneuvers, including the elbow flexion test, Tinel's test at the cubital tunnel, and assessments for Wartenberg's, Froment's, and Jeanne's signs, are performed to localize the pathology.
\item \textbf{Electrodiagnostic Studies (Nerve Conduction Studies and Electromyography):} This is the gold standard for confirming ulnar nerve entrapment.
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Nerve Conduction Velocity (NCV):} Measures the speed and amplitude of electrical signals traveling along the ulnar nerve. A significant slowing of conduction velocity across the elbow (typically less than 50 meters per second) or a drop in amplitude compared to segments above and below the elbow confirms cubital tunnel syndrome.
  \item \textbf{Electromyography (EMG):} Evaluates the electrical activity of muscles innervated by the ulnar nerve. The presence of denervation potentials (such as fibrillations and positive sharp waves) or abnormal motor unit recruitment in muscles like the first dorsal interosseous indicates axonal loss and helps grade the severity of the entrapment.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{High-Resolution Ultrasonography:} An increasingly utilized imaging modality that provides structural information about the nerve. In patients with cubital tunnel syndrome, ultrasound typically reveals swelling of the ulnar nerve immediately proximal to the site of compression. An ulnar nerve cross-sectional area (CSA) of greater than 9 to 10 mm² at the level of the medial epicondyle is highly sensitive and specific for entrapment. Ultrasound also allows dynamic visualization of nerve subluxation during elbow flexion and can detect local anatomical abnormalities like accessory muscles (e.g., anconeus epitrochlearis) or ganglion cysts.
\item \textbf{Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):} While not routinely required, MRI of the elbow or wrist is indicated if a space-occupying lesion (such as a ganglion, lipoma, or vascular anomaly) is suspected, or in cases of persistent symptoms following prior surgical decompression. MRI of the cervical spine may also be ordered to rule out C8--T1 radiculopathy.
\item \textbf{Plain Radiography (X-rays):} Standard anteroposterior and lateral views of the elbow or wrist are obtained to identify skeletal abnormalities, such as bone spurs (osteophytes), heterotopic ossification, osteoarthritic changes, cubitus valgus deformity, or old fractures that may be narrowing the fibro-osseous tunnels.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of ulnar nerve entrapment is tailored to the severity of the symptoms, the degree of nerve dysfunction, and the patient's functional demands. It ranges from conservative measures for mild cases to surgical intervention for severe or progressive neuropathy.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Conservative Treatment (Mild to Moderate Cases):}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Activity Modification:} The primary step is to avoid activities that exacerbate symptoms. Patients are advised to avoid leaning on their elbows on hard surfaces, to position keyboard and mouse setups ergonomically, and to limit prolonged, repetitive elbow flexion.
  \item \textbf{Night Splinting:} Patients are fitted with a lightweight splint or elbow pad that maintains the elbow in a semi-extended position (approximately 30 to 45 degrees of flexion) during sleep. This prevents sustained nocturnal elbow flexion, which stretches the nerve and increases cubital tunnel pressure.
  \item \textbf{Nerve Gliding/Flossing Exercises:} Specific physical therapy exercises designed to facilitate the smooth gliding of the ulnar nerve through its anatomical pathways, reducing mechanical tension and promoting vascularity.
  \item \textbf{Pharmacotherapy:} Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), such as ibuprofen or naproxen, can be prescribed for short-term relief of associated pain and inflammation, although they do not resolve the mechanical compression. Neuropathic pain medications, including gabapentin or pregabalin, may be considered for chronic, burning pain.
  \item \textbf{Corticosteroid Injections:} Unlike in carpal tunnel syndrome, corticosteroid injections are generally avoided around the cubital tunnel due to the risk of direct nerve injury, intraneural injection, and local tissue atrophy.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Surgical Intervention (Severe Cases or Failure of Conservative Care):} Surgery is indicated when there is progressive motor weakness, muscle atrophy, persistent sensory loss, or if conservative management fails to provide relief after 3 to 6 months.
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{In Situ Decompression:} The simplest surgical procedure, involving the division of Osborne's ligament and the fascial bands between the two heads of the flexor carpi ulnaris. This increases the space within the cubital tunnel without displacing the nerve. It is highly effective for patients without nerve subluxation.
  \item \textbf{Anterior Transposition of the Ulnar Nerve:} The nerve is mobilized out of the cubital tunnel and moved anterior to the medial epicondyle. This eliminates the traction force on the nerve during elbow flexion. The nerve can be placed in a subcutaneous (under the skin), intramuscular (within the muscle), or submuscular (beneath the flexor-pronator muscle group) position.
  \item \textbf{Medial Epicondylectomy:} Involves the surgical removal of a portion of the medial epicondyle. This decompression technique removes the bony prominence over which the nerve must slide, reducing friction and tension during elbow movement.
  \item \textbf{Decompression at Guyon's Canal:} For wrist-level compression, surgery involves dividing the volar carpal ligament and removing any compressing structures, such as ganglion cysts.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated or inadequately managed ulnar nerve entrapment can lead to significant, irreversible complications, as can the surgical procedures used to treat it.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Permanent Intrinsic Muscle Atrophy:} Prolonged compression leads to axonal loss. Once axons degenerate, the target muscles in the hand lose their trophic stimulation and undergo irreversible wasting. This results in permanent loss of hand strength, dexterity, and fine motor skills.
\item \textbf{Permanent Sensory Loss and Neuropathic Pain:} Chronic ischaemia can cause permanent damage to sensory nerve fibers, leading to constant numbness, anesthesia, or the development of chronic neuropathic pain (characterized by burning sensations, hyperalgesia, and dysesthesia in the ulnar distribution).
\item \textbf{Deformity:} Progression to a fixed ``claw hand'' deformity occurs due to the contracture of the denervated intrinsic muscles and the unopposed action of the extrinsic extensors and flexors.
\item \textbf{Surgical Complications:}
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Injury to the Medial Antebrachial Cutaneous Nerve:} The sensory branches of this nerve cross the surgical field at the elbow. Injury can lead to permanent numbness along the medial forearm or the formation of a painful neuroma.
  \item \textbf{Recurrence or Incomplete Relief:} Symptoms may persist or recur if there is incomplete decompression, scarring around the transposed nerve, or if the nerve becomes kinked.
  \item \textbf{Nerve Subluxation:} Following in situ decompression, the ulnar nerve may become hypermobile and subluxate over the medial epicondyle, causing dynamic pain and requiring secondary transposition surgery.
  \item \textbf{General Surgical Risks:} Infection, hematoma formation, wound dehiscence, elbow stiffness, and risks associated with local or general anesthesia.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for ulnar nerve entrapment is highly variable and depends heavily on the duration of symptoms, the severity of compression, the presence of axonal degeneration, and the timing of intervention.

For patients with mild to moderate symptoms without electrodiagnostic evidence of muscle denervation or significant conduction delay, conservative management yields high success rates, with up to 50\% to 60\% of patients experiencing resolution of symptoms with splinting and activity modification alone.

When surgery is required, outcomes are generally favorable if performed before the onset of muscle atrophy. In situ decompression and anterior transposition both have reported success rates of 80\% to 90\% in terms of relieving pain and paresthesias. Sensory recovery occurs gradually, as nerves regenerate at a rate of approximately 1 millimeter per day. However, in patients who already exhibit significant intrinsic hand muscle wasting and axonal loss on EMG, the prognosis for motor recovery is guarded. In these advanced cases, surgery is often performed primarily to halt the progression of the disease and prevent further deterioration, rather than with the expectation of restoring full hand strength. Postoperative hand therapy is a critical determinant of functional recovery.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventing ulnar nerve entrapment and managing early symptoms at home involves adopting joint-protection strategies, optimizing ergonomics, and avoiding repetitive mechanical stress.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Ergonomic Workplace Optimization:} Adjust computer workstations so that the elbows are not bent past 90 degrees while typing. Avoid resting the elbows on hard desk surfaces or armrests; use padded elbow wraps or soft rests if necessary. Ensure keyboard height allows the wrists and elbows to remain in a neutral, relaxed position.
\item \textbf{Postural Awareness and Sleep Hygiene:} Avoid sleeping with the arms fully bent or tucked under the head, which puts maximum tension on the ulnar nerve. If nocturnal elbow flexion is a habit, wrap a dry bath towel loosely around the elbow and secure it with tape, or wear a commercial elbow immobilizer to maintain the arm in a straight or semi-extended position.
\item \textbf{Repetitive Motion Management:} Take regular breaks during activities that require repetitive elbow movement or prolonged flexion (such as driving, talking on a hand-held phone, or playing musical instruments). Straighten and stretch the arms every 30 to 45 minutes. Use hands-free devices for phone calls to avoid holding the phone to the ear.
\item \textbf{Sports Safety and Equipment Modifications:} Cyclists should use padded gloves and handlebar tape to reduce pressure on the wrist (Guyon's canal). Change hand positions frequently during long rides. Athletes should maintain proper throwing mechanics and engage in regular flexibility and strengthening programs for the upper extremity.
\item \textbf{Avoid Direct Impact and Pressure:} Refrain from leaning on the elbows while sitting in chairs, driving, or working at a workbench. Protect the elbow with a soft pad if working in environments where accidental bumps to the medial epicondyle are common.
\end{itemize}
