\chapter{Brain Surgery}
\index{Brain Surgery}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Brain surgery, or cranial neurosurgery, is any operation performed on the brain or the structures that surround and protect it. It is used to treat a wide range of conditions, including brain tumours, intracranial bleeding and haematomas, aneurysms and vascular malformations, hydrocephalus, drug-resistant epilepsy, some infections, and movement disorders such as Parkinson's disease. Operations range from comparatively minor image-guided procedures -- such as draining fluid, taking a biopsy, or implanting a small electrode -- to major open operations such as the removal of a large tumour. Modern brain surgery increasingly relies on minimally invasive techniques, neuronavigation and intraoperative imaging, and, for selected cases, awake surgery with electrical mapping of the brain to protect language, movement, and other essential functions. The goal is always the same: to treat the underlying condition while preserving as much normal brain function as possible.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Brain surgery is performed for many conditions and across the entire age range, from infants with congenital hydrocephalus to older adults with brain tumours, subdural haematomas, or bleeding after a fall. The number of operations has grown steadily as imaging has improved, as populations have aged, and as surgical and anaesthetic techniques have become safer, allowing procedures that were once considered too risky. Rates vary with geography and access to specialist services, but cranial surgery is concentrated in tertiary and quaternary neurosurgical centres, usually within larger hospitals, because it requires specialised intensive care, imaging, and multidisciplinary teams. Emergency brain surgery for trauma and bleeding is one of the most time-critical operations in all of medicine.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The conditions that call for brain surgery are diverse, and each reflects a different mechanism:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tumours:} benign or malignant growths (meningiomas, gliomas, vestibular schwannomas, and metastases) that compress the brain, raise pressure, or cause seizures, and require removal or biopsy
    \item \textbf{Bleeding:} epidural and subdural haematomas, and intracerebral haemorrhage, which compress brain tissue and dangerously raise intracranial pressure
    \item \textbf{Aneurysms and vascular malformations:} abnormal vessels that can be clipped, coiled, or embolised to prevent rupture and rebleeding
    \item \textbf{Trauma:} depressed skull fractures, penetrating wounds, and severe brain swelling that requires decompression
    \item \textbf{Hydrocephalus:} an accumulation of cerebrospinal fluid treated by a shunt or by endoscopic third ventriculostomy
    \item \textbf{Epilepsy:} removal (resection) of the seizure-generating focus when medicines fail to control seizures
    \item \textbf{Movement disorders:} deep brain stimulation, in which electrodes implanted into specific nuclei modulate abnormal electrical circuits in Parkinson's disease and related conditions
    \item \textbf{Infection:} drainage of brain abscesses and infected fluid collections
    \item \textbf{Facial pain:} microvascular decompression of the trigeminal nerve for severe trigeminal neuralgia
\end{itemize}

The operation removes or repairs the offending lesion, but the surrounding brain remains vulnerable to swelling, bleeding, and reduced blood flow, so surgeons take great care to minimise trauma, and the patient is monitored closely before, during, and after the procedure.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Before surgery, the symptoms are those of the underlying condition -- typically headache, seizures, focal weakness, balance problems, or cognitive change. After surgery, a typical recovery period includes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A tender, swollen surgical wound with surrounding bruising and swelling
    \item Headache and a feeling of pressure inside the head
    \item Drowsiness, which is common in the first day or two and gradually improves
    \item Fatigue and reduced energy, which may persist for several weeks
    \item Mild fever in the first 24--48 hours, which is common and usually benign
    \item Numbness or tingling around the wound site
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
After surgery, the following complications should be distinguished from normal recovery:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Wound infection versus the expected inflammatory response around the incision
    \item A collection of blood or fluid under the wound versus postoperative brain swelling
    \item A cerebrospinal fluid leak (clear fluid from the wound, nose, or ear) versus normal wound drainage
    \item Postoperative seizures versus transient twitching or other non-epileptic movements
    \item A new neurological deficit from swelling or bleeding versus expected postoperative drowsiness
    \item Hydrocephalus versus simple postoperative headache and nausea
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
After brain surgery, contact the surgical team promptly for fever, increasing wound redness or discharge, worsening headache, new weakness, seizures, or confusion. Do not wait for a scheduled follow-up appointment if a new symptom appears.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A decreasing level of consciousness or difficulty waking the person
    \item A seizure or fitting episode
    \item Sudden weakness, numbness, or difficulty speaking
    \item Clear fluid leaking from the wound or nose (possible cerebrospinal fluid leak)
    \item A sudden, severe headache or collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Preoperative imaging:} CT, MRI, and sometimes angiography to define the lesion, plan the surgical approach, and understand the surrounding anatomy
    \item \textbf{Preoperative assessment:} a full history, physical and neurological examination, blood tests, and an anaesthetic review to assess fitness for surgery
    \item \textbf{Informed consent:} a detailed discussion of the diagnosis, the proposed procedure, its risks and benefits, and the alternatives, including non-operative management
    \item \textbf{Intraoperative techniques:} neuronavigation, ultrasound, functional mapping, and neurophysiological monitoring to protect eloquent brain and confirm the extent of resection
    \item \textbf{Postoperative imaging:} CT or MRI shortly after surgery to confirm the operative result and detect early bleeding or swelling
    \item \textbf{Pathology:} histopathological examination of removed tissue to provide a precise diagnosis and guide further treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management spans the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative phases:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgical approaches:} open surgery through a craniotomy, minimally invasive keyhole techniques, stereotactic (image-guided) biopsy, endoscopic procedures, and endovascular treatment through the blood vessels
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} corticosteroids to reduce brain swelling, antiepileptic drugs when appropriate, prophylactic antibiotics, and balanced analgesia
    \item \textbf{Anaesthesia:} general anaesthesia for most operations; some procedures are performed awake with sedation so that language and movement areas can be mapped safely
    \item \textbf{Intensive care:} meticulous control of blood pressure, fluids, and intracranial pressure after major operations
    \item \textbf{Wound care:} keeping the incision clean and dry, and removing sutures or staples as instructed
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and speech and language therapy, together with psychological support where needed
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular clinical reviews, repeat imaging, and long-term surveillance appropriate to the underlying condition
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding into the brain or at the operation site, sometimes requiring further surgery
    \item Infection, including wound infection, meningitis, or a brain abscess
    \item Cerebral swelling causing raised intracranial pressure and worsening neurological symptoms
    \item Seizures, which may require long-term antiepileptic treatment
    \item New or worsened neurological deficits such as weakness, speech problems, or memory difficulty
    \item Cerebrospinal fluid leak, which can act as a portal for infection
    \item Hydrocephalus, which may develop after bleeding or infection
    \item General surgical and anaesthetic risks, including venous thromboembolism and respiratory complications
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Outcomes depend strongly on the reason for surgery. Complete removal of a benign tumour such as a meningioma is often curative, and most people make a good recovery. Emergency surgery for bleeding or severe injury is life-saving, although the extent of neurological recovery varies widely. Surgery for drug-resistant epilepsy and deep brain stimulation for movement disorders can transform quality of life in carefully selected patients. Whatever the indication, recovery takes time: most people feel tired and unwell for several weeks, and full rehabilitation may take months. Long-term follow-up, including surveillance imaging for tumours and review of antiepileptic or other medication, is usually required.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Stop smoking well before surgery, as it increases the risk of anaesthetic, wound, and respiratory complications
    \item Optimise chronic conditions such as diabetes and high blood pressure before the operation
    \item Follow fasting, medication, and admission instructions exactly as given by the surgical team
    \item Allow time to recover and do not rush back to work, driving, or heavy activity
    \item Protect the head and wound from knocks, and avoid straining and heavy lifting as advised
    \item Take prescribed medicines, including antiepileptic drugs and corticosteroids, exactly as directed
    \item Attend all follow-up appointments and report new or worsening symptoms promptly
    \item Observe the driving restrictions advised by your surgeon, since seizures and fatigue can affect safety
\end{itemize}
