\chapter{Laparoscopy and dye test}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A laparoscopy and dye test (also known as a diagnostic laparoscopy and chromotubation) is a minimally invasive surgical procedure performed under general anaesthesia to evaluate a woman's pelvic organs and assess the patency (openness) of the fallopian tubes. It is a key investigation in the diagnostic workup for female infertility. During the procedure, a laparoscope is inserted through a small incision in the umbilicus to visualised the uterus, ovaries, fallopian tubes, and pelvic peritoneal surfaces. Concurrently, a blue dye (typically methylene blue or patent blue V) is injected into the uterine cavity via a catheter placed in the cervix. The surgeon observes the fimbriated ends of the fallopian tubes to determine if the dye flows through and spills into the pelvic cavity, confirming tubal patency.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Infertility is a common clinical concern, affecting approximately one in six couples in Australia and globally. Female-factor infertility accounts for approximately 40\% of cases, with tubal disease and pelvic pathology (such as endometriosis and pelvic adhesions) representing 25\% to 30\% of these diagnoses. The laparoscopy and dye test is primarily performed on reproductive-aged women, typically between the ages of 20 and 45. It is usually indicated after initial, less invasive infertility investigations (such as semen analysis and ovulation tracking) have been completed, or when history and examination suggest pelvic disease.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The main indication for a laparoscopy and dye test is the investigation of subfertility, particularly to detect mechanical barriers to conception or pelvic inflammatory processes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID):} Ascending sexually transmitted infections (such as \textit{Chlamydia trachomatis} or \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae}) cause inflammation of the fallopian tubes (salpingitis), leading to tubal scarring, occlusion, or hydrosalpinx formation.
    \item \textbf{Endometriosis:} The presence of functioning endometrial tissue outside the uterine cavity. This induces a chronic inflammatory response, leading to pelvic pain, adhesions, and distortion of pelvic anatomy, which can impair tubal-ovarian motility and egg capture.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic adhesions:} Fibrous bands of scar tissue resulting from previous abdominal or pelvic surgeries (such as appendicectomy or ovarian cystectomy), ruptured cysts, or severe infections, which mechanically bind pelvic organs.
    \item \textbf{Ectopic pregnancy:} A prior ectopic pregnancy, especially if managed surgically, can scar or damage the affected fallopian tube, reducing bilateral patency.
    \item \textbf{Uterine fibroids:} Subserosal or intramural uterine fibroids can compress the interstitial portion of the fallopian tube, obstructing the passage of sperm or the fertilised oocyte.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Patients undergoing this procedure typically present with primary or secondary infertility, which may be accompanied by symptoms of underlying pelvic pathology.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infertility:} The inability to conceive after 12 months of regular, unprotected sexual intercourse (or six months for women aged 35 years and older).
    \item \textbf{Dysmenorrhoea:} Painful menstrual periods, which may suggest endometriosis or pelvic congestion.
    \item \textbf{Dyspareunia:} Pain during sexual intercourse, particularly deep thrusting, often associated with endometriosis or pelvic adhesions.
    \item \textbf{Chronic pelvic pain:} Persistent, non-cyclical pelvic pain lasting six months or longer.
    \item \textbf{Asymptomatic presentation:} Many patients with tubal occlusion have no history of pelvic pain or infection, and tubal blockage is discovered only during infertility investigations.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before proceeding to a laparoscopy and dye test, other potential causes of subfertility and pelvic pain must be evaluated.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Male factor infertility:} Impaired semen parameters (such as low sperm count, motility, or morphology), which must be ruled out with a semen analysis.
    \item \textbf{Anovulation:} Ovulatory dysfunction, commonly caused by polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), thyroid disorders, or hyperprolactinaemia.
    \item \textbf{Uterine cavity abnormalities:} Endometrial polyps, submucosal fibroids, or congenital uterine septa, which are typically evaluated via pelvic ultrasound, saline-infusion sonography, or hysteroscopy.
    \item \textbf{Non-gynaecological pelvic pain:} Conditions such as irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), interstitial cystitis, or myofascial pelvic floor pain.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should receive detailed instructions regarding normal postoperative symptoms (such as mild vaginal bleeding, blue-stained vaginal discharge, and shoulder-tip pain) and signs of potential complications.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe, or worsening abdominal pain that is not relieved by analgesia.
    \item Shortness of breath or severe chest pain.
    \item High fever (above 38.5 degrees Celsius) with chills or shaking.
    \item Heavy vaginal bleeding, defined as soaking more than one sanitary pad per hour.
    \item Offensive-smelling vaginal discharge.
    \item Inability to urinate or severe pain and burning on urination.
    \item Persistent vomiting and inability to keep fluids down.
    \item Redness, swelling, or pus drainage from the laparoscopic incisions.
    \item Sudden pain, warmth, and swelling in one leg.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The laparoscopy and dye test is itself a diagnostic tool, but a thorough pre-operative workup is essential.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pelvic ultrasound:} Transvaginal ultrasound is performed to assess the uterus for fibroids or adenomyosis, check ovarian reserve (antral follicle count), and identify features of endometriosis (such as endometriomas) or dilated, fluid-filled tubes (hydrosalpinx).
    \item \textbf{STI screening:} Swabs or urine PCR for Chlamydia and Gonorrhoeae must be performed and treated pre-operatively to prevent post-procedure pelvic infection.
    \item \textbf{Ovarian reserve and hormone testing:} Blood tests including Anti-Müllerian Hormone (AMH), FSH, LH, oestradiol, and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) to evaluate endocrine function.
    \item \textbf{Hysterosalpingo-contrast-sonography (HyCoSy):} A less invasive, outpatient ultrasound-based patency test, often performed as a screening test prior to surgical laparoscopy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The procedure is performed in a hospital setting under general anaesthesia.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgical technique:} The patient is placed in the lithotomy position. A speculum is inserted, and a cervical cannula (uterine manipulator) is secured to the cervix. A pneumoperitoneum is created using carbon dioxide gas. The laparoscope is inserted through the umbilicus, and secondary ports are placed in the lower abdomen. The pelvis is systematically inspected. Diluted blue dye is injected through the cervical cannula. The surgeon visualises the fallopian tubes to see if they distend with dye and if the dye spills out of the fimbria into the peritoneal cavity.
    \item \textbf{Concomitant therapeutic intervention:} If pathology is identified during the diagnostic phase, surgical management can often be performed concurrently. This includes excision or ablation of endometrial implants, adhesiolysis (release of scar tissue), ovarian cystectomy, or salpingectomy (removal of a damaged, fluid-filled tube to improve future IVF success rates).
    \item \textbf{Postoperative management:} Most patients are discharged on the same day once they have voided urine, tolerated oral intake, and can mobilise safely. Simple analgesics, such as paracetamol and ibuprofen, are prescribed.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While laparoscopy and dye test is a routine procedure, it is associated with specific surgical risks.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pelvic infection:} Postoperative endometritis or salpingitis, particularly if pre-existing asymptomatic infection was present, which may require oral or intravenous antibiotics.
    \item \textbf{Visceral injury:} Inadvertent puncture or thermal damage to the bowel, bladder, or uterus during instrumentation or dissection.
    \item \textbf{Vascular injury:} Damage to the major retroperitoneal blood vessels or abdominal wall vessels, which can cause significant haemorrhage.
    \item \textbf{Uterine perforation:} Occurs during insertion of the cervical manipulator, usually healing spontaneously without intervention.
    \item \textbf{False negative tubal patency:} Spasm of the fallopian tube musculature due to anaesthetic agents or cold dye can temporarily block dye flow, mimicking tubal occlusion.
    \item \textbf{Port-site complications:} Incisional bleeding, haematoma, superficial wound infection, or herniation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The diagnostic accuracy of a laparoscopy and dye test is high, providing clear documentation of pelvic anatomy and tubal status. If the fallopian tubes are patent and pelvic pathology is absent or treated, spontaneous pregnancy rates are highest in the first six to twelve months following the procedure, possibly due to a flushing effect of the dye through the tubes. If bilateral, irreversible tubal occlusion is identified, the patient can be directed towards assisted reproductive technologies, such as In Vitro Fertilisation (IVF), bypassing the fallopian tubes entirely.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Adherence to postoperative care guidelines supports recovery and minimises the risk of infection.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infection prevention:} Avoid using tampons, taking baths, swimming, or engaging in sexual intercourse for at least one to two weeks, or until any vaginal bleeding and discharge have fully resolved. Use sanitary pads for vaginal bleeding or blue dye discharge.
    \item \textbf{Wound care:} Keep incisions clean and dry. Gently pat the incisions dry after showering. Do not scrub the wounds or remove the skin glue or steri-strips prematurely.
    \item \textbf{Managing gas discomfort:} Shoulder tip pain is caused by carbon dioxide irritating the diaphragmatic nerve. This can be managed by resting, applying heat packs to the shoulder, and walking gently.
    \item \textbf{Rest and recovery:} Plan to take three to seven days off work depending on the extent of surgical intervention (e.g., whether endometriosis was excised). Avoid strenuous exercise and heavy lifting for at least two weeks.
\end{itemize}
