\chapter{Chemicals Inhaled}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Inhaling chemicals means breathing in gases, fumes, vapours, dusts, or aerosols that irritate or poison the respiratory tract and the body. Many everyday and occupational substances can be harmful when inhaled, including cleaning products, solvents, chlorine, ammonia, pesticides, combustion fumes, and smoke from fires. The airways respond to chemical irritation with coughing, wheezing, and inflammation, and some inhaled agents are absorbed into the bloodstream where they cause systemic poisoning. The severity of injury depends on the chemical, its concentration, the duration of exposure, and the person's underlying lung health. Injuries range from mild throat irritation to chemical pneumonitis, pulmonary oedema, and life-threatening respiratory failure.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Inhalation of chemicals is a common cause of poisoning and respiratory injury. Household exposures frequently occur when cleaning products are mixed -- for example, bleach combined with acidic products releases chlorine gas -- or used in poorly ventilated spaces. Occupational exposure accounts for a substantial burden, particularly in agriculture, industry, construction, and firefighting, where workers inhale dusts, solvents, welding fumes, and combustion products. Accidental exposures in children are also seen with household products. Carbon monoxide poisoning, often from faulty heaters or exhausts, is a leading cause of inhalation injury and death worldwide. Many chemical inhalation injuries are preventable with ventilation, safe storage, and appropriate respiratory protection.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Irritant gases:} chlorine, ammonia, and nitrogen dioxide directly injure the lining of the airways and can cause delayed fluid accumulation in the lungs
    \item \textbf{Asphyxiants:} carbon monoxide and cyanide displace oxygen or block its use by tissues, causing cellular suffocation
    \item \textbf{Organic solvents and volatile substances:} inhaled for industrial use or deliberately abused, they depress the central nervous system and can damage the lungs
    \item \textbf{Pesticides and fumigants:} many are absorbed through the lungs and cause systemic poisoning
    \item \textbf{Dusts and fibres:} chronic inhalation of silica, asbestos, and coal dust causes progressive scarring and fibrosis of the lungs
    \item \textbf{Smoke inhalation:} a complex mixture of hot gases, carbon monoxide, cyanide, and irritant particles injures the whole airway from the throat to the deep lung
    \item \textbf{Mechanism of injury:} chemicals damage airway cells directly, provoke an intense inflammatory response, and may cause swelling, bronchospasm, and leakage of fluid into the alveoli (pulmonary oedema)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cough, hoarseness, sore throat, and irritation of the nose and eyes
    \item Chest tightness, wheezing, and breathlessness
    \item Headache, nausea, dizziness, and confusion -- features that suggest systemic absorption or carbon monoxide poisoning
    \item Rapid breathing, low oxygen levels, and bluish lips or skin in severe cases
    \item Swelling of the face, burns to the lips and mouth, and soot in the airway after smoke inhalation
    \item Symptoms may be delayed for several hours after exposure to some gases, so observation is important
    \item Chronic effects of repeated exposure include persistent cough, bronchitis, and progressive breathlessness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Asthma and allergic bronchoconstriction
    \item Acute infection such as pneumonia, bronchitis, or COVID-19
    \item Pulmonary embolism, in sudden unexplained breathlessness
    \item Heart failure and pulmonary oedema from cardiac causes
    \item Anxiety and hyperventilation
    \item Overdose of drugs or alcohol causing reduced breathing
    \item Exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who has inhaled a chemical and develops a persistent cough, chest tightness, breathlessness, headache, or confusion should be assessed by a doctor. Even if symptoms are mild, some exposures -- especially to gases such as nitrogen dioxide or smoke -- can cause serious lung problems that appear hours later, so medical review is recommended.

\medskip
\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe difficulty breathing, wheezing, or a choking sensation
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, fainting, or a seizure after exposure
    \item Burns to the face, soot in the mouth, or a hoarse voice after smoke or chemical inhalation
    \item Known or suspected carbon monoxide exposure, especially with headache, nausea, or collapse
    \item A child who has inhaled a chemical or smoke
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the chemical involved, its concentration, the duration and circumstances of exposure, ventilation, and the presence of others affected
    \item \textbf{Examination:} assessment of breathing rate, oxygen saturation, breath sounds, and signs of respiratory distress or burns to the face and airway
    \item \textbf{Pulse oximetry and blood gases:} to measure oxygen levels and detect gas-exchange failure
    \item \textbf{Carbon monoxide measurement:} a blood test (carboxyhaemoglobin) confirms carbon monoxide exposure
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} may be normal immediately after exposure but can show pulmonary oedema or pneumonitis, sometimes hours later
    \item \textbf{Spirometry and lung function tests:} useful for assessment and monitoring of chronic occupational exposure
    \item \textbf{Poisoning information:} the local poisons information service provides specific guidance for the chemical involved
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immediate action:} move the person to fresh air, away from the source of the chemical
    \item \textbf{Oxygen therapy:} high-flow oxygen is given to support breathing and is the key treatment for carbon monoxide poisoning
    \item \textbf{Observation:} hospital monitoring for several hours to detect delayed lung injury
    \item \textbf{Bronchodilators:} inhaled medicines such as salbutamol to relieve wheezing and bronchospasm
    \item \textbf{Corticosteroids:} may be given to reduce airway inflammation in significant exposures
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} intravenous fluids, monitoring, and, in severe cases, ventilatory support
    \item \textbf{Antidotes:} specific treatments for particular poisons, such as oxygen for carbon monoxide and appropriate antidotes for cyanide
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a respiratory specialist or burns centre for severe inhalation injury
    \item \textbf{Occupational follow-up:} lung function monitoring and workplace review for those with workplace exposures
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chemical pneumonitis -- inflammation of the lungs from the irritant chemical
    \item Acute pulmonary oedema and respiratory failure
    \item Airway burns and swelling causing obstruction, particularly after smoke inhalation
    \item Carbon monoxide poisoning leading to brain injury in severe cases
    \item Chronic lung disease, including bronchitis and airway hyper-responsiveness, after major or repeated exposure
    \item Pulmonary fibrosis with progressive scarring after prolonged occupational exposure to dusts and fumes
    \item Secondary pneumonia due to damaged airways and impaired clearance
    \item Death in the most severe exposures
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people who inhale a chemical in a household setting recover fully within days to a few weeks, especially when the exposure is brief and treatment with fresh air and oxygen begins promptly. More severe exposures, particularly to smoke, concentrated gases, or industrial chemicals, can cause permanent lung damage and require prolonged treatment. Carbon monoxide poisoning carries a risk of lasting neurological problems in severe cases. With chronic occupational exposure, stopping exposure and using respiratory protection can slow or halt progression of lung disease. The outcome depends on the agent, the dose, and the speed of treatment, and should be discussed with the treating physician.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use cleaning products and chemicals in well-ventilated areas, and never mix bleach with other products
    \item Read and follow the safety instructions and warning labels on all chemical products
    \item Wear appropriate respiratory protection, such as a fitted mask or respirator, when working with fumes, dusts, or solvents
    \item Store chemicals safely and out of reach of children, in their original containers
    \item Install carbon monoxide detectors in the home and have heaters, stoves, and exhausts serviced regularly
    \item Never run engines or generators indoors or in enclosed spaces
    \item At work, follow hazardous-substances procedures and use mandated protective equipment
    \item If exposed to a chemical, move to fresh air immediately and seek advice promptly
    \item Attend workplace health surveillance where occupational exposure occurs
\end{itemize}
