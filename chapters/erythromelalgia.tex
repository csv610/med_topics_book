\chapter{Erythromelalgia}
\index{Erythromelalgia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Erythromelalgia is a rare condition in which the extremities, most often the feet, become red, hot, and intensely painful. The name comes from the Greek words for red, limb, and pain. Attacks are typically triggered by heat and warmth, and are relieved by cooling the affected part. The condition can be primary, with a genetic cause, or secondary to conditions such as blood disorders and certain medicines. Erythromelalgia significantly affects quality of life, but a combination of trigger avoidance, cooling, and medicines helps most people.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Erythromelalgia is rare, affecting perhaps a few people per 100,000, and it is more common in women. It can begin at any age: early-onset cases are often genetic, while adult-onset cases are more often associated with an underlying condition. Because it is rare and poorly recognised, diagnosis is often delayed, and people may suffer for years before the condition is identified.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Erythromelalgia results from abnormal function of the small blood vessels and nerves of the extremities.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary erythromelalgia:} caused by mutations in sodium channel genes that affect pain-signalling nerves; there may be a family history
    \item \textbf{Secondary erythromelalgia:} associated with blood disorders such as polycythaemia and thrombocythaemia, autoimmune disease, diabetes, and certain medicines
    \item \textbf{Vascular mechanism:} the small arteries dilate excessively while blood flow in the smallest vessels is reduced, causing heat, redness, and pain
    \item \textbf{Nerve mechanism:} the pain-signalling nerves are overactive, amplifying the sensation of pain
    \item \textbf{Triggers:} warmth, exercise, and even clothing can trigger attacks because heat worsens the circulation abnormality
    \item \textbf{Relief with cold:} cooling constricts the vessels and reduces the pain, which is a diagnostic feature
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Attacks have a distinctive pattern.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Burning pain in the feet, and less often the hands, ears, or face
    \item The affected area becomes red, hot, and swollen
    \item Attacks are triggered by warmth, exercise, pressure, and sometimes bed covers at night
    \item Relief with cooling, cold water, or elevation
    \item Attacks last from minutes to hours
    \item Between attacks the skin looks normal
    \item Severe attacks can interfere with walking, sleeping, and daily life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions cause similar symptoms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Peripheral neuropathy, including diabetic neuropathy
    \item Raynaud's phenomenon, which causes cold, white fingers rather than heat
    \item Cellulitis and other infections, with fever and spreading redness
    \item Chronic regional pain syndrome
    \item Venous insufficiency and other causes of leg pain
    \item Burning feet from vitamin deficiency or thyroid disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with recurrent redness, heat, and pain in the extremities should be assessed by a GP and referred to a specialist, as the condition can be diagnosed and treated. Because secondary erythromelalgia can signal a blood disorder, investigation is important.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe pain with a red, hot limb and fever, which may indicate a serious infection
    \item Signs of a blood clot
    \item Collapse or severe illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is based on the history and examination.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the classic pattern of attacks, triggers, and relief with cooling
    \item \textbf{Examination:} observation of the affected part during an attack
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count and tests for associated conditions
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} for the known mutations in early-onset cases
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a neurologist, vascular specialist, or pain specialist
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management combines trigger avoidance and medicines.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cooling:} the most effective immediate measure, including cool water, fans, and avoiding heat
    \item \textbf{Trigger avoidance:} avoiding warm environments, thick socks, and excessive exercise
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} medicines that reduce nerve pain and vascular symptoms, including some antidepressants, anti-epileptics, and other agents
    \item \textbf{Treating the underlying cause:} treatment of blood disorders or the associated condition
    \item \textbf{Pain management:} a multidisciplinary approach for severe pain
    \item \textbf{Avoiding harm:} cooling should not be excessive, as prolonged ice application can damage the skin
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe chronic pain and disability
    \item Sleep disturbance and fatigue
    \item Depression and anxiety
    \item Skin damage from excessive cooling
    \item The complications of any underlying blood disorder
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Erythromelalgia is a chronic condition without a cure, but most people achieve meaningful improvement with a combination of cooling, trigger avoidance, and medicines. The course varies: some people have mild, occasional attacks, while others have severe daily pain. Early diagnosis and treatment of any underlying condition improve outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Avoid triggers such as heat, tight footwear, and heavy exertion
    \item Keep a diary of attacks to identify triggers
    \item Use fans and cool environments, and cool the feet with cool water
    \item Wear loose, breathable shoes and socks
    \item Avoid prolonged ice application
    \item Take medicines as prescribed and discuss side effects
    \item Seek specialist pain management for severe symptoms
\end{itemize}
