\chapter{Cosmetic Surgery}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cosmetic surgery is surgery performed primarily to improve a person's appearance. It differs from reconstructive surgery, which restores function or corrects disfigurement from injury, disease, or birth differences. Common cosmetic operations include breast augmentation, breast reduction, liposuction, abdominoplasty (tummy tuck), rhinoplasty (nose reshaping), eyelid surgery, and facelift. Cosmetic surgery is a major decision: it carries the risks of any operation, including anaesthesia, bleeding, infection, and scarring, and results depend heavily on the skill of the surgeon and the realism of expectations. In Australia, cosmetic surgery is regulated, and only qualified surgeons should perform it.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cosmetic surgery is performed in large numbers worldwide, and in Australia tens of thousands of procedures occur each year. Breast augmentation, liposuction, and eyelid surgery are among the most common. Demand has grown steadily, driven by ageing populations, weight loss surgery, social media, and changing attitudes. The great majority of patients are women, though male cosmetic surgery is increasing. There has also been a rise in complications and complaints, particularly after procedures performed by practitioners without appropriate surgical qualifications, prompting tighter regulation.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
People choose cosmetic surgery for a wide range of reasons.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ageing:} sagging skin, volume loss, and wrinkles treated with facelift, eyelid, and brow surgery
    \item \textbf{Body contour:} dissatisfaction with breast size or shape, abdominal skin, or localised fat
    \item \textbf{Post-pregnancy changes:} abdominal skin laxity and breast changes after childbirth
    \item \textbf{Massive weight loss:} excess skin after bariatric surgery or major weight loss
    \item \textbf{Facial features:} rhinoplasty and chin surgery to alter proportions
    \item \textbf{Psychological factors:} self-esteem, body image, and social pressure
    \item \textbf{Body dysmorphic disorder:} a mental health condition in which surgery rarely improves wellbeing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
A cosmetic surgery consultation centres on the person's appearance concerns and health.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A specific feature the person wishes to change
    \item Skin laxity, excess fat, or volume loss in the area of concern
    \item Dissatisfaction that persists despite diet, exercise, or non-surgical treatment
    \item Realistic or unrealistic expectations of what surgery can achieve
    \item Underlying health conditions that affect surgical safety
    \item A history of multiple previous procedures or ongoing dissatisfaction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Careful assessment determines whether surgery is appropriate and safe.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Non-surgical alternatives: injectables, lasers, and skin treatments
    \item Body weight issues that should be addressed before surgery
    \item Medical conditions that raise surgical risk, such as diabetes, heart disease, or bleeding disorders
    \item Body image concerns versus body dysmorphic disorder
    \item Mental health conditions such as depression or anxiety
    \item Functional problems, such as back pain from large breasts, where surgery may be partly reconstructive
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Before proceeding, have a full consultation with a qualified surgeon in an accredited facility, including a discussion of risks and expected outcomes. Seek urgent care for any complication after surgery.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, chest pain, or a sudden painful swollen leg after surgery (possible blood clot)
    \item Severe bleeding or a wound that will not stop bleeding
    \item High fever, spreading redness, or signs of a serious infection
    \item Collapse, confusion, or an allergic reaction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Pre-operative assessment is thorough and includes the following.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Medical history and examination:} general health, medicines, allergies, and examination of the area to be treated
    \item \textbf{Body mass index and weight stability:} surgery is safer at a stable, moderate weight
    \item \textbf{Blood tests and ECG:} as needed for fitness for anaesthesia
    \item \textbf{Photography:} standardised images for planning and comparison
    \item \textbf{Psychological assessment:} discussion of motivation, expectations, and mental health
    \item \textbf{Informed consent:} clear explanation of the operation, scars, recovery, risks, and realistic results
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management depends on the procedure, which may include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Breast surgery:} augmentation with implants, reduction, or uplift
    \item \textbf{Body contouring:} liposuction and abdominoplasty for fat and skin excess
    \item \textbf{Facial surgery:} rhinoplasty, blepharoplasty (eyelids), and facelift
    \item \textbf{Anaesthesia:} general anaesthetic or sedation in an accredited facility
    \item \textbf{Recovery:} dressings, drains, pain relief, and gradual return to activity
    \item \textbf{Scar management:} silicone products and sun protection
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular review, with revision surgery available where needed
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} counselling for body image concerns when appropriate
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding, haematoma, and the need for re-operation
    \item Infection and wound healing problems
    \item Scarring, including thick or poor-quality scars
    \item Anaesthetic complications
    \item Blood clots in the legs or lungs
    \item Asymmetry, contour irregularities, or an unnatural result
    \item Implant problems, including rupture, capsular contracture, and the need for further surgery
    \item Numbness or altered sensation
    \item Psychological distress and dissatisfaction with results
    \item Serious harm from unqualified practitioners or non-accredited facilities
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people who choose cosmetic surgery with realistic expectations are satisfied with the results, and the effects of surgical procedures are long-lasting compared with non-surgical treatments. Full healing takes weeks to months, and final results may take a year to be seen. Satisfaction depends on the skill of the surgeon, the quality of aftercare, and the person's expectations. Significant weight change or pregnancy can alter results, and some procedures need revision later. People with body dysmorphic disorder usually remain dissatisfied and should be offered psychological treatment rather than surgery.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Choose a specialist plastic or cosmetic surgeon registered with AHPRA and working in an accredited facility
    \item Ask about the surgeon's qualifications, experience, and hospital privileges
    \item Have a detailed consultation covering costs, risks, scars, and realistic outcomes
    \item Stop smoking and reach a stable weight before surgery
    \item Follow all pre- and post-operative instructions, including compression garments and wound care
    \item Attend follow-up appointments and report problems early
    \item Protect scars from the sun and manage them as advised
    \item Be cautious about overseas surgery and cheap offers
\end{itemize}
