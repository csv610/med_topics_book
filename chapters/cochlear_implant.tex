\chapter{Cochlear implant}
\index{Cochlear implant}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Educational information; seek professional advice for individual care.}

\section*{What it is}
A cochlear implant is an electronic device for some people with severe or profound hearing loss who receive limited benefit from hearing aids. An external microphone and processor send signals to an implanted component, which stimulates the auditory nerve. It does not restore normal hearing, and the sound is different from natural hearing.

\section*{Assessment}
An implant team usually reviews hearing tests, hearing-aid benefit, communication needs, medical history, imaging, and the person’s goals. For children, assessment also considers language development, educational needs, family preferences, and access to communication support. Eligibility and funding rules vary by health system.

\section*{Treatment and rehabilitation}
Implantation requires surgery followed by activation, programming, and practice. Audiologists, speech and language professionals, teachers, and other specialists may support rehabilitation. Benefit varies; some people use spoken language, sign language, lip-reading, or a combination. The decision should be shared and should include alternatives such as hearing aids and communication services.

\section*{Risks and practical care}
Possible risks include infection, dizziness, changes in taste, facial-nerve injury, device problems, and limited benefit. Follow the implant team’s advice about wound care, water, devices, travel, and medical procedures. Ask before MRI or other procedures because device-specific conditions may apply.

\section*{When to seek medical advice}
Contact the implant team for persistent pain, redness, swelling, drainage, sudden change in hearing, repeated dizziness, or device failure. Seek urgent care for severe illness, rapidly worsening swelling, facial weakness, or breathing difficulty.
