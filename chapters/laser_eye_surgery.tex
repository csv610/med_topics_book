\chapter{Laser eye surgery}
\index{Laser eye surgery}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Laser eye surgery, also known as refractive surgery, refers to a group of minimally invasive surgical procedures that use specialised lasers to reshape the cornea (the clear front surface of the eye) to correct refractive errors, including myopia (nearsightedness), hyperopia (farsightedness), and astigmatism. By altering the curvature of the cornea, laser eye surgery changes how light is focused onto the retina, thereby reducing or eliminating the patient's reliance on prescription eyeglasses or contact lenses. The most common techniques include Laser-Assisted In Situ Keratomileusis (LASIK), Photorefractive Keratectomy (PRK), and Small Incision Lenticule Extraction (SMILE). Refractive surgery is widely performed and boasts high patient satisfaction rates, though candidates must undergo strict screening to ensure corneal health and stability of vision.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Millions of laser eye surgeries are performed worldwide each year, representing one of the most common elective procedures globally. In many countries, thousands of individuals undergo laser refractive surgery annually. The majority of patients are aged between 20 and 45 years, a demographic where refractive errors are stable and visual demands for work or lifestyle are high. Refractive errors affect a significant portion of the global population, with myopia prevalence rising rapidly, particularly in urban areas and developed nations. The adoption of specific techniques varies, with LASIK and SMILE being the most widely performed procedures due to rapid visual recovery and minimal postoperative discomfort.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Refractive errors arise from a mismatch between the focal length of the eye's optical system and the physical length of the eyeball. Laser eye surgery corrects this mismatch by physically altering corneal refractive power. The procedures target:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Myopia (nearsightedness):} The eyeball is too long or the cornea is too curved, causing light to focus in front of the retina. Laser ablation flattens the central cornea to shift the focal point backward.
    \item \textbf{Hyperopia (farsightedness):} The eyeball is too short or the cornea is too flat, causing light to focus behind the retina. Laser ablation steepens the central cornea to shift the focal point forward.
    \item \textbf{Astigmatism:} An irregularly shaped cornea (resembling a rugby ball instead of a soccer ball) causes light to focus at multiple points. The laser reshapes the asymmetrical corneal meridians to create a uniform surface.
    \item \textbf{Corneal biomechanics:} Procedures differ in how they access the corneal stroma. LASIK involves creating a thin flap using a femtosecond laser, PRK removes the outer epithelium before ablation, and SMILE dissects a microscopic lenticule within the stroma which is removed through a small incision.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Candidates presenting for refractive surgery typically present with symptoms related to corrected or uncorrected refractive errors. Key clinical features include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reduced uncorrected visual acuity:} Inability to see distant or near objects clearly without corrective lenses.
    \item \textbf{Asthenopia (eye strain):} Fatigue, aching, or tension around the eyes, particularly after prolonged reading or screen use.
    \item \textbf{Headaches:} Chronic tension headaches associated with constant squinting or visual accommodation strain.
    \item \textbf{Contact lens intolerance:} Dryness, irritation, or recurrent corneal infections associated with long-term contact lens wear.
    \item \textbf{Visual distortion:} Monocular diplopia, glare, or ghosting of images due to high astigmatism.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before proceeding to surgery, other causes of visual impairment or contraindications to refractive surgery must be excluded:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Keratoconus:} A progressive ectatic corneal disorder characterised by corneal thinning and steepening; an absolute contraindication for laser eye surgery.
    \item \textbf{Cataracts:} Opacification of the crystalline lens causing progressive visual decline, which requires cataract extraction rather than refractive surgery.
    \item \textbf{Dry eye syndrome:} Severe pre-existing dry eyes must be identified and managed, as laser surgery can temporarily or permanently exacerbate dry eye symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Unstable refraction:} Progressive changes in prescription due to age, pregnancy, or uncontrolled systemic conditions (e.g. diabetes).
    \item \textbf{Ocular surface disease:} Active blepharitis, herpes simplex keratitis, or uveitis that must be resolved before intervention.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients considering refractive surgery should consult a qualified ophthalmologist for a comprehensive evaluation. Following surgery, patients must monitor their recovery closely.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sudden, severe loss of vision:} Rapid onset of blindness or severe visual decline postoperatively.
    \item \textbf{Trauma to the eye:} Accidental impact, chemical exposure, or dislocation of the corneal flap.
    \item \textbf{Signs of systemic shock:} Severe headache, dizziness, or collapse associated with severe eye pain.
\end{itemize}
Other reasons to contact the surgeon immediately include worsening eye pain, persistent discharge, a gritty feeling that worsens, or sudden changes in vision (such as halos, glare, or shadows).

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A meticulous pre-operative assessment is essential to determine patient suitability and safety:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Corneal topography/tomography:} Maps the anterior and posterior corneal surfaces to screen for subclinical ectasia (keratoconus) and measure corneal curvature.
    \item \textbf{Pachymetry:} Measures corneal thickness to ensure that the remaining stromal bed after ablation is thick enough to maintain structural integrity.
    \item \textbf{Dry eye assessment:} Includes tear breakup time (TBUT) and Schirmer's test to quantify tear production and quality.
    \item \textbf{Pupillometry:} Measures pupil size in dim light to assess the risk of postoperative night vision disturbances (glare and halos).
    \item \textbf{Cycloplegic refraction:} Measures the true refractive error by temporarily paralysing the eye's focusing muscles to prevent accommodation during testing.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management involves patient selection, surgical execution, and diligent postoperative care:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgical procedure:} Performed under local anaesthetic eye drops. An excimer or femtosecond laser is programmed with the patient's corneal mapping data. In LASIK, a corneal flap is folded back, the underlying stroma is ablated, and the flap is replaced. In PRK, the epithelium is brushed away, the stroma ablated, and a bandage contact lens is placed. In SMILE, a small lenticule is cut and removed.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological management:} Postoperative eye drops are critical and include topical antibiotics to prevent infection, topical corticosteroids to modulate healing, and preservative-free artificial tears for lubrication.
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle and supportive care:} Rest immediately post-procedure, wearing protective eye shields while sleeping for the first week, and avoiding swimming, eye makeup, and rubbing the eyes for several weeks.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Potential complications and adverse events include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dry eye syndrome:} Reduced tear production, which is common postoperatively and usually resolves within 3 to 6 months but can become chronic.
    \item \textbf{Visual aberrations:} Glare, halos, starbursts, and reduced contrast sensitivity, particularly in low-light environments.
    \item \textbf{Under- or over-correction:} Inaccurate ablation depth requiring a secondary enhancement procedure.
    \item \textbf{Flap complications:} In LASIK, flap displacement, wrinkles (striae), epithelial ingrowth under the flap, or diffuse lamellar keratitis (DLK).
    \item \textbf{Corneal ectasia:} A rare but serious progressive bulging and thinning of the cornea due to biomechanical instability.
    \item \textbf{Infectious keratitis:} Bacterial or viral infection of the cornea, a rare emergency that can threaten vision.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for the vast majority of patients undergoing laser eye surgery is excellent. Over 95\% of patients achieve 20/20 uncorrected visual acuity or better and report high levels of satisfaction. Visual recovery is rapid in LASIK and SMILE, with many patients returning to work and driving within 24 to 48 hours. Recovery is slower in PRK, taking up to a week for the epithelial layer to heal and several weeks for visual acuity to stabilise. Long-term results are highly stable, though age-related presbyopia (loss of near vision) will still occur naturally around age 40 to 45.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
While refractive errors cannot be prevented, patients can minimise surgical risks and optimise healing through strict self-care:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Avoid rubbing the eyes:} Crucial post-LASIK to prevent flap displacement or epithelial damage.
    \item \textbf{Use prescribed eye drops:} Adhere strictly to the schedule for antibiotic and steroid drops, even if the eyes feel normal.
    \item \textbf{Wear protective eyewear:} Wear UV-blocking sunglasses outdoors and eye shields at night during the early recovery phase.
    \item \textbf{Avoid water exposure:} Stay out of swimming pools, hot tubs, and natural bodies of water for at least two weeks to reduce infection risks.
    \item \textbf{Limit screen time:} Reduce digital screen use in the first few days to minimise eye strain and dryness.
\end{itemize}
