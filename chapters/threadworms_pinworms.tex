\section*{Definition and Overview}
Threadworms, also called pinworms, are small white intestinal parasites (Enterobius vermicularis) that commonly infect children and, through household spread, other family members. Adult worms live in the large bowel; at night, females migrate to the anus to lay eggs on the surrounding skin, causing intense itch. Scratching transfers eggs to fingers, bedding, and surfaces, and swallowing eggs continues the cycle. Threadworms do not usually cause serious illness in many countries but create marked discomfort, sleep disturbance, and family frustration. Diagnosis is often clinical or by the ``sticky tape'' test, and treatment combines medicine with strict hygiene for the whole household. This chapter focuses on recognition, treatment, and breaking reinfection cycles.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Enterobiasis is one of the most common helminth infections in temperate countries, including some countries. Prevalence is highest in preschool and primary-school children, especially where children share rooms, toys, and bathrooms. Institutional settings such as childcare centres facilitate spread. Adults are infected less often but readily acquire worms from children at home. There is little socioeconomic exclusivity---threadworms appear in clean, well-resourced households because eggs are tiny, numerous, and easily transferred. Reinfection is common if hygiene measures are incomplete. Unlike some tropical worms, pinworms are not a sign of travel; they are endemic locally.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ingestion of eggs:} from fingers, food, dust, or shared items contaminated with eggs shed around the anus
    \item \textbf{Maturation in the gut:} larvae hatch and develop into adults in the intestines over several weeks
    \item \textbf{Nocturnal egg-laying:} gravid females exit the anus at night, causing pruritus ani
    \item \textbf{Auto-infection:} scratching then hand-to-mouth behaviour reintroduces eggs
    \item \textbf{Retroinfection (less common):} larvae hatching at the anus may migrate back into the bowel
    \item \textbf{Egg resilience:} eggs can survive for one to two weeks on surfaces, pyjamas, and carpet under the right conditions
    \item \textbf{Not usually invasive:} ectopic migration to the female genital tract is uncommon but can cause vaginal itch or discharge
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Perianal itch:} especially at night; the hallmark symptom
    \item \textbf{Sleep disturbance and irritability:} from itch and scratching
    \item \textbf{Visible worms:} thin, white, thread-like worms two to thirteen millimetres long may be seen near the anus or in stools at night
    \item \textbf{Secondary skin irritation:} excoriation and sometimes bacterial skin infection from scratching
    \item \textbf{Appetite or behaviour change:} non-specific; severe systemic illness is not typical
    \item \textbf{Vaginal itch in girls:} from egg deposition or worm migration
    \item \textbf{Asymptomatic carriage:} some infected children show no obvious itch yet still shed eggs
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Non-specific perianal dermatitis and eczema:} common in toddlers; less strictly nocturnal
    \item \textbf{Candidal nappy rash or intertrigo:} different appearance and distribution
    \item \textbf{Haemorrhoids or fissure in older children and adults:} pain more than itch
    \item \textbf{Other worms:} roundworm, hookworm, and tapeworm are less common causes of pure nocturnal perianal itch in urban the affected region
    \item \textbf{Vulvovaginitis from other causes:} streptococcal infection, foreign body, or hygiene issues
    \item \textbf{Psychogenic itch or habit:} consider if tape tests repeatedly negative and examination normal
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a pharmacist or GP when perianal itch suggests threadworms, when worms are seen, or when over-the-counter treatment fails. Seek medical advice before treating infants, pregnant people, or those with significant other illness.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, or signs of acute abdomen (not typical of simple threadworm)
    \item Allergic reaction to worming medicine with breathing difficulty or facial swelling
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} nocturnal itch, household cases, childcare attendance
    \item \textbf{Visual inspection:} parent may check anus with a torch a few hours after sleep onset
    \item \textbf{Sticky tape test:} clear tape applied to perianal skin in the morning before washing, then examined microscopically for eggs; several days' samples improve yield
    \item \textbf{Stool microscopy:} less sensitive for pinworm eggs than tape test
    \item \textbf{Empiric treatment:} often reasonable in clear-cut household presentations without tests
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anthelmintic medicine:} mebendazole or pyrantel as advised by pharmacist or GP; a second dose after two weeks is commonly recommended to kill worms from hatched eggs
    \item \textbf{Treat the household:} all members usually treated on the same day even if asymptomatic
    \item \textbf{Hygiene bundle on treatment day:} wash sleepwear, sheets, and towels in hot water; vacuum and damp-dust; scrub bathroom surfaces
    \item \textbf{Morning showering:} wash around the anus each morning to remove eggs
    \item \textbf{Hand hygiene:} soap and water after toilet, before food, and after nappy changes; keep nails short and clean
    \item \textbf{Discourage nail-biting and finger-sucking:} temporarily where possible
    \item \textbf{Underwear:} change daily; some families use close-fitting pants at night to reduce egg scatter
    \item \textbf{School and childcare:} children can usually return after treatment starts; follow local exclusion rules if any
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reinfection cycles:} the main practical complication
    \item \textbf{Secondary bacterial skin infection:} from scratching
    \item \textbf{Sleep loss and family stress:} cumulative irritation
    \item \textbf{Rare ectopic disease:} appendicitis has been associated occasionally; genital tract irritation in girls
    \item \textbf{Medicine side effects:} usually mild gastrointestinal upset; allergy uncommon
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Threadworms are curable. Symptoms often improve within days of treatment, but reinfection can make the problem seem endless until hygiene and repeat dosing are thorough. Long-term health damage is rare. Parents should expect possible recurrence when children re-enter group settings and should retreat promptly rather than feeling blamed.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Teach effective handwashing with soap, especially after toilet use and before eating
    \item Keep fingernails short; discourage scratching the bottom
    \item Wash hands after handling shared toys in outbreaks at home
    \item Avoid sharing towels during an active household episode
    \item Launder bedding regularly during treatment weeks
    \item Clean toilet seats and flush handles frequently
    \item Seek pharmacist advice early rather than waiting months of itch
\end{itemize}
