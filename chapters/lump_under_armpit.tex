\chapter{Lump under armpit}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A lump under the armpit (axillary mass) refers to a localised swelling or bump in the axilla, which can arise from skin, subcutaneous tissue, lymph nodes, or breast tissue extension. The axilla contains a complex network of lymph nodes, sweat glands (apocrine glands), hair follicles, blood vessels, nerves, and fat. Axillary lumps are common clinical findings and can be benign or malignant. Most lumps are benign and result from localised infections, inflamed hair follicles, lipomas, or fibroadenomas. However, because the axillary lymph nodes drain the breast, upper chest, and arms, an axillary lump can also be the first sign of a serious underlying condition, such as breast cancer, lymphoma, or systemic infection. A thorough evaluation by a healthcare provider is essential to determine the cause and guide appropriate management.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Axillary lumps can occur in individuals of any age, sex, or background, but their clinical significance and likely causes vary by demographic. In young adults, benign infectious or inflammatory causes, such as hidradenitis suppurativa, folliculitis, or reactive lymphadenopathy due to localised viral or bacterial infections, are highly prevalent. In older adults, particularly women, the likelihood of a malignant cause increases significantly. Breast cancer metastases to axillary lymph nodes are a primary concern; in Australia, breast cancer is the most common cancer in women, and routine mammography screening helps identify early involvement. Lymphomas, including Hodgkin and non-Hodgkin lymphoma, also present with axillary lymphadenopathy across a bimodal age distribution. Global and local travel histories are relevant, as certain regional infections (such as cat scratch disease, tuberculosis, or brucellosis) can present with isolated axillary lymph node swelling.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The aetiologies of an axillary lump are diverse and can be classified based on the tissue of origin.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reactive lymphadenopathy:} Immune response in axillary lymph nodes draining an infection of the hand, arm, breast, or chest wall (e.g., bacterial cellulitis, viral infections, or cat scratch disease caused by \textit{Bartonella henselae}).
    \item \textbf{Infections of skin structures:} Folliculitis, furuncles (boils), or carbuncles arising from bacterial infection (most commonly \textit{Staphylococcus aureus}) of axillary hair follicles.
    \item \textbf{Hidradenitis suppurativa:} A chronic inflammatory skin condition characterised by recurrent, painful nodules, abscesses, and sinus tracts in areas containing apocrine sweat glands, such as the axillae.
    \item \textbf{Malignancy:} Primary lymphoma arising within the lymphatic system, or metastatic deposition of malignant cells from solid tumours, most commonly breast cancer, but also lung cancer, thyroid cancer, or melanoma.
    \item \textbf{Ectopic breast tissue:} Developmental tissue variant where breast tissue extends into the axilla (axillary tail of Spence), which can undergo cyclical hormonal changes, swelling, or benign fibroadenoma formation.
    \item \textbf{Subcutaneous benign masses:} Lipomas (benign fatty tumours) or epidermoid cysts (blocked sebaceous glands) presenting as painless, mobile lumps.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features of an axillary lump vary widely depending on the underlying cause. Detailed physical characteristics are key diagnostic clues.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Painful, tender, warm, and red lumps are characteristic of infectious or inflammatory processes, such as boils, abscesses, or reactive lymphadenopathy.
    \item Hard, firm, fixed (immobile), and painless lumps are concerning features that suggest a malignant origin, such as metastatic cancer or lymphoma.
    \item Multiple, rubbery, and painless swollen lymph nodes in the axilla and other regions (e.g., neck, groin) are typical of lymphoma.
    \item Cyclical swelling and tenderness, coinciding with the menstrual cycle, suggest ectopic breast tissue.
    \item Recurrent, deeply situated, painful nodules that rupture and drain purulent, foul-smelling fluid are diagnostic of hidradenitis suppurativa.
    \item Soft, mobile, and slow-growing subcutaneous lumps that are painless to touch are typical of lipomas.
    \item Systemic symptoms, such as fever, night sweats, unexplained weight loss, fatigue, or a persistent cough, suggest a systemic infection or lymphoma.
    \item Associated breast changes, such as a breast lump, skin dimpling, nipple inversion, or nipple discharge, are critical features of breast cancer with axillary metastasis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The differential diagnosis of an axillary mass is broad and requires distinguishing between local skin conditions, systemic infections, and malignancies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Local skin infection vs. reactive lymphadenopathy:} Distinguishing a superficial skin infection (folliculitis or furuncle) from a deeper, reactive lymph node swollen due to a distal arm wound.
    \item \textbf{Lymphoma vs. metastatic breast cancer:} Differentiating a primary haematological malignancy of the lymph node from a metastatic deposit of epithelial breast cancer.
    \item \textbf{Ectopic breast tissue vs. axillary lipoma:} Distinguishing normal breast tissue variants that swell hormonally from a benign fatty tumour.
    \item \textbf{Cat scratch disease vs. tuberculosis:} Distinguishing acute bacterial reactive lymphadenopathy from chronic granulomatous infections causing caseating lymphadenitis.
    \item \textbf{Hidradenitis suppurativa vs. recurrent simple abscesses:} Differentiating a chronic, systemic follicular occlusion disorder from isolated bacterial skin infections.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any new, unexplained lump in the armpit should be evaluated by a general practitioner. Lumps that are painless, hard, growing, or accompanied by systemic symptoms require urgent medical evaluation.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A rapidly spreading skin infection (cellulitis) around the armpit with high fever, confusion, or difficulty breathing.
    \item Severe bleeding or purulent drainage from a ruptured axillary swelling accompanied by signs of sepsis (e.g., cold hands, shivering, rapid heart rate).
\end{itemize}

Other non-emergency reasons to see a doctor include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The lump is hard, fixed to the skin, or growing larger.
    \item The lump has been present for more than two to three weeks without resolving.
    \item You experience associated symptoms such as a breast lump, skin dimpling, or nipple discharge.
    \item You develop systemic symptoms like persistent fevers, drenching night sweats, or unexplained weight loss.
    \item The lump is extremely painful, red, swollen, and warm, indicating a developing abscess.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Investigation of an axillary lump begins with a thorough clinical examination and is followed by targeted imaging and tissue sampling.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical breast and axillary examination:} A detailed assessment of both breasts, axillae, supraclavicular regions, and arms to check for primary tumours or signs of infection.
    \item \textbf{Axillary and breast ultrasound:} The initial imaging of choice, which can differentiate cystic from solid masses, evaluate the architecture of the lymph nodes (e.g., loss of fatty hilum, cortical thickening), and assess adjacent breast tissue.
    \item \textbf{Mammography:} Performed in women to screen for occult primary breast cancer when metastatic axillary lymphadenopathy is suspected.
    \item \textbf{Fine needle aspiration or core needle biopsy:} The standard procedure for obtaining tissue samples from a suspicious lymph node or mass, performed under ultrasound guidance to allow histopathological or cytological examination.
    \item \textbf{Excisional biopsy:} Complete surgical removal of the lymph node, typically performed when lymphoma is suspected to preserve the node architecture for detailed immunophenotyping.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} Full blood count (to check for leukaemia or infection markers), inflammatory markers (CRP/ESR), and serology for specific infectious agents (such as EBV, CMV, or \textit{Bartonella henselae}).
    \item \textbf{Staging scans:} CT scans of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis, or a PET-CT scan, if malignancy (such as lymphoma or metastatic cancer) is confirmed.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of an axillary lump depends entirely on the identified underlying cause.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conservative management:} Reassurance and observation for soft, clinically benign lipomas, or mild reactive lymphadenopathy that is expected to resolve after a viral infection.
    \item \textbf{Antibiotic therapy:} Oral or intravenous antibiotics (e.g., flucloxacillin) for bacterial skin infections, cellulitis, or acute bacterial lymphadenitis.
    \item \textbf{Surgical incision and drainage:} Performed under local or general anaesthesia for fluctuant axillary abscesses to relieve pain and promote healing.
    \item \textbf{Hidradenitis suppurativa management:} Long-term treatment involving topical or oral antibiotics, hormonal therapies, biological agents (e.g., adalimumab), and surgical excision of chronic sinus tracts.
    \item \textbf{Cancer surgery:} Axillary lymph node dissection or sentinel lymph node biopsy for metastatic breast cancer or melanoma.
    \item \textbf{Oncological treatments:} Systemic chemotherapy, immunotherapy, targeted therapy, or radiotherapy for lymphoma or metastatic breast cancer.
    \item \textbf{Excision of benign masses:} Surgical removal of symptomatic lipomas, epidermoid cysts, or painful ectopic breast tissue.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Abscess formation and sepsis:} Untreated local bacterial infections can form large abscesses or spread hematogenously, leading to life-threatening sepsis.
    \item \textbf{Lymphoedema:} Swelling of the arm due to lymphatic fluid accumulation, a common complication after axillary lymph node dissection or radiotherapy.
    \item \textbf{Nerve injury:} Damage to the long thoracic nerve, thoracodorsal nerve, or intercostobrachial nerve during axillary surgery, causing winged scapula, muscle weakness, or chronic numbness.
    \item \textbf{Chronic pain and scarring:} Particularly in severe, untreated hidradenitis suppurativa or after extensive surgery.
    \item \textbf{Cancer progression:} Delayed diagnosis of a malignant axillary lump can allow the underlying cancer to metastasise further.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for an axillary lump is generally excellent for the vast majority of cases, which are benign and resolve completely with conservative care or short courses of antibiotics. For chronic conditions like hidradenitis suppurativa, the course is often relapsing and remitting, but symptoms can be well-controlled with modern medical and surgical interventions. If the lump is due to cancer, the prognosis depends on the cancer type, stage, and grade. Sentinel lymph node involvement in breast cancer signifies regional spread but remains highly treatable with curative intent, with high 5-year survival rates. Hodgkin lymphoma has an overall cure rate exceeding 80--90\% in children and young adults.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain good personal hygiene, wash the underarms daily with mild soap, and dry the area thoroughly to prevent bacterial infections of hair follicles.
    \item Avoid sharing razors or personal hygiene products, and shave in the direction of hair growth using a clean blade to reduce folliculitis risk.
    \item Avoid squeezing, popping, or cutting an axillary lump yourself, as this can spread infection deeper into the tissue or bloodstream.
    \item Apply a warm compress to painful, inflammatory lumps (like early boils) to help them come to a head and relieve discomfort.
    \item Perform regular breast self-examinations and report any new changes, including axillary swelling, to your doctor.
    \item Manage weight and wear loose-fitting clothing to reduce friction in the underarm area, which is particularly helpful for preventing hidradenitis suppurativa flare-ups.
    \item Keep pets vaccinated and wash any scratches or bites immediately with soap and water to prevent cat scratch disease.
\end{itemize}
