\chapter{Iron Infusion}
\index{Iron Infusion}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An iron infusion is the delivery of iron directly into a vein, bypassing the gut, to treat iron deficiency quickly and completely. It is used when oral iron tablets are ineffective, not tolerated, or insufficiently rapid, such as in late pregnancy, inflammatory bowel disease, chronic kidney disease, or after surgery. The infusion contains iron bound to a carbohydrate carrier, which is taken up by the body's iron-handling systems over days to weeks. An iron infusion is given in a clinic or hospital over 15 to 60 minutes, usually as a single session, and most people feel no ill effects. Minor reactions such as headache and nausea occur in some, and serious allergic reactions are rare.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Iron deficiency is the world's most common nutritional deficiency, and iron infusions are increasingly used because they correct deficiency faster and more reliably than tablets. They are used in a significant proportion of iron-deficient pregnant women, people with chronic kidney disease, people with inflammatory bowel disease who absorb iron poorly, and people preparing for or recovering from major surgery. The development of newer, safer iron preparations has made infusions routine in hospitals and infusion clinics. Around one in five women of reproductive age is iron deficient, and many benefit from parenteral treatment.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Iron deficiency arises from inadequate intake, impaired absorption, or increased loss, as in menstruation, pregnancy, and gut bleeding. Oral iron is absorbed in the upper small intestine, and this route fails when absorption is poor, as in coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, and after gastric surgery, or when tablets cause side effects that stop adherence. An iron infusion delivers the mineral directly into the bloodstream, where it is bound and transported to the bone marrow for red blood cell production and to the body's iron stores. Because the dose is large, haemoglobin rises over weeks and iron stores are replenished, correcting both anaemia and depletion in a single treatment.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The infusion is given through a cannula in the arm, usually over 15 to 60 minutes
    \item A single session may deliver the full iron requirement
    \item Monitoring during and for a period after the infusion
    \item Headache, nausea, dizziness, or a metallic taste in some people
    \item Minor infusion reactions: flushing, itch, and mild chest or joint discomfort
    \item Serious allergic reactions are rare and most often occur within minutes
    \item Improvement in energy and symptoms over the following weeks
    \item Skin staining at the infusion site if iron leaks into the tissue
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The decision to use an iron infusion follows assessment of the anaemia and its cause.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Oral iron tablets:} appropriate first-line treatment for most people
    \item \textbf{Intravenous iron:} for intolerance, malabsorption, severe deficiency, or time constraints
    \item \textbf{Blood transfusion:} reserved for severe, symptomatic, or life-threatening anaemia
    \item \textbf{Treatment of the underlying cause:} bleeding, malabsorption, or dietary deficiency
    \item \textbf{Other anaemias:} B12 or folate deficiency, anaemia of chronic disease, and thalassaemia
    \item \textbf{Erythropoietin therapy:} in chronic kidney disease and some other settings
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with iron deficiency who cannot tolerate tablets, are severely deficient, or need rapid correction should discuss an iron infusion with their doctor. Anaemia in pregnancy and before planned surgery is a common reason for referral. Anyone with a reaction during or after an infusion should report it immediately, and symptoms such as breathlessness, chest pain, or swelling after the infusion warrant urgent review.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, wheezing, or swelling of the face or throat during or after an infusion
    \item Chest pain, palpitations, or collapse
    \item A widespread rash, itching, or dizziness after the infusion
    \item Severe pain or swelling at the infusion site
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full blood count and iron studies:} confirming iron deficiency and anaemia severity
    \item \textbf{Ferritin:} assessment of iron stores
    \item \textbf{Assessment of the cause:} dietary, menstrual, gut, and bleeding assessment
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy review:} timing and dose planned with the obstetric team
    \item \textbf{Renal function:} relevant in chronic kidney disease
    \item \textbf{Pre-infusion checks:} allergy history and suitability for the preparation
    \item \textbf{Post-infusion monitoring:} observation for reactions and later blood tests
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Choice of preparation:} modern iron-carbohydrate complexes that allow large doses in one session
    \item \textbf{Dose calculation:} based on body weight and the degree of deficiency
    \item \textbf{Administration:} slow infusion with monitoring by trained staff
    \item \textbf{Management of reactions:} slowing the infusion or giving antihistamines and steroids for minor reactions
    \item \textbf{Treatment of the cause:} ongoing management of bleeding, malabsorption, or diet
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} repeat blood tests at four to eight weeks to confirm response
    \item \textbf{Continuing care:} maintenance iron or dietary advice as needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Minor reactions: headache, nausea, dizziness, and flushing
    \item Infusion reactions, including severe allergic reactions, which are rare
    \item Skin staining if iron leaks into surrounding tissue
    \item Vein irritation and phlebitis
    \item Iron overload if given inappropriately or repeatedly
    \item Infection risk, which is very low with proper technique
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Iron infusion corrects iron deficiency reliably and more quickly than oral treatment. Haemoglobin begins to rise within days and normalises over four to eight weeks, with iron stores restored for many months. Most people feel a clear improvement in energy and wellbeing. The treatment is safe in pregnancy, in inflammatory bowel disease, and in chronic kidney disease, and it is well tolerated by the majority of people. Recurrence depends on the underlying cause being managed.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Tell the infusion team about any allergies and past reactions
    \item Report any unusual sensation during the infusion immediately
    \item Remain for the recommended observation period after the infusion
    \item Keep the infusion site clean and report pain, swelling, or staining
    \item Continue to treat the underlying cause of the deficiency
    \item Eat an iron-rich diet after treatment to maintain stores
    \item Attend follow-up blood tests to confirm the response
    \item Discuss maintenance options with your doctor
\end{itemize}
