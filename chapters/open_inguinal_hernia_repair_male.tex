\chapter{Open inguinal hernia repair (male)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Open inguinal hernia repair in males is a classic surgical intervention designed to correct a protrusion of abdominal contents—such as extraperitoneal fat or loops of the small and large bowel—through the inguinal canal. The inguinal canal is a natural, oblique passage in the lower anterior abdominal wall that in males transmits the spermatic cord and the ilioinguinal nerve. Inguinal hernias are classified as either indirect, arising lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels and entering the deep inguinal ring due to a congenital patency, or direct, arising medial to these vessels within Hesselbach's triangle due to an acquired weakness in the posterior inguinal wall. The primary goal of open surgical repair (most commonly performed using a tension-free mesh technique such as the Lichtenstein repair) is to reduce the herniated contents back into the peritoneal cavity and reinforce the weakened abdominal floor. This relieves groin discomfort and prevents serious, acute, life-threatening complications, including hernia incarceration, bowel obstruction, and strangulation leading to tissue ischaemia and necrosis.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Inguinal hernias are highly prevalent, with a lifetime risk of approximately 27\% in men compared to only 3\% in women. The incidence of groin hernias displays a bimodal distribution, peaking in infancy due to congenital developmental issues, and rising steeply after the age of 50 as a consequence of age-related tissue degeneration. In Australia and worldwide, open inguinal hernia repair is one of the most frequently performed general surgical procedures, with tens of thousands of operations completed annually. Risk factors include advancing age, male sex, family history of abdominal wall hernias, chronic cough, chronic constipation, heavy occupational lifting, and previous abdominal surgery. Indirect inguinal hernias are the most common type across all age groups, while direct hernias are almost exclusively acquired and are observed predominantly in older men who have developed progressive weakness of the transversalis fascia.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The development of an inguinal hernia involves a combination of anatomical predisposition and conditions that chronically increase intra-abdominal pressure.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Patent processus vaginalis:} In indirect hernias, the processus vaginalis—a pouch of peritoneum that precedes the descent of the testis into the scrotum during fetal development—fails to obliterate. This leaves a preformed sac that can accommodate abdominal contents under pressure.
    \item \textbf{Weakness of the transversalis fascia:} In direct hernias, progressive wear-and-tear, tissue ageing, and metabolic changes lead to the attenuation of the aponeurosis and fascia within Hesselbach's triangle. This area is bounded by the rectus abdominis muscle medially, the inguinal ligament inferiorly, and the inferior epigastric vessels laterally.
    \item \textbf{Elevated intra-abdominal pressure:} Chronic mechanical stressors such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) causing persistent coughing, benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) causing straining during micturition, chronic constipation, and heavy lifting strain the abdominal wall.
    \item \textbf{Connective tissue alterations:} Changes in collagen metabolism, including a reduced ratio of type I to type III collagen, compromise the structural tensile strength of the fascial layers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The presentation of an inguinal hernia can vary from an asymptomatic groin lump detected during a routine physical examination to severe, acute abdominal pain secondary to bowel strangulation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Groin swelling:} A visible and palpable lump in the inguinal region, which may extend into the scrotum. The bulge typically becomes more prominent when the patient stands, coughs, or strains, and may disappear or reduce when the patient lies flat.
    \item \textbf{Localised discomfort:} A dull ache, burning sensation, or feeling of heaviness in the groin, which is often exacerbated by prolonged standing, walking, or physical exertion.
    \item \textbf{Referred pain:} Groin pain that radiates down the medial aspect of the thigh or into the ipsilateral scrotum.
    \item \textbf{Tenderness and irreducibility:} A hernia that becomes painful to touch and can no longer be pushed back into the abdominal cavity suggests incarceration.
    \item \textbf{Signs of strangulation:} Extreme pain, local overlying skin erythema or discolouration, accompanied by nausea, vomiting, abdominal distension, and inability to pass flatus or stool.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Groin swellings and regional pain can stem from various musculoskeletal, vascular, lymphatic, and scrotal conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Femoral hernia:} A protrusion through the femoral ring below the inguinal ligament, lateral to the pubic tubercle, which is more common in women and carries a high risk of early incarceration.
    \item \textbf{Hydrocele or varicocele:} An accumulation of fluid in the tunica vaginalis or abnormal dilatation of the pampiniform venous plexus, respectively, presenting as a scrotal mass.
    \item \textbf{Epididymitis or orchitis:} Inflammatory or infectious conditions of the scrotal contents, characterised by acute pain, swelling, fever, and urinary symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Groin lymphadenopathy:} Enlarged superficial inguinal lymph nodes secondary to regional infection or haematological and solid malignancies.
    \item \textbf{Saphena varix:} A dilatation of the saphenous vein at the saphenofemoral junction, which demonstrates a cough impulse but disappears immediately upon lying down.
    \item \textbf{Spermatic cord lipoma:} A benign collection of fat within the inguinal canal that mimics a hernia but does not communicate with the peritoneal cavity.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients with a new, asymptomatic, or reducible groin bulge should schedule an elective appointment with a general practitioner to plan management. However, if the lump becomes irreducible, intensely painful, or is associated with gastrointestinal symptoms, emergency medical care is required.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A groin bulge that cannot be reduced (pushed back in) when lying down and is increasingly tender.
    \item Sudden, severe, or worsening pain in the groin or lower abdomen.
    \item Nausea, projectile vomiting, and abdominal bloating.
    \item Redness, warmth, or severe skin discolouration over the hernia site.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of an inguinal hernia is primarily clinical, established through physical examination, though imaging may be utilized to clarify ambiguous presentations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} Assessing the patient in both standing and supine positions. The examiner palpates the external inguinal ring while the patient performs a Valsalva manoeuvre (coughs or strains) to feel for an expansile impulse and assess reducibility.
    \item \textbf{Groin ultrasound:} A dynamic ultrasound is the first-line imaging modality to confirm the diagnosis when clinical findings are equivocal, helping to identify hernia contents and rule out testicular or vascular pathology.
    \item \textbf{Computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):} Advanced imaging is reserved for chronic groin pain without a palpable mass, suspected recurrent hernias, or when evaluating for rarer hernia types (e.g., obturator hernias).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The primary management for symptomatic inguinal hernias is surgical. Asymptomatic hernias in older males may occasionally be managed with active surveillance, but open surgical repair remains the definitive treatment.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lichtenstein tension-free repair:} The most common open technique, where a synthetic mesh (usually polypropylene) is sutured over the posterior inguinal canal wall, reinforcing Hesselbach's triangle without putting tension on the surrounding tissue.
    \item \textbf{Herniorrhaphy:} Primary tissue-to-tissue suture repairs (such as the Shouldice technique), which are reserved for cases where mesh is contraindicated, such as in contaminated fields or due to patient preference.
    \item \textbf{Sac dissection and reduction:} Dissection of the hernia sac from the spermatic cord structures, followed by either inversion of the sac back into the abdomen or excision.
    \item \textbf{Anaesthesia options:} Open repair can be performed under local infiltration anaesthesia, regional spinal blocks, or general anaesthesia, making it highly adaptable for patients with significant cardiovascular or pulmonary comorbidities.
    \item \textbf{Postoperative care:} Multimodal analgesia including paracetamol and NSAIDs, early mobilization, and avoiding heavy lifting (typically greater than 5--10 kg) for 4--6 weeks to prevent early recurrence.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While open inguinal hernia repair is a safe procedure, it is associated with specific short- and long-term postoperative risks.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chronic groin pain (inguinodynia):} Neuropathic or nociceptive pain lasting more than three months, affecting 10--12\% of patients, often due to entrapment or injury of the ilioinguinal, iliohypogastric, or genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve.
    \item \textbf{Haematoma or seroma:} Accumulation of blood or serous fluid in the inguinal wound or scrotum, which is generally self-limiting but occasionally requires drainage.
    \item \textbf{Surgical site infection:} Wound infection which may involve the superficial tissues or, in rare cases, lead to deep mesh infection requiring mesh explantation.
    \item \textbf{Ischaemic orchitis and testicular atrophy:} Resulting from dissection injury or compression of the testicular artery and pampiniform venous plexus, leading to testicular pain and subsequent shrinkage.
    \item \textbf{Hernia recurrence:} Long-term recurrence is low (typically under 2--5\% with mesh repairs) but remains a risk if tissue healing is poor or there is persistent high intra-abdominal pressure.
    \item \textbf{Urinary retention:} Acute postoperative inability to void, particularly in older patients with pre-existing prostatic enlargement.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis after open male inguinal hernia repair is excellent. The widespread adoption of tension-free mesh techniques has dramatically reduced long-term recurrence rates to less than 5\% compared to historical non-mesh repairs. Most patients can return to light desk work within a few days and resume full physical activities by 4--6 weeks. The most clinically significant long-term issue is chronic post-herniorrhaphy groin pain, which highlights the importance of nerve preservation during surgery. Overall patient satisfaction is high, with significant improvements in physical functioning and quality of life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Avoid heavy lifting:} Limit lifting heavy objects, and use proper lifting techniques (bending at the knees, not the waist) if lifting is unavoidable.
    \item \textbf{Treat chronic coughing:} Manage conditions like asthma and chronic bronchitis, and seek smoking cessation support to reduce coughing fits.
    \item \textbf{Prevent constipation:} Increase dietary fibre intake, maintain adequate hydration, and use stool softeners if necessary to avoid straining on the toilet.
    \item \textbf{Maintain a healthy weight:} Reduce chronic strain on the abdominal wall musculature by managing body weight.
    \item \textbf{Postoperative support:} Support the surgical wound with a hand or small pillow during coughing, sneezing, or deep breathing in the first few weeks after surgery.
\end{itemize}
