\chapter{Trichotillomania}
\index{Trichotillomania}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Trichotillomania, also classified in clinical manuals as hair-pulling disorder, is a complex, chronic neuropsychiatric condition characterized by the recurrent, irresistible urge to pull out one's own hair. This behavior leads to noticeable hair loss, significant psychological distress, and functional impairment across various domains of daily life. The term itself is derived from the Greek words ``thrix'' (hair), ``tillein'' (to pull), and ``mania'' (madness), and it was first coined in 1889 by the French dermatologist Fran\c{c}ois Henri Hallopeau. Historically conceptualized as an impulse control disorder, modern psychiatric nomenclature, specifically the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR), classifies trichotillomania under the category of Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders (OCRDs). It is also recognized as a key subtype within the broader clinical spectrum of Body-Focused Repetitive Behaviors (BFRBs), which also includes excoriation (skin-picking) disorder and onychophagia (nail-biting).

The clinical manifestation of trichotillomania is heterogeneous. Individuals may pull hair from any part of the body where it grows, with the scalp, eyebrows, and eyelashes being the most common sites. The act of pulling can range from highly focused, conscious behaviors preceded by an intense urge or sense of tension, to automatic or habitual behaviors performed without conscious awareness, often during periods of sedentary activity such as reading, watching television, or driving. For most sufferers, the condition is not merely a ``bad habit'' but a debilitating disorder that carries a profound emotional burden. The visible hair loss frequently leads to intense shame, embarrassment, and guilt, prompting individuals to go to great lengths to conceal their alopecia and avoid social, occupational, or recreational situations. Consequently, trichotillomania represents a significant public health concern, causing substantial reductions in quality of life, yet remaining underdiagnosed and undertreated due to the social stigma associated with the behavior.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of trichotillomania reveals distinct patterns across different age groups and demographics. In the general population, the lifetime prevalence of trichotillomania is estimated to be between 1\% and 2\%, though some surveys suggesting subclinical hair-pulling behavior indicate that mild forms of the condition may be much more common. The distribution of the disorder changes dramatically with age. In infants and young children, the condition is relatively rare, is equally distributed between males and females, and often presents as a benign, transient developmental habit similar to thumb-sucking.

However, when the onset occurs during early adolescence---typically between the ages of 10 and 13 years, coinciding with the onset of puberty---the epidemiological profile shifts. In adolescent and adult clinical populations, females represent the vast majority of cases, with estimates of the female-to-male ratio ranging from 3:1 to as high as 9:1. While this stark imbalance may partially reflect a gender-based discrepancy in treatment-seeking behavior, as females may experience greater societal pressure regarding physical appearance and hair loss, epidemiological surveys of community samples still demonstrate a significant female predominance.

The course of trichotillomania in adults is typically chronic and fluctuating, characterized by periods of remission and exacerbation that can last for weeks, months, or years. The severity of the symptoms often waxes and wanes in response to life stressors, hormonal fluctuations (such as those occurring during the menstrual cycle or pregnancy), and changes in psychiatric comorbidities. Comorbid psychiatric conditions are highly prevalent, affecting up to 80\% of adults with trichotillomania. The most common comorbidities include major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), excoriation disorder, and border-line or avoidant personality traits, which further complicate the clinical presentation and management of the disorder.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The aetiology and pathophysiology of trichotillomania are multifactorial, involving a complex interplay of genetic predisposition, neurobiological alterations, emotional dysregulation, and environmental influences. The primary contributors to the development and maintenance of the disorder include the following:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic Predisposition}: Family and twin studies suggest a strong hereditary component in trichotillomania. First-degree relatives of individuals with the disorder have an increased risk of developing trichotillomania, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and other body-focused repetitive behaviors. Molecular genetic studies have investigated candidate genes involved in brain development and neurotransmitter regulation, highlighting mutations in the SLITRK1 gene (associated with synaptogenesis and axonal growth) and the HOXB8 gene (linked to grooming behavior in animal models) as potential genetic contributors.
    \item \textbf{Neurochemical Dysregulation}: Alterations in brain chemistry, particularly within the monoamine neurotransmitter systems, are heavily implicated in the pathophysiology of the disorder. A dysregulation in serotonin, which is crucial for impulse control and mood regulation, is thought to play a role, as is dopamine, which mediates the brain's reward and habit-formation pathways. Additionally, imbalances in the excitatory neurotransmitter glutamate have been linked to the compulsive nature of hair-pulling, suggesting a disruption in the neural pathways that control motor habits and behavioral inhibition.
    \item \textbf{Neuroanatomical Alterations}: Structural and functional neuroimaging studies have identified abnormalities in the neural circuits responsible for habit formation, motor control, and emotional regulation. Specifically, dysfunction within the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) circuits is believed to underlie the compulsive aspects of the disorder. Structural MRI studies have shown reductions in cerebellar volume and alterations in the grey matter density of the prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and the caudate nucleus, which are areas key to response inhibition and cognitive flexibility.
    \textquoteleft \item \textbf{Emotion Regulation Model}: Psychologically, the hair-pulling behavior is often conceptualized as a maladaptive strategy for regulating emotional states. According to this model, pulling is triggered by negative affect (such as anxiety, boredom, frustration, sadness, or tension) or positive states (such as intense focus or concentration). The act of pulling provides temporary relief, satisfaction, or a sense of control, which serves as negative reinforcement, thereby strengthening and maintaining the behavior over time.
    \item \textbf{Sensory Regulation and Conditioning}: For many individuals, the tactile and visual sensations associated with hair-pulling are highly reinforcing. Sufferers may seek out hairs with specific textures (e.g., coarse, wiry, or out of place) or engage in tactile exploration of the hair shaft and root. The sensory feedback obtained during the pulling act, along with subsequent post-pulling rituals, acts as a primary reinforcer, establishing a conditioned behavioral loop that is difficult to break.
    \item \textbf{Environmental and Developmental Triggers}: Stressful life events, family conflict, academic pressure, or physical trauma can act as catalysts for the onset of trichotillomania in genetically vulnerable individuals. Additionally, hormonal changes during puberty are thought to interact with neurodevelopmental pathways, contributing to the typical early adolescent onset of the disorder.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features of trichotillomania are highly variable, characterized by a range of physical signs, behavioral patterns, and psychological symptoms. The core feature is the recurrent pulling out of one's hair, which can lead to varying degrees of alopecia. The clinical presentation typically encompasses the following aspects:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anatomical Sites of Pulling}: While hair can be pulled from any part of the body, the scalp is the most common site, affected in approximately 70\% to 80\% of individuals. Other frequently targeted areas include the eyelashes, eyebrows, pubic region, underarms, beard, and chest. Many individuals pull from multiple sites concurrently or shift from one site to another over time.
    \item \textbf{Patterns of Hair Loss}: The resulting alopecia is often asymmetrical and irregular, with bizarre shapes that do not conform to dermatological patterns of hair loss like alopecia areata. On the scalp, a classic presentation is the ``tonsure'' pattern or ``Friar Tuck'' sign, where hair is spared around the margins of the scalp but completely cleared or thinned at the crown. The hair loss may range from small, barely noticeable patches to total denudation of the scalp or face.
    \item \textbf{Hair-Pulling Phenotypes}: Clinicians distinguish between two primary styles of pulling, though many patients experience a combination of both:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Focused Pulling}: A conscious, deliberate behavior where the individual experiences an mounting sense of tension and actively searches for specific hairs to pull. This is often accompanied by the belief that the hair must be removed because it feels ``wrong,'' coarse, or asymmetrical.
        \textit{Automatic Pulling}: An unconscious, habitual behavior that occurs without active awareness. The individual may find their hand moving to their head or face while engaged in sedentary activities, only realizing they have been pulling when they notice the accumulated loose hairs.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sensory and Motor Rituals}: The act of pulling is frequently accompanied by elaborate pre- and post-pulling rituals. Sufferers may touch, play with, or examine the pulled hair, run the root across their lips or face, or bite the hair shaft. In some cases, individuals engage in trichophagia, which is the chewing and swallowing of the pulled hair.
    \item \textbf{Psychological and Emotional Distress}: The behavior is characteristically associated with an cycle of increasing tension prior to pulling (or when trying to resist the urge) followed by a temporary sense of gratification, pleasure, or relief once the hair is extracted. Subsequently, individuals often experience intense feelings of shame, guilt, and self-loathing due to their inability to control the behavior and the resulting cosmetic damage.
    \item \textbf{Social and Occupational Avoidance}: To cope with the visible effects of hair-pulling, individuals often develop avoidance behaviors. They may spend hours trying to style their hair, using cosmetics, wigs, hats, or scarves to hide patches. Many avoid social activities where their hair loss might be exposed, such as swimming, sporting events, or windy outdoor outings, leading to significant social isolation and impairment in professional and interpersonal relationships.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When evaluating a patient with hair loss or repetitive hair manipulation, it is critical to distinguish trichotillomania from other dermatological and psychiatric conditions. The differential diagnosis includes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Alopecia Areata}: An autoimmune condition characterized by rapid, patchy hair loss on the scalp, eyebrows, or body. Unlike trichotillomania, alopecia areata typically presents with smooth, non-scarring patches of complete hair loss without broken hair shafts of varying lengths. Diagnostic tests, such as trichoscopy, reveal ``exclamation mark'' hairs, and a biopsy shows a characteristic peribulbar lymphocytic infiltrate (the ``swarm of bees'' appearance), which is absent in trichotillomania.
    \item \textbf{Tinea Capitis}: A superficial fungal infection of the scalp that causes patches of alopecia, scaling, erythema, and pruritus. It is most common in children. Broken hairs at the scalp surface (appearing as ``black dots'') are common, but the presence of inflammation, scaling, and positive fungal cultures or potassium hydroxide (KOH) examinations easily differentiates it from trichotillomania.
    \item \textbf{Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD)}: While closely related, OCD is characterized by intrusive, distressing thoughts (obsessions) and repetitive behaviors (compulsions) performed to reduce anxiety associated with these obsessions. In contrast, hair-pulling in trichotillomania is typically triggered by sensory cues or emotional tension and lacks the complex, ideational obsessions characteristic of OCD.
    \item \textbf{Body Dysmorphic Disorder (BDD)}: Individuals with BDD may remove hair as a compulsive behavior to correct a perceived defect in their physical appearance (e.g., trimming hair to make it look symmetrical or removing body hair they believe is excessive). In trichotillomania, the primary motivation is the relief of tension or sensory gratification, rather than the correction of an imagined cosmetic flaw, although cosmetic distress is a major consequence of the disorder.
    \item \textbf{Excoriation (Skin-Picking) Disorder}: This body-focused repetitive behavior involves the compulsive scratching, picking, or gouging of the skin. While it shares many clinical and neurobiological features with trichotillomania and frequently co-occurs, the target tissue is the skin rather than the hair.
    \item \textbf{Stereotypic Movement Disorder}: Involves repetitive, rhythmic, purposeless movements such as body rocking, head banging, or self-biting that typically begin in early childhood. While hair-pulling can occur, it is usually part of a broader pattern of motor stereotypies and lacks the focused, non-rhythmic, and complex rituals characteristic of trichotillomania.
    \item \textbf{Traumatic Alopecia and Traction Alopecia}: Hair loss caused by physical trauma or mechanical tension (e.g., tight hairstyles, braids, or heat styling). A detailed history of hair styling practices and the absence of compulsive pulling behavior or associated psychological distress differentiate these conditions from trichotillomania.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
If you or a loved one is experiencing recurrent hair-pulling that leads to hair loss, it is important to seek medical evaluation. Early intervention by a general practitioner, dermatologist, or mental health professional can significantly improve outcomes and prevent the condition from becoming chronic. You should consult a healthcare provider if:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The hair-pulling behavior is frequent, causing noticeable hair loss or thinning on the scalp, eyebrows, eyelashes, or other areas.
    \item Repeated self-directed attempts to stop or reduce the pulling behavior have been unsuccessful.
    \item The behavior is causing significant emotional distress, anxiety, depression, low self-esteem, or is interfering with social, educational, or professional activities.
    \item The skin in the affected areas shows signs of infection, such as redness, warmth, swelling, pain, pus, or persistent bleeding.
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{URGENT AND EMERGENCY MEDICAL SITUATIONS:}

\noindent While trichotillomania is primarily a psychiatric condition, it can lead to life-threatening physical emergencies. Immediate medical attention must be sought if there is any suspicion of hair ingestion (trichophagia). Human hair cannot be digested and can accumulate in the gastrointestinal tract, forming a hairball known as a trichobezoar. This can lead to a severe bowel obstruction or perforation.

Go to the nearest emergency department or call emergency services immediately (e.g., \textbf{local emergency services} in many countries, \textbf{911} in the United States, or \textbf{999} in the United Kingdom) if an individual with a history of hair-pulling develops any of the following symptoms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, sudden, or progressive abdominal pain or cramping.
    \item Persistent nausea and projectile vomiting.
    \item Inability to pass gas or have a bowel movement.
    \item Unexplained weight loss, loss of appetite, or feeling full after eating very little.
    \item A palpable, firm mass in the upper abdomen.
    \item Signs of shock or systemic infection (peritonitis) resulting from a ruptured bowel, such as high fever, rapid heart rate, dizziness, confusion, or a rigid, extremely tender abdomen.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of trichotillomania is clinical, based on a comprehensive psychiatric and dermatological evaluation. There are no diagnostic blood tests or imaging studies that can confirm the condition, though investigations are used to rule out alternative causes of hair loss. The diagnostic process typically involves:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic Criteria (DSM-5-TR)}: To be diagnosed with trichotillomania, an individual must meet the following criteria:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Recurrent pulling out of one's hair, resulting in hair loss.
        \item Repeated attempts to decrease or stop hair pulling.
        \item The hair pulling causes clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning.
        \item The hair pulling or hair loss is not attributable to another medical condition (e.g., a dermatological condition).
        \item The hair pulling is not better explained by the symptoms of another mental disorder (e.g., attempts to improve a perceived defect or flaw in appearance in body dysmorphic disorder).
    \end{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Clinical History}: A detailed history is essential, focusing on the age of onset, the specific sites targeted, the frequency and duration of pulling episodes, the presence of focused versus automatic pulling, associated sensory triggers, and any post-pulling rituals (especially trichophagia).
    \item \textbf{Dermatological Examination and Trichoscopy}: A thorough examination of the scalp and hair shafts is performed. Trichoscopy (dermoscopy of the hair and scalp) is a highly useful, non-invasive diagnostic tool. Characteristic trichoscopic findings in trichotillomania include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Hairs of varying lengths with fractured, blunt, or frayed ends.
        \item Coiled or spiral hairs resulting from the elastic recoil of stretched hair shafts.
        \item ``Flame hairs'' and ``tulip hairs'' which are morphologically distorted, damaged hair remnants.
        \item Black dots (representing hair shafts broken at the scalp level) and yellow dots (representing empty follicular infundibula, sometimes containing sebum).
        \item The absence of diffuse scaling, scarring, or follicular plugging (which would suggest an inflammatory dermatosis).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scalp Biopsy}: In rare, clinically ambiguous cases where a primary dermatological condition cannot be ruled out, a punch biopsy of the scalp may be performed. Histopathological examination of a trichotillomania lesion typically reveals:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Trichomalacia}: Distorted, soft, and poorly keratinized hair shafts within the follicular canal.
        \item \textit{Pigmented Casts}: Clumps of free melanin pigment within the follicular canal.
        \item \textit{Catagen/Telogen Shift}: An abnormally high proportion of hair follicles in the catagen (transitional) or telogen (resting) phases, rather than the active anagen phase.
        \item \textit{Empty Follicles}: Preserved follicular structures without active hair shafts and with minimal or no inflammatory infiltrate around the follicle.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of trichotillomania requires a comprehensive, individualized treatment plan that addresses both the behavioral patterns and the underlying emotional and neurobiological factors. A combination of psychological therapy and pharmacotherapy yields the best results.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychological Therapy (First-Line Treatment)}: Cognitive behavioral interventions are the gold standard for treating trichotillomania.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Habit Reversal Training (HRT)}: The most empirically supported behavioral therapy for BFRBs. HRT consists of several components:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Awareness Training}: Helping the patient identify the early warning signs, physical sensations (e.g., itching, tingling), and environmental situations that precede pulling.
            \textbf{Competing Response Training}: Teaching the patient to perform a physically incompatible behavior (such as clenching their fists, sitting on their hands, or interlocking their fingers) for a few minutes when they feel the urge to pull.
            \item \textbf{Stimulus Control}: Modifying the environment to reduce pulling triggers, such as wearing bandages on fingers, using barriers (gloves, hats), or removing mirrors from high-risk areas.
            \item \textbf{Social Support}: Involving family members or friends to provide positive reinforcement and gentle reminders to use competing responses.
        \end{itemize}
        \item \textit{Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)}: Helps individuals learn to accept their urges to pull without acting on them. It teaches mindfulness techniques and helps patients align their behaviors with core personal values rather than focusing solely on symptom suppression.
        \item \textit{Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT)}: Particularly useful for individuals whose hair-pulling is driven by intense emotional states, DBT teaches skills in mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pharmacotherapy}: While no medications are currently approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) specifically for trichotillomania, several agents are used off-label to help manage symptoms:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{N-acetylcysteine (NAC)}: An over-the-counter amino acid supplement that modulates the glutamate system in the brain. Clinical trials have demonstrated that NAC is effective in reducing the severity of hair-pulling symptoms in adults, with a favorable side-effect profile. Typical dosages range from 1200 mg to 2400 mg daily.
        \item \textit{Clomipramine}: A tricyclic antidepressant (TCA) with strong serotonergic properties. It has shown efficacy in reducing pulling behavior in randomized controlled trials, though its use may be limited by side effects such as sedation, dry mouth, and constipation.
        \item \textit{Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs)}: E.g., fluoxetine, sertraline, fluvoxamine. While SSRIs are highly effective for treating comorbid anxiety and depression, clinical trials have shown mixed or limited efficacy for the hair-pulling behavior itself when it occurs in isolation.
        \item \textit{Atypical Antipsychotics}: In severe or treatment-resistant cases, low doses of atypical antipsychotics such as olanzapine or aripiprazole may be prescribed to assist with impulse control.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cosmetic and Dermatological Support}: Topical treatments (such as soothing creams or mild topical steroids) can reduce local itching or irritation that may act as a trigger. Many individuals benefit from cosmetic solutions such as high-quality wigs, hairpieces, eyelash extensions, or microblading for eyebrows, which can restore confidence, reduce public distress, and serve as physical barriers to pulling.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated or severe trichotillomania can lead to a range of physical, psychological, and social complications. These include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Trichobezoar and Rapunzel Syndrome}: The most dangerous physical complication. Individuals who swallow their pulled hair (trichophagia) are at risk of developing a trichobezoar (a mass of undigested hair in the stomach). Over time, this mass can extend into the small intestine, a rare condition known as Rapunzel Syndrome. Complications of bezoars include gastric ulcers, bowel obstruction, bleeding, bowel perforation, peritonitis, and nutritional deficiencies.
    \item \textbf{Local Skin and Follicular Damage}: Repetitive pulling causes mechanical trauma to the hair follicles, which can lead to localized folliculitis (inflammation of the hair follicle) or secondary bacterial infections (such as cellulitis). Chronic pulling can cause permanent scarring of the hair follicles, leading to irreversible traction alopecia, where the hair will no longer grow back.
    \item \textbf{Dental Problems}: Biting or chewing on hair can cause dental wear, enamel erosion, chips, and damage to dental work. It can also contribute to temporomandibular joint (TMJ) dysfunction due to the repetitive, asymmetric jaw movement.
    \item \textbf{Musculoskeletal Issues}: Repetitive motion injury, such as carpal tunnel syndrome or tendonitis in the hands, wrists, or arms, can develop due to the hours spent in the repetitive physical act of searching for and pulling hair.
    \item \textbf{Severe Psychological Distress}: Sufferers often experience chronic low self-esteem, intense shame, guilt, and a sense of loss of control. This can precipitate or worsen major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, and social phobia.
    \item \textbf{Social and Occupational Impairment}: The fear of having one's hair loss discovered can lead to profound social withdrawal. Sufferers may avoid school, work, intimate relationships, and routine medical care, leading to academic underachievement, career stagnation, and severe loneliness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of trichotillomania varies significantly based on the age of onset, the presence of psychiatric comorbidities, and access to appropriate treatment.

In young children (onset before age 6), the prognosis is excellent. In this population, the hair-pulling is typically a transient, habit-like behavior that resolves spontaneously or with simple behavioral redirection, parental education, and minimal intervention.

In contrast, when the onset occurs in adolescence or adulthood, the disorder tends to follow a chronic, relapsing, and remitting course. Without formal treatment, spontaneous remission is uncommon in adults. Symptoms often fluctuate in response to life stress, hormonal shifts, and changes in overall mental health.

However, with appropriate evidence-based interventions---specifically Habit Reversal Training (HRT) and targeted pharmacotherapy like N-acetylcysteine---many individuals achieve significant symptom reduction or complete remission. Relapse is a common feature of the disorder, particularly during periods of high stress, and long-term management often involves developing ongoing coping strategies and seeking periodic booster therapy sessions. The prognosis is also improved when comorbid conditions, such as depression or anxiety, are managed concurrently.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
While it may not be possible to prevent the initial onset of trichotillomania due to its genetic and neurobiological origins, self-care strategies and early intervention can help prevent symptom escalation and manage the urge to pull. Useful self-care and prevention strategies include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stress Management and Relaxation Techniques}: Since stress is a major trigger for hair-pulling, incorporating stress-reduction practices into daily life is crucial. Effective techniques include mindfulness meditation, deep breathing exercises, progressive muscle relaxation, yoga, and regular physical exercise.
    \item \textbf{Identifying and Tracking Triggers}: Keeping a ``pulling diary'' can help individuals identify patterns. Tracking the time of day, location, emotional state, and activities during which pulling occurs allows patients to predict high-risk situations and proactively implement coping strategies.
    \item \textbf{Creating Physical Barriers}: Modifying the immediate environment and one's body can prevent automatic pulling. Strategies include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Wearing cotton gloves, finger cot protectors, or placing adhesive bandages on the fingertips of the dominant pulling hand.
        \item Keeping hair cut short or wearing a hat, scarf, bandana, or wig, especially during times of high vulnerability.
        \item Keeping fingernails trimmed very short to make it physically difficult to grasp and pull individual hairs.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Using Fidgets and Competing Response Tools}: Keeping hands occupied with alternative tactile items can satisfy the sensory urge to pull. Useful tools include stress balls, tangle toys, textured stones, knitting, drawing, or playing with silicone bubble-poppers.
    \item \textbf{Modifying the Environment}: If pulling is triggered by visual cues in the mirror, individuals can limit mirror exposure by dimming bathroom lights, covering mirrors, or using timers to limit time spent in front of them.
    \item \textbf{Joining Support Groups}: Connecting with others who have trichotillomania through support networks (such as the TLC Foundation for Body-Focused Repetitive Behaviors) can reduce feelings of isolation, reduce shame, and provide a forum for sharing successful coping strategies.
\end{itemize}
