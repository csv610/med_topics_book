\chapter{Irritability and Feeling on Edge}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Irritability is a state of being easily annoyed, frustrated, or angered, and feeling on edge describes a sense of tension, restlessness, and heightened alertness, as if waiting for something bad to happen. These feelings are common responses to stress, lack of sleep, and difficult circumstances, and they are a normal part of the human experience. When they are severe, persistent, or affecting relationships and daily life, they may signal an underlying condition such as anxiety, depression, stress-related problems, or a physical cause such as thyroid disease. Recognising the cause and addressing it with self-care, support, and treatment restores calm and wellbeing.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Irritability and feeling on edge are extremely common. Around one in four Australians experiences a mental health condition such as anxiety or depression each year, and irritability is a core feature of both, particularly in young people. Stress, poor sleep, and heavy workloads affect most people at some time and produce these feelings without any diagnosable condition. Irritability is also common with physical conditions such as thyroid disorders, chronic pain, and premenstrual syndrome, and with substance use and withdrawal. Most people manage with self-care, but a substantial number benefit from professional support.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Irritability and feeling on edge arise from the interaction of stress, mood, and the body's threat systems. Stress activates the sympathetic nervous system and stress hormones such as adrenaline and cortisol, producing a state of heightened alertness and reduced tolerance for frustration. In anxiety, this system is overactive, and the brain over-predicts threat. In depression, irritability can reflect low mood, hopelessness, and reduced ability to cope. Poor sleep, low blood sugar, pain, and hormonal changes lower the threshold for irritability, as do alcohol, caffeine, and some medicines. Physical conditions such as hyperthyroidism directly increase arousal and irritability. The brain circuits for emotion regulation are central: when they are overloaded, small triggers produce large reactions.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Easily annoyed, frustrated, or angered by small things
    \item A constant sense of tension, restlessness, or being on guard
    \item Difficulty relaxing, and feeling wound up or jumpy
    \item Short temper with family, friends, or colleagues
    \item Impatience and low tolerance for delay or interruption
    \item Physical features: racing heart, sweating, muscle tension, and poor sleep
    \item Difficulty concentrating and a churning or racing mind
    \item Regret and guilt after outbursts
    \item Withdrawal from others to avoid conflict
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Persistent irritability may reflect a range of conditions that need different treatment.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anxiety disorders:} generalised anxiety, panic, and post-traumatic stress
    \item \textbf{Depression:} low mood, hopelessness, and loss of interest alongside irritability
    \item \textbf{Stress and burnout:} chronic pressure without a diagnosable disorder
    \item \textbf{Sleep disorders:} sleep deprivation and insomnia
    \item \textbf{Physical causes:} thyroid disease, chronic pain, and hormonal changes
    \item \textbf{Substances:} alcohol, caffeine, stimulants, and withdrawal
    \item \textbf{ADHD and autism:} can present with emotional dysregulation
    \item \textbf{Personality and adjustment difficulties:} patterns of emotional reactivity
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Help should be sought when irritability is persistent, severe, or affecting relationships, work, or school; when it is accompanied by low mood, anxiety, or thoughts of self-harm; or when it follows a stressful or traumatic event. Physical symptoms such as weight change, palpitations, or tremor warrant medical review. Families and colleagues can encourage support-seeking in someone whose irritability has changed their behaviour.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Thoughts of suicide or self-harm
    \item Aggression that places the person or others at risk
    \item Sudden severe confusion, agitation, or hallucinations
    \item Racing heart, tremor, and confusion with fever, which may indicate a medical emergency
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} exploring mood, stress, sleep, substances, and life circumstances
    \item \textbf{Screening tools:} validated questionnaires for anxiety and depression
    \item \textbf{Physical examination and blood tests:} thyroid function and other tests as indicated
    \item \textbf{Review of medicines and substances:} caffeine, alcohol, and medications
    \item \textbf{Sleep assessment:} patterns of sleep and insomnia
    \item \textbf{Assessment of stress and trauma:} work, relationships, and life events
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to psychology, psychiatry, or counselling services
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Self-care:} regular sleep, physical activity, healthy eating, and time out
    \item \textbf{Stress management:} relaxation, mindfulness, breathing exercises, and pacing
    \item \textbf{Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT):} the first-line psychological treatment for anxiety and depression
    \item \textbf{Reducing stimulants:} cutting caffeine and alcohol
    \item \textbf{Treating the cause:} thyroid, pain, hormonal, or substance-related contributors
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} antidepressants or other medicines for anxiety and depression where indicated
    \item \textbf{Communication skills:} learning to express frustration constructively
    \item \textbf{Support:} talking to trusted people, peer support, and counselling
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Strained relationships and conflict at home and work
    \item Reduced work and study performance
    \item Social withdrawal and isolation
    \item Depression and anxiety disorders if untreated
    \item Substance use as a way of coping
    \item Effects on children and families living with a chronically irritable parent
    \item Rarely, aggression and harm when irritability escalates
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For most people, irritability and feeling on edge settle as stress reduces, sleep improves, and self-care is restored. When an anxiety or depressive disorder is present, psychological treatment and, where needed, medicines are highly effective, with most people improving significantly within weeks to months. Addressing physical causes such as thyroid disease produces rapid improvement. The sooner support is sought, the better the outcome, and most people return to their usual selves with appropriate care.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Protect sleep: aim for consistent, adequate rest
    \item Exercise regularly to release tension
    \item Reduce caffeine, alcohol, and stimulants
    \item Practise relaxation and breathing when tense
    \item Notice early signs and take breaks before reaching breaking point
    \item Talk to someone trusted about what is bothering you
    \item Limit time on social media and news that fuels agitation
    \item Seek help early if irritability is persistent or affecting your life
    \item If you are caring for someone irritable, encourage them and set boundaries kindly
\end{itemize}
