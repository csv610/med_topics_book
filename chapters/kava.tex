\chapter{Kava}
\index{Kava}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Kava is a drink made from the roots of the kava plant (Piper methysticum), native to the Pacific Islands, where it has been used for centuries in ceremonies and social gatherings for its calming effects. It contains compounds called kavalactones, which reduce anxiety and promote relaxation. In many countries, kava is regulated, and its use has been a concern in some Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, where heavy use has been associated with harm. Kava use can cause liver damage, and it affects driving and coordination. This chapter explains what kava is, its effects, risks, and the advice for anyone using or considering it.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Traditional kava use in Pacific cultures is low-dose and ceremonial, but heavy, non-traditional use can be harmful. In many countries, kava use has been reported particularly in some northern Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, prompting the federal government to regulate its import and use. Kava is increasingly sold in many countries as a supplement for anxiety and sleep, although its safety and quality vary. Globally, kava supplements have been linked to liver injury, leading to restrictions in some countries.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Kava's active ingredients, the kavalactones, act on the brain to produce sedation, muscle relaxation, and reduced anxiety, similar in some ways to benzodiazepine medicines. The effects come on within about 20 to 30 minutes and last for a few hours. Heavy or prolonged use can damage the liver, causing hepatitis and, rarely, liver failure, and it can also cause scaly skin changes, weight loss, and malnutrition. Kava impairs coordination and reaction time, increasing the risk of accidents, and it interacts with alcohol, sedatives, and other medicines. Its effects on the brain mean it can cause dependence-like patterns in heavy users.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Relaxation, calmness, and reduced anxiety shortly after drinking
    \item Drowsiness and muscle relaxation
    \item Numbness of the mouth and tongue with some preparations
    \item Impaired coordination and reaction time
    \item With heavy use: weight loss, poor appetite, and skin changes
    \item Signs of liver damage: jaundice, dark urine, fatigue, and abdominal pain
    \item Interactions with alcohol and other sedatives
    \item Effects on work, driving, and daily function
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Symptoms related to kava use may have other causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Liver disease:} viral hepatitis, alcohol-related, and other causes
    \item \textbf{Other substances:} alcohol and sedative medicines causing similar effects
    \item \textbf{Malnutrition:} from poor diet or other causes
    \item \textbf{Anxiety and mood disorders:} which people may self-treat with kava
    \item \textbf{Skin conditions:} other causes of scaling and rash
    \item \textbf{Neurological conditions:} causing weakness or coordination problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with symptoms of liver damage, such as jaundice, dark urine, or persistent fatigue, should be assessed urgently, especially if using kava. People using kava regularly, or combining it with alcohol or medicines, should discuss this with their doctor. Seek help for dependence or for anyone concerned about their use.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or unconsciousness after using kava or mixing it with alcohol
    \item Signs of liver failure: jaundice, vomiting, confusion, or bleeding
    \item Difficulty breathing or collapse
    \item Severe abdominal pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} pattern of kava use, dose, and concurrent substances
    \item \textbf{Liver function tests:} essential in anyone using kava regularly
    \item \textbf{Clotting tests:} for advanced liver disease
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} ultrasound of the liver where damage is suspected
    \item \textbf{Review of other medicines and substances:} interactions and contributions
    \item \textbf{Mental health assessment:} for anxiety and dependence
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reducing or stopping kava:} the most important step if harms are occurring
    \item \textbf{Treatment of liver injury:} medical assessment and monitoring
    \item \textbf{Management of withdrawal and dependence:} support and counselling
    \item \textbf{Treatment of underlying conditions:} anxiety and other health problems
    \item \textbf{Nutritional support:} for weight loss and malnutrition
    \item \textbf{Avoiding alcohol and sedatives:} which worsen the effects
    \item \textbf{Education:} about the risks, especially for communities and young people
    \item \textbf{Regulatory awareness:} understanding the legal status of kava
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Liver damage, including hepatitis and liver failure
    \item Malnutrition and weight loss
    \item Skin changes with heavy use
    \item Accidents and injuries from impaired coordination
    \item Interactions with alcohol and medicines
    \item Dependence and effects on work, family, and community
    \item Economic costs of heavy use
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most kava-related harm resolves when use stops, including liver changes in many people, though severe liver injury can be fatal. The traditional, low-dose use in Pacific cultures is generally safe, and the harms in many countries are linked to heavy, non-traditional consumption, often with alcohol. Early recognition of harm, reducing use, and treating complications lead to good outcomes, and community education and support programs can prevent heavy use.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Limit kava use and avoid heavy, daily consumption
    \item Never mix kava with alcohol or sedative medicines
    \item Do not drive or operate machinery after using kava
    \item Be aware of the signs of liver damage and seek help early
    \item If you have liver disease or take medicines, avoid kava
    \item Do not use kava during pregnancy or breastfeeding
    \item Seek help for anxiety or sleep problems through proper channels
    \item Support community programs that address kava harm
    \item Follow the law on kava possession and use
\end{itemize}
