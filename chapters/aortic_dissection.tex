\chapter{Aortic Dissection}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Aortic dissection is a life-threatening medical emergency in which a tear develops in the inner lining (intima) of the aorta, the body's largest artery. Blood surges through the tear into the wall of the artery, splitting its layers apart and creating a second channel -- a \textbf{false lumen} -- alongside the normal lumen. The dissection can then extend along the aorta, block the origins of branch arteries, weaken the wall so that it ruptures, or leak blood into the space around the heart.

Dissections that involve the first part of the aorta, in the chest (Stanford type A), are the most dangerous and require emergency surgery. Dissections that begin beyond the origins of the arm vessels (type B) are sometimes managed without immediate surgery. Because the presentation can mimic a heart attack, and because minutes matter, aortic dissection is one of the classic ``can't-miss'' diagnoses in emergency medicine.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Aortic dissection is uncommon but not rare, estimated to affect roughly 3 to 4 people per 100,000 per year. It is two to three times more common in men than in women and occurs most often between the ages of 60 and 80. Chronic high blood pressure is the single most important risk factor.

In younger people, and especially in younger women, dissection is more often linked to a connective tissue disorder such as Marfan syndrome, a bicuspid aortic valve, familial aortopathy, or pregnancy. Because the condition is frequently fatal if untreated and is easily misdiagnosed, a high index of suspicion is important in any patient with sudden severe chest, back, or abdominal pain.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Dissection occurs when the aortic wall fails under the combined effects of structural weakness and the forces of blood flow and blood pressure:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High blood pressure:} chronic hypertension weakens the aortic media and provides the driving force for the tear
    \item \textbf{Connective tissue disorders:} Marfan syndrome, Ehlers--Danlos syndromes, and Loeys--Dietz syndrome make the aortic wall abnormally fragile
    \item \textbf{Bicuspid aortic valve:} associated with an enlarged, structurally weaker ascending aorta
    \item \textbf{Inherited aortopathy:} families with unexplained dissection, aneurysm, or sudden death at a young age
    \item \textbf{Vasculitis:} inflammatory diseases such as giant cell arteritis and Takayasu arteritis can damage the wall
    \item \textbf{Stimulant drugs:} cocaine and amphetamines can precipitate dissection through surges in blood pressure and heart rate
    \item \textbf{Trauma and procedures:} blunt chest injury, or damage during cardiac surgery or arterial catheterisation
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} a rare but recognised trigger, most often in the third trimester or around delivery, partly related to hormonal changes in the aortic wall
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe, tearing or ripping pain in the chest, back, or abdomen, often described as the worst pain ever experienced
    \item Pain that migrates or changes location as the dissection extends along the aorta
    \item Fainting, collapse, or near-syncope
    \item A difference in blood pressure between the two arms
    \item Weak or absent pulses in one limb
    \item Signs of pericardial tamponade: breathlessness, low blood pressure, distended neck veins, and a rapid pulse
    \item Symptoms of blocked branch arteries: stroke-like weakness, chest pain from coronary involvement, abdominal pain, or an acutely ischaemic leg
    \item A new heart murmur if the dissection tears the aortic valve or valve ring
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute myocardial infarction:} chest pain with ECG changes; the two can coexist, and a dissection may occlude a coronary artery
    \item \textbf{Pulmonary embolism:} sudden breathlessness and chest pain
    \item \textbf{Ruptured or symptomatic aortic aneurysm}
    \item \textbf{Pericarditis:} pleuritic chest pain that improves sitting forward
    \item \textbf{Musculoskeletal chest and back pain:} muscular strain, costochondritis, or spinal problems
    \item \textbf{Abdominal emergencies:} pancreatitis, cholecystitis, perforated ulcer, or mesenteric ischaemia
    \item \textbf{Stroke:} when the dissection occludes the cerebral vessels
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Aortic dissection is a time-critical emergency. Anyone with sudden severe chest, back, or abdominal pain -- especially with a tearing quality, fainting, or neurological symptoms -- needs immediate hospital assessment. Do not wait to see whether the pain settles.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe, tearing pain in the chest, back, or abdomen
    \item Collapse, fainting, or feeling faint with the pain
    \item Sudden weakness, numbness, facial droop, or difficulty speaking
    \item Breathlessness, a rapid or irregular pulse, or cold, clammy skin
    \item New chest or back pain in a person with Marfan syndrome, a bicuspid valve, or a family history of aortic disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CT angiography:} the fastest and most reliable test, showing the intimal tear and the full extent of the dissection
    \item \textbf{Transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE):} an ultrasound probe passed into the oesophagus, useful in unstable patients and at the bedside
    \item \textbf{MR angiography:} detailed imaging, used mainly in stable patients and for surveillance
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} may show a widened mediastinum or abnormal aortic contour, but cannot rule dissection in or out
    \item \textbf{ECG:} to help distinguish dissection from myocardial infarction
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} troponin to assess cardiac involvement, D-dimer as a negative predictor when the risk is low, and renal function before imaging with contrast
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} blood pressure in both arms, examination of all pulses, and auscultation for aortic regurgitation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Type A dissection:} emergency open-heart surgery to replace the torn segment of the aorta and restore normal flow, often with repair of the aortic valve
    \item \textbf{Type B dissection:} usually managed initially with intensive intravenous blood pressure control and pain relief, with endovascular stent grafting or surgery if complications such as rupture, malperfusion, or persistent pain develop
    \item \textbf{Pain control:} effective analgesia is both humane and a treatment target, because pain drives blood pressure up
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure control:} beta-blockers first, then other agents, to reduce wall stress in the acute phase and long term
    \item \textbf{Long-term medical therapy:} lifelong antihypertensive treatment, usually including a beta-blocker, to protect the aorta
    \item \textbf{Surveillance:} regular CT or MRI to monitor the aorta for aneurysm formation, further dissection, or enlargement of the false lumen
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} avoiding heavy lifting and strenuous straining, and management of connective tissue disorder complications under specialist care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rupture of the aorta with catastrophic internal bleeding
    \item Pericardial tamponade, in which blood compresses the heart and can cause cardiac arrest
    \item Stroke from occlusion of the carotid or vertebral arteries
    \item Acute myocardial infarction from involvement of the coronary ostia
    \item Kidney failure, bowel ischaemia, or limb loss if branch arteries are blocked (malperfusion)
    \item Spinal cord ischaemia with paraplegia
    \item Aortic regurgitation from disruption of the valve
    \item Late complications: aneurysm formation, re-dissection, and enlargement of the residual dissected aorta
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Without treatment, a type A dissection is rapidly fatal; most affected people die within hours to days from tamponade, rupture, or malperfusion. Prompt emergency surgery greatly improves survival, although it remains a high-risk operation and a substantial minority do not survive. Type B dissections managed medically often do well in the short term but can develop late complications such as aneurysm formation.

Survivors need lifelong blood pressure control and regular imaging of the aorta. With careful follow-up, many people live for years with a good quality of life after recovery. The key to improving outcomes is the same everywhere: recognising the diagnosis early and treating it urgently.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Control high blood pressure, and have it checked regularly even when you feel well
    \item Never smoke, and maintain a healthy weight and activity level
    \item Avoid stimulant drugs such as cocaine and amphetamines
    \item If you have Marfan syndrome, a bicuspid valve, or a family history of dissection, stay under specialist review with regular aortic imaging
    \item Genetic testing and screening of relatives is advised for families with connective tissue disorders or unexplained sudden death
    \item Follow cardiology advice about activity limits, including avoiding heavy lifting
    \item Get urgent help immediately for sudden severe chest or back pain -- early treatment saves lives
\end{itemize}
