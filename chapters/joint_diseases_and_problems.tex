\chapter{Joint Diseases and Problems}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Joint diseases and problems are conditions that affect the joints, the places where bones meet, causing pain, swelling, stiffness, and reduced movement. The most common is osteoarthritis, a wear-and-tear condition of the cartilage that affects millions of Australians, especially with age. Other important causes include inflammatory arthritis such as rheumatoid arthritis and gout, infections, and injuries. Because the causes differ greatly in their treatment, accurate diagnosis matters: osteoarthritis is managed with exercise, weight control, and pain relief, while inflammatory arthritis requires medicines that suppress inflammation. This chapter provides an overview of the common joint conditions, how they are assessed, and the principles of treatment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Joint problems are among the most common reasons for seeing a doctor, and osteoarthritis affects around 2.2 million Australians, with the knee and hip most affected. It is more common in women, in older people, and in those who are overweight or have had joint injuries. Rheumatoid arthritis affects around 1 per cent of the population and is more common in women. Gout affects around 7 per cent of Australian men and is increasing. Many joint problems are chronic, and their burden includes pain, disability, reduced work capacity, and costs to the health system.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The joint is made of cartilage-covered bone ends, a lubricating lining (synovium), and supporting ligaments and tendons. Osteoarthritis develops when the cartilage wears down faster than it can repair, from age, load, injury, obesity, and genetics, leaving bone to rub on bone and causing pain, stiffness, and bone spurs. Inflammatory arthritis occurs when the immune system attacks the joint lining, as in rheumatoid arthritis, causing swelling and damage that medicines can prevent. Gout results from uric acid crystals in the joint causing intense inflammation. Infection, or septic arthritis, is a medical emergency that destroys cartilage quickly. Understanding which process is at work is essential, because the treatments differ.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Joint pain, which may be worse with use (osteoarthritis) or with rest (inflammatory arthritis)
    \item Morning stiffness lasting more than 30 minutes, typical of inflammatory arthritis
    \item Swelling, warmth, and redness over the joint
    \item Reduced range of movement and difficulty with daily tasks
    \item Grating or clicking sensations in osteoarthritis
    \item Sudden severe pain in a single joint, as in gout or infection
    \item Fever and a hot, swollen joint, which may indicate septic arthritis
    \item Fatigue and systemic symptoms in inflammatory arthritis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The type of joint problem must be identified for correct treatment.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Osteoarthritis:} gradual, use-related pain in weight-bearing joints
    \item \textbf{Rheumatoid arthritis:} symmetrical joint swelling with morning stiffness
    \item \textbf{Gout and pseudogout:} sudden flares of severe joint pain
    \item \textbf{Psoriatic and reactive arthritis:} inflammatory arthritis with skin or preceding infection
    \item \textbf{Septic arthritis:} a hot, swollen, painful joint with fever, requiring urgent treatment
    \item \textbf{Injury:} sprains, strains, and fractures
    \item \textbf{Other conditions:} bursitis, tendinopathy, fibromyalgia, and referred pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Joint pain that is persistent, worsening, or affecting function should be assessed. Urgent care is needed for a hot, red, swollen joint, especially with fever, as this may be septic arthritis, and for pain after significant injury. People with symptoms suggesting inflammatory arthritis should be referred early, because early treatment prevents joint damage.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A hot, swollen, red joint with fever, which may indicate septic arthritis
    \item Severe joint pain after an injury, with deformity or inability to move the joint
    \item Sudden severe chest pain or breathlessness in a person with inflammatory arthritis, which may indicate a blood clot
    \item Collapse, confusion, or severe illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} which joints are affected, the pattern of pain and stiffness, and risk factors
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} inflammatory markers, rheumatoid factor, and other antibodies
    \item \textbf{Uric acid:} for gout
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} for osteoarthritis, fractures, and joint damage
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound and MRI:} for soft tissue, inflammation, and early damage
    \item \textbf{Joint aspiration:} removing fluid from a joint for gout, infection, and inflammatory causes
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a rheumatologist or orthopaedic surgeon as indicated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Exercise:} the cornerstone for osteoarthritis, including strength and aerobic training
    \item \textbf{Weight management:} reducing load on weight-bearing joints
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple analgesics and topical or oral anti-inflammatories
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} strengthening, stretching, and mobility programs
    \item \textbf{Disease-modifying medicines:} for rheumatoid and other inflammatory arthritis
    \item \textbf{Gout treatment:} medicines to settle flares and lower uric acid
    \item \textbf{Joint injections:} corticosteroid injections for flares
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} joint replacement for advanced osteoarthritis
    \item \textbf{Self-management programs:} education and support for chronic joint disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic pain and disability
    \item Joint destruction in untreated inflammatory arthritis
    \item Reduced mobility, falls, and fractures in older people
    \item Effects on work, independence, and quality of life
    \item Depression and anxiety with chronic pain
    \item Serious complications of infection and of treatment
    \item Side effects of long-term medicines
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the type of joint disease. Osteoarthritis is managed, not cured, but most people maintain good function with exercise, weight control, and pain management, and joint replacement is highly effective for advanced disease. Inflammatory arthritis is transformed by modern medicines, which control inflammation and prevent damage when started early. Gout is controllable with medicines and lifestyle change. Septic arthritis is curable with urgent treatment. Early diagnosis and appropriate care are the keys to good outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a healthy weight to protect the knees and hips
    \item Stay active with low-impact and strengthening exercise
    \item Protect joints from injury with proper technique and equipment
    \item See a doctor early for joint pain and swelling
    \item Take medicines as prescribed for inflammatory arthritis and gout
    \item Attend physiotherapy and rehabilitation
    \item Manage pain with heat, cold, and pacing of activities
    \item Seek urgent care for a hot, swollen joint
    \item Join self-management and arthritis support programs
\end{itemize}
