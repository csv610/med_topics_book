\section*{Definition and Overview}
A retained foreign body refers to any object unintentionally left within a body cavity, tissue, or surgical site after a procedure. The most serious category is the retained surgical item (RSI), which includes surgical instruments, sponges (gossypiboma/textiloma), needles, and other equipment left after surgery. Retained tampons, condoms, dental roots, ear and nasal foreign bodies, and ingested objects (batteries, magnets) are also common presentations. Retained surgical sponges are the most frequently reported RSI and a recognised preventable patient-safety event. Depending on location and composition, retained objects can cause local inflammation, infection, obstruction, perforation, or fistulation, sometimes remaining asymptomatic for years before incidental discovery.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Retained surgical items are reported in approximately 1 in 5,500 surgical operations, though true incidence is likely underestimated because many are never reported or identified. In Australia, RSIs are a leading patient-safety sentinel event monitored by state health authorities and the Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care. The highest rates occur in emergency procedures, prolonged operations, high body-mass-index patients, procedures with unexpected change in plan, and those involving multiple surgical teams. Retained vaginal tampons and condoms are common presentations to emergency departments and sexual-health clinics. Ingested foreign bodies in children (coins, small toys, button batteries) account for many emergency attendances; most pass spontaneously, but battery and magnet ingestion can be life-threatening.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Retained surgical items:} miscounting of sponges/instruments, use of radiolucent or non-counted materials, emergency or complex procedures, staff fatigue or changeover, hurried closure, high body-mass index limiting visualisation
    \item \textbf{Retained sponge (gossypiboma):} cotton matrix triggers either an exudative inflammatory response with abscess/fistula or an aseptic fibrotic reaction forming a granuloma
    \item \textbf{Barrier contraceptives:} forgotten vaginal tampons or condoms causing vaginal infection, toxic shock syndrome, or retained intrauterine device (IUD) fragments
    \item \textbf{Ingested objects:} coins, fish bones, button batteries (electrolyte burn and necrosis within hours), magnets (bowel wall ischaemia/perforation), sharp objects
    \item \textbf{Aspirated objects:} food bolus, teeth, small toys causing airway or oesophageal obstruction
    \item \textbf{Ear/nasal foreign bodies:} common in children and intellectually disabled adults; may cause otitis, sinusitis, or perforation
    \item \textbf{Dental/operative debris:} retained tooth roots, bone fragments, or broken needles after dental or surgical extraction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Retained surgical sponge:} may be asymptomatic for years; or pain, fever, palpable mass, abscess, wound infection, bowel obstruction, or fistula to skin/viscera
    \item \textbf{Retained tampon:} vaginal discharge, offensive odour, pelvic pain, fever; rarely staphylococcal toxic shock syndrome (fever, rash, hypotension, multi-organ failure)
    \item \textbf{Ingested button battery:} drooling, vomiting, chest or abdominal pain, dysphagia, fever, dark stools; high risk of oesophageal burn
    \item \textbf{Magnet ingestion:} abdominal pain, vomiting, distension, peritonism from bowel ischaemia/perforation
    \item \textbf{Oesophageal/gastric objects:} dysphagia, odynophagia, retrosternal pain, drooling
    \item \textbf{Ear foreign body:} ear pain, hearing loss, discharge, bleeding; nasal: unilateral purulent discharge, obstruction, epistaxis
    \item \textbf{Airway foreign body:} sudden cough, choking, wheeze, stridor, respiratory distress (often in children)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Postoperative:} surgical-site infection, haematoma, seroma, incisional hernia, anastomotic leak, bowel obstruction from adhesions
    \item \textbf{Retained tampon/condom:} pelvic inflammatory disease, bacterial vaginosis, vulvovaginitis, uterine fibroid, dysmenorrhoea
    \item \textbf{Large-bowel obstruction:} stool impaction, tumour, volvulus, diverticulitis
    \item \textbf{Ingested object in children:} gastroenteritis, intussusception, constipation, gastro-oesophageal reflux
    \item \textbf{Chronic sinusitis (nasal object):} allergic rhinitis, nasal polyp, tumour
    \item \textbf{Peritonitis:} appendicitis, perforated viscus, inflammatory bowel disease flare
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek prompt medical review for any suspected retained object. Urgent assessment is required after surgery with unexplained pain, fever, or wound problems, for disk or button-battery or multiple-magnet ingestion in children, and for suspected airway obstruction.

\textbf{Call 000 for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Choking or acute respiratory distress (suspected airway foreign body)
    \item Children who have swallowed a button battery or multiple magnets -- immediate
    \item Signs of bowel perforation or obstruction (severe abdominal pain, vomiting, distension, peritonism)
    \item Symptoms of toxic shock syndrome (high fever, rash, hypotension, confusion)
    \item Severe bleeding after surgery or from a body cavity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} timing and nature of procedure, known instrumentation, symptoms, type of object swallowed
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} plain X-ray for radio-opaque objects (sponges require radiopaque markers; gauze without markers may be visible as a mass); CT abdomen/pelvis for suspected RSI, gastrointestinal complications, or perforation
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} useful for superficial or pelvic retained foreign bodies and gossypiboma detection
    \item \textbf{Endoscopy:} gastroscopy/oesophagoscopy for ingested oesophageal or gastric objects; bronchoscopy for airway; vaginal speculum examination for tampons; otoscopy/rhinoscopy for ear/nasal objects
    \item \textbf{Bloods:} FBC, inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR) if infection suspected
    \item \textbf{Bowel obstruction workup:} erect and supine abdominal X-ray, CT with contrast if severe
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Retained surgical item:} formal surgical retrieval, ideally as soon as identified; report as a sentinel patient-safety event with root-cause analysis; detect and document the item to guide removal
    \item \textbf{Retained tampon:} removal in the emergency department or clinic with speculum; treat any infection (e.g., clindamycin/flucloxacillin for toxic shock syndrome, supportive care with fluids and vasopressors)
    \item \textbf{Ingested button battery:} endoscopic removal within 2 hours if in the oesophagus; honey or sucralfate may help if swallowed within the last 12 hours and object in oesophagus, only with advice
    \item \textbf{Magnets:} urgent endoscopic or surgical removal because of bowel ischaemia risk
    \item \textbf{Oesophageal object:} endoscopic retrieval; most gastric objects pass spontaneously -- monitor and repeat imaging
    \item \textbf{Ear/nasal objects:} removal with appropriate instruments (alligator forceps, suction) usually under direct vision; refer to ENT for impacted or posterior objects
    \item \textbf{Prophylactic antibiotics:} consider in perforation, vascular injury, or colorectal surgery with retained material
    \item \textbf{Antitetanus:} ensure tetanus immunisation up to date in penetrating injuries
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Abscess formation, intra-abdominal or pelvic sepsis
    \item Bowel obstruction, perforation, and peritonitis
    \item Fistula formation (enterocutaneous, vesicovaginal, rectovaginal)
    \item Toxic shock syndrome from retained tampons -- potentially fatal
    \item Oesophageal erosion, stricture, or mediastinitis from batteries
    \item Chronic sinusitis, otitis, or otitis externa from ear/nasal objects
    \item Adhesions and re-operation morbidity after surgical retrieval
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis depends on the object, location, and timeliness of removal. Most ingested objects that reach the stomach pass spontaneously without harm, and most ear/nasal and vaginal objects resolve with simple removal. Retained button batteries in the oesophagus carry a significant risk of severe complications, including oesophageal perforation, tracheo-oesophageal fistula, and death, especially in toddlers, when removal is delayed. Surgical sponges may cause chronic morbidity if discovered late, but outcomes are generally good after timely retrieval. Mortality from retained surgical items overall is low. Prevention through meticulous counting, intra-operative imaging, and checklists reduces incidence, though no single measure eliminates the risk entirely.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hospitals: standardised surgical counts, intra-operative X-ray screening for high-risk procedures, surgical safety checklists, and structured handover at closure
    \item Use radiologically detectable sponges and instruments throughout surgery
    \item Patients: remember tampons are for single-use and must be removed within 8 hours; set reminders
    \item Keep button batteries and small magnets out of reach of young children; check products for secure battery compartments
    \item Do not attempt to remove ear/nasal or deeply lodged objects with household tools
    \item For penetrating wounds, update tetanus immunisation
\end{itemize}
