\section*{Definition and Overview}
Heart failure, sometimes called congestive heart failure, means the heart is unable to pump blood effectively enough to meet the body's needs. It is not a single disease but a syndrome in which the heart muscle becomes weakened, stiff, or damaged, so that blood backs up in the lungs and body tissues. The term ``congestive'' refers to the fluid congestion that results, causing breathlessness and swelling. Heart failure may develop gradually over years or come on suddenly after an event such as a heart attack.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Heart failure is common and becomes more frequent with age. Roughly 1 in 100 people under 65 are affected, rising to around 1 in 10 people over 75 years. Coronary artery disease and high blood pressure are the leading underlying causes. The number of people living with heart failure is growing as populations age and as better treatments mean more people survive heart attacks. It is slightly more common in men than women, though women tend to develop it at an older age.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coronary artery disease:} narrowing of the heart's blood vessels, and heart attacks that damage the heart muscle, are the most common causes
    \item \textbf{High blood pressure:} long-standing hypertension forces the heart to work harder and can stiffen the heart muscle
    \item \textbf{Damaged heart valves:} leaky or narrowed valves overwork the heart
    \item \textbf{Cardiomyopathy:} disease of the heart muscle itself, which may be inherited, alcohol-related, or caused by certain medicines (e.g.\ some chemotherapy agents)
    \item \textbf{Rapid or irregular heart rhythms:} such as atrial fibrillation, which reduce pumping efficiency
    \item \textbf{Other causes:} thyroid disease, diabetes, anaemia, and infections of the heart muscle (myocarditis)
\end{itemize}

In heart failure, the weakened heart compensates by retaining salt and water, which increases blood volume and stretches the heart further. This sets up a vicious cycle: the heart becomes progressively less efficient, fluid builds up in the lungs and legs, and the body's organs receive less oxygen-rich blood.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Breathlessness, at first only on exertion and later at rest or when lying flat (orthopnoea)
    \item Waking at night gasping for breath (paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea)
    \item Swelling (oedema) of the ankles, feet, and legs that is worse in the evening
    \item Persistent fatigue, weakness, and reduced exercise tolerance
    \item A dry, persistent cough, sometimes with pink-tinged sputum
    \item Weight gain from fluid retention and a swollen abdomen
    \item Palpitations, poor appetite, and a sensation of fullness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic lung disease (e.g.\ COPD, asthma) causing breathlessness
    \item Obesity and poor physical fitness
    \item Anaemia, which causes fatigue and breathlessness
    \item Kidney disease or liver disease causing fluid retention
    \item Thyroid disease and other metabolic disorders
    \item Venous insufficiency causing ankle swelling
    \item Anxiety or panic attacks mimicking breathlessness
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor promptly if you develop persistent breathlessness, ankle swelling, or a reduced ability to exercise that is new or getting worse.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe breathlessness at rest or an inability to speak in full sentences
    \item Chest pain or pressure, especially with sweating or nausea
    \item Fainting, collapse, or a very rapid or very slow heartbeat
    \item Sudden worsening of swelling with weight gain over a day or two
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} symptoms, risk factors, and signs such as fluid in the lungs, ankle swelling, and a raised jugular vein in the neck
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} brain natriuretic peptide (BNP or NT-proBNP) is a key marker; full blood count, kidney and thyroid function are also checked
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} shows an enlarged heart and fluid in the lungs
    \item \textbf{ECG:} detects heart rhythm problems, previous heart attacks, and strain patterns
    \item \textbf{Echocardiogram (ultrasound of the heart):} measures the pumping strength (ejection fraction) and valve function, and distinguishes heart failure with reduced vs.\ preserved pumping function
    \item \textbf{Further tests as needed:} exercise stress testing, cardiac MRI, or coronary angiography
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} low-salt diet, fluid monitoring with daily weighing, smoking cessation, and individually tailored exercise
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} diuretics (water tablets) relieve congestion; ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, beta-blockers, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists reduce the strain on the heart; newer agents such as SGLT2 inhibitors may also be used
    \item \textbf{Device therapy:} an implantable defibrillator (ICD) or cardiac resynchronisation therapy (pacemaker) for selected patients with weak heart muscle
    \item \textbf{Cardiac rehabilitation:} a supervised programme of exercise and education
    \item \textbf{Surgery or procedures:} valve repair or replacement, and occasionally coronary bypass; transplant or mechanical pumps (ventricular assist devices) for advanced disease
    \item \textbf{Flu vaccination:} recommended, as infections can worsen heart failure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute pulmonary oedema, a medical emergency in which fluid floods the lungs
    \item Kidney damage from reduced blood flow and from diuretic treatment
    \item Abnormal heart rhythms, including dangerous ventricular arrhythmias
    \item Atrial fibrillation, which increases the risk of stroke
    \item Anaemia and worsening frailty
    \item Liver congestion and, in advanced disease, cardiac cachexia (muscle wasting)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Heart failure is a chronic condition that usually requires lifelong management, but many people live well for years with treatment. Outlook depends on the cause, how much pumping function is preserved, and how well treatment is followed. Those who take medicines as prescribed, keep fluid under control, and stay physically active within their limits generally have fewer hospital admissions and better quality of life. In advanced heart failure, prognosis is more limited, and palliative care can help manage symptoms and support patients and families.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Control high blood pressure, diabetes, and high cholesterol
    \item Avoid smoking and limit alcohol
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and stay physically active
    \item Eat a balanced diet and limit salt
    \item Take heart medicines exactly as prescribed and do not stop them without advice
    \item Weigh yourself daily and report a gain of 2 kilograms or more in a few days
    \item Keep up to date with flu and pneumococcal vaccinations
    \item Seek medical advice promptly for worsening symptoms
\end{itemize}
