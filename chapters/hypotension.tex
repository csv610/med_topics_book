\chapter{Hypotension}
\index{Hypotension}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hypotension is the medical term for low blood pressure. For most people, a blood pressure below 90/60 mmHg is considered low, but what matters most is whether symptoms occur. Many healthy people, especially the young and fit, have low blood pressure with no symptoms and excellent health. Hypotension becomes a problem when it causes dizziness, light-headedness, fainting, or reduced blood flow to organs. Causes include dehydration, blood loss, medications, heart problems, and conditions such as postural (orthostatic) hypotension. Treatment depends on the cause and the severity of symptoms.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Low blood pressure is common, particularly in young, healthy adults and athletes, where it is usually harmless. Postural hypotension, a drop in pressure on standing, affects up to 20\% of older adults and is a major cause of falls. It is more common in people taking blood pressure medicines, with diabetes, and with neurological conditions such as Parkinson's disease. Fainting from hypotension is common, and in older people it is associated with falls, fractures, and hospitalisation.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dehydration:} reduced blood volume from poor intake, sweating, or illness
    \item \textbf{Blood loss:} acute or chronic bleeding
    \item \textbf{Medications:} blood pressure medicines, diuretics, and antidepressants
    \item \textbf{Postural hypotension:} the body fails to adjust blood pressure on standing
    \item \textbf{Heart conditions:} heart failure, arrhythmias, and valve disease
    \item \textbf{Endocrine causes:} thyroid disease, adrenal insufficiency, and low blood sugar
    \item \textbf{Neurological causes:} autonomic dysfunction in diabetes and Parkinson's disease
    \item \textbf{Sepsis and shock:} dangerous causes of severe hypotension
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} blood pressure falls when cardiac output or vascular resistance is inadequate
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dizziness and light-headedness, especially on standing
    \item Fainting or near-fainting
    \item Blurred vision and weakness
    \item Fatigue and difficulty concentrating
    \item Cold, pale, or clammy skin
    \item Nausea
    \item In severe cases: confusion, rapid breathing, and reduced consciousness
    \item Many people with low blood pressure have no symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Asymptomatic low blood pressure, which needs no treatment
    \item Postural hypotension, diagnosed by measuring pressure lying and standing
    \item Dehydration and blood loss
    \item Medication-related drops in pressure
    \item Cardiac, endocrine, and neurological causes
    \item Sepsis and shock, which are emergencies
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for recurring dizziness or fainting, or for low blood pressure that affects daily life. Seek urgent care for fainting with injury, chest pain, or severe illness.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fainting with collapse or injury
    \item Dizziness with chest pain, palpitations, or breathlessness
    \item Bleeding with hypotension
    \item Confusion, fever, and low blood pressure (possible sepsis)
    \item Vomiting blood or black stools
    \item Loss of consciousness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure measurement:} lying, sitting, and standing
    \item \textbf{Ambulatory monitoring:} 24-hour blood pressure recording
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, electrolytes, kidney function, and thyroid
    \item \textbf{ECG and heart tests:} for arrhythmias and heart disease
    \item \textbf{Medication review:} blood pressure and other medicines
    \item \textbf{Assessment of fainting:} history, triggers, and eyewitness accounts
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asymptomatic hypotension:} no treatment needed
    \item \textbf{Dehydration:} increased fluids and electrolytes
    \item \textbf{Postural hypotension:} rising slowly, compression stockings, and adequate fluids
    \item \textbf{Medication adjustment:} reducing or changing blood pressure medicines with medical advice
    \item \textbf{Salt:} increased dietary salt under medical guidance
    \item \textbf{Treatment of the cause:} heart, endocrine, and neurological conditions
    \item \textbf{Shock:} emergency hospital treatment with fluids and support
    \item \textbf{Falls prevention:} addressing the risk of falls in older people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Falls, fractures, and head injuries from fainting
    \item Reduced quality of life from dizziness
    \item Reduced blood flow to organs in severe cases
    \item Kidney injury and cardiac events in shock
    \item Injuries from fainting while driving or operating machinery
    \item Social withdrawal and fear of fainting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For most people, low blood pressure is harmless and requires no treatment. Symptomatic hypotension is usually managed effectively by addressing the cause: fluids, medication adjustment, and lifestyle measures help most people. Falls from postural hypotension are reduced with simple precautions and treatment. Severe hypotension from bleeding or sepsis is a medical emergency that responds well to prompt hospital care. The outlook depends on the cause, which is usually treatable.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Drink enough fluids, especially in heat and with exercise
    \item Rise slowly from lying or sitting
    \item Avoid standing still for long periods
    \item Use compression stockings if advised
    \item Review medicines with your doctor
    \item Avoid alcohol, which lowers blood pressure
    \item Learn to recognise early symptoms of fainting
    \item Seek review for recurring symptoms
\end{itemize}
