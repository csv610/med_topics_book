\chapter{Hypoglycaemia (Low Blood Sugar)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hypoglycaemia is the medical term for low blood sugar, when the blood glucose level falls below the normal range, typically below 4 mmol/L. It occurs most often in people with diabetes who take insulin or certain diabetes medicines, but it can also affect people without diabetes in specific situations. Hypoglycaemia causes symptoms such as sweating, tremor, hunger, and confusion, and severe episodes can cause seizures, unconsciousness, and require emergency treatment. Recognising and treating low blood sugar quickly is essential, and most episodes are managed simply with fast-acting carbohydrate.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hypoglycaemia is the most common acute complication of diabetes treatment, affecting most people with type 1 diabetes and many with type 2 diabetes on certain medicines. People with diabetes may experience several episodes a year, and severe episodes requiring help from others occur in a significant minority. Hypoglycaemia can be dangerous at night and while driving. With education, monitoring, and careful medicine adjustment, most episodes are preventable.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Insulin:} too much insulin, whether too large a dose or mistimed, lowers blood sugar
    \item \textbf{Sulfonylureas:} some diabetes tablets can cause hypoglycaemia
    \item \textbf{Missed meals:} skipping meals or eating less than planned
    \item \textbf{Exercise:} increased activity uses more glucose
    \item \textbf{Alcohol:} drinking without food can cause late hypoglycaemia
    \item \textbf{Illness:} changes in intake and metabolism
    \item \textbf{In people without diabetes:} rare causes include certain medicines, hormone deficiency, and insulin-producing tumours
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} blood glucose falls below the brain's needs, causing impaired brain function
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Early symptoms: sweating, tremor, hunger, palpitations, and anxiety
    \item Later symptoms: confusion, difficulty concentrating, slurred speech, and irritability
    \item Headache and fatigue
    \item Severe hypoglycaemia: seizures, unconsciousness, and loss of ability to treat oneself
    \item Symptoms can vary between episodes and between people
    \item Nocturnal hypoglycaemia causes night sweats and morning headaches
    \item In people without diabetes, symptoms are less specific
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hypoglycaemia must be distinguished from other causes of confusion, collapse, and seizures
    \item Panic and anxiety can mimic early symptoms
    \item In people without diabetes, reactive hypoglycaemia and other rare causes are considered
    \item Blood glucose measurement confirms the diagnosis
    \item The response to treatment with glucose helps confirm it
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with diabetes should follow their hypoglycaemia treatment plan, and frequent or severe episodes need medical review to adjust treatment.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seizures, unconsciousness, or inability to swallow
    \item Confusion that does not improve with treatment
    \item An unresponsive person known to have diabetes
    \item Hypoglycaemia with chest pain or collapse
    \item Repeated episodes despite treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood glucose testing:} finger-prick testing confirms low blood sugar
    \item \textbf{Continuous glucose monitors:} detect lows and trends in people with diabetes
    \item \textbf{Review of treatment:} insulin and medicine doses are assessed
    \item \textbf{HbA1c:} overall glucose control
    \item \textbf{In people without diabetes:} fasting tests and further investigations for rare causes
    \item \textbf{Education review:} recognition and treatment skills
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rule of 15:} take 15 g of fast-acting carbohydrate, recheck after 15 minutes, and repeat if still low
    \item \textbf{Fast-acting options:} glucose tablets, juice, or sugary drinks
    \item \textbf{Follow with food:} a longer-acting carbohydrate snack after recovery
    \item \textbf{Severe episodes:} glucagon injection or intravenous glucose by emergency services
    \item \textbf{Medicine adjustment:} reduce insulin or change medicines with medical advice
    \item \textbf{Prevention planning:} adjust food, activity, and alcohol with diabetes education
    \item \textbf{Driving safety:} check blood glucose before driving
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seizures and unconsciousness
    \item Falls and injuries during episodes
    \item Motor vehicle accidents
    \item Hypoglycaemia unawareness: loss of warning symptoms after repeated episodes
    \item Cognitive effects of severe or repeated episodes
    \item Anxiety and fear of hypoglycaemia
    \item Rarely, death in severe untreated episodes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most hypoglycaemia episodes are managed quickly and fully recovered from. With education, monitoring, and treatment adjustment, the frequency of episodes falls substantially, and severe episodes can usually be prevented. Continuous glucose monitoring and modern insulins have reduced hypoglycaemia in people with diabetes. The key is recognition, prompt treatment, and learning from each episode to prevent the next.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Always carry fast-acting glucose
    \item Check blood glucose regularly, including before driving
    \item Eat regular meals and match food to insulin
    \item Adjust for exercise and alcohol with advice
    \item Recognise your early warning symptoms
    \item Wear a medical alert if you have frequent severe episodes
    \item Tell family and colleagues how to help
    \item Review treatment with your diabetes team after episodes
\end{itemize}
