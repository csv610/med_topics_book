\chapter{Fistula}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A fistula is an abnormal passage that connects two body surfaces or spaces that are not normally connected, such as between two organs, or between an organ and the skin. Fistulas most commonly occur around the anus (anal fistulas), but they can also form between the bowel and bladder, the vagina and bladder or rectum (obstetric fistulas), or between blood vessels and organs (arteriovenous fistulas). The causes include infection and abscess, inflammatory bowel disease such as Crohn's disease, childbirth trauma, surgery, radiotherapy, and cancer. A fistula rarely heals on its own and usually requires treatment, which may involve drainage, antibiotics, medicines, or surgery. This chapter explains how fistulas form, the types, and the treatment options.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Anal fistulas are the most common type, affecting around 1--2 in 10,000 people per year, more often men, and typically arising after an anal abscess. Fistulas occur in up to half of people with Crohn's disease at some point, and perianal fistulas are a major source of illness in that group. Obstetric fistulas, which were historically common where childbirth care was unavailable, still affect hundreds of thousands of women in low-resource countries, though they are very rare in Australia. Arteriovenous fistulas are deliberately created for dialysis access in thousands of Australians. Fistulas cause substantial discomfort, discharge, and reduced quality of life, but most are successfully treated.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infection and abscess:} an anal gland infection forms an abscess that drains, creating a track from the anal canal to the skin
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory bowel disease:} Crohn's disease causes deep inflammation that burrows through the bowel wall, creating fistulas between loops of bowel, the bladder, or the skin
    \item \textbf{Childbirth:} prolonged or obstructed labour can damage the tissues between the vagina and bladder or rectum, causing obstetric fistulas
    \item \textbf{Surgery and radiotherapy:} pelvic surgery and radiotherapy for cancer can injure tissues and lead to fistulas
    \item \textbf{Cancer:} advanced pelvic cancers can erode into adjacent organs
    \item \textbf{Diverticulitis and other infections:} can create fistulas from the bowel to other organs
    \item \textbf{Mechanism of harm:} the abnormal connection allows contents such as stool, urine, or digestive fluid to leak into places where they cause infection, inflammation, and continuous discharge
    \item \textbf{Classification:} fistulas are described by their location and track, which determines the surgical approach
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms depend on the type and location of the fistula.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Anal fistulas: persistent discharge of pus or blood from an opening near the anus, itching, pain, and recurrent abscesses
    \item Rectovaginal and vesicovaginal fistulas: leakage of stool or gas through the vagina, or continuous leakage of urine
    \item Enterocutaneous fistulas: drainage of bowel contents through an opening in the skin of the abdomen
    \item Colovesical fistulas: air or stool in the urine, recurrent urinary infections, and cloudy or foul-smelling urine
    \item General features: pain, swelling, recurrent infections, and the social and psychological impact of uncontrolled leakage
    \item In Crohn's disease: fistulas often coexist with active bowel inflammation, pain, and weight loss
    \item An arteriovenous fistula used for dialysis is a planned connection that is not harmful and is essential to treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Conditions with similar symptoms must be distinguished.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Anal fissure, haemorrhoids, and pilonidal sinus can mimic anal fistulas
    \item Perianal abscess without a fistula track
    \item Proctitis and other inflammatory conditions of the rectum
    \item Urinary incontinence, which can be confused with vesicovaginal fistula
    \item Vaginal discharge from infection or prolapse
    \item Diverticulitis and other causes of recurrent urinary infection
    \item The diagnosis of a fistula is usually confirmed by examination and imaging
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with persistent anal discharge, recurrent abscesses, leakage of urine or stool, or air in the urine should see a doctor. Early diagnosis of anal fistulas improves treatment options and outcomes.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe pain, high fever, and swelling in the anal or perineal region, which may indicate a spreading infection (Fournier gangrene in severe cases)
    \item Signs of sepsis: fever, confusion, rapid heart rate, and collapse
    \item Sudden severe abdominal pain with vomiting
    \item Any emergency symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Examination:} the external opening is often visible, and a gentle examination with a finger can define the track
    \item \textbf{Anoscopy and proctoscopy:} visualisation of the anal canal and rectum
    \item \textbf{MRI:} the best imaging for complex fistulas, showing the full track and its relationship to the sphincter muscles
    \item \textbf{Fistulography and ultrasound:} used in selected cases
    \item \textbf{Examination under anaesthesia:} allows the surgeon to trace the fistula completely
    \item \textbf{Investigations for underlying disease:} colonoscopy and inflammatory markers for suspected Crohn's disease; cystoscopy for bladder involvement
    \item \textbf{Culture of discharge:} guides antibiotic treatment when infection is present
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anal fistulas:} surgery is the mainstay; the track is opened (fistulotomy) or, if it passes through the sphincter, managed with a seton (a thread left in place to drain and allow healing) or a sphincter-preserving procedure such as a fistula plug or flap
    \item \textbf{Drainage:} acute abscesses are drained surgically to control infection before definitive treatment
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} for active infection, though they do not cure the fistula itself
    \item \textbf{Crohn's disease fistulas:} biological medicines such as infliximab heal many fistulas; surgery is used when medicines fail or for complex disease
    \item \textbf{Obstetric fistulas:} surgical repair with specialist care restores continence in the majority of women
    \item \textbf{Colovesical and enterocutaneous fistulas:} nutritional support, control of sepsis, and often surgery to remove and repair the involved bowel
    \item \textbf{Stoma:} a temporary colostomy or ileostomy may be used to divert stool while a complex fistula heals
    \item \textbf{Skin and wound care:} protecting the skin around the fistula opening and managing output with appropriate dressings or appliances
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Recurrent abscesses and persistent discharge without treatment
    \item Incontinence after surgery if the sphincter muscles are damaged, which is why specialist techniques are used
    \item Recurrence of the fistula, especially in Crohn's disease
    \item Sepsis from untreated infection
    \item Skin breakdown around the opening
    \item Malnutrition and fluid loss with high-output enterocutaneous fistulas
    \item The psychological and social burden of leakage and odour
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most fistulas are successfully treated. Simple anal fistulas heal in over 90\% of cases with surgery, and even complex fistulas heal in the majority with specialist techniques, though there is a risk of recurrence, particularly in Crohn's disease. Biological medicines heal many Crohn's fistulas and control the underlying disease. Obstetric fistulas are repaired successfully in most women with access to specialist care. Fistulas rarely heal without treatment, so the key to a good outcome is prompt specialist assessment and an individualised surgical and medical plan.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek early treatment of anal abscesses, which are the usual precursor of anal fistulas
    \item Maintain good perianal hygiene and keep the area clean and dry
    \item In Crohn's disease, adhere to treatment to control inflammation and reduce fistula risk
    \item Avoid straining and manage constipation, which aggravate perianal disease
    \item Protect the skin around a fistula opening with barrier creams and appropriate dressings
    \item Attend follow-up after surgery, and report recurrence of discharge or pain early
    \item Seek psychosocial support for the impact of fistula symptoms
\end{itemize}
