\chapter{Amenorrhoea}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Amenorrhoea is the absence of menstrual periods. It is divided into two categories: primary amenorrhoea, when a girl has not started her periods by about the age of 15 (or by age 13 with no other pubertal changes), and secondary amenorrhoea, when a woman who previously menstruated stops having periods for three or more consecutive months. Occasional missed periods are common and usually harmless, but persistent amenorrhoea can signal an underlying hormonal, anatomical, or medical problem and can affect fertility and bone health. Because normal menstruation depends on a chain of events linking the brain, pituitary gland, ovaries, and uterus, the cause may lie at any of these levels. Most causes are identifiable and treatable, and early assessment prevents complications such as bone loss.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Secondary amenorrhoea is common, affecting roughly 3 to 5\% of women at some point in their reproductive lives, while primary amenorrhoea affects about 1 in 500 girls. The most common causes vary with age and circumstance. In young women, low body weight, excessive exercise, and psychological stress predominate, whereas polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), which affects approximately 8 to 13\% of women of reproductive age, is one of the most frequent causes overall. Pregnancy and the menopause are the most common physiological causes of amenorrhoea, and long-acting hormonal contraception is a frequent cause of suppressed bleeding.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Normal periods depend on a coordinated hormonal chain linking the hypothalamus, the pituitary gland, the ovaries, and the uterus; disruption at any level can stop the cycle:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} the most common cause of secondary amenorrhoea in women of reproductive age.
    \item \textbf{Hypothalamic dysfunction:} stress, marked weight loss, low body weight, eating disorders, or excessive exercise suppress gonadotrophin-releasing hormone and the drive to ovulation.
    \item \textbf{Polycystic ovary syndrome:} a hormone imbalance with infrequent or absent ovulation and elevated androgens.
    \item \textbf{Thyroid and prolactin disorders:} hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism, and raised prolactin (hyperprolactinaemia), disrupt the menstrual cycle.
    \item \textbf{Premature ovarian insufficiency:} the ovaries stop functioning before the age of 40, often due to autoimmune or genetic causes.
    \item \textbf{Anatomical causes:} an absent uterus or vagina, an imperforate hymen, or scarring of the uterine lining (Asherman's syndrome).
    \item \textbf{Hormonal contraception:} progestogen-only and some combined methods suppress menstrual bleeding by design.
    \item \textbf{Menopause:} the natural end of menstruation, usually occurring around age 50.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The main feature is the absence of periods, but additional symptoms reflect the underlying cause:

\begin{itemize}
    \item No period by about age 15, or by age 13 with no breast development.
    \item Three or more missed periods in a row after previously regular cycles.
    \item Weight change, either loss or gain, or disordered eating.
    \item Acne, excess facial or body hair, and oily skin, suggesting PCOS.
    \item Headaches, visual disturbance, or milky nipple discharge (galactorrhoea), suggesting high prolactin.
    \item Hot flushes, night sweats, and vaginal dryness, suggesting ovarian insufficiency.
    \item Difficulty conceiving.
    \item Delayed or absent secondary sexual characteristics in primary amenorrhoea.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The cause is found by working systematically through each level of the reproductive system:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Pregnancy.
    \item Polycystic ovary syndrome.
    \item Hypothalamic causes, including stress, low weight, over-exercise, and eating disorders.
    \item Hyperprolactinaemia and pituitary disorders, including prolactinoma.
    \item Thyroid disease.
    \item Premature ovarian insufficiency and the menopause.
    \item Congenital anatomical abnormalities, such as an imperforate hymen or absent uterus or vagina.
    \item Asherman's syndrome after uterine surgery or infection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if periods have not started by about age 15, if you have missed three or more periods in a row, or if you are trying to conceive without success. Seek early review if periods stop together with headaches, visual changes, or milky nipple discharge.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services), particularly if pregnancy is possible, for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe abdominal or pelvic pain.
    \item Shoulder-tip pain with vaginal bleeding, which can signal an ectopic pregnancy.
    \item Heavy vaginal bleeding.
    \item Fainting, dizziness, or collapse.
    \item Severe headache with visual disturbance.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The first test in a woman of reproductive age is a pregnancy test; the rest of the assessment follows from there:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} age, growth and pubertal history, menstrual pattern, weight and exercise habits, medications, and the possibility of pregnancy.
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test:} always the first step in secondary amenorrhoea.
    \item \textbf{Examination:} height, weight, pubertal stage, and, where appropriate, pelvic examination.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} FSH, LH, prolactin, thyroid function, oestradiol, and, in selected cases, testosterone.
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} pelvic imaging of the uterus, ovaries, and endometrial lining.
    \item \textbf{Brain imaging (MRI):} if a pituitary cause such as a prolactinoma is suspected.
    \item \textbf{Bone density scan:} for prolonged amenorrhoea to assess bone health and the risk of osteoporosis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on the cause and on whether fertility is desired:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} balanced nutrition, a healthy weight, and moderating excessive exercise are the mainstay for hypothalamic causes.
    \item \textbf{PCOS:} weight management, metformin where indicated, and hormonal treatment to regulate cycles.
    \item \textbf{Hormonal therapy:} the combined oral contraceptive or cyclic progestogen to restore regular withdrawal bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Hyperprolactinaemia:} medication such as cabergoline or bromocriptine to lower prolactin and restore cycles.
    \item \textbf{Thyroid disorders:} appropriate thyroid hormone replacement or suppression.
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} for structural causes such as an imperforate hymen or Asherman's syndrome.
    \item \textbf{Fertility treatment:} ovulation induction for women wishing to conceive.
    \item \textbf{Bone protection:} adequate calcium and vitamin D, and oestrogen replacement when oestrogen is persistently low.
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} monitoring of cycles, symptoms, fertility plans, and bone density.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Prolonged amenorrhoea matters well beyond the absence of periods:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Infertility, depending on the cause and its treatment.
    \item Reduced bone density and an increased risk of fractures from low oestrogen.
    \item Endometrial hyperplasia and a higher risk of uterine cancer when cycles are infrequent.
    \item Osteoporosis in long-standing oestrogen deficiency.
    \item Psychological distress, including anxiety about fertility and body image.
    \item Complications of the underlying cause, such as a pituitary tumour.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the cause. Hypothalamic amenorrhoea from stress or weight loss usually resolves when the trigger is corrected, and periods typically return. PCOS is a long-term condition, but cycles and fertility often improve with weight loss and medical treatment. Premature ovarian insufficiency is not reversible, but hormone replacement protects bone health, and some women still conceive with assisted reproductive technology. Early assessment and treatment prevent complications such as bone loss and endometrial disease.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Not all causes are preventable, but lifestyle measures protect menstrual health:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and eat a balanced diet.
    \item Avoid rapid weight loss and under-fuelling heavy training.
    \item Manage stress and get adequate sleep.
    \item Track cycles so that changes are noticed early.
    \item Take adequate calcium and vitamin D.
    \item Do not leave long gaps between periods untreated.
    \item If using hormonal contraception, understand its expected effect on bleeding.
\end{itemize}
