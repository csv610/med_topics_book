\section*{Definition and Overview}
Compulsive disorders are a group of mental health conditions in which a person feels driven to perform repetitive behaviours or mental acts, called compulsions, often to relieve anxiety or prevent a feared outcome. They include obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), body dysmorphic disorder, hoarding disorder, and body-focused repetitive behaviours such as trichotillomania (hair pulling) and skin picking. In OCD, compulsions are usually performed in response to unwanted, intrusive thoughts or images (obsessions). The disorders cause significant distress and take up large amounts of time, even though the person often recognises the behaviour as excessive or unreasonable.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Obsessive-compulsive disorder affects roughly 1 in 50 people at some point in life, with onset most commonly in late childhood, adolescence, or early adulthood. It affects men and women fairly equally, although onset in childhood is more common in boys. Body dysmorphic disorder affects about 1--2\% of the population, and hoarding is thought to affect around 2--5\% of adults. These conditions are often under-recognised because people hide their symptoms out of shame.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Compulsive disorders arise from a combination of genetic, brain, and learning factors:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} the conditions run in families, and OCD is more likely in people with a first-degree relative who has it
    \item \textbf{Brain circuits:} overactivity in circuits linking the front of the brain with deeper structures (the cortico-striatal pathways) is linked to repetitive thoughts and behaviours
    \item \textbf{Chemical messengers:} imbalances in serotonin and related neurotransmitter systems are involved
    \item \textbf{Behavioural learning:} performing a compulsion briefly reduces the anxiety caused by an obsession, which reinforces the behaviour and makes it harder to stop
    \item \textbf{Stress and adversity:} stressful life events and childhood adversity can trigger or worsen symptoms
    \item \textbf{Rare triggers:} a small number of children develop sudden OCD-like symptoms after certain infections (PANDAS/PANS)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Obsessions:} recurrent, unwanted thoughts, images, or urges (e.g.\ fears of contamination, harm, or things not being ordered)
    \item \textbf{Compulsions:} repetitive behaviours (e.g.\ washing, checking, counting, ordering, repeating) or mental rituals (e.g.\ praying, counting silently)
    \item Symptoms that take more than about an hour a day or cause marked distress
    \item Avoidance of situations that trigger obsessions (e.g.\ avoiding door handles or public toilets)
    \item In body dysmorphic disorder: intense preoccupation with a perceived flaw in appearance and repetitive checking, comparing, or grooming
    \item In hoarding: difficulty discarding possessions with distressing feelings about getting rid of them
    \item In trichotillomania or skin picking: repeated pulling or picking despite visible damage, with repeated attempts to stop
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Generalised anxiety disorder or panic disorder, where worry, not specific obsessions, dominates
    \item Depression with rumination (persistent worrying thoughts about past events or self-worth)
    \item Tic disorders and Tourette syndrome, which involve involuntary movements or sounds
    \item Autism spectrum conditions, which can involve repetitive behaviours and fixed interests
    \item Psychotic disorders, when beliefs are held with delusional certainty
    \item Substance-induced repetitive behaviour or anxiety
    \item Normal habits, superstitious behaviours, or routines that do not cause distress
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a GP or mental health professional if compulsions or obsessions take up a significant part of the day, cause distress, or interfere with school, work, or relationships. Early treatment is associated with better outcomes, so help-seeking should not be delayed.

\textbf{Call 000 or go to an emergency department if there are thoughts of suicide or self-harm.} You can also call Lifeline on 13 11 14. Severe distress, an inability to function, or harm to the skin or body from washing, pulling, or picking also warrant urgent assessment.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Compulsive disorders are diagnosed through clinical assessment rather than tests:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical interview:} a detailed account of the obsessions, compulsions, and their impact, plus a review of mood, anxiety, and family history
    \item \textbf{Structured scales:} questionnaires such as the Yale--Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) help measure severity
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} checking for suicide risk, self-harm, and any physical injury from repetitive behaviours
    \item \textbf{Medical review:} a physical check to exclude conditions that can mimic or worsen symptoms, such as thyroid disease or substance use
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychological therapy:} cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT) with exposure and response prevention (ERP) is the first-line treatment, helping the person face triggers without performing compulsions
    \item \textbf{Medication:} antidepressants called SSRIs (e.g.\ fluoxetine, sertraline) are effective and may be used alone or with therapy, prescribed and monitored by a doctor
    \item \textbf{Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT):} can help reduce the struggle with intrusive thoughts
    \item \textbf{Family involvement:} education and family support, and reducing accommodation of rituals by relatives
    \item \textbf{Specialist services:} referral to an OCD or anxiety clinic for more severe or complex cases
    \item \textbf{Intensive treatment:} residential or intensive outpatient programs for people who do not respond to standard care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Depression and anxiety, which are very common alongside these disorders
    \item Substance misuse as a way of coping
    \item Social isolation and relationship strain
    \item Work and study impairment, and time lost to rituals
    \item Physical damage from repetitive behaviour, such as sore or broken skin, hair loss, or skin infections
    \item Avoiding medical and dental care (e.g.\ fears about contamination)
    \item Suicidal thoughts, particularly in severe or untreated cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Many people with OCD and related disorders improve significantly with CBT and medication, although symptoms can be persistent and may flare up during stress. Around half of people achieve a substantial reduction in symptoms with first-line treatment, and many others improve with more intensive or combined approaches. The condition is often long-term, so most people learn to manage symptoms rather than expecting them to disappear entirely. Better outcomes are linked to early treatment and a strong commitment to exposure-based therapy.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Learn about the condition so that symptoms are understood as a disorder, not a personal failing
    \item Practise tolerating anxiety without performing rituals, in small, graded steps
    \item Avoid repeatedly asking others for reassurance, which can reinforce the cycle
    \item Maintain a regular sleep routine, exercise, and balanced diet
    \item Identify and manage stress, which tends to worsen symptoms
    \item Take medication and attend therapy appointments consistently
    \item Seek help early if symptoms return after a period of improvement
\end{itemize}
