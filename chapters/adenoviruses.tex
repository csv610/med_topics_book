\chapter{Adenoviruses}
\index{Adenoviruses}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Adenoviruses are a group of non-enveloped DNA viruses that cause a broad range of infections in humans, most commonly involving the respiratory tract, the eye, and the gastrointestinal system. More than 50 distinct serotypes are known to infect humans, and different serotypes show a striking preference for different tissues, which is why one type causes a cold-like illness while another causes conjunctivitis or gastroenteritis. Infections are extremely common, are usually mild and self-limiting, and are responsible for a substantial proportion of childhood respiratory infections worldwide.

Adenoviruses spread readily between people, particularly in crowded or closed settings such as schools, childcare centres, military barracks, and swimming-pool environments. Although most healthy people recover without specific treatment, certain serotypes can cause more serious illness, including pneumonia, hepatitis, or inflammation of the brain, particularly in newborns, transplant recipients, and people with compromised immune systems. Outbreaks in institutional settings can be difficult to control because the viruses survive well on surfaces and in water. Recognition of the clinical patterns produced by adenoviruses helps clinicians avoid unnecessary antibiotics and identify situations in which supportive care or infection-control measures are required.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Adenoviruses are distributed worldwide and cause disease throughout the year, although respiratory infections peak in winter and early spring and some serotypes show summer seasonality linked to swimming. By adulthood, virtually everyone has been infected with multiple serotypes. Children under five years of age have the highest attack rates, and adenoviruses are a leading cause of viral pharyngitis, conjunctivitis, and gastroenteritis in this age group.

Outbreaks are characteristically seen in closed or semi-closed communities, including childcare centres, schools, summer camps, military training establishments, healthcare facilities, and boarding schools. Swimming pools and small recreational lakes can transmit the virus when inadequately chlorinated, causing epidemics of pharyngoconjunctival fever. Because immunity following natural infection is serotype-specific rather than broadly protective, reinfection with different serotypes is common throughout life, and the cumulative exposure to many serotypes explains why adults continue to experience adenovirus infections despite previous illness.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Adenoviruses are icosahedral, non-enveloped viruses with a double-stranded DNA genome. The absence of a lipid envelope makes them remarkably resistant to heat, detergents, and many disinfectants, and they can survive for weeks on inanimate surfaces and in water, which underpins their ease of transmission.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} spread occurs by respiratory droplets and aerosols from coughs and sneezes, by direct contact with contaminated hands or fomites followed by touching the eyes, nose, or mouth, and by the faecal-oral route when hand hygiene is poor
    \item \textbf{Entry and replication:} the virus attaches to cell-surface receptors, enters the cell, and releases its genome, which is replicated within the nucleus; new virus particles then destroy the host cell and spread to neighbouring cells
    \item \textbf{Tissue tropism:} fibre proteins on the viral capsid determine receptor binding, which largely explains why particular serotypes preferentially infect the airways, conjunctiva, bowel, urinary tract, or liver
    \item \textbf{Immune response:} neutralising antibodies develop to the infecting serotype, but because there are many serotypes and cross-protection is limited, repeat infections are possible
    \item \textbf{Latency:} adenoviruses can persist in tonsillar and adenoid tissue, being shed intermittently from the respiratory or intestinal tract for months after infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms typically appear 2--14 days after exposure and depend on the site of infection and the serotype involved. Many infections are asymptomatic, particularly in older children and adults.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Respiratory tract:} fever, sore throat, runny or blocked nose, and cough; pharyngitis with fever, tonsillitis, and, less commonly, bronchitis or pneumonia
    \item \textbf{Eye:} acute conjunctivitis with a red, watery, gritty eye; pharyngoconjunctival fever combines fever, sore throat, and conjunctivitis and is common around swimming pools; epidemic keratoconjunctivitis is a more severe, highly contagious form
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal:} watery diarrhoea, vomiting, and abdominal pain, mainly in young children; some serotypes also cause mesenteric adenitis, which can mimic appendicitis
    \item \textbf{Urinary tract:} cystitis with painful, frequent urination and sometimes blood in the urine
    \item \textbf{Other:} generalised symptoms such as malaise, headache, myalgia, and swollen lymph glands; a fever with a fine rash may occur in children
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The clinical features of adenovirus infection overlap extensively with those of many other respiratory, ocular, and gastrointestinal pathogens, and the diagnosis is usually made on the pattern of illness.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Respiratory:} rhinovirus, respiratory syncytial virus, parainfluenza virus, influenza, and SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19) all cause similar cold and flu-like symptoms
    \item \textbf{Throat:} streptococcal pharyngitis and tonsillitis, infectious mononucleosis (Epstein--Barr virus), and pharyngitis due to \textit{Mycoplasma pneumoniae}
    \item \textbf{Eye:} bacterial, allergic, or chlamydial conjunctivitis, and herpes simplex keratitis
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal:} viral gastroenteritis caused by rotavirus or norovirus, and bacterial gastroenteritis
    \item \textbf{Urinary:} bacterial cystitis and, in older patients, other causes of haematuria
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most adenovirus infections are mild and resolve with supportive care. A medical review is advisable when symptoms are severe, persistent, or worsening, when a fever lasts more than a few days, when there are concerns about dehydration, or when a red eye is painful, swollen, or sensitive to light.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, rapid breathing, or wheezing, especially in an infant or young child
    \item Confusion, unusual drowsiness, or a stiff neck
    \item Signs of dehydration in an infant: reduced wet nappies, sunken eyes, dry mouth, or limpness
    \item Persistent vomiting so that fluids cannot be kept down
    \item A high fever that does not settle and makes the person appear very unwell
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
In the great majority of cases, adenovirus infection is diagnosed clinically and no investigations are needed. Testing is reserved for severe illness, suspected outbreaks, immunocompromised patients, or situations in which the diagnosis would change management.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the combination of fever, pharyngitis, conjunctivitis, or gastroenteritis in the right epidemiological context is often sufficient
    \item \textbf{Respiratory and conjunctival swabs:} PCR testing is rapid and sensitive and is the investigation of choice when confirmation is required; viral culture is slower but can identify serotypes during outbreaks
    \item \textbf{Stool testing:} useful in gastroenteritis outbreaks and in immunocompromised patients with prolonged diarrhoea
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count and inflammatory markers may help exclude bacterial infection in unwell patients
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} performed when pneumonia is suspected, particularly in children and immunocompromised hosts
    \item \textbf{Serology:} detection of a rise in antibodies can be useful in epidemiological studies but plays little role in routine diagnosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
There is no specific antiviral treatment for the vast majority of adenovirus infections, and management is supportive.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} rest, adequate fluid intake, and simple analgesia such as paracetamol or ibuprofen for fever and discomfort; aspirin should be avoided in children owing to the risk of Reye syndrome
    \item \textbf{Eye care:} cool compresses, lubricant eye drops, and gentle cleaning of the eyelids; affected individuals should not share towels, pillows, or eye products and should avoid swimming
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal symptoms:} oral rehydration solutions for young children with diarrhoea and vomiting, alongside regular small feeds
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} not indicated, because adenoviruses are not susceptible to antibacterial agents
    \item \textbf{Antiviral therapy:} cidofovir and related agents have been used in specialist centres for severe infection in transplant recipients and other immunocompromised patients, but they are associated with significant toxicity and are reserved for serious disease
    \item \textbf{Infection control:} isolation of hospitalised patients, strict hand hygiene, and enhanced environmental cleaning help contain outbreaks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Complications are uncommon in otherwise healthy individuals but are more frequent and more severe in infants, young children, and immunocompromised patients.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Pneumonia or bronchiolitis, particularly with serotypes that target the lower respiratory tract
    \item Dehydration resulting from vomiting and diarrhoea, which may require hospital fluid replacement
    \item Severe keratoconjunctivitis, which can occasionally leave residual corneal scarring and visual disturbance
    \item Disseminated disease with hepatitis, nephritis, myocarditis, or encephalitis in transplant recipients and other severely immunocompromised patients
    \item Prolonged faecal shedding of virus in immunocompromised hosts, which can sustain outbreaks in hospital wards
    \item Chronic or recurrent diarrhoea in HIV-infected and other immunodeficient patients
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Healthy children and adults nearly always recover fully within a few days to two weeks without specific treatment. Infection with one serotype confers durable immunity to that serotype, although other serotypes may still cause later illness. In immunocompromised patients, infection can run a more prolonged and serious course, and disseminated disease carries a substantial mortality rate despite antiviral therapy. Overall, however, the prognosis for the vast majority of people with adenovirus infection is excellent, and serious outcomes are confined largely to those with underlying immune impairment.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hand hygiene:} wash hands frequently with soap and water, particularly after using the toilet and before handling food
    \item \textbf{Cough etiquette:} cover coughs and sneezes with a tissue or the elbow, and dispose of tissues promptly
    \item \textbf{Environmental cleaning:} disinfect shared toys, door handles, and frequently touched surfaces, as adenoviruses survive well on them
    \item \textbf{Personal items:} do not share towels, pillows, eye drops, or cosmetics during an eye infection
    \item \textbf{Avoid swimming:} refrain from using swimming pools while unwell with diarrhoea or conjunctivitis, and keep chlorination of public pools at recommended levels
    \item \textbf{Stay home:} keep children away from school, childcare, or camps while symptomatic to reduce spread
    \item \textbf{Vaccination:} there is no routine vaccine, although a live oral vaccine against two serotypes is administered to certain military recruits
\end{itemize}
