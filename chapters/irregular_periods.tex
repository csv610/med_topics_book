\chapter{Irregular Periods}
\index{Irregular Periods}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An irregular period is a menstrual cycle that is longer or shorter than usual, or that varies significantly from month to month. A normal cycle ranges from 21 to 35 days, and some variation is common, especially in the first years after periods start and in the years before menopause. Irregularity becomes a problem when cycles are consistently outside the normal range, periods are missed, or bleeding is very frequent or unpredictable. Causes range from normal life stages and stress to hormonal conditions such as polycystic ovary syndrome, thyroid disease, and, in some cases, more serious problems. Assessment identifies the cause and guides treatment, which may be lifestyle change, medicines, or hormonal therapy.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Around one in four women experiences irregular periods at some time, and it is one of the most common reasons for gynaecological consultation. Irregular cycles are normal in adolescence for two to three years after the first period and again in the years leading up to menopause. Polycystic ovary syndrome affects around one in ten women and is the most common cause of persistent irregularity. Heavy bleeding, stress, weight change, thyroid disease, and the use or stopping of hormonal contraceptives are also common contributors. Missed periods warrant particular attention, especially in pregnancy and in relation to hormonal or medical conditions.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The menstrual cycle is controlled by a precise sequence of hormones from the brain and ovaries, and any disruption to this system changes cycle length. In polycystic ovary syndrome, higher levels of androgens and insulin resistance disturb ovulation, so periods are infrequent or absent. Thyroid disorders, raised prolactin, and stress act on the brain's control of the cycle. Significant weight loss, excessive exercise, and disordered eating can stop ovulation entirely, while obesity can also disturb cycles. Structural problems such as fibroids and polyps are more likely to cause heavy or prolonged bleeding than a change in cycle length. In the perimenopause, cycles lengthen and shorten as hormone levels fluctuate before stopping.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cycles consistently shorter than 21 days or longer than 35 days
    \item Cycles that vary widely in length from month to month
    \item Missed periods, or fewer than four periods a year
    \item Unpredictable or very frequent bleeding
    \item Associated symptoms: heavy bleeding, acne, excess hair, weight gain, or fatigue
    \item Headaches, breast tenderness, or mood changes around cycles
    \item Symptoms related to the cause, such as milky nipple discharge with raised prolactin
    \item Bleeding after menopause is always abnormal and requires investigation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The cause of irregularity is identified through history, examination, and tests.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Polycystic ovary syndrome:} the most common cause of persistent irregularity
    \item \textbf{Life stages:} adolescence and perimenopause
    \item \textbf{Thyroid disease and raised prolactin:} hormonal causes
    \item \textbf{Stress, weight change, and exercise:} effects on ovulation
    \item \textbf{Hormonal contraception:} effects after starting or stopping
    \item \textbf{Structural problems:} fibroids, polyps, and endometrial pathology
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} must always be excluded in missed periods
    \item \textbf{Endometrial cancer:} rare, but considered with post-menopausal or abnormal bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Women should seek assessment if cycles are persistently irregular, periods are missed, bleeding is very heavy or prolonged, or symptoms such as acne and excess hair are troublesome. Periods that stop for more than three months, bleeding after menopause, and bleeding between periods warrant review. Pregnant women and those with symptoms of anaemia should also be seen.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Very heavy bleeding that soaks pads or tampons within an hour
    \item Heavy bleeding with faintness, dizziness, or collapse
    \item Sudden severe pelvic or abdominal pain
    \item Bleeding in pregnancy with pain or faintness, which may indicate ectopic pregnancy
    \item Signs of severe infection: fever with pelvic pain or discharge
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} cycle pattern, bleeding, symptoms, medicines, weight, and exercise
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test:} in any woman of reproductive age with missed periods
    \item \textbf{Hormone tests:} FSH, LH, oestradiol, thyroid function, prolactin, and androgens
    \item \textbf{Pelvic ultrasound:} assessment of the ovaries, uterus, and endometrium
    \item \textbf{Full blood count and iron studies:} for anaemia from heavy bleeding
    \item \textbf{Endometrial biopsy or hysteroscopy:} for abnormal bleeding, especially after 45
    \item \textbf{Screening for diabetes and insulin resistance:} in suspected PCOS
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} healthy weight, balanced diet, and appropriate exercise
    \item \textbf{Combined oral contraceptive or progestogen therapy:} to regulate cycles and protect the endometrium
    \item \textbf{Hormonal intrauterine device:} effective for heavy or irregular bleeding
    \item \textbf{Treatment of the cause:} thyroid medicine, prolactin-lowering drugs, or treatment of PCOS
    \item \textbf{Fertility treatment:} ovulation induction for women wanting pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Endometrial protection:} ensuring regular shedding or progestogen cover to prevent overgrowth
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} removal of fibroids or polyps, or treatment of other structural causes
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular review and screening of the endometrium where indicated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Anaemia from heavy or prolonged bleeding
    \item Infertility from absent or irregular ovulation
    \item Endometrial hyperplasia and cancer risk if cycles are infrequent and unopposed oestrogen persists
    \item Osteoporosis with prolonged absence of periods and low oestrogen
    \item Effects on quality of life, mood, and fertility planning
    \item Missed pregnancy or underlying disease when irregularity is ignored
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the cause. Adolescent irregularity usually settles as the cycle matures, and perimenopausal irregularity resolves at menopause. Polycystic ovary syndrome is managed long term with lifestyle measures and hormonal therapy, and endometrial protection prevents the cancer risk of infrequent periods. Most causes of irregularity respond well to treatment, and pregnancy can usually be achieved with ovulation induction when desired. The most important steps are confirming the cause and protecting the endometrium.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Track your cycle with a diary or app to identify patterns
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and be physically active
    \item Manage stress and sleep
    \item Avoid extreme dieting and excessive exercise
    \item Seek assessment early for persistent irregularity
    \item Attend regular reviews if you have PCOS or another cause
    \item Ensure you are not pregnant before assuming a cause
    \item Discuss cycle regulation and endometrial protection with your doctor
    \item Report post-menopausal bleeding promptly
\end{itemize}
