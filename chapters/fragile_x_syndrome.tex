\chapter{Fragile X Syndrome}
\index{Fragile X Syndrome}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Fragile X syndrome is an inherited genetic condition that is the most common cause of inherited intellectual disability and a leading single-gene cause of autism spectrum disorder. It results from a mutation in the FMR1 gene on the X chromosome, which stops the production of a protein needed for normal brain development. The condition produces intellectual disability, characteristic physical features, and behavioural characteristics including anxiety, hyperactivity, and autistic traits, with males more severely affected than females. Diagnosis is by a simple blood test, and while there is no cure, early intervention, education, and medical care for associated conditions substantially improve outcomes. This chapter covers the causes, features, and management of fragile X syndrome.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Fragile X syndrome affects approximately 1 in 4,000 males and 1 in 6,000--8,000 females, and it accounts for a small but important proportion of all intellectual disability and autism diagnoses. It occurs in all populations and ethnic groups. Around 1 in 250 women is a carrier of a premutation, which can expand into the full mutation when passed to children, and carriers themselves are at risk of fragile X-associated conditions including premature ovarian insufficiency and, in later life, tremor/ataxia syndrome (FXTAS). Despite the availability of accurate genetic testing, the diagnosis is often delayed by several years.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The FMR1 gene:} located on the X chromosome, it produces the fragile X mental retardation protein (FMRP), which is critical for synaptic development and function in the brain
    \item \textbf{Repeat expansion:} a CGG DNA sequence is repeated; over 200 repeats (the full mutation) silences the gene, so no FMRP is made
    \item \textbf{Premutation:} 55--200 repeats is a premutation, not causing the syndrome itself but able to expand to the full mutation in offspring, especially when inherited from the mother
    \item \textbf{X-linked inheritance:} because males have only one X chromosome, they are affected more severely; females have a second, normal X chromosome that provides some FMRP, so they are usually less affected
    \item \textbf{Brain effects:} without FMRP, synapses form in excess and are not pruned normally, producing intellectual disability and behavioural features
    \item \textbf{Anticipation:} the repeat tends to expand in successive generations, so the condition can appear in a family where previous generations were unaffected carriers
    \item \textbf{Carrier disorders:} premutation carriers can develop FXTAS (tremor, ataxia, cognitive decline in older age) and fragile X-associated primary ovarian insufficiency (FXPOI)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Intellectual disability of variable severity, from borderline learning difficulties to severe disability
    \item Behavioural features: anxiety, social avoidance, poor eye contact, hand flapping and hand biting, hyperactivity, and autistic traits
    \item Speech and language delay, with repetitive speech and difficulty with social language
    \item Physical features: a long, narrow face, large prominent ears, a prominent jaw, and macro-orchidism after puberty
    \item Connective tissue features: loose joints, flat feet, soft skin, and ear infections
    \item Medical problems: seizures in about 15%, strabismus, and sleep disturbance
    \item Sensory sensitivities and difficulty with change and transitions
    \item In females: a wide range from unaffected to mild intellectual disability, anxiety, and shyness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The features overlap with other conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Autism spectrum disorder of other causes
    \item ADHD and anxiety disorders
    \item Other genetic causes of intellectual disability and developmental delay
    \item Down syndrome, Rett syndrome, and other genetic syndromes
    \item Speech and language disorders without global delay
    \item Genetic testing for the FMR1 gene reliably distinguishes fragile X syndrome from these
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Families should seek assessment for any child with developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism features, or a family history of intellectual disability or fragile X syndrome. Genetic testing is widely available and should not be delayed.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seizures or fitting
    \item Any medical emergency
    \item Severe distress, self-harm, or suicidal thoughts, which can accompany anxiety and depression
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic blood test:} measures the FMR1 repeat number and confirms the full mutation or premutation; the definitive test
    \item \textbf{Developmental assessment:} psychology, speech, and occupational therapy assessment of abilities and needs
    \item \textbf{Family and carrier testing:} testing of at-risk relatives, with genetic counselling
    \item \textbf{Prenatal and preimplantation testing:} available to carrier couples planning children
    \item \textbf{Medical review:} hearing, vision, and assessment for seizures and other associated problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Early intervention:} the foundation of care, including speech, occupational, and physiotherapy from early childhood
    \item \textbf{Education:} individualised programs with visual supports, routine, and structure
    \item \textbf{Behavioural and psychological support:} strategies for anxiety, attention, and sensory issues
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} treatment of co-occurring ADHD, anxiety, seizures, and sleep problems
    \item \textbf{Communication support:} augmentative communication where speech is limited
    \item \textbf{Medical care:} treatment of ear infections, vision problems, and other associated conditions
    \item \textbf{Transition to adulthood:} vocational training, employment support, and life skills programs
    \item \textbf{national disability-support scheme and support services:} funding for therapies and supports
    \item \textbf{Genetic counselling:} for the family's understanding, recurrence risks, and carrier status
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Anxiety, depression, and challenging behaviour, which are common and need active treatment
    \item Epilepsy in a minority
    \item Recurrent middle ear infections in childhood
    \item Sleep disturbance
    \item Premature ovarian insufficiency in female premutation carriers
    \item FXTAS in older premutation carriers, with tremor, balance problems, and cognitive decline
    \item Carer strain in families supporting a person with significant disability
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Fragile X syndrome is lifelong, but the outlook has improved substantially with early diagnosis and modern support. People with the condition learn, form friendships, and contribute to their communities, and many achieve literacy, work, and a degree of independence. Early intervention, structured education, and treatment of anxiety and ADHD markedly improve outcomes. Research into treatments targeting the underlying protein deficiency continues. With the right supports, most people with fragile X syndrome lead full and valued lives.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Request genetic testing early for developmental delay or intellectual disability of unknown cause
    \item Arrange carrier testing and genetic counselling for at-risk family members
    \item Consider prenatal or preimplantation testing in affected families
    \item Begin early intervention services as soon as the diagnosis is made
    \item Build structure, routine, and visual supports into daily life
    \item Treat anxiety, attention problems, and seizures actively
    \item Connect with support organisations and other families
    \item Care for the caregivers, including respite and support networks
\end{itemize}
