\chapter{Fibromyalgia Treatment}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Treatment for fibromyalgia aims to reduce pain, improve sleep and energy, and restore function and quality of life. Because fibromyalgia involves abnormal pain processing rather than tissue damage, no single medicine or procedure cures it, and the most effective approach combines graded exercise, psychological therapies, pacing, sleep care, and, for some people, medicines that act on the nervous system. Treatment is individualised and delivered over months, with the person with fibromyalgia as an active partner. This chapter outlines the treatment options, how they work, and how to build a personal treatment plan.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Most people with fibromyalgia, which affects around 2--5\% of the population, use some form of treatment, but many are dissatisfied with the results of medicines alone. Surveys show that the treatments people rate as most helpful are exercise, pacing, and psychological support, while simple painkillers and anti-inflammatories help little. Around half of people try complementary therapies, and many stop medicines because of side effects. Structured multidisciplinary programs, which combine education, exercise, and psychology, produce the largest and most durable improvements, and improving access to them is a priority in chronic pain services.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The target of treatment:} fibromyalgia is driven by central sensitisation, the amplification of pain signals in the brain and spinal cord
    \item \textbf{Exercise effects:} regular activity lowers pain sensitivity, improves sleep, reduces fatigue, and lifts mood through endorphin release and nervous system conditioning
    \item \textbf{Psychological effects:} cognitive behavioural therapy reduces the fear, catastrophising, and avoidance that amplify pain, and increases coping
    \item \textbf{Pacing:} balancing activity and rest prevents the flare-up--crash cycles that keep pain high
    \item \textbf{Medicine effects:} certain medicines increase the availability of neurotransmitters such as serotonin and noradrenaline in pain-modulating pathways, or dampen the transmission of pain signals
    \item \textbf{Sleep:} improving sleep reduces pain sensitivity, since sleep deprivation directly amplifies pain
    \item \textbf{Why simple analgesics fail:} paracetamol, NSAIDs, and opioids do not target the central pain-processing problem and have little or no benefit, with opioids causing harm
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Effective treatment produces recognisable changes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Reduced intensity and frequency of pain, and less generalised tenderness
    \item Better sleep quality and less unrefreshing sleep
    \item Increased energy and less fatigue after activity
    \item Improved concentration and memory ("fibro fog" lifts)
    \item Greater ability to work, exercise, and take part in daily life
    \item Fewer flare-ups, and better ability to manage those that occur
    \item Improved mood and reduced anxiety
    \item Progress is gradual, over weeks to months, rather than immediate
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Treatment planning must ensure the diagnosis is right and coexisting conditions are addressed.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hypothyroidism, inflammatory arthritis, and other conditions that can mimic fibromyalgia
    \item Coexisting chronic fatigue syndrome, irritable bowel syndrome, and mood disorders, which need their own treatment
    \item The distinction between medicines that help (neuromodulators) and those that do not (simple analgesics and opioids)
    \item Side effects of medicines, which are common and may require dose adjustment or change
    \item The need to rule out new problems when symptoms change, since chronic pain can mask other illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Treatment should be coordinated with a doctor, often with physiotherapists, psychologists, and pain specialists. Medical review is needed for side effects, new symptoms, or if the treatment plan is not working.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe pain, weakness, or numbness different from usual symptoms
    \item Chest pain or breathlessness
    \item Suicidal thoughts
    \item Any emergency symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Confirmation of diagnosis:} review of the symptom pattern and exclusion of other conditions
    \item \textbf{Baseline assessment:} pain scores, sleep quality, fatigue, mood, and function measured to track progress
    \item \textbf{Medicine review:} checking for interactions and stopping medicines that do not help
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular review of outcomes and adjustment of the plan
    \item \textbf{No routine tests:} treatment does not require repeated blood tests or imaging
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Graded exercise:} start with low-intensity, enjoyable activity such as walking, swimming, or cycling, and increase slowly and consistently; supervised programs and physiotherapy help
    \item \textbf{Cognitive behavioural therapy:} delivered by a psychologist, addressing pain beliefs, fear of activity, and coping
    \item \textbf{Pacing and activity management:} plan activity, break tasks down, and balance rest so that flares are avoided
    \item \textbf{Sleep hygiene:} consistent bedtimes, a dark cool bedroom, and daytime light and activity
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} amitriptyline or nortriptyline at low doses, duloxetine or milnacipran, and pregabalin or gabapentin; started low, increased slowly, and reviewed for effect
    \item \textbf{Complementary therapies:} acupuncture, massage, and mindfulness help some people; they work best alongside the core treatments
    \item \textbf{Multidisciplinary programs:} structured chronic pain programs combine all elements and produce the best outcomes
    \item \textbf{Employment support:} workplace modifications and occupational therapy help people stay at work
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Side effects of medicines, including drowsiness, dry mouth, weight gain, and dizziness
    \item Worsening with strong opioids or medicines that cause dependence
    \item Overactivity leading to flare-ups, and underactivity leading to deconditioning
    \item Depression and anxiety if treatment is not comprehensive
    \item Poor outcomes when treatment is limited to medicines alone
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people with fibromyalgia improve with a comprehensive treatment plan, and improvements in pain, sleep, and function are well documented with exercise and psychological therapy. Gains build gradually over months, and many people return to work and full lives, though flares still occur. The most consistent predictor of success is an active, engaged approach combining several treatments rather than expecting a single medicine to work. With persistence and the right support, the majority of people rate their quality of life as much improved.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Commit to regular exercise as the foundation of treatment, and increase it gradually
    \item Learn pacing and plan each day to balance activity and rest
    \item Protect sleep with a regular routine and a cool, dark bedroom
    \item Practise relaxation and mindfulness daily
    \item Take medicines as prescribed, and report side effects rather than stopping abruptly
    \item Avoid strong painkillers and opioids, which do not help and can harm
    \item Join a self-management program or support group
    \item Keep communication open with your doctor and treatment team
\end{itemize}
