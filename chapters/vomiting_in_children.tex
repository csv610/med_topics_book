\chapter{Vomiting in children}
\index{Vomiting in children}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Vomiting in children is the forceful emptying of the stomach contents through the mouth. It is extremely common in childhood, and most episodes are brief, self-limiting, and caused by a viral infection of the stomach and bowel (viral gastroenteritis). The main concern with vomiting in a child is not usually the vomiting itself but its consequences, particularly dehydration, because young children can lose fluid rapidly and become seriously dehydrated within hours.

Less common but more serious causes must always be considered, especially in young babies. These include bowel obstruction, infections outside the gut such as meningitis and urinary tract infection, and, in the first weeks of life, conditions that need urgent surgical attention. Vomiting should be distinguished from posseting (small, effortless spills of milk in young babies) and from reflux, which is common and usually benign. The key tasks for parents and clinicians are to keep the child hydrated, to recognise the features that suggest something serious, and to know when to seek urgent help.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Vomiting is one of the most common reasons parents bring a child to see a doctor. Viral gastroenteritis accounts for the vast majority of cases, and rotavirus in particular was a leading cause of severe vomiting and diarrhoea in young children before vaccination was introduced. Since rotavirus vaccination became routine in many countries in 2007, hospital admissions and emergency presentations for rotavirus gastroenteritis have fallen dramatically, a reminder of how effective immunisation is.

Vomiting is most common in the first three years of life, partly because infants and toddlers are more susceptible to infection and partly because their fluid reserves are small in relation to their body size. Around 1 in 30 children under five will attend an emergency department with gastroenteritis in a given year, and dehydration severe enough to need hospital care remains one of the most common reasons for childhood admission. Other causes, such as gastro-oesophageal reflux, motion sickness, and food allergy, have their own typical age ranges, while serious surgical causes such as intussusception are rare but cluster in particular age groups.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\subsection*{Infections}
Viral gastroenteritis, most often due to rotavirus, norovirus, adenovirus, or sapovirus, is the commonest cause. The virus infects the cells lining the small intestine, damaging their ability to absorb fluid and triggering the release of fluids and salts into the bowel. Bacterial causes such as \textit{Salmonella}, \textit{Campylobacter}, and \textit{Shigella} are less common but produce more severe illness and often blood in the stool. A urinary tract infection can present with vomiting alone, especially in young children, and other infections including ear infections, chest infections, and meningitis can all cause vomiting through fever and general illness.

\subsection*{Obstruction and surgical problems}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pyloric stenosis:} thickening of the outlet valve of the stomach, causing forceful "projectile" vomiting in babies of 3 to 6 weeks of age, classically boys
    \item \textbf{Intussusception:} a telescoping of one part of the bowel into another, causing intermittent severe pain and eventually red, jelly-like stools, usually in infants of 3 months to 3 years
    \item \textbf{Bowel obstruction:} from a twist, a band, or a blockage, producing bile-stained (green) vomiting, which is always an emergency
    \item \textbf{Malrotation:} an abnormal position of the bowel present from birth that can twist and block, producing bile-stained vomiting in the first months of life
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Other causes}
Gastro-oesophageal reflux is common and usually benign in young babies, producing small effortless possets. Food allergy or intolerance, a swallowed foreign body, head injury, migraine and cyclical vomiting syndrome, motion sickness, and poisoning or ingestion of a toxic substance are further possibilities. In older children, anxiety, viral illnesses, and more rarely raised pressure inside the skull can cause vomiting, particularly morning vomiting.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Onset and content:} the number of episodes, how forceful they are, and whether the vomit is milky, clear, bile-stained (green), or blood-stained
    \item \textbf{Diarrhoea:} whether there is associated loose or watery stool, which points strongly to gastroenteritis
    \item \textbf{Fever:} fever is common with infection but a high fever with vomiting and a stiff neck suggests meningitis
    \item \textbf{Pain:} localised or severe abdominal pain, especially if the child is drawing up the knees, suggests obstruction or intussusception
    \item \textbf{Hydration:} reduced wet nappies, a dry mouth, few or no tears, sunken eyes, and listlessness are the key signs of dehydration
    \item \textbf{Behaviour:} a child who is alert and interactive is reassuring, whereas increasing drowsiness, irritability, or reduced responsiveness is concerning
    \item \textbf{Red flag symptoms:} green (bile-stained) vomiting, projectile vomiting in a young baby, blood in the vomit or stool, and a rash that does not fade under pressure all demand urgent review
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Viral gastroenteritis (the commonest cause, usually with diarrhoea)
    \item Posseting and gastro-oesophageal reflux in young babies
    \item Urinary tract infection, especially in young children who cannot describe symptoms
    \item Other infections, including ear infections, chest infections, and meningitis
    \item Pyloric stenosis, intussusception, and bowel obstruction
    \item Food allergy or intolerance
    \item Migraine, cyclical vomiting syndrome, and motion sickness
    \item Head injury, ingestion of a toxic substance, and appendicitis in older children
    \item Raised pressure within the skull, including from a brain tumour, which can cause persistent early-morning vomiting
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor promptly if a child has been unable to keep down fluids, has had reduced wet nappies, has a dry mouth or sunken eyes, is unusually sleepy, has persistent abdominal pain, has been vomiting for more than a day or two, or is failing to improve. Young babies, especially under six months, need earlier review because they dehydrate quickly.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Green (bile-stained) vomit, which suggests a bowel obstruction
    \item Projectile vomiting in a baby, which suggests pyloric stenosis
    \item Blood in the vomit or in a nappy, or red, jelly-like stools
    \item A high fever with a stiff neck, severe headache, or a non-blanching rash
    \item Severe dehydration, with no wet nappies for many hours, sunken eyes, and listlessness
    \item A head injury, a possible poisoning, or any child who is difficult to rouse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} a careful history and examination are usually enough; the priority is to assess the degree of dehydration and whether any red flags are present
    \item \textbf{Weight and hydration assessment:} weighing the child and assessing skin, eyes, mouth, and responsiveness; in hospital, an accurate weight is essential for calculating fluids
    \item \textbf{Urine testing:} a urine sample checks for a urinary tract infection and can help assess hydration
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} arranged for children who are severely dehydrated, to measure salts and kidney function, and in children needing intravenous fluids
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} an ultrasound or X-ray may be needed if obstruction, pyloric stenosis, or intussusception is suspected; intussusception can sometimes also be treated with an air or contrast enema under X-ray guidance
    \item \textbf{Stool tests:} rarely needed, but can identify bacterial infection in severe or persistent cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\subsection*{Fluids and feeding}
The core of management is preventing and treating dehydration. Breastfed babies should continue to breastfeed, and other children should be offered small, frequent amounts of fluid. Oral rehydration solution (such as Gastrolyte or Hydralyte) is the most effective choice because it replaces water, salts, and sugar together; for children who are mildly dehydrated, it is better than water, juice, or sports drinks. Large single drinks often come straight back up, so small sips every few minutes work better. If a child vomits, wait ten to fifteen minutes and try again. Most children tolerate food within 24 hours and do not need a special diet, though it is sensible to avoid very fatty or very sugary foods at first. Anti-nausea medicines such as ondansetron are sometimes used in hospital for children with gastroenteritis but are not a routine substitute for fluids.

There are also clear messages about what not to do. Parents should not force a vomiting child to drink, as this simply provokes more vomiting. Water should not be the only fluid offered to a child who is losing fluid, because plain water replaces losses without the salts and sugar the body also needs, which can lead to low sodium levels. Soft drinks and fruit juice are too sugary and can worsen diarrhoea, and there is no evidence that any medicine shortens viral gastroenteritis. Patience, small volumes, and frequent offers are far more effective than urgency and large drinks.

\subsection*{Hospital care}
Children with severe dehydration, those who cannot keep down any fluid, and those with suspected surgical problems need hospital assessment. Treatment involves measured oral rehydration under supervision or intravenous fluids, with monitoring of weight, urine output, and clinical state until the child is safely rehydrating by mouth.

\subsection*{Specific conditions}
Pyloric stenosis and intussusception need surgical or radiological treatment, and bile-stained vomiting always requires urgent paediatric surgical review. Underlying infections such as a urinary tract infection are treated with antibiotics, and food allergy is managed by identifying and avoiding the trigger under dietetic guidance.

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dehydration, the most common and important complication, which can progress to reduced kidney function and, rarely, shock
    \item Salt and electrolyte disturbances, particularly if only plain water is given to replace losses
    \item Aspiration of vomit into the lungs, causing choking or pneumonia, especially in drowsy children
    \item Weight loss and poor feeding that can prolong illness
    \item The complications of the underlying cause, such as bowel damage from an untreated obstruction or the spread of infection from untreated meningitis
    \item Rarely, oesophageal irritation or tears from repeated forceful retching
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Viral gastroenteritis in an otherwise healthy child is a self-limiting illness that resolves in three to seven days, and with careful fluid management most children never need hospital care. The outlook is excellent with adequate hydration, and complications are almost entirely confined to dehydration, which is preventable. Surgical causes such as pyloric stenosis and intussusception have an excellent outcome when treated early and can be fatal if left untreated, which is why the red flag features are so important. In a child who is well hydrated, alert, and improving, vomiting is almost always a short-lived inconvenience; the danger lies not in the symptom but in failing to recognise the child who is deteriorating.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ensure the child is up to date with immunisation, including rotavirus vaccine, which prevents the commonest severe cause of vomiting and diarrhoea
    \item Wash hands thoroughly, especially after nappy changes and before preparing food, to reduce the spread of gastroenteritis
    \item Keep a child who is vomiting or has diarrhoea away from other children and childcare while unwell
    \item Offer small, frequent sips of fluid and use oral rehydration solution if available
    \item Watch for the signs of dehydration: reduced wet nappies, dry mouth, and sunken eyes
    \item Never leave a child unattended while vomiting, and position babies on their side if drowsy
    \item Keep household medicines, cleaning products, and toxic substances well out of reach
\end{itemize}
