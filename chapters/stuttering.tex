\section*{Definition and Overview}
Stuttering, also called stammering, is a speech disorder in which the normal flow of speaking is interrupted by repetitions of sounds or syllables, prolongations of sounds, or blocks where no sound comes out. It is a disruption of timing and coordination in speech production, not a sign of low intelligence, nervousness, or poor parenting, and people who stutter often have perfectly clear ideas that they cannot get out smoothly. Stuttering typically begins in early childhood, usually between two and five years of age, when language skills are developing rapidly. For many children it is a passing phase, but for others it persists into adulthood, and it can affect a person's confidence, social life, education, and career. It affects people of all cultures and languages, and famous examples such as past local politicians and international performers show that stuttering does not limit achievement. This chapter explains the types of stuttering, why it develops, how it is assessed, the effective treatments available for children and adults, and practical ways for families and people who stutter to manage the condition with confidence and dignity.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Stuttering is one of the commonest speech disorders in the world. Around 5 percent of children stutter at some point during the preschool years, while about 1 percent of adults continue to stutter, a ratio that reflects the fact that most childhood stuttering resolves spontaneously. Boys are two to four times more likely to stutter than girls, and girls who stutter are more likely to recover. Stuttering is found in every culture and language studied, and it is not related to intelligence, socioeconomic background, or parenting style. There is a strong genetic contribution: around 60 percent of people who stutter have a family history of the disorder, and several genes have been implicated. Onset is usually gradual between ages two and five, but stuttering can also begin or recur later, for example after a head injury, stroke, or severe psychological stress, and this is important because the cause and management differ. The public profile of stuttering has risen through national stuttering awareness campaigns, and support organisations in many countries provide resources and community for people who stutter.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Stuttering arises from a problem in the brain networks that plan and execute the rapid, coordinated movements of the lips, tongue, and larynx needed for fluent speech. Brain imaging shows differences in the structure and activity of speech areas in people who stutter, including reduced activity in the left hemisphere regions that programme speech movements and increased activity on the right. The exact cause is not fully understood, but the condition clearly involves a combination of genetics, brain development, and environmental influences.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} stuttering runs in families, and genetic studies have identified variations in genes involved in brain cell function and speech-motor control
    \item \textbf{Brain differences:} altered connectivity and activity in the networks linking speech planning, motor control, and the basal ganglia, the brain region that coordinates smooth movement
    \item \textbf{Speech-motor coordination:} a difficulty timing the movements of breathing, voice, and articulation, made worse when speech is fast or pressured
    \item \textbf{Linguistic demands:} stuttering often worsens with longer sentences, new words, or pressure to speak quickly, as the speech system is overloaded
    \item \textbf{Temperament and environment:} sensitive, perfectionist, or anxious temperaments and rushed or critical communication environments can worsen symptoms, though they do not cause the disorder
    \item \textbf{Developmental:} many children recover as their speech-motor system matures, and recovery is associated with strong language skills and family support
    \item \textbf{Acquired causes:} rarely, stuttering follows stroke, head injury, or neurodegenerative disease, when the underlying brain damage is the cause
    \item \textbf{Psychogenic factors:} in a very small number of cases, emotional trauma is linked to onset or recurrence in older children and adults
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The core features of stuttering are the interruptions of speech, which vary with situation, fatigue, and emotion.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Repetitions:} repeating sounds, syllables, or words, such as "b-b-b-ball" or "and-and-and"
    \item \textbf{Prolongations:} stretching a sound out, as in "sssss-sun"
    \item \textbf{Blocks:} pauses where the mouth is ready but no sound comes out for a moment
    \item \textbf{Associated physical tension:} facial grimacing, blinking, head movements, or tense breathing during blocks
    \item \textbf{Situational variability:} fluency often improves when singing, reading aloud in rhythm, or speaking alone, and worsens under time pressure, in phone calls, or when anxious
    \item \textbf{Avoidance behaviour:} substituting words, rephrasing, or avoiding speaking situations to hide stuttering
    \item \textbf{Secondary emotions:} frustration, embarrassment, and anxiety about speaking, which can become a larger problem than the stutter itself
    \item \textbf{Natural recovery in children:} many preschool children who stutter stop without treatment, and disfluency that comes and goes over months is common
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Normal developmental disfluency and other speech conditions must be separated from true stuttering.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal childhood disfluency:} many two- to four-year-olds repeat whole words or phrases ("I want, I want...") without tension or blocks, and this usually resolves as language matures
    \item \textbf{Cluttering:} a different disorder of rapid, irregular, and unclear speech, often with jumbled sentences, which is less effortful than stuttering
    \item \textbf{Language delay:} a child who stumbles because of limited vocabulary or grammar needs language assessment rather than stuttering treatment
    \item \textbf{Tics and Tourette syndrome:} involuntary sounds and movements can be mistaken for the physical accompaniments of stuttering
    \item \textbf{Apractic speech and dysarthria:} motor speech disorders from neurological causes, distinguished by examination and history
    \item \textbf{Autism spectrum disorder:} repetitive language and atypical communication can resemble stuttering in some children
    \item \textbf{Acquired stuttering:} sudden onset after a stroke or head injury in an older person is a neurological symptom requiring urgent assessment
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents who are concerned about a child's fluency should raise it with a general practitioner or see a speech pathologist directly; in many countries, speech pathology services are available through community health centres, public hospitals, schools, and private practices. Early assessment is worthwhile because treatment for young children is highly effective and can prevent the avoidance and anxiety that build up over years. A child should be assessed if disfluency persists for more than six to twelve months, if it involves tension or blocks, if it causes the child distress or avoidance, or if there is a strong family history of stuttering, since these factors reduce the chance of natural recovery. Adults who stutter can also benefit from speech pathology at any age, and sudden new stuttering in an adult or older child warrants urgent medical review.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden loss of the ability to speak, slurred speech, or new stuttering with weakness of the face or limbs, which can indicate stroke
    \item New speech difficulty with confusion, severe headache, or collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Stuttering is diagnosed by a speech pathologist, usually without any medical tests, based on observation and conversation.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Speech sample:} the child or adult is recorded speaking in different situations, and the type and frequency of disfluencies are analysed
    \item \textbf{Case history:} the clinician asks when the stutter began, how it varies, the family history, and the impact on daily life and emotions
    \item \textbf{Standardised assessment:} formal tests measure severity and the effect of stuttering on communication
    \item \textbf{Hearing test:} hearing loss can contribute to speech difficulties and is routinely checked
    \item \textbf{Language assessment:} rules out an underlying language disorder, particularly in children
    \item \textbf{Medical and neurological review:} for sudden or acquired stuttering in older children and adults, imaging and specialist assessment look for stroke or other brain disease
    \item \textbf{Emotional and social assessment:} anxiety, avoidance, and the impact on schooling or work are explored sensitively, as these often need attention alongside speech therapy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Modern treatment of stuttering focuses on building fluency, reducing tension and avoidance, and protecting confidence, rather than simply eliminating the stutter.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Speech restructuring:} techniques such as the Lidcombe Program for young children, in which parents praise fluent speech and gently acknowledge stuttering, are evidence-based and delivered with a speech pathologist
    \item \textbf{Fluency-shaping therapy:} retraining speech with slower, smoother, more deliberate production, practised until it becomes natural
    \item \textbf{Stuttering modification therapy:} teaching people who stutter to stutter more easily, with less tension, and to handle blocks without struggling
    \item \textbf{Parent guidance:} for preschool children, coaching parents to slow the pace of family conversation, give the child unhurried turns to speak, and respond warmly without criticism
    \item \textbf{Cognitive behavioural support:} addressing the anxiety, avoidance, and negative beliefs about speaking that often accompany stuttering
    \item \textbf{School and workplace support:} teachers and employers can be advised on how to support a person who stutters, such as allowing extra time and not finishing sentences for them
    \item \textbf{Support groups:} connecting with other people who stutter, through organisations such as the local Speak Easy Association, reduces isolation and builds confidence
    \item \textbf{Electronic and mobile devices:} delayed auditory feedback devices and apps can help some adults in specific situations, though benefit varies
    \item \textbf{Medication:} there is no medication approved for stuttering in many countries; drugs are rarely used and generally have limited benefit
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Stuttering itself is not physically harmful, but its social and emotional consequences can be significant if untreated.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anxiety and avoidance:} fear of speaking can limit participation in class, work, and social life, and can be more disabling than the stutter itself
    \item \textbf{Reduced self-esteem:} repeated difficult speaking experiences, especially with teasing at school, can damage confidence
    \item \textbf{Educational and career impact:} avoidance of oral presentations, interviews, and phone calls can limit opportunities
    \item \textbf{Teasing and bullying:} children who stutter are more likely to be targeted at school
    \item \textbf{Social isolation:} some people withdraw from conversations and relationships to avoid stuttering
    \item \textbf{Frustration and fatigue:} the physical effort of forcing words can be exhausting over a day
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for stuttering is far better than is commonly believed. Up to 75 to 80 percent of children who stutter recover naturally, usually within a few years of onset, and those who start treatment in the preschool years have excellent outcomes with modern approaches such as the Lidcombe Program. Early treatment, a supportive family environment, and strong language skills all favour recovery. For those who continue to stutter into adolescence and adulthood, therapy does not always eliminate the stutter, but it reliably reduces its severity and, just as importantly, reduces the fear, avoidance, and shame around speaking. Many adults who stutter speak fluently for stretches, use strategies to manage blocks, and lead full professional and personal lives; the research community increasingly emphasises that acceptance and confident communication matter as much as fluency itself. Sudden acquired stuttering from stroke has a more guarded prognosis that depends on the underlying brain injury. Overall, with early support and the right attitudes at home, school, and work, most people who stutter communicate effectively and achieve their goals.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item If your child stutters, slow the pace of family conversation and give the child unhurried, undivided turns to speak
    \item Respond to what your child says, not how they say it, and avoid finishing their sentences or telling them to slow down and start again
    \item Never punish, correct, or mock stuttering, and protect the child from teasing at school
    \item Seek a speech pathology assessment early if disfluency persists, involves tension, or causes distress
    \item For adults, practise speaking in safe, supportive settings and gradually rebuild avoided situations rather than avoiding them forever
    \item Join a support group such as the local Speak Easy Association to meet others who stutter
    \item Educate teachers, colleagues, and employers, who can support by listening patiently and giving extra time
    \item Look after general health: adequate sleep, reduced caffeine, and manageable stress levels help all communication
\end{itemize}
