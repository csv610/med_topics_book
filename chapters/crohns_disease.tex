\chapter{Crohn's Disease}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Crohn's disease is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) that can affect any part of the digestive tract, from the mouth to the anus, but most commonly involves the end of the small bowel and the beginning of the large bowel. It causes inflammation that can penetrate all layers of the bowel wall, leading to abdominal pain, diarrhoea, weight loss, and fatigue. The disease follows a relapsing and remitting course, with periods of active inflammation and periods of remission. There is no cure, but modern treatment can control inflammation, heal the bowel, and allow most people to live full and active lives.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Crohn's disease affects around 1 in 250--300 Australians, and Australia has one of the highest rates of IBD in the world. It most often begins in late adolescence and early adulthood, between 15 and 35 years, with a second smaller peak in older age. The incidence has risen over recent decades, especially in developed countries and among people moving from low- to high-incidence regions. Smoking is the strongest modifiable risk factor and is associated with more severe disease, while a family history increases risk. Crohn's disease affects men and women similarly.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Crohn's disease results from an abnormal immune response to gut contents in genetically susceptible people.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic susceptibility:} many gene variants, especially involving immune and bacterial recognition pathways, increase risk
    \item \textbf{Abnormal immune response:} the immune system attacks the bowel lining as if it were foreign
    \item \textbf{Gut microbiome:} changes in the balance of gut bacteria contribute to inflammation
    \item \textbf{Environmental triggers:} smoking, diet, antibiotics, and infections may trigger disease
    \item \textbf{Transmural inflammation:} inflammation extends through all layers of the bowel wall
    \item \textbf{Complications of inflammation:} ulceration, strictures, fistulas, and abscesses
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms vary with the site and severity of inflammation.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Abdominal pain and cramping, often in the lower right abdomen
    \item Persistent or frequent diarrhoea
    \item Weight loss and poor appetite
    \item Fatigue and generally feeling unwell
    \item Blood or mucus in the stool in some people
    \item Mouth ulcers
    \item Fever during flares
    \item Perianal problems, including fistulas and abscesses
    \item Delayed growth and puberty in children and adolescents
    \item Anaemia from bleeding and poor absorption
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of abdominal pain and diarrhoea are considered.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Ulcerative colitis, the other main form of inflammatory bowel disease
    \item Irritable bowel syndrome, which causes pain and altered bowel habit without inflammation
    \item Infectious gastroenteritis, including from bacteria, viruses, and parasites
    \item Coeliac disease
    \item Appendicitis and other causes of acute abdominal pain
    \item Diverticulitis
    \item Intestinal tuberculosis or other infections
    \item Bowel cancer, especially in older adults
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if you have persistent diarrhoea, abdominal pain, weight loss, or blood in the stool, or if you have been diagnosed with Crohn's disease and develop symptoms of a flare.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe abdominal pain
    \item Vomiting with a distended abdomen (possible bowel obstruction)
    \item High fever with severe abdominal pain (possible abscess or perforation)
    \item Heavy bleeding from the bowel
    \item Signs of severe dehydration
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is made by a gastroenterologist using endoscopy, imaging, and biopsy.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} symptoms, family history, smoking, and examination of the abdomen and perianal area
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, iron studies, inflammatory markers (CRP and ESR), and liver tests
    \item \textbf{Stool tests:} calprotectin to measure gut inflammation, plus infection screening
    \item \textbf{Colonoscopy with biopsy:} the key test, allowing direct inspection and tissue sampling
    \item \textbf{Gastroscopy:} examination of the upper gut when symptoms suggest involvement
    \item \textbf{MRI or CT of the small bowel:} imaging to assess the extent of disease
    \item \textbf{Capsule endoscopy:} a swallowed camera for disease beyond the reach of standard endoscopes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment aims to control inflammation, heal the bowel, and maintain remission.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Corticosteroids:} for rapid control of flares, used short-term
    \item \textbf{Immunomodulators:} azathioprine, mercaptopurine, or methotrexate for long-term control
    \item \textbf{Biological therapies:} infliximab, adalimumab, vedolizumab, ustekinumab, and others target specific inflammatory pathways
    \item \textbf{Small-molecule drugs:} such as tofacitinib, for some patients
    \item \textbf{Nutrition:} exclusive enteral nutrition can treat Crohn's in children; dietary advice supports health
    \item \textbf{Smoking cessation:} essential, as smoking worsens the disease
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} removal of diseased bowel segments, stricture repair, or drainage of abscesses when needed
    \item \textbf{Support:} IBD nurse specialists, psychology, and patient organisations
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Strictures causing bowel obstruction
    \item Fistulas connecting the bowel to other organs or the skin
    \item Abscesses
    \item Malnutrition, anaemia, and vitamin deficiencies
    \item Osteoporosis from inflammation and steroids
    \item Increased risk of bowel cancer with longstanding disease
    \item Complications of treatment, including infection risk with immunosuppression
    \item Psychological impact, including anxiety and depression
    \item Reduced work and social participation during flares
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Crohn's disease is a lifelong condition, but the outlook has improved substantially with modern therapy. Most people alternate between flares and remission, and up to half require surgery at some point, although surgery does not cure the disease. With effective treatment, most people maintain good quality of life, work, and raise families. The main predictors of a more difficult course include smoking, young age at diagnosis, perianal disease, and stricturing or fistulising patterns. Ongoing gastroenterology follow-up and adherence to treatment are central to good outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Do not smoke; smoking increases the risk of relapse and surgery
    \item Take medicines exactly as prescribed, even when well
    \item Keep regular gastroenterology follow-up
    \item Eat a balanced diet, with dietitian advice for deficiencies or restrictive symptoms
    \item Manage stress and maintain sleep and physical activity
    \item Stay up to date with immunisations, and discuss live vaccines with your specialist
    \item Attend cancer screening, including colonoscopy, as recommended
    \item Join a patient support group for information and community
\end{itemize}
