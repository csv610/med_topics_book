\chapter{External Cardioversion}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
External cardioversion is a procedure that uses a controlled electric shock to the chest to restore a normal heart rhythm in people with abnormal rhythms such as atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, and some other arrhythmias. It is performed under sedation or anaesthesia, so the person is asleep and feels no pain. Pads or paddles are placed on the chest, a shock is delivered, and the heart's rhythm is reset. Cardioversion is safe and highly effective, and it is used when symptoms are severe or when medication has not restored a normal rhythm.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cardioversion is a common procedure in hospitals, most often performed for atrial fibrillation, which affects a significant and growing number of Australians, particularly older adults. It is used both in planned (elective) procedures and in emergencies when an arrhythmia causes severe symptoms. Cardioversion restores normal rhythm in most cases, and it is an important part of the management of heart rhythm disorders.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Cardioversion works by resetting the heart's electrical system.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Abnormal rhythms:} atrial fibrillation and flutter involve chaotic electrical activity in the upper chambers, causing an irregular and often fast heartbeat
    \item \textbf{The shock:} a synchronised electric shock briefly stops the heart's electrical activity, allowing the natural pacemaker to resume control
    \item \textbf{Synchronisation:} the shock is timed to the heartbeat to avoid triggering a more dangerous rhythm
    \item \textbf{Why it is used:} to relieve symptoms such as palpitations, breathlessness, and fatigue, and to reduce the risk of complications
    \item \textbf{Anticoagulation:} because cardioversion carries a small risk of stroke from blood clots, most people take blood thinners before and after
    \item \textbf{Not the same as defibrillation:} cardioversion uses a synchronised shock for rhythm disorders, while defibrillation treats cardiac arrest
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Cardioversion is considered in particular situations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Atrial fibrillation or flutter that is causing symptoms
    \item A fast heartbeat that has not responded to medicines
    \item New-onset arrhythmias that need urgent treatment
    \item A planned procedure to restore normal rhythm
    \item The decision is made with the cardiologist, balancing the risks and benefits
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Cardioversion is one of several treatments for arrhythmias.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Medicines to control the heart rate or restore rhythm
    \item Catheter ablation, which treats the abnormal electrical pathway directly
    \item Pacemakers for slow rhythms
    \item Observation, for arrhythmias that settle on their own
    \item The choice depends on the rhythm, symptoms, and the person's health
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with palpitations, breathlessness, chest pain, or fainting should be assessed by a doctor. An arrhythmia causing severe symptoms requires urgent care.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, fainting, or collapse
    \item A very fast or irregular heartbeat with dizziness
    \item Severe breathlessness
    \item Signs of stroke, including weakness or difficulty speaking
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Before cardioversion, the heart is assessed.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ECG:} confirms the rhythm
    \item \textbf{Echocardiogram:} assesses the heart structure and function, and checks for clots
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} including thyroid and electrolyte checks
    \item \textbf{Anticoagulation assessment:} determining the need for blood thinners
    \item \textbf{Fasting:} for the sedation, fasting is required beforehand
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The procedure is planned and safe.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sedation or anaesthesia:} delivered by an anaesthetist, so the person is unaware
    \item \textbf{The shock:} pads deliver a synchronised shock through the chest
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} the rhythm is watched before and after the shock
    \item \textbf{Repeat shocks:} sometimes more than one shock is needed
    \item \textbf{After the procedure:} recovery from sedation, then the rhythm is monitored
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} medicines, including anticoagulation, continue as planned
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Minor skin irritation or redness at the pad sites
    \item The small risk of stroke, reduced by anticoagulation
    \item The effects of sedation and anaesthesia
    \item The rhythm may not convert, or may return later
    \item Rarely, the procedure triggers other rhythm problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Cardioversion successfully restores normal rhythm in the majority of cases, and it relieves symptoms quickly. However, atrial fibrillation often returns, and many people need ongoing medicines or further procedures such as ablation. For others, cardioversion provides long-term normal rhythm. The procedure is safe, and serious complications are uncommon when anticoagulation is managed correctly.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take blood thinners and other medicines as prescribed
    \item Attend follow-up appointments
    \item Report palpitations or other symptoms after the procedure
    \item Manage the risk factors for atrial fibrillation, including blood pressure, weight, and alcohol
    \item Ask the cardiologist about the plan for maintaining normal rhythm
    \item Rest after the procedure as advised
\end{itemize}
