\chapter{Honey Bee Stings}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Honey bee stings are a common summer hazard in Australia. When a honey bee stings, it leaves its barbed stinger and venom sac in the skin and dies. The venom causes immediate pain, redness, and swelling at the site. For most people, the reaction is local and settles within hours, but a minority are allergic to bee venom and can develop a severe, life-threatening allergic reaction called anaphylaxis. Treatment involves prompt removal of the stinger, simple first aid for local reactions, and immediate adrenaline for anaphylaxis.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bee stings are common, particularly in spring and summer and around flowering plants. Most people are stung at some point, and most reactions are mild. Around 3\% of adults have had a systemic allergic reaction to an insect sting, and bee stings cause more deaths in Australia than snake bites, mostly from anaphylaxis in people with undiagnosed allergy. European honey bees, and increasingly aggressive hybrid bees, are the main culprits. People with a known bee allergy face the greatest risk and carry adrenaline.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Venom:} bee venom contains proteins and peptides that cause pain, inflammation, and immune responses
    \item \textbf{Local reaction:} venom triggers histamine release at the sting site, causing pain, redness, and swelling
    \item \textbf{Large local reaction:} extensive swelling spreading beyond the sting site over 24--48 hours
    \item \textbf{Anaphylaxis:} in sensitised people, venom triggers a rapid, widespread allergic response with release of massive amounts of histamine and other mediators
    \item \textbf{Multiple stings:} a swarm attack can cause venom toxicity even in non-allergic people
    \item \textbf{The stinger:} honey bees leave the stinger embedded, and it continues to pump venom
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} mediators dilate blood vessels, constrict airways, and cause the symptoms of anaphylaxis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Immediate sharp pain at the sting site
    \item A small red welt with swelling and itching
    \item The stinger and attached venom sac may remain visible
    \item Large local reactions: swelling over a joint or large area, lasting days
    \item Anaphylaxis: hives away from the site, swelling of the lips and throat, difficulty breathing, wheezing, vomiting, dizziness, and collapse
    \item Symptoms of anaphylaxis usually begin within minutes of the sting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wasps, ants, and other insect stings cause similar local reactions but different venom allergies
    \item Spider bites and other skin reactions
    \item Infection of the sting site, which develops days later with spreading redness, pain, and fever
    \item Cellulitis, which can be mistaken for a large local reaction
    \item The diagnosis of allergy is confirmed by specialist testing after a systemic reaction
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for large local reactions, or for advice and testing after any systemic reaction. People with a known bee allergy should be under specialist care and carry adrenaline.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Any difficulty breathing, wheezing, or throat tightness after a sting
    \item Swelling of the lips, tongue, or face
    \item Hives away from the sting site, vomiting, or dizziness
    \item Collapse, confusion, or loss of consciousness
    \item Many stings from a swarm attack
    \item Use an adrenaline autoinjector immediately if available, even before calling
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} local reactions are diagnosed by history and examination
    \item \textbf{Allergy testing:} skin tests and specific IgE blood tests confirm bee venom allergy
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to an allergy specialist after any systemic reaction
    \item \textbf{Action plan:} anaphylaxis plans are written for people with confirmed allergy
    \item \textbf{Infection assessment:} for sting sites that become painful or spreading after days
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Remove the stinger:} scrape it out sideways with a fingernail or credit card; do not squeeze, as this injects more venom
    \item \textbf{Local reaction care:} cold compresses, elevation, and simple analgesia
    \item \textbf{Antihistamines:} oral antihistamines reduce itching and swelling
    \item \textbf{Large local reactions:} antihistamines and, if severe, a short course of steroids
    \item \textbf{Anaphylaxis:} adrenaline given promptly via autoinjector, then emergency care
    \item \textbf{Venom immunotherapy:} desensitisation treatment for people with confirmed allergy, which prevents future severe reactions
    \item \textbf{Observation:} people with significant reactions are observed in emergency care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Anaphylaxis, which can be fatal without prompt adrenaline
    \item Secondary bacterial infection of the sting site
    \item Large local reactions causing discomfort and swelling
    \item Swarm attacks causing toxic venom effects
    \item Anxiety about stings and avoidance of outdoor activity
    \item Reactions to subsequent stings in allergic people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most bee stings cause a brief, self-limiting reaction that settles without treatment. Anaphylaxis is treated successfully when adrenaline is given promptly, and venom immunotherapy prevents severe reactions in around 90\% of treated people. The most important factor in good outcomes is preparedness: people with known allergy carry adrenaline, and everyone knows to call 000 and treat promptly. With appropriate management, the risks of bee stings are small.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep food and drinks covered outdoors, where bees gather
    \item Wear light-coloured clothing and avoid perfume and scented products
    \item Remain calm and still, or walk away slowly, if a bee approaches
    \item Do not swat at bees or disturb hives and nests
    \item Have bee hives on your property removed by professionals
    \item People with bee allergy carry adrenaline at all times and wear a medical alert
    \item Learn to use an adrenaline autoinjector and teach family and friends
    \item Consider venom immunotherapy if you have confirmed allergy
\end{itemize}
