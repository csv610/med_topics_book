\chapter{Indigestion (Dyspepsia)}
\index{Indigestion (Dyspepsia)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dyspepsia, commonly called indigestion, is a collection of symptoms originating in the upper digestive tract: pain or discomfort in the upper abdomen, fullness after meals, early satiety (feeling full very quickly), bloating, belching, and nausea. It is a symptom, not a disease, and it may be caused by a range of conditions from lifestyle factors to peptic ulcer disease. When no cause is found despite appropriate investigation, it is called functional dyspepsia. Around one in five people with persistent dyspepsia have a peptic ulcer, and a small number have more serious disease, so symptoms that persist or occur with warning features require medical assessment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Dyspepsia is one of the most common reasons for consulting a doctor and for the use of acid-suppressing medicines. Surveys suggest around 20 to 25 per cent of people have dyspepsia in any year, although only a minority seek medical care. It is slightly more common in women and increases with age. Helicobacter pylori infection, which underlies most ulcers, is present in about a quarter of people but in a higher proportion of older people and those born overseas. Use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and aspirin accounts for many cases, particularly in older people.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Dyspepsia arises from irritation or dysfunction of the stomach, oesophagus, or duodenum. Gastric acid and the enzyme pepsin can damage the protective mucosal lining when defences are impaired by H. pylori infection, NSAIDs, smoking, or alcohol. Acid refluxing into the oesophagus causes heartburn. In functional dyspepsia, investigations are normal but the stomach empties slowly, or its nerve endings are hypersensitive to normal distension, so even small meals cause fullness and pain. The gut-brain axis means stress, anxiety, and depression amplify symptoms. Understanding the specific mechanism in each person guides treatment, because an ulcer needs different care from functional dyspepsia.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent or recurring pain, burning, or discomfort in the upper abdomen
    \item Feeling full very quickly when eating, or uncomfortably full after meals
    \item Bloating, excessive wind, and frequent belching
    \item Nausea, and occasionally retching or vomiting
    \item Heartburn and regurgitation when acid reflux is present
    \item Symptoms may be triggered by fatty, spicy, or large meals, alcohol, or stress
    \item Symptoms may disturb sleep or be worse on an empty stomach
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because dyspepsia is a symptom complex, the underlying cause must be distinguished.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Functional dyspepsia:} symptoms with no abnormality found on investigation
    \item \textbf{Peptic ulcer disease:} gastric or duodenal ulcers, often H. pylori-related or NSAID-related
    \item \textbf{Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease:} acid reflux with heartburn and regurgitation
    \item \textbf{Gastritis:} inflammation of the stomach lining
    \item \textbf{Gallstone disease:} right upper abdominal pain after fatty meals
    \item \textbf{Coeliac disease:} gluten sensitivity causing bloating and nausea
    \item \textbf{Gastroparesis:} delayed stomach emptying, especially in diabetes
    \item \textbf{Stomach or oesophageal cancer:} rare, but presenting with weight loss, bleeding, or difficulty swallowing
    \item \textbf{Cardiac disease:} atypical angina may present as upper abdominal discomfort
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Medical assessment is recommended for dyspepsia that is severe, persistent, or recurring, or that occurs with weight loss, difficulty swallowing, vomiting, black stools, or anaemia. Anyone over 55 with new-onset dyspepsia should be investigated, as should people taking NSAIDs or with a family history of stomach cancer. Review regular medicines with a doctor or pharmacist.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain or pressure, especially with breathlessness, sweating, or pain spreading to the arm or jaw
    \item Vomiting blood or material resembling coffee grounds
    \item Black, tarry, or blood-stained stools
    \item Sudden, severe abdominal pain
    \item Inability to swallow food or fluids
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} symptom pattern, alarm features, medicines, smoking, and alcohol
    \item \textbf{H. pylori testing:} urea breath test, stool antigen test, or biopsy at endoscopy
    \item \textbf{Trial of acid suppression:} a four- to eight-week course of a proton pump inhibitor
    \item \textbf{Gastroscopy:} endoscopy with biopsies to examine the oesophagus, stomach, and duodenum, recommended in older people and those with alarm features
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count for anaemia, plus tests for coeliac disease, thyroid, liver, or pancreas as indicated
    \item \textbf{Abdominal ultrasound:} for suspected gallstones or other upper abdominal pathology
    \item \textbf{Gastric emptying studies:} where gastroparesis is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle change:} small regular meals, avoiding trigger foods, limiting alcohol and caffeine, and stopping smoking
    \item \textbf{Weight loss:} if overweight, to reduce intra-abdominal pressure
    \item \textbf{Acid suppression:} proton pump inhibitors or H2 receptor antagonists
    \item \textbf{Antacids and alginates:} for quick symptom relief
    \item \textbf{H. pylori eradication:} a two-week course of antibiotics with a proton pump inhibitor
    \item \textbf{Medication review:} stopping or reducing NSAIDs where possible
    \item \textbf{Functional dyspepsia:} prokinetic medicines, low-dose tricyclic antidepressants, or psychological therapies
    \item \textbf{Stress management:} relaxation training, mindfulness, and treatment of anxiety or depression
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review of response and further investigation if symptoms persist
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Peptic ulcer complications: bleeding, perforation, and obstruction
    \item Iron-deficiency anaemia from chronic blood loss
    \item Oesophageal strictures and Barrett's oesophagus in long-standing reflux
    \item Weight loss and nutritional deficiency from poor intake
    \item Chronic pain, anxiety, and reduced quality of life
    \item Risks of long-term acid suppression, including infection and reduced absorption of some nutrients
    \item Delay in diagnosing serious disease when symptoms are self-treated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for dyspepsia is generally good. Functional dyspepsia is a chronic but manageable condition, with most people improving with lifestyle change, acid suppression, and stress management, though symptoms often relapse. Ulcer disease usually heals completely with acid suppression and, where present, H. pylori eradication, and recurrence is much lower once the infection is treated. The small proportion of people with cancer are best served by early investigation, which is why persistent symptoms and warning features should never be ignored.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Eat regular, smaller meals and chew food well
    \item Avoid known trigger foods and late meals
    \item Limit alcohol, caffeine, and carbonated drinks
    \item Stop smoking
    \item Keep a healthy weight
    \item Take NSAIDs only when necessary and with food, or under medical advice
    \item Avoid tight clothing and lying flat for a few hours after eating
    \item Manage stress with exercise, relaxation, and adequate sleep
    \item Seek medical advice for persistent symptoms rather than self-medicating indefinitely
\end{itemize}
