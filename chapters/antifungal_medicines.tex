\chapter{Antifungal Medicines}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Antifungal medicines are drugs that kill fungi or stop them from growing and multiplying. They are used to treat fungal infections ranging from common superficial conditions such as ringworm, athlete's foot, and oral or vaginal thrush, to rare but life-threatening infections of the bloodstream and internal organs.

Antifungals are given in two main forms. \textbf{Topical} preparations -- creams, lotions, powders, sprays, and vaginal pessaries -- are used for infections of the skin, nails, and mucous membranes. \textbf{Systemic} treatment -- tablets, capsules, or intravenous infusions -- is needed for infections that are widespread, resistant, or deep. The major classes include the \textbf{azoles} (fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole, clotrimazole, miconazole), \textbf{terbinafine}, the \textbf{polyenes} (nystatin and amphotericin B), and the \textbf{echinocandins}, the last of which are used mainly in hospital for severe invasive disease.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Superficial fungal infections are among the most common conditions seen in primary care. Most people will experience at least one in their lifetime: athlete's foot and ringworm affect children and adults across all age groups, and vulvovaginal candidiasis (thrush) affects the majority of women at some point. Fungal nail infections become increasingly common with age and are more frequent in men, in people with diabetes, and in those with reduced circulation to the feet.

Invasive fungal infections are much rarer but have a high death rate. They occur almost exclusively in people with weakened immune systems, including those receiving chemotherapy or immunosuppressant drugs, organ and stem cell transplant recipients, and people with advanced HIV infection. Rates of invasive candidiasis, aspergillosis, and cryptococcal disease have risen in parallel with these therapies, making fungal disease an increasingly important hospital problem.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Fungi are eukaryotic organisms, far more similar to human cells than bacteria are, which makes selective antifungal therapy difficult. Antifungals exploit the small biochemical differences that do exist between fungal and human cells:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ergosterol synthesis:} fungal cell membranes are built around the sterol ergosterol. The azoles block the enzyme that produces it, while the polyenes (nystatin and amphotericin B) bind directly to it and punch holes in the membrane
    \item \textbf{Cell wall integrity:} the echinocandins interfere with the synthesis of glucan, a key component of the fungal cell wall that human cells do not possess
    \item \textbf{Squalene epoxidase:} terbinafine blocks this enzyme, which is needed to construct fungal membranes, and accumulates within the skin and nails
\end{itemize}

Predisposing factors for infection include recent broad-spectrum antibiotics, poorly controlled diabetes, impaired blood flow to the feet, immune suppression, pregnancy, and the warm, moist conditions that allow fungi to flourish in skin folds and footwear.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Expected effect:} progressive clearing of itch, rash, scaling, discharge, or nail discolouration over days to months
    \item \textbf{Skin infection:} an itchy, scaly, ring-shaped patch with a clearer centre (ringworm); cracked, peeling, and sore skin between the toes (athlete's foot); or scaly, discoloured patches in the groin (jock itch)
    \item \textbf{Vaginal thrush:} vulval itching and burning, soreness, and a thick, white, curd-like discharge
    \item \textbf{Oral thrush:} creamy white patches on the tongue, inner cheeks, or palate that can be wiped off to leave red, sore mucosa
    \item \textbf{Nail infection:} thickened, yellowed, brittle, or crumbling nails, often with surrounding skin involvement
    \item \textbf{Side effects:} topical products may sting or burn briefly; oral agents can cause nausea, headache, abdominal discomfort, and taste disturbance
    \item \textbf{Liver effects:} oral azoles can raise liver enzyme levels, usually without symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Eczema, psoriasis, or contact dermatitis:} inflammatory rashes that can closely resemble ringworm or candidiasis
    \item \textbf{Bacterial skin infections} or mixed fungal-bacterial infections
    \item \textbf{Nail psoriasis or trauma:} nail changes from causes other than fungus, which do not respond to antifungals
    \item \textbf{Recurrence from an untreated predisposing cause:} such as uncontrolled diabetes or an infected family member or pet
    \item \textbf{Resistant or second fungal species:} or an incorrect initial diagnosis
    \item \textbf{For mouth patches:} lichen planus, leukoplakia, or oral hairy leukoplakia
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if a fungal infection is spreading despite treatment, is not improving after a couple of weeks, or keeps returning. Medical review is also needed before starting oral antifungal treatment in pregnancy, if you take other regular medicines, or if you have liver disease.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing or swelling of the face, lips, or throat (severe allergic reaction)
    \item High fever with confusion, rapid heart rate, or severe illness in a person at risk of invasive fungal infection
    \item Any suspected fungal infection in a person with a severely weakened immune system
\end{itemize}

Also seek urgent advice if, while taking oral antifungal tablets, you develop yellowing of the skin or eyes, very dark urine, severe stomach pain, or persistent vomiting.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical inspection:} many skin and nail infections are diagnosed on appearance and location alone
    \item \textbf{Skin scraping or nail clippings:} examined under a microscope after potassium hydroxide preparation to demonstrate fungal filaments (hyphae)
    \item \textbf{Fungal culture:} growing the organism to identify the species, which guides treatment choice and detects resistance
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} for invasive disease, including blood cultures, antigen tests (such as galactomannan), and molecular (PCR) assays
    \item \textbf{Liver function tests:} performed before and during treatment with systemic azoles and terbinafine
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT or other scanning for suspected lung, sinus, or abdominal fungal infection in immunocompromised patients
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Topical treatment:} creams or lotions for most skin infections, applied to the affected area and a margin of healthy skin around it, usually for one to four weeks
    \item \textbf{Longer courses for nails and scalp:} nail infections require weeks to months of treatment, often with oral terbinafine or itraconazole, and may not fully resolve
    \item \textbf{Oral medicines:} for widespread, recurrent, or internal infections; the dose, choice, and duration are determined by a doctor
    \item \textbf{Complete the course:} stopping early is the single most common cause of recurrence
    \item \textbf{Treat predisposing factors:} optimise blood sugar control, keep the skin dry, and treat household contacts and pets for ringworm to prevent reinfection
    \item \textbf{Intravenous therapy:} echinocandins, liposomal amphotericin B, and other agents given in hospital for invasive infection, with specialist infectious diseases or haematology input
    \item \textbf{Recurrent thrush:} may require longer courses, maintenance therapy, or treatment of the sexual partner
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Treatment failure or frequent recurrence, especially with nail, scalp, and groin infections
    \item Drug interactions: the azoles, particularly itraconazole and voriconazole, can raise levels of warfarin, statins, and many other medicines
    \item Liver injury with systemic azoles and terbinafine, requiring monitoring
    \item Allergic reactions, from contact dermatitis to anaphylaxis
    \item Antifungal resistance, particularly in recurrent thrush repeatedly treated with azoles, and increasingly in invasive candidiasis
    \item Kidney damage and infusion reactions with amphotericin B, which is why lipid formulations are preferred
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most superficial fungal infections clear completely with correct treatment: skin infections typically respond within weeks, whereas nails may require months of therapy and do not always return to normal. Vaginal thrush usually settles quickly but is prone to recurrence, especially if predisposing factors such as uncontrolled diabetes or repeated antibiotic courses are not addressed.

Invasive fungal infections are serious and carry a real risk of death, particularly in the immunocompromised. Outcomes improve substantially with early recognition, prompt antifungal therapy, and management of the underlying immune problem. Antifungal resistance is an emerging concern, so cultures and sensitivity testing are important for recurrent or severe infection.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep the skin clean and dry, especially the feet, groin, and skin folds
    \item Wear breathable footwear, change socks daily, and dry carefully between the toes
    \item Avoid sharing towels, shoes, socks, and nail clippers
    \item Wear gloves for wet work and change out of damp clothing promptly
    \item Finish full courses of treatment, even when the skin already looks better
    \item Manage diabetes and other conditions that raise the risk of fungal infection
    \item In hospital or while immunosuppressed, follow advice about avoiding mould and fungal exposure, including ventilation precautions
\end{itemize}
