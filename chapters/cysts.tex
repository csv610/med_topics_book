\chapter{Cysts}
\index{Cysts}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A cyst is a closed sac or pocket of tissue that is filled with fluid, air, or other material. Cysts can develop almost anywhere in the body, including the skin, kidneys, liver, ovaries, breasts, and bones. Most cysts are benign and cause no symptoms, and many are found incidentally on imaging performed for other reasons. Some cysts resolve on their own, while others persist and occasionally need treatment if they grow, become painful, infected, or press on nearby structures. The management of a cyst depends on its location, size, and appearance, and most can be confidently assessed with imaging.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cysts are extremely common. Skin cysts such as epidermoid and pilar cysts affect many people; kidney cysts are present in a quarter or more of older adults; and ovarian cysts occur in many women during the reproductive years. Most are harmless, and the majority are discovered incidentally. Simple cysts rarely become cancerous, but complex cysts and those that grow or change may need further investigation. The frequency of cysts generally increases with age.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Cysts form through a variety of mechanisms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blocked ducts:} a blocked gland or duct causes fluid to accumulate, as in skin and breast cysts
    \item \textbf{Infections:} some cysts, such as pilonidal cysts, follow infection
    \item \textbf{Chronic conditions:} polycystic kidney disease and other inherited conditions cause many cysts
    \item \textbf{Developmental remnants:} some cysts form from tissue left over in development
    \item \textbf{Trauma:} injury can cause fluid collections, such as ganglion cysts
    \item \textbf{Degeneration:} cysts can form within organs as part of ageing, as in the kidneys and ovaries
    \item \textbf{Tumours:} some cysts are part of a benign or, rarely, malignant growth
    \item \textbf{Structure:} a cyst is lined by cells that secrete fluid, and it grows as fluid accumulates
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Many cysts are asymptomatic and found incidentally.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A painless lump or swelling that can be felt under the skin
    \item A smooth, round, mobile swelling, sometimes with a central opening or punctum
    \item Pain or tenderness if the cyst grows, presses on structures, or becomes infected
    \item Redness, swelling, and discharge if infected
    \item Symptoms related to the organ involved, such as abdominal discomfort with ovarian or kidney cysts
    \item No symptoms at all, with the cyst found on imaging
    \item Rarely, bleeding, rupture, or compression of nearby structures
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A lump is not always a cyst, and other possibilities are considered.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Solid tumours, benign or malignant, which appear different on imaging
    \item Abscesses, which are infected collections of pus
    \item Hernias, enlarged lymph nodes, and other lumps
    \item Lipomas and other benign growths of fat or connective tissue
    \item Ganglions, which arise from joints or tendons
    \item Cancers of organs such as the ovary, kidney, or breast, which can be cystic in appearance
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if you notice a new lump, a lump that changes, grows, or becomes painful, or symptoms related to a cyst. Most cysts are harmless, but assessment is important.

\textbf{Seek medical care promptly if:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A lump becomes painful, red, hot, or discharges
    \item A cyst grows rapidly
    \item There is fever with pain, suggesting infection
    \item You have new abdominal pain, swelling, or bleeding
    \item A breast or other organ lump is new or changing
    \item You have a family history of polycystic kidney disease
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:} sudden severe abdominal pain, collapse, or heavy bleeding, which can occur with a ruptured or bleeding cyst.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment depends on the location of the cyst.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the size, site, and feel of the lump
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} the most common first imaging test, distinguishing simple cysts from complex or solid lesions
    \item \textbf{CT or MRI:} for cysts in the abdomen, chest, bones, or brain
    \item \textbf{Aspiration:} a needle can drain fluid for examination
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} for suspicious or complex cysts
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} for specific organ cysts, such as kidney function or tumour markers
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to the relevant specialist, such as a dermatologist, surgeon, or gynaecologist
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Most cysts need no treatment, and management is guided by risk.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observation:} simple, asymptomatic cysts are usually left alone and monitored
    \item \textbf{Drainage:} aspiration for cysts that are painful or uncomfortable
    \item \textbf{Surgical removal:} for cysts that grow, recur, press on structures, or look suspicious
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} for infected cysts
    \item \textbf{Incision and drainage:} for skin cysts that are infected or discharging
    \item \textbf{Specialist treatment:} organ-specific management, such as hormonal treatment or surgery for ovarian cysts
    \item \textbf{Treatment of underlying conditions:} such as polycystic kidney disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection and abscess formation
    \item Rupture, causing sudden pain, especially with ovarian cysts
    \item Bleeding into the cyst
    \item Pressure on nearby structures, such as nerves, blood vessels, or organs
    \item Torsion (twisting) of an ovarian cyst, a surgical emergency
    \item Recurrence after drainage
    \item Rarely, cancer within a complex cyst
    \item Anxiety about the nature of a lump
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for cysts is generally excellent. Simple cysts are harmless and often remain unchanged or resolve on their own, and the majority need no treatment. Cysts that are drained or removed usually do not return, although some types recur. Complex or suspicious cysts are investigated further, and the outcome depends on what is found. With imaging, it is usually possible to tell a benign cyst from something more concerning, providing reassurance and guiding treatment.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item See a doctor about any new or changing lump
    \item Do not squeeze, poke, or try to drain skin cysts, which can cause infection
    \item Keep the skin over a cyst clean
    \item Apply a warm compress to an inflamed cyst and seek medical advice
    \item Attend follow-up imaging if monitoring is recommended
    \item Be aware of your family history, especially for polycystic kidney disease
    \item Seek prompt care for sudden severe pain, which can indicate rupture or torsion
\end{itemize}
