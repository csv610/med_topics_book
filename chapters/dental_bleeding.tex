\chapter{Dental Bleeding}
\index{Dental Bleeding}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dental bleeding refers to bleeding from the gums, the teeth, or the mouth after dental procedures, injury, or as a result of gum disease. Bleeding gums when brushing is one of the most common signs of gum inflammation (gingivitis). Bleeding can also occur after tooth extraction, from trauma, or as a complication of medicines that thin the blood. Most dental bleeding is minor and easily managed, but persistent or heavy bleeding can indicate a problem requiring dental or medical attention. This chapter covers the causes, management, and prevention of dental bleeding.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bleeding gums are extremely common, affecting a large proportion of adults; around one in three people has moderate gum disease, and gingivitis is widespread. Bleeding after tooth extraction occurs in a small proportion of cases, and the risk is higher in people taking anticoagulant or antiplatelet medicines. In children, dental trauma is common and can cause bleeding from the mouth. Many people take blood-thinning medicines for heart conditions or blood clots, making dental bleeding an important consideration in dental care planning.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gingivitis:} plaque build-up along the gum line inflames the gums, which bleed easily on brushing or flossing
    \item \textbf{Periodontitis:} advanced gum disease damages the tissues supporting the teeth, causing deeper pockets and bleeding
    \item \textbf{Trauma:} injury to the teeth or gums from falls, sport, or accidents
    \item \textbf{Dental procedures:} extraction, cleaning, and other treatments can cause temporary bleeding
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} anticoagulants such as warfarin, apixaban, and rivaroxaban, and antiplatelets such as aspirin and clopidogrel, increase bleeding
    \item \textbf{Medical conditions:} bleeding disorders such as haemophilia, liver disease, and low platelets
    \item \textbf{Poor oral hygiene:} inadequate brushing and flossing allow plaque to accumulate
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Blood on the toothbrush, floss, or in the sink after brushing
    \item Pink or red gums, swelling, and tenderness
    \item Bleeding from a specific tooth or site after trauma or treatment
    \item Persistent oozing after tooth extraction, which usually settles within a few hours
    \item Bad breath and a bad taste, associated with gum disease
    \item Loose teeth or receding gums in periodontitis
    \item Signs of significant blood loss: light-headedness, weakness, or pallor, in rare cases of heavy bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Gingivitis versus periodontitis, distinguished by pocket depth and bone loss
    \item Trauma-related bleeding, including fractured teeth or soft tissue injury
    \item Bleeding after dental treatment, which is expected in the short term
    \item Bleeding disorders or medication effects, which cause abnormal or prolonged bleeding
    \item Mouth ulcers or infections that bleed when touched
    \item Rarely, oral cancer, which can cause persistent unexplained bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a dentist for persistent bleeding gums, bleeding that does not stop, or bleeding after trauma. People taking blood-thinning medicines should tell their dentist before any procedure. Seek dental review if bleeding after an extraction continues beyond 24 hours or restarts after stopping.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding that will not stop despite firm pressure for 20--30 minutes
    \item Heavy bleeding with light-headedness, dizziness, or fainting
    \item Bleeding after a serious head or facial injury
    \item A tooth knocked out with bleeding that continues
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dental examination:} assessment of the gums, teeth, and oral tissues
    \item \textbf{Periodontal probing:} measuring gum pocket depths to assess gum disease
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} identify bone loss around teeth and damage from trauma
    \item \textbf{Medication review:} identifying anticoagulants and antiplatelets
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} clotting studies, platelet count, and other tests where a bleeding disorder is suspected
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a periodontist or haematologist for complex cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gum bleeding:} improved brushing and flossing, professional dental cleaning, and treatment of gum disease
    \item \textbf{After extraction:} bite firmly on gauze for 30--60 minutes, avoid vigorous rinsing, and avoid hot drinks and strenuous activity for 24 hours
    \item \textbf{Trauma:} dental first aid and urgent dental review; a knocked-out tooth should be kept moist and taken to a dentist immediately
    \item \textbf{Blood thinners:} dental treatment is usually planned with the prescriber; in most cases medicines are continued and bleeding is managed locally
    \item \textbf{Local measures:} pressure, haemostatic dressings, and stitches may be used by the dentist
    \item \textbf{Medical treatment:} management of bleeding disorders or adjustment of medicines, coordinated between dentist and doctor
    \item \textbf{Treating the cause:} scaling and root planing for periodontitis, and treatment of any infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progression of gum disease to periodontitis with tooth loss
    \item Prolonged or excessive bleeding after dental procedures
    \item Infection in a socket after extraction (dry socket)
    \item Blood loss causing anaemia or collapse in rare severe cases
    \item Anxiety about dental care, leading to avoidance of treatment
    \item Complications of underlying bleeding disorders
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Bleeding gums from gingivitis resolve completely with good oral hygiene and professional cleaning, and healthy gums stop bleeding within a few days to weeks of consistent care. Periodontitis can be managed to prevent further damage, though supporting bone loss is not regained. Bleeding after dental procedures settles with appropriate local care in the vast majority of cases. With coordinated care, people taking blood thinners can undergo dental treatment safely, and complications are uncommon.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Brush twice daily with fluoride toothpaste and floss or clean between teeth daily
    \item Do not stop brushing bleeding gums; bleeding reduces as gums become healthier
    \item Visit the dentist regularly for check-ups and professional cleaning
    \item Inform your dentist about all medicines, especially blood thinners
    \item Wear a mouthguard for contact sport
    \item Follow dentist advice after extractions and other procedures
    \item Eat a balanced diet and limit sugary foods and drinks
\end{itemize}
