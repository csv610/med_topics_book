\chapter{Epidural Anaesthetic}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An epidural is a type of regional anaesthetic in which medicines are injected through a small tube into the space around the spinal cord, numbing the lower half of the body. It is most commonly used for pain relief in labour and childbirth, and it is also used for surgical procedures on the lower body and for the management of severe pain. During labour, an epidural provides excellent pain relief while allowing the mother to remain awake and aware. Epidurals are safe and effective, and they are given by trained anaesthetists.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Epidurals are used by a large proportion of women in labour in Australia, and they are one of the most common forms of pain relief in childbirth. They are also used for major surgery of the lower limbs, pelvis, and abdomen, and for the treatment of severe postoperative and chronic pain. The use of epidurals has increased as techniques and safety have improved.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
An epidural works by blocking nerve signals from the lower body.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The epidural space:} the area between the spinal cord's covering and the bones of the spine
    \item \textbf{Insertion:} a needle is placed in the lower back, and a fine catheter is threaded into the epidural space
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} local anaesthetics and sometimes opioids are injected through the catheter
    \item \textbf{Effect:} the medicines block the nerves carrying sensation from the uterus, pelvis, and legs, providing pain relief without loss of consciousness
    \item \textbf{Ongoing relief:} the catheter allows continuous or repeat doses throughout labour or surgery
    \item \textbf{Mobility:} the legs are weakened, so the woman is limited to bed during labour
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
An epidural is offered in specific situations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain relief in labour, on request or for medical reasons
    \item Anaesthesia for caesarean section and other lower-body surgery
    \item Postoperative pain relief after major surgery
    \item Management of some chronic pain conditions
    \item The procedure takes about 15 to 30 minutes
    \item Effects begin within 10 to 20 minutes
    \item Pain relief can be topped up during labour
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
There are alternatives to an epidural.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Other labour pain relief: nitrous oxide gas, opioids, and water immersion
    \item Spinal anaesthetic, a single injection used for caesarean sections
    \item Combined spinal-epidural
    \item General anaesthesia for surgery
    \item The choice depends on the situation and the woman's preferences
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Women considering an epidural should discuss it with their midwife and obstetric team. The anaesthetist assesses suitability and explains the procedure and risks.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe headache after an epidural that does not settle
    \item Numbness or weakness that does not wear off
    \item Fever or signs of infection at the insertion site
    \item Difficulty breathing or other concerning symptoms after the procedure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Before an epidural, the woman is assessed.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Assessment:} medical history, blood tests, and any conditions affecting the spine or blood clotting
    \item \textbf{Consent:} discussion of the benefits, risks, and alternatives
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} blood pressure and the baby's heart rate are monitored during labour with an epidural
    \item \textbf{Insertion:} performed sitting or lying, with local anaesthetic to numb the skin
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The epidural is managed during labour and afterwards.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Continuous or intermittent doses:} through the catheter
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure monitoring:} because epidurals can lower blood pressure
    \item \textbf{Fluids:} an intravenous line is placed in case fluids or medicines are needed
    \item \textbf{Bladder care:} a catheter may be needed because the epidural reduces the urge to pass urine
    \item \textbf{Assistance with delivery:} the epidural can be topped up for a forceps, vacuum, or caesarean delivery
    \item \textbf{Removal:} the catheter is removed when no longer needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Low blood pressure, which is monitored and treated
    \item Headache from leakage of spinal fluid, which usually settles and is treated if persistent
    \item Patchy or incomplete pain relief
    \item Backache and bruising at the insertion site
    \item Difficulty passing urine
    \item Nerve damage, which is rare
    \item Very rare serious complications, including infection and bleeding near the spine
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Epidurals are safe and highly effective, and serious complications are very rare. They provide excellent pain relief in labour and allow many women to have a comfortable and positive birth experience. Epidurals do not harm the baby, and they can be used when a caesarean or instrumental delivery is needed.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discuss pain relief options with your midwife and obstetric team during pregnancy
    \item Ask questions and raise concerns before the procedure
    \item Report numbness, weakness, or headache after the epidural
    \item Keep mobile in early labour before the epidural
    \item Follow the team's advice on positioning and monitoring
    \item Seek care promptly for any new symptoms after discharge
\end{itemize}
