\chapter{Medicines for colds}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Medicines for colds encompass a diverse range of over-the-counter (OTC) and prescription pharmacological agents designed to alleviate the symptomatic distress associated with the common cold (acute viral rhinosinusitis). The common cold is a self-limiting viral infection of the upper respiratory tract, primarily caused by rhinoviruses, coronaviruses, adenoviruses, and respiratory syncytial virus. Because there are no approved antiviral therapies that eradicate these viral pathogens, pharmacotherapy is entirely supportive, aiming to reduce the severity of symptoms such as nasal congestion, rhinorrhoea, sore throat, cough, and systemic myalgia or low-grade fever. Cold medications include analgesics, decongestants, antihistamines, antitussives, expectorants, and combination products. A critical clinical principle in using cold medicines is to match the treatment to the specific symptoms, avoiding unnecessary medications and being mindful of age-restricted formulations (particularly in paediatric patients) and potential drug interactions or contraindications.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs) are the most frequent infectious diseases in humans, making cold medicines some of the most widely consumed pharmaceutical products globally. Adults experience an average of 2 to 4 colds annually, while young children typically experience 6 to 10 episodes per year due to their developing immune systems and close contact in school or childcare settings. Consequently, the economic impact of over-the-counter cold remedy sales is multi-billion dollar worldwide, including in Australia. Epidemiological concerns surrounding cold medicines focus heavily on safety: accidental overdoses, inappropriate paediatric usage, and cardiovascular complications in older adults using oral decongestants are significant causes of emergency department presentations.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The pharmacological targets of cold medicines correspond directly to the physiological pathways triggered by upper respiratory viral infections.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory mediator release:} Viral invasion of the nasal mucosa triggers the release of histamine, bradykinin, prostaglandins, and cytokines, causing mucosal vasodilation, vascular permeability, and local oedema.
    \item \textbf{Nasal congestion:} Vasodilation of the venous sinusoids within the nasal mucosa increases blood volume and swelling, narrowing the nasal airways.
    \item \textbf{Rhinorrhoea and sneezing:} Stimulation of local parasympathetic nerves by inflammatory mediators, causing glandular hypersecretion and triggering sensory nerve endings.
    \item \textbf{Sore throat and cough:} Direct viral irritation of the pharyngeal mucosa and stimulation of cough receptors in the respiratory tract by post-nasal drip.
    \item \textbf{Systemic symptoms:} Viral-induced cytokine release (e.g. interferon-alpha) acting on the hypothalamus to raise body temperature, causing low-grade fever, headache, and myalgia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Cold medicines are selected based on the specific clinical features presenting during the course of the viral infection.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nasal congestion:} Subjective feeling of nasal blockage, difficulty breathing through the nose, and sinus pressure.
    \item \textbf{Rhinorrhoea and post-nasal drip:} Clear, watery nasal discharge that may become thick, yellow, or green over the course of the infection, causing throat irritation.
    \item \textbf{Pharyngeal discomfort:} A dry, scratchy, or painful sensation in the throat, especially during swallowing.
    \item \textbf{Cough:} A protective reflex that may be dry (non-productive) due to upper airway irritation, or chesty (productive) with clear sputum.
    \item \textbf{Constitutional symptoms:} Mild headache, general tiredness, low-grade fever (typically under 38.5 degrees Celsius), and mild muscle aches.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before recommending or prescribing cold medicines, other serious respiratory or systemic conditions must be excluded.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Influenza (the flu):} Characterised by sudden onset of high fever, severe myalgia, prostration, and dry cough, which may require antiviral treatment (e.g. oseltamivir) in high-risk patients.
    \item \textbf{Allergic rhinitis:} Non-infectious inflammation presenting with paroxysmal sneezing, itchy eyes, and clear rhinorrhoea, responding to intranasal corticosteroids and non-sedating antihistamines.
    \item \textbf{Acute bacterial sinusitis:} Secondary bacterial infection presenting with unilateral facial pain, maxillary toothache, purulent nasal discharge, and worsening symptoms after initial improvement ("double sickening").
    \item \textbf{Streptococcal pharyngitis (strep throat):} Bacterial throat infection characterised by severe throat pain, tonsillar exudates, anterior cervical lymphadenopathy, and absence of cough.
    \item \textbf{Asthma or acute bronchitis:} Lower respiratory tract conditions presenting with wheezing, dyspnoea, or productive cough, requiring bronchodilators or inhaled steroids.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Colds are generally self-limiting, resolving within 7 to 10 days. Patients should consult a doctor if symptoms worsen, fail to improve after 10 days, or are accompanied by high fever. Immediate emergency medical services should be contacted for severe respiratory distress or signs of systemic toxicity.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Severe respiratory distress:} Difficulty breathing, stridor, wheezing, or central cyanosis (blue lips or tongue).
    \item \textbf{Altered mental state:} Confusion, extreme lethargy, or inability to wake up, particularly in young children or the elderly.
    \item \textbf{Signs of meningismus:} Severe headache accompanied by neck stiffness, photophobia, and a non-blanching petechial rash.
    \item \textbf{Anaphylaxis:} Severe allergic reaction to any cold medication, presenting with hives, facial swelling, or throat constriction.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis of a common cold is clinical, based on history and examination findings. Diagnostic testing is reserved for excluding complications or other diagnoses.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical evaluation:} Detailed symptom assessment, inspection of the nasal mucosa, oropharynx, and auscultation of the lung fields.
    \item \textbf{Respiratory viral PCR swab:} Swabbing the nasopharynx to identify specific viral pathogens (e.g. influenza, RSV, COVID-19) in high-risk patients.
    \item \textbf{Throat swab and culture:} Indicated if acute group A streptococcal pharyngitis is suspected to guide antibiotic therapy.
    \item \textbf{Sinus CT imaging or dental X-rays:} Performed only in chronic or atypical cases to evaluate for anatomical obstruction or dental abscesses mimicking sinusitis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Pharmacological management of a cold focuses on symptomatic relief matching the patient's specific presentation, combined with supportive self-care.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Analgesics and antipyretics:} Paracetamol or ibuprofen to relieve headache, sore throat, and low-grade fever.
    \item \textbf{Nasal or oral decongestants:} Sympathomimetic agents (e.g. oxymetazoline nasal spray or oral pseudoephedrine) to constrict mucosal blood vessels and relieve congestion; nasal sprays must not be used for more than 3--5 consecutive days to prevent rebound congestion (rhinitis medicamentosa).
    \item \textbf{First-generation antihistamines:} Sedating antihistamines (e.g. pheniramine or chlorpheniramine) used in combination products to dry nasal secretions through anticholinergic effects.
    \item \textbf{Antitussives and expectorants:} Cough suppressants (e.g. dextromethorphan) for dry, irritating coughs, and mucolytics (e.g. guaifenesin) to thin bronchial mucus and aid expectoration.
    \item \textbf{Paediatric precautions:} Under Australian guidelines, OTC cough and cold medicines must not be given to children under 6 years, and should only be given to children aged 6 to 11 on the advice of a doctor or pharmacist.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute otitis media:} Spread of infection to the middle ear via the Eustachian tube, a common complication in young children.
    \item \textbf{Acute bacterial sinusitis:} Secondary bacterial infection of the paranasal sinuses due to compromised mucociliary clearance.
    \item \textbf{Lower respiratory tract infections:} Progression of viral infection or secondary bacterial infection causing bronchitis or pneumonia.
    \item \textbf{Exacerbation of chronic diseases:} Viral colds triggering acute attacks in patients with asthma or COPD, or causing decompensation in heart failure.
    \item \textbf{Rhinitis medicamentosa:} Chronic rebound nasal congestion caused by overuse of topical decongestant sprays.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for patients with the common cold is excellent. Colds are benign, self-limiting viral illnesses, with the majority of symptoms resolving completely within 7 to 10 days. A dry cough or mild post-infectious nasal hypersensitivity may persist for up to 2 to 3 weeks in some individuals. Cold medicines do not shorten the duration of the viral infection or prevent secondary bacterial complications; their sole clinical utility is to provide temporary symptomatic relief and improve patient comfort. Recovery is characterised by a gradual return to normal energy levels and resolution of respiratory symptoms, with no long-term sequelae in healthy individuals.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Practice frequent hand washing:} Washing hands with soap and water or using alcohol-based hand sanitisers to prevent viral transmission.
    \item \textbf{Maintain adequate hydration:} Drinking plenty of water, warm broths, or herbal teas to soothe the throat and prevent dehydration.
    \item \textbf{Use saline nasal sprays:} Irrigating the nasal passages with saline sprays or rinses to clear mucus and relieve dryness.
    \item \textbf{Ensure adequate rest:} Restricting physical activity and getting sufficient sleep to support immune function.
    \item \textbf{Practice respiratory hygiene:} Covering the mouth and nose with a tissue or elbow when coughing or sneezing, and disposing of tissues promptly.
\end{itemize}
