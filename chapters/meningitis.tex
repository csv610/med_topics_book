\chapter{Meningitis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Meningitis is an acute inflammation of the meninges, the protective membranes covering the brain and spinal cord. It is most commonly caused by viral or bacterial infections, although fungal, parasitic, or non-infectious causes (such as autoimmune diseases or drugs) can also occur. Bacterial meningitis is a medical emergency with high mortality and morbidity rates if not treated immediately, whereas viral meningitis is usually less severe and often resolves without specific therapy.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Meningitis affects people of all ages but has higher incidence in infants, young children, adolescents, and older adults. In Australia, the widespread introduction of vaccines (targeting \textit{Haemophilus influenzae} type b, \textit{Streptococcus pneumoniae}, and \textit{Neisseria meningitidis} serogroups A, C, W, Y, and B) has significantly reduced the incidence of bacterial meningitis. Viral meningitis remains the most common form, with seasonal peaks in summer and autumn.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Meningitis arises when pathogens cross the blood-brain barrier and replicate in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), triggering an inflammatory response:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bacterial pathogens:} Common agents include \textit{Streptococcus pneumoniae} (pneumococcus), \textit{Neisseria meningitidis} (meningococcus), and \textit{Listeria monocytogenes} (particularly in newborns, pregnant women, and the elderly).
    \item \textbf{Viral pathogens:} Enteroviruses (such as Coxsackievirus or Echovirus) are the most common cause, followed by Herpes simplex virus (HSV) and Varicella zoster virus (VZV).
    \item \textbf{Blood-brain barrier crossing:} Pathogens colonise the nasopharynx, enter the bloodstream, and penetrate the choroid plexus or cerebral capillaries into the subarachnoid space.
    \item \textbf{Subarachnoid inflammation:} Replication of pathogens in the CSF induces release of inflammatory cytokines, causing cerebral oedema, increased intracranial pressure, and potential ischaemic brain damage.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Meningitis presents with a classic triad of symptoms, though the presentation can be atypical in young infants:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Classic triad:} Fever, headache, and neck stiffness (nuchal rigidity) are present in the majority of bacterial cases.
    \item \textbf{Other neurological signs:} Photophobia (sensitivity to light), phonophobia, confusion, altered mental status, and seizures.
    \item \textbf{Non-blanching rash:} A petechial or purpuric rash that does not fade under pressure (a glass test), which is highly characteristic of meningococcal septicaemia.
    \item \textbf{Infant presentation:} Poor feeding, irritability, high-pitched crying, bulging fontanelle, hypotonia (floppiness), and vomiting.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other neurological and systemic conditions must be distinguished from meningitis:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Encephalitis:} Inflammation of the brain parenchyma itself, presenting with prominent altered consciousness, focal neurological deficits, and seizures.
    \item \textbf{Subarachnoid haemorrhage:} Sudden, severe "thunderclap" headache with neck stiffness, but without fever or infective symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Brain abscess:} Localised infection in the brain tissue, often presenting with focal signs and progressive headache.
    \item \textbf{Sepsis without meningitis:} Severe systemic infection causing delirium and fever without direct meningeal involvement.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Meningitis is a life-threatening medical emergency. If meningitis is suspected, the patient must be taken to the nearest emergency department immediately.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A high fever accompanied by a stiff neck, severe headache, or photophobia
    \item A dark red or purple rash that does not fade when a glass is pressed firmly against it
    \item Sudden confusion, delirium, difficulty waking up, or seizures
    \item Bulging fontanelle, floppiness, or continuous high-pitched crying in an infant
    \item Severe, persistent vomiting accompanied by rapid breathing or cold hands and feet
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Immediate diagnosis is critical to guide lifesaving antimicrobial therapy:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture (LP):} The primary test to collect cerebrospinal fluid for analysis of white blood cells, protein, glucose, Gram stain, and PCR.
    \item \textbf{CT scan of the brain:} Performed before lumbar puncture in patients with focal neurological signs, papilloedema, or altered consciousness to rule out raised intracranial pressure or herniation risk.
    \item \textbf{Blood cultures:} Collected immediately to identify bacteria in the bloodstream and guide antibiotic selection.
    \item \textbf{Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) assays:} Fast, highly sensitive DNA tests on blood and CSF to identify specific viral or bacterial pathogens.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Bacterial meningitis requires immediate hospitalisation and aggressive medical therapy, while viral meningitis is managed supportively:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Empiric antibiotics:} Intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics (typically ceftriaxone or cefotaxime, plus benzylpenicillin or ampicillin in older/younger patients to cover \textit{Listeria}) started immediately, often before the lumbar puncture if delayed.
    \item \textbf{Dexamethasone:} Administered prior to or with the first dose of antibiotics in suspected pneumococcal meningitis to reduce the risk of hearing loss and neurological complications.
    \item \textbf{Antiviral therapy:} Intravenous aciclovir if herpes simplex virus or varicella zoster virus meningitis/encephalitis is suspected.
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} Intravenous fluids to maintain hydration, anti-pyretics for fever control, and anticonvulsants for seizures.
    \item \textbf{Prophylaxis for close contacts:} Antibiotics (such as ciprofloxacin or rifampicin) given to close contacts of patients with meningococcal disease.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Permanent hearing loss or deafness (the most common long-term complication)
    \item Cognitive impairment, learning difficulties, or developmental delays in children
    \item Epilepsy or recurrent seizures
    \item Focal neurological deficits, such as hemiparesis or cranial nerve palsies
    \item Hydrocephalus (excess fluid in the brain) requiring a surgical shunt
    \item Limb amputation or severe scarring due to tissue necrosis in meningococcal septicaemia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis depends on the causative agent, the patient's age, and how quickly treatment begins. Bacterial meningitis has a high mortality rate (approximately 10--15\%) and up to 20--30\% of survivors suffer permanent neurological sequelae. In contrast, most patients with viral meningitis recover fully within 1 to 2 weeks with supportive care, although fatigue, headache, and mild cognitive issues may persist for several weeks or months.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain up-to-date immunisation under the Australian National Immunisation Program, including vaccines against meningococcal, pneumococcal, and Hib diseases
    \item Practice good hand hygiene: wash hands frequently, especially before eating and after coughing or sneezing
    \item Avoid sharing drinks, eating utensils, toothbrushes, or lip balms with others
    \item Practice safe food handling and avoid unpasteurised dairy and deli meats during pregnancy to prevent \textit{Listeria} infection
    \item Seek prompt medical attention for close contacts if notified of a confirmed case of meningococcal meningitis
\end{itemize}
