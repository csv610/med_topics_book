\chapter{Dupuytren's Contracture}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dupuytren's contracture is a condition in which the layer of tissue beneath the skin of the palm, known as the palmar fascia, thickens and forms cords of tissue. Over time, these cords tighten and pull one or more fingers into a bent position that cannot be straightened. The ring and little fingers are most commonly affected. The condition is not dangerous but can become disabling as it interferes with hand function. Treatment, including surgery and newer non-surgical techniques, can improve the position and use of the fingers.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Dupuytren's contracture is common in people of northern European descent and is more common in men than in women. It usually begins after middle age and becomes more frequent with advancing years. Many people with the condition also have thickening of tissue elsewhere, such as the soles of the feet or the penis, and there is a strong family tendency. It affects a significant proportion of people over 60, although only a minority develop contractures severe enough to require treatment.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The cause of Dupuytren's contracture is not fully understood, but it involves the abnormal growth of fibrous tissue in the palmar fascia.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} a strong inherited tendency; family history is a major risk factor
    \item \textbf{Age:} the condition becomes more common with increasing age
    \item \textbf{Male sex:} men are affected more often and more severely than women
    \item \textbf{Ancestry:} most common in people of northern European descent
    \item \textbf{Associated conditions:} diabetes, epilepsy, and alcohol use are associated with a higher risk
    \item \textbf{Smoking:} may increase the risk and severity
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} fibroblasts in the palmar fascia proliferate and contract, forming nodules and cords that pull the fingers into flexion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Dupuytren's contracture develops slowly, often over many years.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Firm nodules or lumps in the palm, which may be tender early on
    \item Cords, or thick bands, running from the palm towards the fingers
    \item Gradual inability to straighten the affected finger or fingers
    \item The ring finger is most commonly affected, followed by the little finger
    \item Pits or dimples in the palm where the fascia attaches to the skin
    \item Difficulty with everyday tasks such as putting a hand in a pocket, shaking hands, or placing the hand flat on a surface
    \item Rarely, pain, and rarely involvement of both hands and multiple fingers
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions can cause lumps or finger contractures.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Trigger finger, in which a finger clicks or locks due to a tendon problem
    \item Ganglion cysts and other benign hand lumps
    \item Camptodactyly, a congenital finger deformity
    \item Arthritis of the finger joints, causing stiffness and deformity
    \item Flexor tendon injury
    \item Ledderhose disease and Peyronie's disease, which are related fibrous conditions affecting the feet and penis
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who notices nodules or cords in the palm, or difficulty straightening a finger, should have the hand assessed by a GP. Referral to a hand specialist is appropriate when the contracture interferes with daily activities, when the finger is becoming significantly bent, or when the condition is progressing.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe hand pain, which is unusual in Dupuytren's disease
    \item Signs of infection, including redness, heat, and swelling with fever
    \item Sudden inability to move the fingers, which may indicate another problem
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is clinical and requires no imaging in typical cases.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} family history, risk factors, and the pattern of symptoms
    \item \textbf{Examination:} palpation of nodules and cords, and measurement of the angle of contracture at each finger joint using the table-top test
    \item \textbf{Table-top test:} the inability to place the hand flat on a table is a simple indicator of functional contracture
    \item \textbf{Photographic documentation:} used to monitor progression over time
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} rarely needed; ultrasound may help in atypical cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is offered when the contracture affects function, and choices depend on the severity.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observation:} mild contractures that do not interfere with function can simply be monitored
    \item \textbf{Needle fasciotomy:} a fine needle is used to break the cord under local anaesthetic, straightening the finger without surgery
    \item \textbf{Collagenase injection:} an enzyme is injected into the cord to dissolve it, and the finger is then manipulated to break the cord
    \item \textbf{Surgery (fasciectomy):} the thickened tissue is removed through an incision in the palm and finger; this is the most definitive treatment
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} low-dose radiation can slow progression in early disease but is not widely used in Australia
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy and splinting:} used after treatment to maintain the gained range of movement
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive contracture that interferes with hand function and daily living
    \item Recurrence after treatment, which is common over time, particularly with the less invasive techniques
    \item Complications of surgery, including nerve injury, infection, bleeding, and stiffness
    \item Complex regional pain syndrome, a rare but serious complication of hand surgery
    \item Psychological effects of hand deformity and reduced function
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Dupuytren's contracture is progressive but usually slow, and many people never need treatment. When treatment is needed, both non-surgical and surgical techniques are effective at straightening the finger, with the choice depending on the severity and the individual's circumstances. Recurrence is common over the long term, particularly in people with early onset, bilateral disease, or a strong family history, and further treatment may be required.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item There is no known way to prevent Dupuytren's contracture, but early recognition allows timely treatment
    \item Monitor the palm regularly for new nodules or cords
    \item Maintain hand flexibility with gentle stretching
    \item Stop smoking and limit alcohol, which are associated with more severe disease
    \item Seek a hand specialist review when the contracture begins to affect daily activities
    \item Consider treatment before the contracture becomes severe, as results are better with early intervention
\end{itemize}
