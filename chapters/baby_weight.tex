\chapter{Baby Weight}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A baby's weight is one of the most valuable measures of health and wellbeing in early life. In the first months and years, weight gain is used to check that an infant is feeding well, growing normally, and free of hidden illness. Weight is also the basis for dosing many medicines and calculating fluid requirements in children, which is why accurate measurements matter.

Most newborns lose a small amount of weight in the first few days after birth, usually no more than about 10\% of their birth weight, and most regain their birth weight by around two weeks of age. After that, babies typically grow quickly in the first year -- tripling birth weight by about 12 months -- and then slow down. Weight is always interpreted together with length (or height) and head circumference on standard growth charts, rather than in isolation. Both being too light (poor weight gain or failure to thrive) and being too heavy (overweight and obesity in childhood) are reasons for attention, because both can have long-term health effects.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Birth weight is influenced by the length of the pregnancy, genetics, maternal health, and nutrition during pregnancy. Low birth weight (under 2,500~grams) affects around 1 in 14 babies born in Australia, and it is more common after premature birth, in multiple pregnancies, and where the mother has conditions such as pre-eclampsia or high blood pressure. Large babies (over 4,000~grams) are more common when the mother has diabetes in pregnancy.

At the other end of the spectrum, childhood overweight and obesity have become increasingly common in Australia, with roughly 1 in 4 children now above a healthy weight range. Both poor weight gain in infancy and excess weight in childhood are frequent reasons for health visits and are closely followed through maternal and child health checks. Internationally, the World Health Organization (WHO) growth standards are used in most countries so that growth can be compared across populations.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antenatal factors:} length of gestation (prematurity), placental function, maternal nutrition, smoking or alcohol use in pregnancy, maternal diabetes (large baby), and multiple pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Feeding factors:} breastfeeding difficulties, poor latch or milk supply, difficulties with bottle feeding, and inadequate feeding frequency
    \item \textbf{Medical causes of poor gain:} reflux and vomiting, food allergy, coeliac disease, recurrent infections, congenital heart disease, hypothyroidism, kidney or gut conditions, and genetic syndromes
    \item \textbf{Excess weight gain:} high-energy formula, early introduction of solids, sugary drinks, low physical activity, and family eating habits
    \item \textbf{Social factors:} feeding neglect, maternal mental illness, poor access to support, and poverty
\end{itemize}

At a physiological level, weight reflects the balance between energy intake and energy expenditure. In a growing infant, most energy is used to build new tissue, so any condition that reduces intake, impairs absorption, or increases energy requirements will be reflected in slowing weight gain. Conversely, a persistent excess of energy intake relative to expenditure is stored as fat, and the number of fat cells increases during infancy and childhood, which may make later weight control harder.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal pattern:} steady weight gain plotted along a percentile line on a growth chart
    \item \textbf{Poor weight gain:} weight crossing downward through centile lines, weight for age below the 2nd--3rd centile, or delayed weight gain compared with the expected rate
    \item \textbf{Feeding difficulty:} poor latch, sleepy baby, frequent short feeds, fussiness, and vomiting after feeds
    \item \textbf{Dehydration:} fewer wet nappies, sunken fontanelle, dry mouth, and lethargy
    \item \textbf{Hidden illness:} persistent fever, pallor, diarrhoea, breathing difficulty, and failure to feed
    \item \textbf{Excess weight gain:} weight crossing upward through centile lines, or weight disproportionately high for length or height
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Inadequate intake versus malabsorption (for example, coeliac disease, food allergy, or cystic fibrosis)
    \item Increased energy use: chronic infection, congenital heart disease, or hyperthyroidism
    \item Genetic or chromosomal conditions with slow growth
    \item Normal variation and ``constitutional'' smallness versus true failure to thrive
    \item Overfeeding and poor diet versus endocrine or genetic causes of excess weight
    \item Neglect or non-accidental starvation in rare circumstances
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Weighing is part of routine checks, but see a health professional promptly if you are worried about your baby's feeding, weight, or growth.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A baby who is floppy, very hard to wake, or unresponsive
    \item Difficulty breathing or turning blue
    \item Convulsions (fits)
    \item A baby who refuses all fluids or has very few wet nappies
\end{itemize}

See a GP or maternal and child health nurse for:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Birth weight not regained by 2--3 weeks of age
    \item Weight loss of more than about 10\% of birth weight
    \item Poor feeding, persistent vomiting, or projectile vomiting
    \item Very few wet nappies, dark urine, or a dry mouth
    \item Falling across growth chart lines or delayed developmental milestones
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} feeding pattern, wet and dirty nappy count, maternal health and pregnancy history, and family growth patterns
    \item \textbf{Serial weighing:} accurate, ideally naked, using the same scales, plotted on WHO growth charts
    \item \textbf{Examination:} overall appearance, hydration status, abdomen, heart, and muscle tone; measurement of length and head circumference
    \item \textbf{Feeding observation:} a watched feed with a lactation consultant or maternal and child health nurse
    \item \textbf{Investigations for poor gain:} urine tests, blood tests (full blood count, electrolytes, thyroid function, coeliac screen), a sweat test, or imaging guided by clinical suspicion
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a paediatrician or dietitian when growth problems persist or weight is extreme
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Breastfeeding support:} position and latch review, feeding on demand, and expressing to protect milk supply
    \item \textbf{Supplementation:} expressed milk, formula top-ups, or specialist infant formula when intake is inadequate
    \item \textbf{Frequent monitoring:} repeat weighing and review until growth is back on track
    \item \textbf{Treating underlying causes:} reflux treatment, allergy-avoidance formulas or dietary change, and treatment of infections or other illness
    \item \textbf{Excess weight:} feeding advice, age-appropriate solids, limits on sugary drinks, physical activity, and family-based healthy habits
    \item \textbf{Multidisciplinary care:} paediatricians, lactation consultants, dietitians, and social services where needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dehydration and electrolyte disturbance in poorly feeding infants
    \item Hypoglycaemia (low blood sugar), especially in large or small babies in the newborn period
    \item Prolonged jaundice linked with poor intake
    \item Long-term growth stunting and developmental delay with severe, untreated poor weight gain
    \item Childhood obesity increasing the risk of later type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, and joint problems
    \item Anaemia and micronutrient deficiencies related to a poor diet
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most babies who lose a little weight after birth regain it within two weeks and go on to grow normally. Mild feeding problems usually improve quickly with support, and most children with poor weight gain catch up once the cause is addressed. Many small-for-gestational-age and premature babies ``catch up'' in growth over the first two years. Excess weight, once established, can be hard to shift, which is why early attention to feeding and family eating habits matters. Long-term outcomes depend mainly on the underlying cause and how early it is identified.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend scheduled maternal and child health checks so growth is plotted and monitored
    \item Feed on demand and look for feeding cues; get early help with latch or supply concerns
    \item Feed during the night and day as needed in the newborn period to support weight gain
    \item Introduce solids at around six months, starting with iron-rich foods
    \item Avoid sugary drinks and offer water as the main drink from around six months
    \item Breastfeed if possible, as it is associated with a lower risk of later obesity
    \item In pregnancy: good nutrition, no smoking, and regular antenatal care support a healthy birth weight
\end{itemize}
