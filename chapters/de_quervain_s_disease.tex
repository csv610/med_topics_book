\chapter{De Quervain's Disease}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
De Quervain's disease, also called De Quervain's tenosynovitis, is a painful condition affecting the tendons on the thumb side of the wrist. The two tendons that move the thumb pass through a tight tunnel of tissue at the wrist, and when this tunnel narrows or the tendons are repeatedly irritated, the space becomes inflamed and movement is painful. It most commonly affects people who perform repetitive hand and wrist activities, including new parents lifting babies. Most cases respond well to conservative treatment, although injection or surgery may be needed in persistent cases.

\section*{Epidemiology}
De Quervain's disease is one of the most common forms of tenosynovitis seen in clinical practice. It affects women three to ten times more often than men and is particularly common in the 30--50 year age range. It is strongly associated with repetitive or forceful use of the hand and wrist, including occupations such as manual labour, hairdressing, and assembly work, as well as hobbies such as gardening and racket sports. The condition is frequently seen in new mothers, and the associated hormonal changes of pregnancy and the postpartum period are thought to contribute.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anatomical narrowing:} the abductor pollicis longus and extensor pollicis brevis tendons run through a snug fibrous sheath at the radial styloid
    \item \textbf{Overuse:} repetitive thumb and wrist motion causes friction and microtrauma to the tendons and sheath
    \item \textbf{Inflammation and thickening:} the sheath becomes swollen, further narrowing the tunnel and trapping the tendons, producing pain on movement
    \item \textbf{Hormonal factors:} pregnancy and breastfeeding are associated with fluid retention and increased risk
    \item \textbf{Direct injury:} a blow or fall on the wrist can precipitate the condition
    \item \textbf{Systemic conditions:} inflammatory arthritis such as rheumatoid disease can predispose to tenosynovitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain on the thumb side of the wrist, which may radiate up the forearm
    \item Swelling and tenderness over the "anatomical snuffbox" area
    \item Pain worsened by gripping, pinching, turning the wrist, or lifting, especially lifting a baby with thumbs spread
    \item A sensation of catching or creaking when moving the thumb
    \item Difficulty performing everyday tasks such as turning keys, opening jars, or holding a cup
    \item Positive Finkelstein test: pain reproduced by bending the thumb into the palm and deviating the wrist toward the little finger
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Osteoarthritis or thumb carpometacarpal joint arthritis
    \item Wrist ganglion cyst over the radial side
    \item Intersection syndrome, affecting tendons crossing higher up the forearm
    \item Radial nerve compression (Wartenberg syndrome)
    \item Scaphoid fracture or other wrist injury, especially after a fall
    \item Inflammatory arthritis such as rheumatoid arthritis or gout
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with persistent thumb-wrist pain lasting more than a week or two, or pain that interferes with work or caring for a baby, should see a doctor or a hand therapist. Early treatment improves outcomes and reduces the risk of the condition becoming chronic.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wrist pain after a significant fall or injury with deformity, severe swelling, or inability to move the hand
    \item Sudden severe pain with numbness or coldness of the hand, which may indicate a vascular problem
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} the history of radial wrist pain with repetitive activity and tenderness over the first extensor compartment strongly suggest the diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Finkelstein test:} reproduction of pain is the key diagnostic manoeuvre
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} plain X-rays are usually normal but help exclude arthritis or fracture; ultrasound shows tendon sheath thickening and fluid and can guide injections
    \item \textbf{Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):} occasionally used in complex or atypical cases
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} inflammatory markers or autoimmune serology if inflammatory arthritis is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Activity modification:} rest the thumb and wrist, and change the way repetitive tasks are performed
    \item \textbf{Splinting:} a thumb spica splint immobilises the wrist and thumb and is the first-line conservative treatment
    \item \textbf{Anti-inflammatory medication:} oral non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and topical gels reduce pain and inflammation
    \item \textbf{Cold therapy:} ice packs applied to the wrist for 10--15 minutes several times daily
    \item \textbf{Corticosteroid injection:} a targeted injection into the tendon sheath is highly effective and often curative; if injection is performed during breastfeeding, guidance about timing can be discussed with the doctor
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} stretching and strengthening exercises, ultrasound, and ergonomic advice
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} release of the first extensor compartment is considered when conservative treatment and injections fail; it is a small outpatient procedure with high success rates
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic pain and disability if the condition is untreated or aggravated
    \item Tendon sheath scarring or rupture with repeated unguided injections
    \item Prolonged restriction of childcare, work, or leisure activities
    \item Surgical complications such as scar tenderness, nerve irritation, or recurrence, which are uncommon
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for De Quervain's disease is excellent. Most people improve within a few weeks of splinting and activity modification, and corticosteroid injection resolves symptoms in the majority of cases. Surgery is successful in over 90\% of people who need it. With appropriate treatment and avoidance of the aggravating activity, most people regain full, pain-free use of the thumb and wrist.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use ergonomic tools and techniques to reduce repetitive thumb strain
    \item Take regular breaks from repetitive hand tasks
    \item Use the palm and whole hand rather than pinching with the thumb
    \item Apply ice and gentle stretching at the first sign of wrist pain
    \item Wear a supportive splint during heavy or repetitive activities
    \item Seek early treatment to prevent the condition from becoming chronic
\end{itemize}
