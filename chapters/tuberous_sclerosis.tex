\chapter{Tuberous sclerosis}
\index{Tuberous sclerosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Tuberous Sclerosis Complex (TSC) is a multisystem, autosomal dominant genetic disorder characterized by the development of benign, non-cancerous tumors known as hamartomas across multiple vital organ systems, including the brain, kidneys, heart, lungs, skin, eyes, and liver. Historically referred to as Bourneville's disease, named after the French neurologist D\'esir\'e-Magloire Bourneville who first described the cortical lesions in 1880, TSC is now recognized as a complex syndrome with highly variable clinical expressivity. The severity of the disease ranges from extremely mild forms---wherein individuals may live normal lives unaware of their diagnosis---to severe, life-threatening manifestations involving profound cognitive impairment, intractable epilepsy, and progressive organ failure.

The significance of TSC extends beyond its clinical burden. Its study has provided fundamental insights into human developmental biology, oncology, and cellular metabolism. The discovery of the causative genes, \textit{TSC1} and \textit{TSC2}, and their role in regulating the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) intracellular signaling pathway has transformed the understanding of tumor biology. This pathway acts as a central hub for sensing nutrient availability, cellular energy status, and growth factors, coordinating cell growth and division. In TSC, the loss of function in either gene leads to hyperactivation of mTOR, driving uncontrolled cellular growth. Consequently, TSC has become a prototype for ``mTORopathies'' and has pioneered the clinical application of mTOR inhibitors, representing a landmark success in translating molecular genetics into targeted, personalized medicine.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiological profile of Tuberous Sclerosis Complex illustrates its status as a relatively rare yet significant global health concern. The prevalence of TSC is estimated to be approximately 1 in 6,000 to 1 in 10,000 live births, translating to an estimated 1 million affected individuals worldwide. It shows no bias toward any particular racial, ethnic, or geographic population, and it affects males and females in equal proportions.

The diagnosis of TSC can occur at any stage of life, from prenatal detection via fetal echocardiography to late adulthood. Historically, epidemiological studies underestimated the prevalence of TSC because milder cases, characterized by minimal skin lesions and absence of seizures or cognitive deficits, remained undiagnosed. With the advent of modern neuroimaging (such as magnetic resonance imaging) and genetic testing, the diagnostic sensitivity has improved markedly, revealing a higher prevalence than previously reported.

Genetically, TSC is characterized by high rates of spontaneous mutation. Approximately two-thirds (60\% to 70\%) of all diagnosed cases arise from \textit{de novo} somatic or germline mutations, where neither parent exhibits signs of the disease. The remaining one-third of cases are inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern from an affected parent. Familial cases often demonstrate a higher proportion of \textit{TSC1} mutations and generally present with a milder phenotype compared to sporadic cases, which are more frequently associated with mutations in the \textit{TSC2} gene.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The molecular pathogenesis of Tuberous Sclerosis Complex is rooted in the dysfunction of a critical cellular regulatory pathway. The disease is caused by inactivating mutations in either the \textit{TSC1} gene (located on chromosome 9q34, encoding the protein hamartin) or the \textit{TSC2} gene (located on chromosome 16p13.3, encoding the protein tuberin). Under physiological conditions, hamartin and tuberin, along with a third protein called TBC1D7, assemble to form a stable heterotrimeric functional complex.

This hamartin-tuberin complex functions as a crucial negative regulator of the mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signaling cascade. Specifically, the complex acts as a GTPase-activating protein (GAP) for the small GTPase Rheb (Ras homolog enriched in brain). The active, GTP-bound form of Rheb directly binds and activates mTORC1. The hamartin-tuberin complex facilitates the hydrolysis of Rheb-GTP to its inactive GDP-bound state, thereby dampening mTORC1 activity. When a mutation disrupts either \textit{TSC1} or \textit{TSC2}, the complex cannot assemble or function, leaving Rheb constitutively active in its GTP-bound state. This leads to persistent, unchecked activation of mTORC1.

mTORC1 is a serine/threonine kinase that controls protein translation, ribosomal biogenesis, cell size, lipid synthesis, and autophagy by phosphorylating downstream targets such as p70S6 kinase (p70S6K) and eukaryotic initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1 (4E-BP1). In TSC, persistent mTORC1 signaling drives cell growth, proliferation, and abnormal cellular differentiation, culminating in the classic hamartomatous lesions.

The molecular mechanisms are detailed below:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TSC1 Gene Mutation}: Located on chromosome 9q34, the \textit{TSC1} gene contains 23 exons and encodes hamartin, a 130 kDa protein. Hamartin interacts with the ezrin-radixin-moesin (ERM) family of actin-binding proteins and is essential for stabilizing the tuberin protein. Mutations in \textit{TSC1} are typically nonsense, frameshift, or splice-site mutations, and they generally correlate with a milder clinical phenotype.
    \item \textbf{TSC2 Gene Mutation}: Located on chromosome 16p13.3, the \textit{TSC2} gene is larger, containing 41 exons, and encodes tuberin, a 180 kDa protein containing a GAP-domain near its carboxyl terminus. Mutations in \textit{TSC2} include large deletions, rearrangements, and missense mutations. \textit{TSC2} mutations are more common in sporadic cases and are associated with a higher risk of cognitive impairment, severe epilepsy, and renal angiomyolipomas.
    \item \textbf{mTORC1 Hyperactivation}: Constitutive activation of mTORC1 increases the translation of specific mRNAs that encode proteins required for cell cycle progression and metabolic reprogramming. It also suppresses autophagy, preventing the clearance of damaged organelles, and promotes angiogenesis by upregulating hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1-alpha) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF).
    \item \textbf{Knudson's Two-Hit Hypothesis}: The cellular development of TSC lesions follows the ``two-hit'' model of tumor suppressor genes. Individuals with TSC inherit or develop a germline mutation in one allele of \textit{TSC1} or \textit{TSC2} (the first hit). The formation of focal lesions (such as renal angiomyolipomas, subependymal giant cell astrocytomas, and cortical tubers) often requires a somatic mutation or loss of heterozygosity (LOH) in the remaining wild-type allele (the second hit), leading to complete loss of hamartin-tuberin function in those cells.
    \item \textbf{Abnormal Neuronal Migration and Differentiation}: During embryonic development, the hyperactivation of mTORC1 disrupts radial glial cell division and neuronal migration. Neurons and glia fail to migrate properly to the cerebral cortex, resulting in disorganized cortical lamination and the appearance of giant, dysplastic cells (``balloon cells'') that constitute the histopathological hallmark of cortical tubers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of Tuberous Sclerosis Complex is remarkably heterogeneous, affecting multiple organ systems with manifestations that evolve over the patient's lifetime. The primary clinical features are organized below by organ system:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurological Manifestations}: Neurological symptoms are the leading cause of morbidity and developmental challenges in TSC. They include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Epilepsy}: Affecting over 85\% of individuals, seizures typically begin in the first year of life, frequently presenting as infantile spasms (West syndrome), which carry a high risk of subsequent cognitive impairment if untreated. Seizure types evolve to focal and generalized epilepsy, often refractory to standard anticonvulsants.
        \item \textit{Tuberous Sclerosis-Associated Neuropsychiatric Disorders (TAND)}: This term encompasses the multi-level neuropsychiatric manifestations, including intellectual disability (in approximately 50\% of patients), autism spectrum disorder (in up to 40\% of patients), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), aggressive behaviors, anxiety, depressive symptoms, and sleep disturbances.
        \item \textit{Cortical Tubers}: These are developmental abnormalities of the cerebral cortex characterized by localized areas of disorganized cortical structure, astrogliosis, and giant balloon cells. The number and volume of tubers correlate with the severity of epilepsy and cognitive deficits.
        \item \textit{Subependymal Nodules (SENs)}: Small, benign collections of glial and giant cells located along the walls of the lateral ventricles. They are typically asymptomatic but can calcify over time.
        \item \textit{Subependymal Giant Cell Astrocytomas (SEGAs)}: These are slow-growing, benign brain tumors that develop from SENs near the foramen of Monro in 5\% to 20\% of TSC patients, usually during childhood or adolescence. If they grow large enough, they can block the flow of cerebrospinal fluid, causing life-threatening obstructive hydrocephalus.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dermatological Manifestations}: Skin lesions are present in over 90\% of patients and are highly useful for clinical diagnosis:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Hypomelanotic Macules (``Ash-Leaf Spots'')}: Flat, pale patches of skin, often shaped like the leaf of an ash tree. They are usually present at birth or appear in early infancy, and they are best visualized using a Wood's (ultraviolet) lamp.
        \item \textit{Facial Angiofibromas}: Benign vascular and fibrous papules that appear on the cheeks, nose, and chin in a symmetrical butterfly pattern. They typically develop between the ages of 2 and 5 and can become disfiguring, bleed easily, or cause psychological distress.
        \item \textit{Shagreen Patches}: Leathery, raised plaques of collagenous tissue, typically located on the lower back or sacral region, resembling pigskin.
        \item \textit{Ungual and Periungual Fibromas (Koenen's Tumors)}: Fleshy projections that grow out of or under the fingernails or toenails, usually appearing in adolescence or adulthood. They can be painful and disrupt nail growth.
        \item \textit{Confetti Lesions}: Numerous small, hypopigmented macules scattered across the limbs.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Renal Manifestations}: Renal lesions are the second most common clinical feature and a major cause of adult mortality:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Renal Angiomyolipomas (AMLs)}: Benign tumors composed of abnormal blood vessels, smooth muscle cells, and adipose tissue, occurring in up to 80\% of patients. They are typically bilateral and multiple. As they grow, they can cause pain, impair renal function, and develop microaneurysms that are prone to spontaneous, life-threatening retroperitoneal hemorrhage.
        \item \textit{Renal Cysts}: Present in approximately 30\% of patients, leading to chronic kidney disease. A subset of patients has a contiguous gene deletion affecting both \textit{TSC2} and the adjacent \textit{PKD1} gene, resulting in severe, early-onset polycystic kidney disease.
        \item \textit{Renal Cell Carcinoma}: While rare, patients with TSC have an increased risk of developing renal malignancies at a younger age than the general population.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pulmonary Manifestations}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM)}: A progressive, cystic lung disease characterized by the proliferation of atypical smooth muscle-like cells (LAM cells) along the airways, blood vessels, and lymphatics. It occurs almost exclusively in post-pubertal females (affecting 30\% to 40\% of adult women with TSC). Symptoms include progressive exertional dyspnea, recurrent pneumothorax (collapsed lung), and chylothorax.
        \item \textit{Multifocal Micronodular Pneumocyte Hyperplasia (MMPH)}: A benign proliferation of type II pneumocytes, presenting as multiple tiny nodules on chest imaging, usually asymptomatic.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cardiac Manifestations}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Cardiac Rhabdomyomas}: Benign striated muscle tumors of the heart wall, present in up to 70\% to 80\% of infants with TSC, often detected prenatally during routine obstetric ultrasound. While they can cause outflow tract obstruction, arrhythmias, or congestive heart failure in newborns, they typically undergo spontaneous regression during the first few years of life.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ophthalmic Manifestations}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Retinal Astrocytic Hamartomas}: Benign lesions on the retina, which can be flat and translucent or raised, nodular, and calcified (``mulberry-like''). They rarely affect vision and are valuable diagnostic clues.
        \item \textit{Achromic Patches}: Depigmented areas on the retina or iris.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The diagnosis of Tuberous Sclerosis Complex can be challenging due to its overlapping symptoms with other genetic and dermatological disorders. The primary conditions to consider in the differential diagnosis include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurofibromatosis Type 1 (NF1)}: This autosomal dominant disorder is characterized by multiple skin lesions and neurological issues. Unlike TSC, NF1 features hyperpigmented macules (\textit{caf\'e-au-lait} spots) rather than hypopigmented macules, Lisch nodules in the iris, axillary/inguinal freckling, and neurofibromas rather than angiofibromas or shagreen patches.
    \item \textbf{Sturge-Weber Syndrome (SWS)}: A congenital, non-familial neurocutaneous disorder characterized by a facial port-wine stain (capillary malformation) in the ophthalmic distribution of the trigeminal nerve, ipsilateral leptomeningeal angiomatosis, glaucoma, and seizures. SWS lacks the multi-organ hamartomas, hypomelanotic macules, and genetic mutations characteristic of TSC.
    \item \textbf{Ito's Hypomelanosis (Incontinentia Pigmenti Achromians)}: A neurocutaneous syndrome presenting with streaks, whorls, or patches of hypopigmentation following the lines of Blaschko. While it can be associated with seizures, developmental delay, and skeletal abnormalities, it is differentiated from TSC by the specific pattern of skin lesions and the absence of facial angiofibromas, shagreen patches, or internal hamartomas.
    \item \textbf{Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease (ADPKD)}: Caused by mutations in \textit{PKD1} or \textit{PKD2}, ADPKD causes progressive renal failure due to multiple renal cysts. While it shares renal features with TSC (especially the TSC2/PKD1 contiguous gene deletion syndrome), patients with isolated ADPKD lack the extra-renal features of TSC, such as epilepsy, cortical tubers, and facial angiofibromas.
    \item \textbf{Birt-Hogg-Dub\'e Syndrome (BHD)}: A rare autosomal dominant disorder characterized by fibrofolliculomas (which can look similar to facial angiofibromas), pulmonary cysts, recurrent pneumothorax, and renal tumors. BHD is distinguished from TSC by genetic testing (mutations in the \textit{FLCN} gene) and the absence of neurological features like seizures or cognitive impairment.
    \item \textbf{Sporadic Lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM)}: Occurs exclusively in females, causing cystic lung disease identical to TSC-associated LAM. However, sporadic LAM is not associated with the other clinical manifestations of TSC, and genetic analysis reveals somatic \textit{TSC1} or \textit{TSC2} mutations restricted to the lung tissue rather than a germline mutation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Because Tuberous Sclerosis Complex affects multiple organ systems, patients and caregivers must be aware of warning signs that require urgent or emergency medical evaluation.

Immediate emergency medical assistance should be sought---such as calling \textbf{local emergency services} in many countries, \textbf{911} in the United States, or \textbf{999} in the United Kingdom---for any of the following life-threatening symptoms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A seizure lasting longer than 5 minutes, or multiple seizures occurring in rapid succession without recovery of consciousness between them (status epilepticus).
    \item Sudden, severe, one-sided flank or abdominal pain, accompanied by dizziness, fainting, paleness, or blood in the urine, which can indicate the rupture and hemorrhage of a renal angiomyolipoma (Wunderlich syndrome).
    \item Sudden onset of severe shortness of breath, sharp chest pain that worsens with breathing, or coughing up blood, which may indicate a pneumothorax (collapsed lung) or severe pulmonary hemorrhage in patients with lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM).
    \item Signs of acutely elevated intracranial pressure, such as a sudden and severe headache, projectile vomiting, double vision, loss of coordination, or progressive lethargy and confusion, suggesting that a subependymal giant cell astrocytoma (SEGA) has blocked cerebrospinal fluid flow, causing acute hydrocephalus.
    \item Sudden chest pain, palpitations, or difficulty breathing in infants, which could indicate cardiac outflow obstruction or arrhythmia caused by cardiac rhabdomyomas.
\end{itemize}

Prompt, non-emergency medical consultation should be scheduled if a patient develops new skin lesions, experiences an increase in seizure frequency or severity, shows a decline in cognitive, behavioral, or school performance, notices persistent blood in the urine, or experiences chronic, unexplained cough or mild shortness of breath.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of Tuberous Sclerosis Complex relies on clinical criteria established by the International TSC Consensus Group (updated in 2012) and genetic testing. A definite diagnosis is established by the identification of a pathogenic mutation in the \textit{TSC1} or \textit{TSC2} gene, or by meeting specific clinical criteria based on major and minor features.

Investigations and diagnostic criteria are detailed below:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic Testing}: Next-generation sequencing and deletion/duplication analysis of the \textit{TSC1} and \textit{TSC2} genes from a blood or saliva sample. Finding a pathogenic variant is sufficient for a definite diagnosis. However, 10\% to 15\% of patients with clinically definite TSC have no mutation identified by standard genetic testing (termed ``no mutation identified'' or NMI), meaning a normal genetic test does not rule out the diagnosis.
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic Criteria - Major Features}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Hypomelanotic macules (three or more, at least 5 mm diameter).
        \item Angiofibromas (three or more) or fibrous cephalic plaque.
        \item Ungual fibromas (two or more).
        \item Shagreen patch.
        \item Multiple retinal hamartomas.
        \item Cortical dysplasias (includes cortical tubers and cerebral white matter radial migration lines).
        \item Subependymal nodules (SENs).
        \item Subependymal giant cell astrocytoma (SEGA).
        \item Cardiac rhabdomyoma.
        \item Lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM) (note: if both LAM and renal angiomyolipomas are present without other features, a definite diagnosis cannot be made based on these two criteria alone).
        \item Renal angiomyolipoma (two or more).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic Criteria - Minor Features}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item ``Confetti'' skin lesions.
        \item Dental enamel pits (more than three).
        \item Intraoral fibromas (two or more).
        \item Retinal achromic patch.
        \item Multiple renal cysts.
        \item Nonrenal hamartomas.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic Categories}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Definite TSC}: Two major features OR one major feature plus two or more minor features.
        \item \textit{Possible TSC}: Either one major feature OR two or more minor features.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurological Investigations}: High-resolution brain MRI with and without gadolinium contrast is the gold standard to identify and monitor cortical tubers, SENs, and SEGAs. Electroencephalography (EEG) or video-EEG monitoring is essential for characterizing seizure types, especially when infantile spasms or focal epilepsy are suspected.
    \item \textbf{Abdominal and Renal Investigations}: Abdominal MRI is preferred over CT (to minimize radiation exposure) to monitor the number and growth of renal angiomyolipomas and cysts. Renal ultrasound is useful for routine, low-risk screening.
    \item \textbf{Pulmonary and Cardiac Investigations}: High-resolution chest CT (HRCT) is performed to screen for cystic changes of LAM in adult females (starting around age 18 or if symptomatic). Echocardiography is performed in neonates and infants to detect cardiac rhabdomyomas.
    \item \textbf{Dermatological and Ophthalmic Evaluations}: A comprehensive skin exam using a Wood's lamp to identify hypopigmented patches, and a dilated eye exam (fundoscopy) by an ophthalmologist to evaluate for retinal astrocytic hamartomas.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management of Tuberous Sclerosis Complex requires a lifelong, multidisciplinary approach involving specialists in neurology, nephrology, pulmonology, cardiology, dermatology, genetics, and psychiatry. Treatment focuses on managing active symptoms, reducing tumor size, and preventing life-threatening complications.

The primary components of management include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{mTOR Inhibitor Therapy}: The introduction of mTOR inhibitors has revolutionized TSC care. Drugs like everolimus (oral) and sirolimus (oral and topical) target the underlying pathophysiological pathway.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Systemic Everolimus}: Approved for shrinking growing SEGAs that are not candidates for surgical resection, treating enlarging or symptomatic renal angiomyolipomas (typically those greater than 4 cm in diameter), stabilizing lung function in women with progressive LAM, and as an adjunctive treatment for refractory TSC-associated focal epilepsy.
        \item \textit{Topical Sirolimus}: Formulated as a cream or gel, it is highly effective at reducing the size, redness, and bleeding of facial angiofibromas with minimal systemic absorption.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Epilepsy Management}: Controlling seizures is critical to preserving cognitive development.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Vigabatrin}: An irreversible inhibitor of gamma-aminobutyric acid transaminase (GABA-T), it is the established first-line treatment for infantile spasms in infants with TSC. Visual field testing is monitored due to the risk of vigabatrin-induced retinal toxicity.
        \item \textit{Other Medications}: Broad-spectrum antiepileptic drugs (e.g., levetiracetam, topiramate, valproate, clobazam) and newer options like cannabidiol are used for refractory focal seizures.
        \item \textit{Ketogenic Diet}: A high-fat, low-carbohydrate diet managed by a specialized dietitian can help reduce seizure frequency in drug-resistant cases.
        \item \textit{Epilepsy Surgery}: Resection of a single, dominant epileptogenic cortical tuber may be considered if a patient has drug-resistant epilepsy and a clear, localized seizure focus.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgical and Interventional Procedures}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{SEGA Resection}: Surgical craniotomy to remove a SEGA may be required if the tumor causes progressive ventricular obstruction and hydrocephalus, or if mTOR inhibitor therapy is contraindicated or ineffective.
        \item \textit{Renal Angiomyolipoma Embolization}: For renal angiomyolipomas that are bleeding or have a high risk of rupture (size greater than 4 cm or containing aneurysms greater than 5 mm), transcatheter selective arterial embolization is performed. This procedure cuts off the blood supply to the tumor, promoting shrinkage and preserving normal kidney tissue.
        \item \textit{Nephron-Sparing Surgery}: Partial nephrectomy is preferred over total nephrectomy if surgery is required, to protect long-term kidney function and prevent chronic renal failure.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neuropsychiatric and Educational Support (TAND Management)}: Annual screening for TAND is recommended using the TAND Checklist. Interventions include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA) and speech therapy for autism.
        \item Pharmacological therapy for ADHD (e.g., stimulants or non-stimulants) and anxiety/depression (e.g., selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors).
        \item Individualized Education Plans (IEPs) to support learning disabilities.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Routine Surveillance Schedule}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Brain MRI: Every 1 to 3 years in children and young adults up to age 25 to monitor for SEGAs.
        \item Abdominal MRI: Every 1 to 3 years throughout life to monitor renal lesions.
        \item Chest HRCT: Every 5 to 10 years in asymptomatic adult females (or annually/bi-annually if LAM is diagnosed to monitor progression).
        \item Clinical exams: Regular skin, eye, dental, and blood pressure checks (hypertension can indicate renal disease).
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated or poorly managed Tuberous Sclerosis Complex can lead to severe, life-threatening complications. Key complications include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Obstructive Hydrocephalus}: Progressive enlargement of subependymal giant cell astrocytomas (SEGAs) near the foramen of Monro can block the flow of cerebrospinal fluid, causing increased pressure inside the skull, leading to severe neurological decline, coma, or death if not decompressed.
    \item \textbf{Severe Retroperitoneal Hemorrhage (Wunderlich Syndrome)}: Large renal angiomyolipomas (greater than 4 cm) are prone to spontaneous rupture and bleeding. This is a medical emergency that can lead to hemorrhagic shock, requiring urgent embolization or surgery.
    \item \textbf{End-Stage Renal Disease (ESRD)}: The replacement of normal functional kidney tissue by bilateral, expanding angiomyolipomas and multiple cysts causes progressive chronic kidney disease. Over time, this can lead to renal failure, requiring lifelong dialysis or kidney transplantation.
    \item \textbf{Status Epilepticus and SUDEP}: Intractable epilepsy carries the risk of prolonged, non-terminating seizures (status epilepticus) and Sudden Unexpected Death in Epilepsy (SUDEP).
    \item \textbf{Respiratory Failure and Pneumothorax}: In patients with pulmonary LAM, the progressive destruction of lung tissue leads to severe shortness of breath, chronic hypoxemia requiring supplemental oxygen, and recurrent, painful collapses of the lung (pneumothoraces).
    \item \textbf{Treatment-Related Complications}: Chronic use of mTOR inhibitors can cause side effects such as mouth ulcers (stomatitis), dyslipidemia, immunosuppression (increased susceptibility to infections), impaired wound healing, and menstrual irregularities.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The long-term prognosis for individuals with Tuberous Sclerosis Complex varies widely depending on the specific organs affected and the severity of the symptoms. Many individuals with mild forms of the disease lead normal, active, and productive lives with a normal lifespan.

However, severe manifestations can significantly impact life expectancy and quality of life. The major causes of premature mortality in TSC are renal complications (specifically hemorrhage from angiomyolipomas or chronic renal failure), brain tumors (SEGA-induced hydrocephalus), status epilepticus, and respiratory failure secondary to progressive lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM).

The prognosis has improved dramatically over the past two decades. Advances in early detection, such as prenatal diagnosis of cardiac rhabdomyomas and early genetic screening, allow for proactive management. The clinical introduction of mTOR inhibitors (everolimus and sirolimus) has transformed the treatment landscape, allowing non-invasive reduction of brain and kidney tumors and stabilization of pulmonary disease. Furthermore, the targeted use of vigabatrin for infantile spasms has improved cognitive outcomes for many children. With structured, lifelong multidisciplinary surveillance and early therapeutic intervention, the risk of severe complications is significantly reduced.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
As a genetic disorder, Tuberous Sclerosis Complex cannot be prevented in the traditional sense, but self-care strategies, proactive monitoring, and genetic planning can minimize its clinical impact:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic Counseling and Family Planning}: Because TSC is inherited in an autosomal dominant manner, affected individuals have a 50\% chance of passing the condition to their offspring in each pregnancy. Genetic counseling is highly recommended. For couples with a known mutation, options such as preimplantation genetic testing (PGT) during in vitro fertilization (IVF) or prenatal testing (chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis) can prevent transmission. Parents of a child with sporadic TSC should also undergo genetic evaluation to rule out parental germline mosaicism before subsequent pregnancies.
    \item \textbf{Sun Protection for Angiofibromas}: Facial angiofibromas are sensitive to ultraviolet (UV) light. Exposure to sunlight can cause the lesions to become more prominent, red, and prone to bleeding. Patients should practice diligent sun protection, including applying a broad-spectrum sunscreen with an SPF of 30 or higher daily, wearing wide-brimmed hats, and avoiding peak sun hours.
    \item \textbf{Immunizations and Infection Prevention}: Remaining up-to-date with vaccinations is important to prevent infections, especially when taking immunosuppressive mTOR inhibitors like everolimus or sirolimus. Patients should complete routine vaccine schedules (such as pneumococcal and influenza vaccines) prior to starting mTOR inhibitors, and live vaccines must be avoided while on these therapies.
    \item \textbf{Avoidance of Estrogen-Containing Medications}: Estrogen promotes the growth and migration of LAM cells in the lungs. Adult women with TSC (especially those diagnosed with or at risk for LAM) should avoid estrogen-containing contraceptives and hormone replacement therapies. Progesterone-only or non-hormonal contraceptive options should be discussed with a gynecologist.
    \item \textbf{Seizure Management and Home Safety}: Compliance with anticonvulsant regimens is vital. Patients and caregivers should maintain a seizure diary to track patterns and triggers. Home safety measures, such as avoiding sharp-edged furniture, using safety gates, and taking showers instead of baths (to prevent drowning during a seizure), are important for individuals with active epilepsy.
    \item \textbf{Regular Surveillance Adherence}: Self-care in TSC relies heavily on active participation in the recommended clinical surveillance guidelines. Keeping scheduled appointments for routine MRIs, lung function tests, and blood pressure checks is critical to identifying and treating silent complications (like growing tumors or hypertension) before they become emergency situations.
\end{itemize}
