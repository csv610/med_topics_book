\chapter{Candidal Paronychia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Candidal paronychia is a chronic inflammation and infection of the soft tissue fold that surrounds the nail, caused by \textit{Candida} yeasts, most commonly \textit{Candida albicans}. Unlike the acute, painful bacterial paronychia that comes on suddenly and often forms a pus-filled collection, the candidal form develops gradually, tends to be less painful, and usually produces little or no pus. It typically affects the fingernails of people whose hands are exposed to prolonged moisture, and it is frequently associated with separation of the nail from the nail bed. The term paronychia comes from the Greek for "around the nail", and the condition sits within the broader family of candidal infections of the skin and its appendages, often coexisting with candidal onychomycosis and with chronic inflammatory changes of the nail apparatus.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Candidal paronychia is a common condition, most often seen in women and in occupations that involve frequent wet work, such as catering, cleaning, fishing, nursing, and hairdressing. Children who suck their thumbs or fingers are also prone to it, as the constant moisture keeps the nail folds damp. People with diabetes, atopic eczema, or a weakened immune system are at higher risk, as are those taking medicines that suppress immunity. The condition is frequently persistent, with symptoms that come and go over months or years, and it accounts for a substantial proportion of chronic nail-fold disease seen in general practice and dermatology clinics.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\textit{Candida} is a yeast that lives harmlessly as a commensal on the skin and mucous membranes of most people. Paronychia develops when the normal protective barrier of the nail fold is damaged, allowing the yeast to enter and multiply in the warm, moist space between the nail fold and the nail plate. Contributing factors include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Repeated or prolonged immersion of the hands in water,} which softens and macerates the nail fold
    \item \textbf{Injury to the cuticle} from picking, biting, or aggressive manicuring
    \item \textbf{Contact with irritants} such as detergents, solvents, and chemical solutions
    \item \textbf{Skin diseases} such as eczema and psoriasis, which break the epidermal barrier
    \item \textbf{Diabetes} and other causes of impaired local immunity
    \item \textbf{Medicines} that suppress the immune system, such as corticosteroids and some chemotherapy agents
\end{itemize}
Once established, \textit{Candida} causes chronic inflammation of the nail fold, which becomes swollen and loses its tight seal against the nail plate. The inflammatory response and yeast growth gradually undermine the base of the nail, causing it to lift away from the nail bed and allowing moisture and further organisms to collect beneath the nail.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Candidal paronychia usually affects one or more fingernails and is characterised by:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Redness and swelling of the skin fold at the base of the nail, and often the sides
    \item A rounded, puffy appearance of the nail fold that loses its sharp edge
    \item Mild tenderness rather than severe pain
    \item Loss of the cuticle
    \item Separation of the nail from the nail bed, with a whitish, yellowish, or greenish discolouration
    \item Ridging, grooving, or distortion of the nail as the condition continues
    \item The swollen fold gradually spreading over the nail and narrowing its visible surface
\end{itemize}
There is usually little or no pus, which helps distinguish the condition from acute bacterial paronychia. The symptoms fluctuate, with episodes of increased swelling and tenderness, and several nails are often affected at the same time.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions can cause a similar appearance at the nail fold, and the diagnosis is clinical in most cases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute bacterial paronychia, usually due to \textit{Staphylococcus aureus} or \textit{Streptococcus}, which is sudden, painful, and often purulent
    \item Herpetic whitlow, caused by herpes simplex virus, with small grouped blisters and tingling pain
    \item Dermatophyte fungal infection of the nail (tinea unguium)
    \item Nail psoriasis, with pitting, thickening, and onycholysis
    \item Eczema of the hands with involvement of the periungual skin
    \item A foreign body or splinter lodged in the nail fold
    \item Early skin cancers of the nail fold, such as squamous cell carcinoma and melanoma, which are rare but important to exclude in atypical or persistent lesions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if the nail fold is persistently swollen and sore, especially if:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The swelling has lasted more than a few weeks
    \item The nail is separating or changing shape
    \item There is discharge, increasing redness, or spreading pain
    \item You have diabetes or a weakened immune system
    \item Self-care measures are not helping after a few weeks
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe pain with swelling of the fingertip
    \item Rapidly spreading redness, heat, or red streaks extending up the arm
    \item Fever, chills, or feeling generally unwell
    \item Numbness or loss of movement in the finger
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} reviewing occupation, water exposure, skin conditions, and the appearance of the nail, cuticle, and surrounding skin
    \item \textbf{Fungal microscopy and culture:} of swabs or nail scrapings treated with potassium hydroxide to confirm \textit{Candida} and exclude dermatophytes
    \item \textbf{Bacterial culture:} if a bacterial infection is suspected, particularly when there is discharge or acute worsening
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} such as blood glucose, thyroid function, or immune studies if a predisposing condition is suspected
    \item \textbf{Biopsy} is rarely needed but may be considered when malignancy is suspected or the lesion fails to respond
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Successful treatment depends on keeping the affected fingers dry and eliminating \textit{Candida} from the nail fold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Drying the hands:} the cornerstone of treatment; wear gloves for wet work and dry the hands carefully afterwards
    \item \textbf{Topical antifungal creams or solutions:} applied to the nail fold, sometimes combined with a mild corticosteroid to reduce inflammation
    \item \textbf{Oral antifungal medicines:} such as fluconazole or itraconazole for cases that are resistant to topical treatment
    \item \textbf{Avoiding irritants:} reduce contact with detergents, chemicals, and repeated wetting
    \item \textbf{Gentle nail care:} avoid cutting the cuticle and stop picking or biting the nails
    \item \textbf{Treating underlying conditions:} improving blood sugar control in diabetes, or adjusting immunosuppressive medicines with medical advice
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review over months, since the nail grows slowly and relapse is common
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic swelling and deformity of the nail apparatus if left untreated
    \item Permanent nail separation, ridging, or complete loss of the nail
    \item Recurrent episodes of acute bacterial paronychia superimposed on the chronic infection
    \item Secondary infection spreading into the deeper tissues of the finger (cellulitis or felon)
    \item Cosmetic concerns, discomfort, and impaired function, especially in wet occupations
    \item In immunocompromised people, the theoretical risk of spread of \textit{Candida} beyond the nail
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With consistent drying and antifungal treatment, most cases of candidal paronychia settle within a few weeks to months, although the nail itself may take longer to return to a normal appearance. The condition frequently recurs if the hands are repeatedly exposed to moisture or the underlying predisposition is not addressed. Where a contributing cause such as diabetes is controlled and the patient complies with dry-hand measures, the outlook is generally excellent and permanent nail damage is uncommon.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep the hands dry; wear waterproof gloves for cleaning, dishwashing, and wet work, and remove them promptly when the task is finished
    \item Dry the hands thoroughly, especially around the nails, after washing
    \item Use an emollient or moisturising cream on the hands to protect the skin barrier
    \item Do not bite the nails, pick the cuticles, or push the cuticles back aggressively
    \item Treat eczema and other skin conditions with medical help
    \item Control diabetes and other immune-weakening conditions
    \item Seek early treatment for redness or swelling of the nail fold before it becomes chronic
\end{itemize}
