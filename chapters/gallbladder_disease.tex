\chapter{Gallbladder Disease}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Gallbladder disease refers to the conditions that affect the gallbladder, the small pear-shaped organ beneath the liver that stores and concentrates bile. The most common gallbladder disease is gallstones, which cause biliary colic when they block the outlet, and acute cholecystitis when they cause the gallbladder to become inflamed and infected. Other forms include gallbladder polyps, and rarely gallbladder cancer. Many people with gallstones have no symptoms and need no treatment, but symptomatic disease causes significant pain and complications, and it is usually treated by removing the gallbladder. This chapter explains the types of gallbladder disease, their symptoms, and their treatment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Gallstones affect around 10--15% of Australian adults, rising to a third of those over 60, and they are more common in women, in people who are overweight, and in certain ethnic groups. Around 80% of people with gallstones never develop symptoms. Of those with symptoms, many go on to have an attack of cholecystitis or other complications without treatment. Gallbladder removal (cholecystectomy) is one of the most commonly performed operations in Australia, with over 30,000 procedures a year. Gallbladder cancer is rare, affecting around 1 in 100,000 people.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gallstone formation:} bile contains cholesterol, bile salts, and bilirubin; when the balance changes, crystals form and grow into stones
    \item \textbf{Cholesterol stones:} most common, associated with obesity, a high-fat diet, rapid weight loss, pregnancy, and genetics
    \item \textbf{Pigment stones:} associated with haemolysis and liver disease
    \item \textbf{Biliary colic:} a stone obstructs the cystic duct temporarily, the gallbladder contracts against the blockage, and pain results
    \item \textbf{Cholecystitis:} sustained obstruction inflames the gallbladder wall, and bacteria can superinfect it
    \item \textbf{Risk of complications:} a stone can pass into the common bile duct, causing jaundice, cholangitis, and pancreatitis
    \item \textbf{Gallbladder polyps:} growths in the wall, mostly benign, with size guiding management
    \item \textbf{Chronic cholecystitis:} repeated episodes thicken and scar the gallbladder wall
    \item \textbf{Gallbladder cancer:} rare, but more common with long-standing large stones and a calcified "porcelain" gallbladder
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item No symptoms in most people with gallstones
    \item Biliary colic: sudden, severe pain in the upper right abdomen or the centre, radiating to the back or right shoulder, lasting 15 minutes to several hours, often after a fatty meal or at night
    \item Nausea and vomiting with the pain
    \item Cholecystitis: pain that persists beyond a few hours, with fever, tenderness, and feeling unwell
    \item Jaundice with dark urine and pale stools when a stone blocks the bile duct
    \item Indigestion, bloating, and discomfort after meals in some people
    \item Symptoms that recur intermittently over months
    \item Complications present with severe illness: high fever, rigors, and shock in cholangitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Upper abdominal pain has many causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Peptic ulcer disease and reflux
    \item Pancreatitis and liver disease
    \item Irritable bowel syndrome and functional dyspepsia
    \item Heart disease, including heart attack
    \item Kidney stones and urinary infections
    \item Pneumonia and chest conditions
    \item Appendicitis and other surgical conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with recurrent upper abdominal pain, especially after fatty meals, should be assessed. Pain lasting more than a few hours, or pain with fever, jaundice, or vomiting, requires urgent care.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain that does not settle
    \item Fever with abdominal pain, which may indicate cholecystitis or cholangitis
    \item Jaundice with shaking chills and confusion
    \item Vomiting with inability to keep fluids down
    \item Chest pain or breathlessness
    \item Collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the pattern of pain, tenderness under the right ribs, and signs of jaundice
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} the first test, showing stones, a thickened gallbladder wall, and dilated ducts
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} liver function, bilirubin, inflammatory markers, and pancreatic enzymes
    \item \textbf{MRCP:} magnetic resonance imaging of the bile ducts when duct stones are suspected
    \item \textbf{ERCP:} endoscopic imaging and treatment of duct stones
    \item \textbf{CT:} for complications and surgical planning
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asymptomatic stones:} no treatment; most remain harmless for life
    \item \textbf{Biliary colic:} analgesia and planned cholecystectomy for recurring attacks
    \item \textbf{Cholecystitis:} admission, antibiotics, fluids, and cholecystectomy, often within days
    \item \textbf{Cholecystectomy:} the definitive treatment, usually laparoscopic keyhole surgery with day-case or overnight stay
    \item \textbf{Duct stones:} ERCP to remove them, then cholecystectomy to prevent recurrence
    \item \textbf{Cholangitis:} emergency treatment with antibiotics, fluids, and urgent duct drainage
    \item \textbf{Polyps:} surveillance ultrasound, or surgery for larger polyps
    \item \textbf{Diet:} a healthy diet and weight management reduce risk and symptoms
    \item \textbf{After surgery:} most people digest normally; a minority needs to moderate fatty meals
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute cholecystitis with abscess, gangrene, or perforation
    \item Cholangitis, a life-threatening infection
    \item Acute pancreatitis
    \item Obstructive jaundice and liver injury
    \item Gallstone ileus, a rare bowel obstruction
    \item Bile duct injury during surgery, which is uncommon
    \item Rarely, gallbladder cancer with long-standing disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for gallbladder disease is excellent. Asymptomatic gallstones require no treatment, and symptomatic disease is cured by cholecystectomy, which is safe and quick with modern keyhole techniques. Complications such as cholecystitis and pancreatitis resolve with prompt treatment, and cholangitis is treatable when treated urgently. Gallbladder cancer is rare and mostly preventable by treating long-standing disease. After treatment, most people return to a normal diet and life without further trouble.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and avoid crash diets
    \item Eat a diet rich in fibre and lower in saturated fat
    \item Exercise regularly
    \item Seek assessment early for recurrent upper abdominal pain
    \item Go to hospital promptly for severe pain, fever, or jaundice
    \item Follow post-operative advice, and introduce fatty foods gradually after surgery
    \item Attend scheduled ultrasound for gallbladder polyps
\end{itemize}
