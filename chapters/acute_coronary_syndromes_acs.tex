\chapter{Acute Coronary Syndromes (ACS)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Acute coronary syndromes (ACS) are a group of medical emergencies caused by a sudden reduction in blood flow to the heart muscle, usually because one of the coronary arteries becomes blocked by a blood clot. The three forms of ACS are unstable angina, non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). A heart attack (myocardial infarction) occurs when the blockage is severe enough to damage or destroy part of the heart muscle.

The distinction between the forms matters for treatment and urgency. Unstable angina causes chest pain without detectable heart-muscle damage, whereas NSTEMI and STEMI are both heart attacks, with STEMI reflecting the complete blockage of a major artery and requiring the most urgent treatment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Coronary heart disease is one of the leading causes of death and disability worldwide. In Australia, cardiovascular disease affects around one in five adults, and heart attacks remain a major cause of emergency hospital admission. Men tend to develop ACS at a younger age than women, although risk rises steeply for both sexes after menopause.

Rates have declined in recent decades because of better treatment and prevention, but risk remains higher in people with diabetes, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, smoking, and in those with a family history of early heart disease. ACS accounts for a substantial proportion of all cardiac deaths and imposes a large burden on health systems.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The usual cause of ACS is atherosclerosis, the build-up of fatty plaques within the walls of the coronary arteries over many years. An event occurs when a plaque cracks or ruptures, exposing its contents to the blood. This triggers a blood clot to form, which can partially or completely block the artery and starve the heart muscle of oxygen. If the blockage is brief or partial, the damage is limited (unstable angina or NSTEMI); if it is complete, a larger area of muscle may die (STEMI).

The main modifiable risk factors are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Smoking and exposure to tobacco smoke
    \item High blood pressure and high cholesterol
    \item Diabetes mellitus
    \item Obesity, inactivity, and poor diet
    \item Excess alcohol and long-term stress
\end{itemize}

Non-modifiable risk factors include increasing age, male sex, and a family history of early heart disease.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The classic symptom is chest pain or discomfort, often described as pressure, tightness, squeezing, or heaviness in the centre of the chest. The pain may:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Spread to the arms, neck, jaw, back, or shoulders
    \item Last longer than a few minutes or come and go
    \item Occur at rest (in unstable angina) or with exertion
    \item Be accompanied by sweating, nausea, vomiting, or light-headedness
    \item Be associated with shortness of breath
\end{itemize}

Not everyone has chest pain. Women, older people, and people with diabetes may instead feel fatigue, breathlessness, indigestion, nausea, or discomfort in the back or jaw. Atypical presentations are important to recognise, as they are more likely to be dismissed or misdiagnosed.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Chest pain has many causes, and not all are cardiac:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Stable angina (predictable pain on exertion that settles with rest)
    \item Heartburn or acid reflux (gastro-oesophageal reflux disease)
    \item Musculoskeletal pain (chest wall strain, costochondritis)
    \item Panic attack or anxiety
    \item Pulmonary embolism or pleurisy
    \item Pneumonia or other chest infections
    \item Aortic dissection (a rare but life-threatening cause)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Chest pain that is new, severe, persistent, or that comes on at rest is a medical emergency and should never be ignored. Anyone at risk of a heart attack should learn the warning signs and act quickly, because rapid treatment is the single most important factor in limiting heart-muscle damage.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Crushing or heavy chest pain lasting more than a few minutes
    \item Chest pain spreading to the jaw, arm, or back
    \item Chest pain with breathlessness, sweating, or vomiting
    \item Collapse, confusion, or loss of consciousness
    \item Pain or discomfort that does not settle with rest
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
In the emergency department, the priority is to confirm or exclude ACS quickly:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ECG:} a 12-lead electrocardiogram, taken quickly and repeated, may show changes of a heart attack
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} troponin is a protein released when heart muscle is damaged; rising levels indicate a heart attack
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} to look for other causes of chest pain and complications such as fluid on the lungs
    \item \textbf{Coronary angiography:} a procedure using dye and X-rays to see blockages in the coronary arteries
    \item \textbf{Echocardiogram:} ultrasound to assess how well the heart muscle is pumping
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
ACS is treated urgently to restore blood flow and limit damage to the heart muscle:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Emergency medicines:} aspirin, anti-clotting medicines, and medicines to relieve pain and reduce the heart's workload
    \item \textbf{Angioplasty and stenting:} a balloon opens the blocked artery and a stent keeps it open (the preferred treatment for STEMI)
    \item \textbf{Thrombolysis:} clot-dissolving medicine given when angioplasty is not available quickly
    \item \textbf{Coronary bypass surgery:} for people with blockages not suitable for stenting
    \item \textbf{Secondary prevention:} long-term medicines such as aspirin, statins, beta-blockers, and blood pressure control
    \item \textbf{Cardiac rehabilitation:} a supervised programme of exercise, education, and support after the event
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dangerous heart rhythms (arrhythmias), including cardiac arrest
    \item Heart failure, when damaged muscle cannot pump effectively
    \item Cardiogenic shock, a severe drop in blood pressure
    \item Pericarditis or bleeding within the heart
    \item Valve problems, such as leaking of the mitral valve
    \item Repeat heart attacks or strokes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Outlook depends on how quickly treatment begins, how much heart muscle is damaged, and the person's overall health. Rapid treatment restores blood flow in most cases and greatly improves survival. Many people return to work and normal activities with the help of cardiac rehabilitation.

However, ACS is a warning sign of widespread artery disease, so long-term medicines and lifestyle change are essential to reduce the risk of a further event. With modern care, most people survive an acute event, and the major determinants of long-term outcome are adherence to treatment and control of risk factors.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
For those who have had ACS, and for people at risk:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Do not smoke and avoid second-hand smoke
    \item Control blood pressure, cholesterol, and diabetes with medicines and monitoring
    \item Eat a heart-healthy diet low in salt, saturated fat, and added sugar
    \item Be physically active most days, building up gradually after an event
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and limit alcohol
    \item Learn the warning signs of a heart attack and act immediately
    \item Take prescribed medicines exactly as directed and attend follow-up appointments
\end{itemize}
