\chapter{Mastitis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Mastitis is an inflammatory condition of the breast tissue, which may or may not be accompanied by an infection. It is most commonly observed in lactating women (lactational mastitis), occurring when milk stasis leads to localised milk accumulation, triggering a robust inflammatory cascade in the surrounding tissue. Non-lactational mastitis can also occur, though less frequently, often taking the form of periductal mastitis or idiopathic granulomatous mastitis. Symptoms of mastitis include localised breast pain, swelling, warmth, redness, and systemic flu-like symptoms. While lactational mastitis is generally straightforward to manage, delayed or inadequate treatment can lead to complications such as breast abscess or chronic recurrence. It is a significant clinical issue that can lead to early cessation of breastfeeding if not managed with supportive, evidence-based care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Lactational mastitis is highly prevalent, affecting approximately 10\% to 30\% of breastfeeding mothers worldwide, with the vast majority of cases occurring within the first 6 to 12 weeks postpartum. In Australia, it represents a frequent reason for postpartum primary care consults and calls to breastfeeding support lines. There is no significant geographic or racial variation in its incidence, though first-time mothers (primiparas) appear to have a higher risk, likely due to difficulties establishing breastfeeding techniques. Non-lactational mastitis is far less common, predominantly affecting women of reproductive age, with a strong epidemiological link to cigarette smoking in cases of periductal mastitis. Key risk factors for lactational mastitis include poor infant attachment, missed feeds, oversupply, nipple trauma (cracks or fissures), and maternal fatigue.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The development of mastitis involves a progression from mechanical ductal congestion to chemical inflammation, and in some cases, bacterial colonisation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Milk stasis:} The primary trigger, where incomplete breast emptying due to poor latch, scheduling, or milk oversupply causes milk to back up and leak into surrounding tissue, causing chemical inflammation.
    \item \textbf{Bacterial entry:} Pathogens, most commonly Staphylococcus aureus (including MRSA strains) or Streptococcus species, enter the breast ducts through cracked or traumatised nipple skin.
    \item \textbf{Hyperlactation:} Overproduction of milk that increases pressure within the breast lobules, predisposing the tissue to ductal compression and localised stasis.
    \item \textbf{Local mechanical pressure:} Tight bras, seatbelts, or sleeping position that compress breast tissue and block specific lactiferous ducts.
    \item \textbf{Nipple shield use:} Inappropriate use of shields that can impair efficient milk transfer, contributing to sub-optimal breast emptying.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Mastitis is characterised by a combination of localised breast findings and systemic inflammatory signs.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Localised breast pain:} Intense, aching, or burning pain in a specific segment of one breast, which is exacerbated during breastfeeding.
    \item \textbf{Erythema and warmth:} A wedge-shaped area of redness, swelling, and localised heat on the skin overlying the affected breast lobe.
    \item \textbf{Breast induration:} A firm, tender lump or area of hardness representing the engorged, inflamed segment.
    \item \textbf{Systemic flu-like symptoms:} High fever (typically 38 degrees Celsius or higher), chills, rigors, generalised body aches, and severe fatigue.
    \item \textbf{Nipple trauma:} Visible cracks, fissures, or grazing on the nipple-areola complex, which often serve as the portal of entry for bacteria.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
It is important to differentiate mastitis from other breast lesions to ensure appropriate management.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Breast engorgement:} Bilateral, diffuse breast fullness and tenderness occurring in the first week postpartum, which lacks the focal erythema and high fever of mastitis.
    \item \textbf{Plugged duct:} A localised, tender breast lump without systemic symptoms or severe skin erythema, representing localised stasis before inflammation develops.
    \item \textbf{Breast abscess:} A localised collection of pus presenting as a fluctuant, exquisitely tender mass, often with overlying skin discolouration, complicating unresolved mastitis.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory breast cancer:} A rare, aggressive malignancy presenting with diffuse breast erythema, oedema (peau d'orange), and warmth, which must be suspected if symptoms do not respond to antibiotics.
    \item \textbf{Galactocele:} A benign, milk-filled retention cyst presenting as a painless, mobile, well-circumscribed lump, without inflammatory signs.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Breastfeeding mothers who develop localised breast redness, swelling, and pain should seek prompt medical advice if symptoms do not improve within 24 hours of optimal breast emptying. Immediate emergency services are required if severe systemic symptoms or signs of sepsis occur.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Signs of systemic sepsis:} High fever, rapid breathing, confusion, extreme dizziness, or a rapid heart rate accompanying breast infection.
    \item \textbf{Spreading cellulitis:} Rapidly spreading redness, swelling, or red streaks travelling up the breast toward the armpit or chest.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of mastitis is primarily clinical, based on history and physical findings. Investigations are reserved for atypical or refractory cases.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical evaluation:} Thorough assessment of breastfeeding technique, nipple integrity, and physical examination of both breasts and axillary lymph nodes.
    \item \textbf{Breast milk culture:} Indicated if the patient does not respond to initial antibiotics, has recurrent mastitis, or if the infection is hospital-acquired, to guide narrow-spectrum therapy.
    \item \textbf{Breast ultrasound:} The primary imaging test, highly useful for distinguishing mastitis from a suspected deep breast abscess.
    \item \textbf{Mammography:} Not routinely performed during acute lactational mastitis due to discomfort and altered tissue density, but indicated for persistent non-lactational cases to rule out malignancy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The core of mastitis management is maintaining milk flow and treating infection when clinically indicated.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Frequent and effective breast emptying:} Continuing breastfeeding or expressing milk from the affected breast, ensuring correct positioning and latch to optimise drainage.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological antibiotics:} Oral antibiotics targeting Staphylococcus aureus (e.g. flucloxacillin or cefalexin) for 5--10 days if symptoms are severe or do not improve within 12--24 hours of drainage.
    \item \textbf{Analgesia and anti-inflammatories:} Use of paracetamol and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs like ibuprofen) to reduce pain and local tissue inflammation, which are safe during breastfeeding.
    \item \textbf{Cold packs and warm compresses:} Applying cold compresses after feeds to reduce swelling and pain, and gentle warmth before feeds to encourage the let-down reflex.
    \item \textbf{Clinical support:} Lactation consultant review to optimise infant positioning, attachment, and feeding schedules.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Breast abscess:} A localised accumulation of purulent material within the breast, requiring ultrasound-guided needle aspiration or surgical drainage.
    \item \textbf{Recurrent mastitis:} Repeated episodes of breast inflammation, often due to persistent risk factors (e.g. chronic oversupply) or inadequate primary antibiotic duration.
    \item \textbf{Chronic subclinical mastitis:} Persistent low-grade inflammation leading to altered milk composition, causing infant feeding aversion or poor weight gain.
    \item \textbf{Early cessation of breastfeeding:} Psychological and physical distress leading the mother to stop breastfeeding prematurely.
    \item \textbf{Sepsis:} Spread of bacteria into the bloodstream, a rare but life-threatening systemic complication.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for lactational mastitis is excellent with prompt, appropriate management. Most women experience a significant reduction in systemic symptoms within 24 to 48 hours of starting antibiotics and optimising breast emptying. Localised redness and tenderness may take up to a week to resolve completely. Continued breastfeeding during the illness is safe for the infant and crucial for maternal recovery, as it prevents worsening milk stasis. Non-lactational mastitis can be more challenging, with a higher rate of recurrence and chronic fistulisation, but responds well to combined medical and lifestyle measures (such as smoking cessation).

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ensure proper latch:} Seeking early guidance from a midwife or lactation consultant to ensure optimal infant positioning and attachment.
    \item \textbf{Feed on demand:} Allowing the infant to feed frequently and avoid scheduled feeding times to prevent breast engorgement.
    \item \textbf{Avoid sudden weaning:} Gradually reducing feeds rather than stopping abruptly, to allow milk production to adjust.
    \item \textbf{Inspect nipples regularly:} Monitoring the nipples for cracks or soreness and applying purified lanolin or breast milk to promote healing.
    \item \textbf{Avoid tight clothing:} Wearing supportive but non-restrictive bras and avoiding underwire designs that can compress milk ducts.
\end{itemize}
