\chapter{Genital Warts}
\index{Genital Warts}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Genital warts are small, fleshy growths that appear on or around the genitals and anus, caused by certain strains of the human papillomavirus (HPV), most commonly types 6 and 11. They are one of the most common sexually transmitted infections worldwide. Warts usually appear weeks to months after exposure to the virus and vary in size and appearance, from flat spots to cauliflower-like clusters. In most people, the immune system clears the virus within one to two years, and the warts resolve without treatment. While genital warts are harmless and do not cause cancer, they can be distressing and sometimes recur. The HPV vaccine provides excellent protection against the wart-causing strains.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Genital warts are very common, particularly among sexually active young adults. Around 1 in 10 people will experience genital warts at some stage in their life. The highest rates are in people aged 15--30. Since the introduction of the HPV vaccine into national immunisation programs, the incidence of genital warts has fallen dramatically in countries including some countries, especially among young people vaccinated before sexual debut. local school-based HPV vaccination program has been so effective that genital warts are now rare in young heterosexual women and in young men who have sex with women.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Genital warts are caused by infection of the skin with low-risk strains of human papillomavirus.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Causative strains:} HPV types 6 and 11 cause about 90\% of genital warts; they are classified as low-risk because they do not cause cancer
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} by skin-to-skin contact during vaginal, anal, or oral sex, and sometimes from mother to baby during birth
    \item \textbf{Incubation:} warts may appear weeks, months, or even years after exposure, and many infected people never develop visible warts
    \item \textbf{Immune response:} most infections are cleared by the immune system within one to two years, and some people clear the virus without ever developing warts
    \item \textbf{Persistence:} in a minority, the virus persists and warts recur or enlarge, especially if immunity is reduced
    \item \textbf{Distinction from high-risk HPV:} different strains (notably 16 and 18) cause cervical and other cancers but do not cause visible warts
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Genital warts can appear anywhere in the genital or anal area and vary considerably in appearance.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Small, flesh-coloured or pink bumps on the penis, scrotum, vulva, vagina, cervix, or around the anus
    \item Warts that are flat, raised, or shaped like tiny cauliflowers
    \item Single warts or clusters; they may occur singly or in crops
    \item Usually painless, but they can itch, bleed, or cause discomfort during sex
    \item Warts may enlarge during pregnancy or in people with weakened immunity
    \item Anal warts may cause itching, bleeding, or a feeling of a lump
    \item Often no symptoms at all, with warts found incidentally during examination
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Not every bump in the genital area is a wart, and accurate diagnosis guides management.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Skin tags (acrochordons), which are soft, benign outgrowths of skin
    \item Molluscum contagiosum, a viral infection causing small, pearly bumps
    \item Seborrhoeic keratoses, harmless pigmented skin growths
    \item Pearly penile papules, normal anatomical features of the penis
    \item Fordyce spots, small normal sebaceous glands on the genitals
    \item Genital herpes, which causes blisters and ulcers rather than warts
    \item Squamous cell carcinoma and other rare cancers of the genital skin, which can mimic warts
    \item Syphilis (condylomata lata), flat wart-like lesions of secondary syphilis
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor or sexual health clinic if you notice any new growths on or around the genitals or anus, to confirm the diagnosis and discuss treatment options. Testing for other sexually transmitted infections is recommended for anyone diagnosed with genital warts.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Very heavy bleeding from a wart during sex, trauma, or pregnancy
    \item Sudden severe pain, swelling, or signs of infection in the genital area
    \item Difficulty passing urine due to extensive warts around the urinary opening
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Genital warts are usually diagnosed by visual inspection alone, and most need no further investigation.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} inspection of the genital, perianal, and anal areas, sometimes with a magnifying instrument
    \item \textbf{Vaginal examination:} women may need an internal examination to check for warts on the cervix or vagina
    \item \textbf{Acetic acid test:} applying dilute acetic acid can help identify flat warts, though it is not always necessary
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} rarely needed, but performed if a wart looks unusual, is pigmented, fails to respond to treatment, or is suspected to be cancerous
    \item \textbf{STI screening:} testing for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, syphilis, and HIV is recommended because these commonly coexist
    \item \textbf{Cervical screening:} women should be up to date with their routine cervical screening, which checks for high-risk HPV and cell changes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment aims to remove visible warts and reduce symptoms. No treatment eliminates the virus itself, and warts may recur after successful removal.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Topical treatments:} creams and solutions applied at home, such as imiquimod, podophyllotoxin, or sinecatechins, which destroy wart tissue or stimulate immunity
    \item \textbf{Cryotherapy:} freezing warts with liquid nitrogen, performed in the clinic over several sessions
    \item \textbf{Other clinic-based treatments:} electrocautery, laser therapy, or surgical removal for larger or resistant warts
    \item \textbf{Trichloroacetic acid:} a chemical applied by a clinician for small warts
    \item \textbf{Treatment of partners:} not required routinely, as partners usually clear the virus themselves, though they may benefit from examination
    \item \textbf{Observation:} because many warts resolve spontaneously, some people choose to wait, especially if warts are small and symptom-free
    \item \textbf{Recurrence:} warts often recur within weeks to months and may need several courses of treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Recurrence of warts after treatment, which is common and can be distressing
    \item Psychological distress, embarrassment, and effects on sexual relationships
    \item Bleeding, infection, or scarring at treatment sites
    \item Obstruction of the urinary or anal opening in extensive disease
    \item Enlargement and obstruction during pregnancy, occasionally requiring caesarean delivery
    \item Rarely, warts in immunocompromised people that grow large and are difficult to treat
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for genital warts is good. Most people clear the virus within one to two years, and even without treatment many warts disappear spontaneously. Treated warts resolve in most people, although recurrence within three months is common and may require further treatment. Once the immune system has cleared the infection, warts do not return unless the person is re-exposed. Genital warts do not affect fertility and do not cause cancer. Reassurance and accurate information are important parts of care because the condition is so common and treatable.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item HPV vaccination, which protects against the wart-causing types 6 and 11 as well as cancer-causing types; it is free through school programs in many countries and available for older people through their GP
    \item Consistent condom use, which reduces but does not fully prevent transmission because the virus can affect skin not covered by the condom
    \item Consideration of vaccination for people who have already had warts, as it may protect against other HPV types
    \item Avoiding sexual contact while warts are visible reduces transmission
    \item Not smoking, because smoking is associated with more persistent HPV infection
    \item Keeping up to date with cervical screening if you are a woman
    \item Discussing any new bumps with a doctor rather than using over-the-counter wart treatments designed for other parts of the body
\end{itemize}
