\chapter{Autoimmune Conditions}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Autoimmune conditions are a large family of disorders in which the immune system, which normally protects the body against infection, mistakenly attacks its own healthy tissues. More than 80 different autoimmune conditions have been identified, and they can affect almost any part of the body. Well-known examples include rheumatoid arthritis (joints), type 1 diabetes (pancreas), coeliac disease (gut), Hashimoto's thyroiditis (thyroid), multiple sclerosis (nervous system) and lupus (skin, joints and kidneys).

Most autoimmune conditions are chronic, meaning they persist over time. Many follow a relapsing-remitting pattern, with periods when symptoms flare and periods when they settle. There is no single cure, but modern treatment can control symptoms, limit organ damage and allow most people to live full and active lives.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Autoimmune conditions are common. Roughly one in twenty adults lives with at least one of them, and many people have more than one at the same time. They can begin at any age, but many first appear in early to middle adulthood.

Women are affected far more often than men -- around three-quarters of people with autoimmune conditions are female. Some conditions show strong family clustering, and several are more common in particular regions or ethnic groups. For example, type 1 diabetes is more frequent in European populations, while lupus is more common in people of African, Asian or Pacific Islander background.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
In a healthy immune system, immune cells learn to recognise the body's own tissues and leave them alone. In autoimmunity, this tolerance breaks down and the immune system attacks normal tissue as if it were foreign.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic susceptibility:} certain genes (particularly the HLA family) make some people more likely to develop autoimmunity, though inheriting them does not guarantee disease
    \item \textbf{Environmental triggers:} viral or bacterial infections, smoking, sunlight and some medicines can trigger disease in susceptible people
    \item \textbf{Hormonal influences:} the strong female predominance of many autoimmune conditions suggests hormones play a role
    \item \textbf{Immune regulation:} disturbances in the cells that normally switch off immune responses may contribute
\end{itemize}

Once triggered, immune cells and antibodies cause inflammation and damage in the affected tissues. The mechanism varies by condition: some are driven mainly by autoantibodies against the body's own tissue, others by self-reactive white blood cells, and some by both.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on which organs are attacked, so autoimmune conditions look very different from one another. However, some features are shared across many of them.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent fatigue out of proportion to activity
    \item Joint pain, swelling or morning stiffness (e.g.\ rheumatoid arthritis, lupus)
    \item Skin changes, such as rashes, dryness or sensitivity to sunlight
    \item Unexplained fever, weight change or night sweats
    \item Organ-specific features, such as thirst and frequent urination in type 1 diabetes, or numbness and tingling in multiple sclerosis
    \item Symptoms that flare and settle over time
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Autoimmune conditions can be hard to diagnose because their symptoms overlap with many other problems.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Infections, such as a viral illness, tuberculosis or Lyme disease
    \item Cancers, particularly blood cancers such as lymphoma
    \item Fibromyalgia and chronic fatigue syndrome
    \item Endocrine disorders, such as thyroid disease or diabetes
    \item Nutritional deficiencies, such as vitamin B12 or iron deficiency
    \item Side effects of medicines or recreational drugs
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if you have new, persistent or unexplained symptoms such as prolonged fatigue, joint pain, unexplained fever or a rash that does not settle. Early assessment can help identify the cause and start treatment sooner. It is also important to report any sudden change in an existing condition.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, difficulty breathing or sudden shortness of breath
    \item Sudden weakness, especially on one side of the body, or difficulty speaking
    \item Sudden loss of vision or a severe new headache
    \item Seizures, confusion or loss of consciousness
    \item High fever with a fast heart rate and confusion (possible severe infection)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
There is no single test for autoimmunity, and diagnosis usually combines clinical assessment with several investigations.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} a detailed account of symptoms, family history and a full physical examination
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} inflammatory markers (ESR and C-reactive protein), full blood count and organ-specific tests
    \item \textbf{Autoantibody tests:} such as antinuclear antibody (ANA) and rheumatoid factor, though these can be positive in healthy people and are not diagnostic alone
    \item \textbf{Disease-specific tests:} for example, islet antibodies in type 1 diabetes or coeliac antibodies
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} X-rays, ultrasound or MRI of affected joints or organs
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} a small tissue sample (e.g.\ skin, kidney or bowel) examined under a microscope
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} to a rheumatologist, endocrinologist, neurologist or other specialist
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment aims to control inflammation, relieve symptoms and prevent organ damage rather than to cure the disease outright.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anti-inflammatory medicines:} NSAIDs and short courses of corticosteroids for flares
    \item \textbf{Disease-modifying drugs:} such as methotrexate or hydroxychloroquine, taken regularly to dampen the abnormal immune response
    \item \textbf{Biological therapies:} newer medicines that target specific parts of the immune system, given by injection or infusion
    \item \textbf{Organ-specific treatment:} for example, insulin in type 1 diabetes or thyroid hormone replacement in Hashimoto's disease
    \item \textbf{Allied health support:} physiotherapy, occupational therapy and dietetic advice
    \item \textbf{Regular monitoring:} blood tests and specialist reviews to check the disease and watch for medicine side effects
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Organ damage from ongoing inflammation, such as joint destruction, kidney disease or nerve damage
    \item Increased risk of cardiovascular disease and osteoporosis in several conditions
    \item Infections related to treatments that suppress the immune system
    \item Side effects of long-term corticosteroids, such as weight gain, high blood sugar and bone thinning
    \item Pregnancy complications in some conditions, requiring specialist planning
    \item The emotional and social impact of living with a chronic illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most autoimmune conditions are lifelong but manageable. With modern treatment, many people achieve good control of their symptoms and near-normal life expectancy. Some conditions respond well to a single treatment, such as a strict gluten-free diet in coeliac disease. Outcomes depend on the condition, how early it is diagnosed and how consistently treatment is followed. Relapses may still occur, and treatment is often adjusted over time.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Most autoimmune conditions cannot be prevented, but the following can reduce the risk of flares and complications.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Stop smoking and limit alcohol
    \item Protect skin from strong sunlight if you have a photosensitive condition
    \item Eat a balanced diet and stay physically active within the limits of your condition
    \item Take medicines as prescribed, even when you feel well
    \item Keep up to date with recommended vaccinations, asking your doctor which are safe for you
    \item Attend regular check-ups and report new symptoms early
    \item Manage stress, which is a common trigger for flares
\end{itemize}
