\chapter{Blackouts}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A blackout is a temporary loss of consciousness or awareness. The term covers several distinct phenomena: fainting (syncope), in which the brain briefly loses its blood supply; seizures, caused by abnormal electrical activity in the brain; and alcohol-related memory loss, in which a person remains awake but cannot recall events.

Syncope is by far the most common cause and is usually harmless, but a blackout can occasionally signal a serious heart, neurological, or metabolic problem. Because the underlying cause matters greatly, anyone who has experienced an unexplained blackout should be assessed by a doctor.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Blackouts are very common at all ages.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fainting:} affects up to 1 in 3 people at some point in life, with the first episode often occurring in adolescence or young adulthood
    \item \textbf{Age and sex patterns:} simple faints are most frequent in young women, while heart-related blackouts become increasingly common in older people
    \item \textbf{Seizures:} epilepsy affects roughly 1 in 100 people
    \item \textbf{Alcohol-related blackouts:} common among young people who drink heavily
    \item \textbf{Consequences:} falls and injuries from blackouts are a major cause of emergency department attendance, particularly in the elderly
\end{itemize}

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
A blackout occurs when the brain briefly loses its normal blood supply, its normal electrical activity, or its normal function.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vasovagal syncope (simple faint):} strong emotion, pain, prolonged standing, or heat triggers a sudden drop in heart rate and blood pressure, reducing blood flow to the brain
    \item \textbf{Situational faints:} triggered by coughing, urinating, straining, or swallowing
    \item \textbf{Orthostatic hypotension:} blood pressure falls sharply on standing, often in older people or those taking blood pressure medicines
    \item \textbf{Heart-rhythm problems:} arrhythmias such as a very slow or very fast heart rate can interrupt the blood supply to the brain
    \item \textbf{Structural heart disease:} conditions affecting the heart valves or the pumping function of the heart
    \item \textbf{Seizures:} abnormal electrical activity in the brain causes loss of awareness, often with jerking movements
    \item \textbf{Alcohol and drugs:} heavy drinking or drug use can cause memory loss or loss of consciousness
    \item \textbf{Metabolic and other causes:} low blood sugar, low oxygen, and some neurological disorders
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features of a blackout help to distinguish between its causes.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Syncope:} brief loss of consciousness with rapid recovery, often preceded by light-headedness, sweating, pallor, or nausea
    \item \textbf{Postural triggers:} blackouts that occur when standing up quickly or after standing still for a long time
    \item \textbf{Seizure features:} jerking or stiffening, tongue biting, loss of bladder control, confusion after waking, or a warning sensation beforehand
    \item \textbf{Heart-related clues:} palpitations or chest pain before collapse, blackouts during exertion, or a family history of sudden death
    \item \textbf{Injury:} some people hurt themselves when they fall
    \item \textbf{Alcohol blackout:} being awake and functioning but with a gap in memory for the drinking period
    \item \textbf{Aura or warning:} some people feel an unusual sensation just before losing awareness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vasovagal (simple) syncope versus orthostatic hypotension versus heart-rhythm causes
    \item Epileptic seizures versus non-epileptic (psychogenic) attacks
    \item Low blood sugar, which can cause confusion, sweating, and collapse in people with diabetes
    \item Stroke or transient ischaemic attack, especially with one-sided weakness or speech difficulty
    \item Alcohol or drug intoxication
    \item Concussion or other head injury causing loss of consciousness
    \item Sleep disorders such as narcolepsy, which can mimic a blackout
    \item Panic attacks or anxiety-related fainting
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor after any first unexplained blackout, if blackouts occur more than once, or if they happen during exercise. Older people and those with heart disease should be assessed promptly. Anyone with warning features before or during the blackout, or who was injured, also needs medical review.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A blackout with chest pain, palpitations, or during exertion, which may indicate a cardiac cause
    \item A seizure lasting more than five minutes, repeated seizures, or difficulty breathing after a seizure
    \item A blackout followed by confusion, weakness on one side, or difficulty speaking, which may indicate a stroke
    \item A blackout during pregnancy, with a severe headache, or with an irregular heart rhythm felt at the wrist
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detailed history:} the most important step, covering what happened before, during, and after the event, including eyewitness accounts
    \item \textbf{Examination:} lying and standing blood pressure, pulse, and a full heart and neurological examination
    \item \textbf{ECG:} a resting heart tracing to look for rhythm problems
    \item \textbf{Ambulatory monitoring:} a portable heart monitor worn for days or weeks to catch intermittent rhythm problems
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} for anaemia, blood sugar, and electrolyte disturbances
    \item \textbf{Echocardiogram:} ultrasound of the heart if structural disease is suspected
    \item \textbf{Tilt-table testing:} used for recurrent faints when vasovagal syncope is suspected
    \item \textbf{EEG:} recording of brain waves if seizures are suspected
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT or MRI of the brain when a neurological cause is considered
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on the cause identified.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vasovagal syncope:} reassurance, recognition of warning symptoms, sitting or lying down at the first sign, and maintaining good hydration
    \item \textbf{Orthostatic hypotension:} standing up slowly, avoiding dehydration, and reviewing blood pressure medicines
    \item \textbf{Heart-rhythm problems:} medicines, a pacemaker, or treatment of the underlying heart condition
    \item \textbf{Epilepsy:} antiseizure medicines prescribed and monitored by a specialist
    \item \textbf{Low blood sugar:} prompt glucose intake and review of diabetes management
    \item \textbf{Alcohol-related blackouts:} reducing or stopping alcohol and avoiding binge drinking
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} avoiding known triggers such as heat, prolonged standing, and dehydration
    \item \textbf{Driving advice:} anyone who has had a blackout may be required to stop driving temporarily; this is a legal requirement in many places and must be discussed with the doctor
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Injuries from falls, including fractures, head injury, and concussion
    \item Injury to others, for example if a blackout occurs while driving
    \item Aspiration or injury occurring during a seizure
    \item Repeated episodes causing fear, anxiety, and restriction of normal activity
    \item A missed serious cause, such as a cardiac arrhythmia or undiagnosed epilepsy
    \item Social consequences, including loss of driving privileges and impact on work
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the cause. Simple faints generally carry an excellent prognosis, and many people have only occasional episodes that do not affect health or life expectancy.

Heart-related blackouts can be more serious, but effective treatment of the underlying rhythm problem greatly improves the outlook. With treatment, most people with epilepsy become seizure-free or have very few episodes. Alcohol-related blackouts resolve when drinking stops. Anyone with an unexplained blackout should be assessed, because identifying the cause is the key to preventing harm.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item At the first warning feeling, sit or lie down and raise the feet, or put the head between the knees, to restore blood flow to the brain
    \item Drink enough fluid and avoid heat, prolonged standing, and dehydration
    \item Stand up slowly from lying or sitting, especially if you are older
    \item Avoid alcohol to the point of memory loss, and never combine it with drugs or sedatives
    \item If you are prone to faints, learn your triggers and avoid them where possible
    \item Take heart and blood pressure medicines as prescribed, and report dizziness to the doctor
    \item Manage diabetes carefully to avoid low blood sugar
    \item Follow the driving advice given by your doctor after any blackout
\end{itemize}
