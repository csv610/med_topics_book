\chapter{Dysmenorrhea}
\index{Dysmenorrhea}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dysmenorrhea is the medical term for painful periods, referring to cramping pain in the lower abdomen that occurs before or during menstruation. It is one of the most common gynaecological complaints and a leading cause of missed school and work among women of reproductive age. Primary dysmenorrhea is period pain without an underlying pelvic disease, while secondary dysmenorrhea is caused by conditions such as endometriosis, fibroids, or pelvic inflammatory disease. Effective treatment is available, and no woman should simply accept severe period pain as normal.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Dysmenorrhea affects a large proportion of menstruating women, with prevalence estimates ranging from 45 to 95 per cent, and it is severe in a significant minority. It is most common in adolescents and young women, and it tends to improve with age and after childbirth. Secondary dysmenorrhea becomes more common in the 30s and 40s as conditions such as endometriosis and fibroids become more prevalent. Despite its frequency, many women do not seek medical help, treating severe pain as a normal part of menstruation.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Period pain is caused by the contraction of the uterine muscle as it sheds its lining, with the intensity of the pain driven by chemicals called prostaglandins.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prostaglandins:} produced in high levels in the uterine lining before menstruation, these chemicals cause strong uterine contractions and reduce blood flow to the uterus, producing cramping pain
    \item \textbf{Primary dysmenorrhea:} occurs without any structural or disease-related cause, typically beginning within a few years of menarche
    \item \textbf{Secondary dysmenorrhea:} pain caused by an underlying condition such as endometriosis, adenomyosis, uterine fibroids, or pelvic inflammatory disease
    \item \textbf{Other factors:} heavy flow, early menarche, smoking, stress, and a family history of painful periods are associated with more severe pain
    \item \textbf{Nerve sensitisation:} in chronic pain states, the pelvic nerves become sensitised and the pain may persist beyond the period
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms typically begin just before or at the start of the period and last one to three days.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cramping, gripping pain in the lower abdomen, often radiating to the lower back and thighs
    \item A dull, continuous ache or sharp, spasmodic pain
    \item Nausea, vomiting, and diarrhoea during the period
    \item Headache, fatigue, and dizziness
    \item Pain that is relieved when the period finishes
    \item In secondary dysmenorrhea, pain may begin earlier, last longer, and be accompanied by heavy bleeding or pain during intercourse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Not all pelvic pain during menstruation is simple period pain.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Endometriosis, with pain that often begins before the period, lasts longer, and may be associated with infertility
    \item Adenomyosis, with heavy, painful periods in older women
    \item Uterine fibroids, which cause heavy bleeding and pain
    \item Pelvic inflammatory disease, with pain that is not confined to the period
    \item Irritable bowel syndrome and other gastrointestinal conditions
    \item Urinary tract and kidney problems causing lower abdominal pain
    \item Ectopic pregnancy in women of reproductive age
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a GP if period pain is severe, not relieved by over-the-counter treatment, interfering with school or work, or accompanied by heavy bleeding, pain between periods, or pain during intercourse. New, severe pain in older women also warrants assessment.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe abdominal or pelvic pain, particularly if pregnancy is possible
    \item Pain with heavy bleeding and faintness or dizziness
    \item Fever with pelvic pain, which may indicate infection
    \item Shoulder tip pain with pelvic pain and a positive pregnancy test, which may indicate ectopic pregnancy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis distinguishes primary from secondary dysmenorrhea.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} timing, character, and severity of pain, and its relationship to the menstrual cycle
    \item \textbf{Examination:} abdominal and pelvic examination to detect tenderness or masses
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test:} to exclude pregnancy in women of reproductive age
    \item \textbf{Pelvic ultrasound:} to detect fibroids, ovarian cysts, and adenomyosis
    \item \textbf{Laparoscopy:} the definitive investigation for endometriosis, in which a camera is passed into the pelvis
    \item \textbf{Trial of treatment:} a good response to NSAIDs and the contraceptive pill supports the diagnosis of primary dysmenorrhea
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Most cases of primary dysmenorrhea respond well to simple treatment.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{NSAIDs:} medicines such as ibuprofen and naproxen reduce prostaglandin production and are highly effective; they work best started at the first sign of pain or bleeding
    \item \textbf{Hormonal contraceptives:} the combined oral contraceptive pill, progestogen-only methods, and the hormonal IUD reduce period pain by thinning the uterine lining
    \item \textbf{Heat:} a hot water bottle or heat pack on the lower abdomen relaxes the uterine muscle
    \item \textbf{Exercise and relaxation:} regular physical activity and relaxation techniques can reduce pain
    \item \textbf{Treating the cause:} for secondary dysmenorrhea, treatment of endometriosis, fibroids, or other conditions
    \item \textbf{Other options:} transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) and acupuncture help some women
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} rarely, procedures to treat the underlying cause, such as removal of fibroids or endometriosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Missed school, work, and social activities, with significant economic and educational impact
    \item Reduced quality of life and chronic pelvic pain if untreated
    \item Undiagnosed underlying conditions such as endometriosis
    \item Dependence on strong painkillers if simple treatment is ineffective
    \item Emotional distress and anxiety about periods
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for dysmenorrhea is good. Most women with primary dysmenorrhea obtain effective relief with NSAIDs or hormonal contraception, and the pain tends to improve with age and after childbirth. Secondary dysmenorrhea improves when the underlying condition is treated. The key to a good outcome is seeking help early rather than accepting severe pain as normal.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep a symptom diary to understand the pattern of pain and response to treatment
    \item Take NSAIDs early, at the first sign of pain or bleeding, with food
    \item Apply heat to the lower abdomen during the period
    \item Exercise regularly throughout the month
    \item Practise relaxation and stress management
    \item Use a hormonal contraceptive if it is appropriate and wanted
    \item See a doctor if pain is not controlled by simple measures
\end{itemize}
