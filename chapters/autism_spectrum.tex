\chapter{Autism Spectrum}
\index{Autism Spectrum}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The autism spectrum describes a broad range of conditions that affect how a person communicates, relates to others and processes the world. People on the autism spectrum may understand and respond to social situations differently from what is typical, may have intense or focused interests, and may be more or less sensitive to sounds, lights, textures and movement. Because the features vary so widely from person to person, autism is described as a spectrum rather than a single condition. Many autistic people and advocates prefer to think of it as a natural variation in human brain function (neurodiversity) rather than an illness. Autistic people have many strengths, including attention to detail, honesty, deep knowledge in their areas of interest and strong visual skills.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Autism is diagnosed in roughly one in 70 to one in 100 people in many countries, and it is diagnosed more often in boys than in girls, although this may partly reflect differences in how the condition presents. It is found in all countries, cultures and income groups. The number of people diagnosed has risen considerably in recent decades, which is thought to reflect greater awareness, broader diagnostic criteria and better identification rather than an increase in the underlying condition. Many people with autism also have other conditions, such as intellectual disability, ADHD, anxiety, epilepsy or sleep problems, which commonly require separate support.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The causes of autism are not fully understood, but research points to a combination of genetic and early developmental factors that shape how the brain's networks form and connect.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} autism runs in families, and many genes are thought to contribute, usually together
    \item \textbf{Brain development:} differences in how brain cells connect and how networks communicate are found in autistic people
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy and birth factors:} a small number of cases are linked to complications around birth or certain conditions during pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Associated conditions:} some genetic syndromes (e.g.\ fragile X syndrome, tuberous sclerosis) include autism
    \item \textbf{Not caused by vaccines or parenting:} there is strong evidence that autism is not caused by vaccination or by how a child is raised
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features of autism appear in early childhood and are lifelong, but they look very different in different people and change across the lifespan.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Differences in social communication: difficulty reading facial expressions, tone of voice or unwritten social rules
    \item Difficulty starting or keeping conversations, or a preference for talking about specific interests
    \item Repetitive movements, routines or speech, such as hand-flapping or lining up objects
    \item Intense, focused interests that may dominate attention
    \item Strong preference for routine and distress at change or the unexpected
    \item Heightened or reduced sensitivity to sound, light, touch, taste or smell
    \item Strong sensory preferences, such as enjoying certain textures or avoiding others
    \item These features often coexist with strengths such as deep focus, reliability and creativity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Features that look like autism can also be explained by other conditions, which need to be considered during assessment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intellectual disability:} can occur with autism but also alone, with similar delays in communication
    \item \textbf{ADHD:} inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity can overlap with autistic traits
    \item \textbf{Language disorders:} delayed speech development without the social differences of autism
    \item \textbf{Social anxiety and selective mutism:} social avoidance driven by anxiety rather than autism
    \item \textbf{Hearing impairment:} poor response to sound or speech that mimics social difficulty
    \item \textbf{Attachment or trauma-related difficulties:} can affect social development in early childhood
    \item \textbf{Conditions with restricted interests:} such as obsessive-compulsive disorder
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Talk to a GP or child health nurse if a child does not respond to their name, does not smile back or point, has limited or unusual speech, loses skills they had gained, or has very restricted interests or repetitive behaviours. Adults who suspect they are autistic can also discuss assessment with a GP, who can arrange a referral.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A seizure or fit
    \item A serious injury or accident, including wandering away from home
    \item Sudden severe illness
    \item Any immediate threat to safety, including a mental health crisis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Autism is diagnosed through careful developmental and behavioural assessment rather than a blood test or scan. Assessment involves gathering information from many sources over time.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Developmental history:} detailed discussion of early milestones, play, speech and behaviour
    \item \textbf{Observation:} structured observation of how the person communicates and plays (e.g.\ ADOS assessment)
    \item \textbf{Information from others:} reports from parents, teachers and carers about everyday behaviour
    \item \textbf{Questionnaires:} standardised checklists and rating tools completed by families and schools
    \item \textbf{Hearing and vision tests:} to rule out sensory problems that could explain the difficulties
    \item \textbf{Medical assessment:} by a paediatrician, psychiatrist or clinical psychologist experienced in autism
    \item \textbf{Associated testing:} for hearing, speech, cognition or other conditions where needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
There is no cure for autism, and management is not about changing who a person is. Instead, the goal is to support development, learning and quality of life, and to help with the difficulties that can accompany autism.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Early intervention:} supportive programmes that build communication, play and daily-living skills
    \item \textbf{Speech pathology:} for communication and language, including alternative ways of communicating
    \item \textbf{Occupational therapy:} for sensory differences, coordination and everyday skills
    \item \textbf{Psychology:} support with emotions, anxiety, social skills and behaviour
    \item \textbf{Education support:} adjustments and planning with schools to help learning
    \item \textbf{Family support:} information, coaching and respite for parents and carers
    \item \textbf{Medication:} not for autism itself, but sometimes for coexisting problems such as severe anxiety or ADHD
    \item \textbf{national disability-support scheme funding:} supports for many people with autism to access services
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Autism itself is not a complication, but some difficulties are more common in autistic people and can be reduced with good support.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Anxiety, depression and low mood, especially in adolescents and adults
    \item Bullying and social isolation at school or work
    \item Difficulty with transitions, such as starting school or leaving home
    \item Epilepsy, which affects a minority of autistic people
    \item Sleep problems and feeding difficulties in some children
    \item High demand on families, with financial and emotional strain
    \item Use of unproven "treatments" that can be harmful if advice is not sought from trusted professionals
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Outcomes for autistic people vary greatly and depend more on the person's supports than on the diagnosis itself. Many autistic adults live independently, study, work and build families and friendships. Early support, understanding environments and acceptance are associated with better wellbeing, while anxiety and other coexisting conditions are the strongest influences on quality of life. Some autistic people need substantial daily support throughout life. With the right adjustments, autistic people contribute widely across the arts, sciences, technology and everyday communities.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Autism cannot be prevented, because it is largely determined before and around birth. The important work is in supporting wellbeing and reducing avoidable hardship.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek assessment early if autism is suspected, so supports can start sooner
    \item Build routines and predictability, and prepare for changes in advance
    \item Identify sensory triggers and create calming spaces at home and school
    \item Support the person's interests and strengths rather than discouraging them
    \item Provide clear, concrete communication, and check understanding
    \item Look after the wellbeing of parents and carers; ask for help and respite
    \item Be cautious of unproven therapies; discuss any proposed treatment with a trusted clinician
    \item Learn about autism from reputable organisations and autistic people themselves
\end{itemize}
