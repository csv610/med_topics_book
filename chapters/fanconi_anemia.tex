\chapter{Fanconi Anaemia}
\index{Fanconi Anaemia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Fanconi anaemia is a rare inherited disorder of the body's ability to repair deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), the genetic material in every cell. Because damaged DNA cannot be repaired properly, affected individuals have bone marrow failure, an increased risk of cancer, and a range of physical birth defects. Despite the name, the anaemia is only one feature of the condition, and the disorder is best understood as a chromosomal instability syndrome. Fanconi anaemia is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern, meaning a child must receive a faulty gene from both parents. There is no cure, but advances in bone marrow transplantation and supportive care have transformed outcomes, and many affected people now survive into adulthood. This chapter explains the condition, how it is diagnosed, and how it is managed over a lifetime.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Fanconi anaemia is very rare, affecting roughly 1 in 130,000 to 1 in 300,000 people worldwide. It is found across all ethnic groups, although certain populations with high rates of consanguineous marriage or founder mutations, such as Ashkenazi Jewish communities and some South African populations, have higher carrier frequencies. Approximately one in every 200 to 300 people carries a faulty gene for the condition without knowing it. Because many affected children die young or are misdiagnosed, the true incidence is likely underestimated. Improved recognition and genetic testing are gradually identifying more cases, including adults who reach diagnosis after developing cancer or bone marrow problems.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Fanconi anaemia is caused by mutations in any of more than 20 genes that cooperate in a single DNA repair pathway known as the FA/BRCA pathway.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic defect:} the faulty genes prevent cells from repairing the type of DNA damage that occurs during normal cell division, particularly damage that links the two strands of the DNA helix together
    \item \textbf{Chromosomal instability:} unrepaired damage leads to chromosome breaks and rearrangements, which are visible on specialised testing and are the hallmark of the condition
    \item \textbf{Bone marrow failure:} haematopoietic stem cells accumulate damage and die prematurely, so the marrow progressively fails to produce red cells, white cells, and platelets, typically beginning in childhood
    \item \textbf{Cancer predisposition:} the same instability drives malignant change, producing an elevated risk of acute myeloid leukaemia, myelodysplasia, and solid tumours such as squamous cell carcinomas of the head, neck, and genital tract
    \item \textbf{Developmental effects:} faulty DNA repair disturbs embryonic development, causing skeletal, skin, and organ malformations in some affected children
    \item \textbf{Inheritance:} autosomal recessive in nearly all cases, with each parent an unaffected carrier of one faulty gene copy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features of Fanconi anaemia are highly variable, even within families, and range from severe childhood disease to mild adult-onset presentations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive bone marrow failure: fatigue, pallor, infections, and bruising or bleeding from low blood counts, usually appearing between 5 and 10 years of age
    \item Physical birth defects in around two-thirds of affected children: short stature, thumb and forearm abnormalities, skin pigmentation changes such as café-au-lait spots, and small head size
    \item Malformations of the heart, kidneys, and gastrointestinal tract in some individuals
    \item Learning difficulties and behavioural problems in a minority
    \item Early-onset cancers, particularly leukaemia and squamous cell carcinomas of the mouth, throat, and reproductive tract, often at much younger ages than in the general population
    \item Endocrine problems in later life, including low thyroid function and reduced fertility
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the features overlap with several other conditions, testing is needed to confirm the diagnosis.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Other bone marrow failure syndromes:} Diamond--Blackfan anaemia, Shwachman--Diamond syndrome, and dyskeratosis congenita also cause low blood counts in childhood
    \item \textbf{Aplastic anaemia:} immune-mediated marrow failure can mimic the blood picture
    \item \textbf{Other chromosome breakage syndromes:} such as ataxia-telangiectasia and Bloom syndrome
    \item \textbf{Thalidomide or other teratogen effects} when limb defects are the presenting feature
    \item \textbf{VACTERL association and other multi-malformation patterns} when birth defects predominate
    \item \textbf{Hereditary cancer syndromes} in adults presenting with early cancer and no blood problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents should see a doctor if a child has unexplained bruising, bleeding, pallor, recurrent infections, unusual birth defects, or short stature, especially when more than one family member is affected. Adults with a family history of the condition should seek genetic counselling before planning a family.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe bleeding or uncontrolled bruising in a person known to have low platelet counts
    \item High fever or signs of serious infection, which can escalate rapidly when white cell counts are low
    \item Sudden collapse or seizure, which may indicate intracranial bleeding
    \item Any sudden severe symptom in a person undergoing intensive treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis requires specialist haematology and genetics services.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full blood count and blood film:} detects low red cells, white cells, and platelets and their progression over time
    \item \textbf{Chromosome breakage test:} the definitive diagnostic test, in which the patient's blood cells are exposed to a DNA cross-linking agent and the resulting chromosome breaks are counted; cells from people with Fanconi anaemia show excessive breakage
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} identifies the specific faulty gene and enables carrier testing of family members and prenatal or preimplantation diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Bone marrow biopsy:} assesses the severity of marrow failure and excludes leukaemia or myelodysplasia
    \item \textbf{Surveillance tests:} annual blood counts, and regular examinations for mouth, throat, and genital cancers from adolescence
    \item \textbf{Organ imaging:} renal ultrasound, cardiac echo, and other tests to detect birth defects at diagnosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is delivered by a specialised multidisciplinary team and tailored to the individual's features.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood product support:} red cell and platelet transfusions for symptomatic cytopenias
    \item \textbf{Growth factors:} medicines such as androgens (oxymetholone) stimulate blood production and can delay the need for transplantation
    \item \textbf{Bone marrow transplantation:} the only curative treatment for marrow failure, ideally performed early using a matched sibling or unrelated donor and a reduced-intensity conditioning regimen, because standard chemotherapy is too toxic for these patients
    \item \textbf{Cancer surveillance and treatment:} regular screening and prompt treatment of leukaemia and solid tumours, using modified protocols to minimise DNA-damaging therapy
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} management of endocrine deficiencies, growth, nutrition, and educational support
    \item \textbf{Genetic counselling:} for affected individuals and carriers, covering inheritance risks and family planning options
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive bone marrow failure requiring lifelong transfusion support if transplantation is not possible
    \item Acute myeloid leukaemia and myelodysplasia
    \item Squamous cell carcinomas of the head, neck, and anogenital region, sometimes recurring
    \item Toxicity from standard chemotherapy and radiotherapy, which these patients tolerate poorly
    \item Graft-versus-host disease and other transplant complications
    \item Endocrine disorders, growth failure, infertility, and reduced life expectancy without successful treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for Fanconi anaemia has improved markedly. Without treatment, bone marrow failure and leukaemia usually prove fatal in the second or third decade. With successful bone marrow transplantation, long-term survival of 70--80\% or more is achievable in children transplanted early from well-matched donors. Even after transplant, lifelong surveillance for cancer is essential, because the elevated risk of solid tumours persists. For adults with mild disease, monitoring and cancer screening can allow a relatively normal life. Research into gene therapy, which repairs the faulty gene in the patient's own stem cells, offers hope for a future cure.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek genetic counselling and carrier testing if there is a family history of the condition or of unexplained childhood marrow failure
    \item Consider preimplantation genetic diagnosis or prenatal testing in at-risk pregnancies
    \item Attend all scheduled blood count monitoring and cancer surveillance appointments
    \item Protect skin from sun and report any new mouth, throat, or skin lesion promptly
    \item Maintain good dental and oral hygiene, and avoid tobacco and alcohol to reduce head and neck cancer risk
    \item Access supportive services, including psychology, social work, and patient support organisations
    \item Have a plan for urgent medical care, including how to reach emergency services quickly
\end{itemize}
