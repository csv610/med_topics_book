\chapter{Bedsores (Pressure Ulcers)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bedsores, also called pressure ulcers or pressure injuries, are areas of damaged skin and underlying tissue caused by prolonged pressure on the skin, usually over a bony part of the body. They occur most often in people who are confined to a bed or a chair for long periods and cannot move or change position easily. Pressure ulcers range from mild reddening of the skin to deep wounds that reach muscle and bone. They are largely preventable with good positioning, regular repositioning, and skin care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Pressure ulcers are common in hospitals, nursing homes, and people receiving care at home. Around 1 in 10 hospital inpatients develops a pressure injury, and the risk rises sharply with age and immobility. People with spinal cord injury, stroke, hip fracture, or advanced dementia are particularly affected, as are people with reduced sensation or poor nutrition. The sacrum, heels, hips, and elbows are the most frequently affected sites. Pressure ulcers increase length of hospital stay and are associated with significant cost and suffering.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pressure:} sustained pressure over a bony prominence compresses small blood vessels, blocking blood flow to the skin and tissues
    \item \textbf{Shear:} sliding forces (for example, sliding down in a bed or chair) stretch and damage the skin and the tissues beneath
    \item \textbf{Friction:} rubbing of the skin against bedding or clothing removes the outer skin layer
    \item \textbf{Tissue damage:} without blood flow, cells die; the damage begins deep and extends to the surface, which is why small visible wounds may hide large deeper injuries
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} immobility, reduced sensation, poor nutrition and hydration, moisture from incontinence or sweat, older age, and chronic illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A persistent area of redness or discoloration on the skin that does not go pale when pressed (blanching test)
    \item Skin that feels warm, firm, or different from the surrounding skin
    \item A shallow open wound, blister, or broken skin
    \item A deeper wound extending into fat, muscle, or bone
    \item Black, hardened, or dead tissue (eschar) over the wound
    \item Pain or, in advanced cases with nerve damage, surprisingly little pain
    \item Most common over the sacrum (tailbone), heels, hips, shoulder blades, and elbows
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Moisture-associated skin damage from incontinence, sweat, or wound drainage
    \item Contact dermatitis from dressings, creams, or continence products
    \item Skin tears or bruises from friction or trauma
    \item Cellulitis or other skin infections
    \item Diabetic foot ulcers or vascular (arterial or venous) leg ulcers
    \item Burn or chemical injury to the skin
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical advice as soon as any pressure area appears, especially if the skin does not return to normal colour when pressure is removed, or if the skin is broken, discolored, or painful.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A wound that is deep, spreading, or exposing muscle or bone
    \item Signs of infection: spreading redness, warmth, swelling, pain, or pus
    \item Fever, chills, or feeling generally unwell
    \item Black, dead-looking tissue in the wound
    \item A wound that is not healing after a few weeks of appropriate care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} assessment of the person's mobility, sensation, nutrition, and continence, and inspection of all pressure-prone areas
    \item \textbf{Staging:} pressure ulcers are graded from stage 1 (non-blanching redness) to stage 4 (full-thickness tissue loss reaching muscle or bone); unstageable wounds are covered with dead tissue
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} tools such as the Braden scale are used in care settings to identify people at risk and plan prevention
    \item \textbf{Wound swab and culture:} taken if infection is suspected
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} may include full blood count and inflammatory markers if infection is a concern
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} ultrasound, CT, or MRI may be used for deep or suspected bone infection (osteomyelitis)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Relief of pressure:} frequent repositioning (every two hours), special pressure-relieving mattresses, cushions, and careful positioning techniques
    \item \textbf{Wound care:} regular cleaning, appropriate dressings, and removal of dead tissue (debridement) by trained staff
    \item \textbf{Management of moisture:} keep the skin clean and dry; treat incontinence with suitable products
    \item \textbf{Nutrition:} adequate protein, calories, and fluids to support healing
    \item \textbf{Infection treatment:} antibiotics for infected wounds, and surgical drainage of deep collections if needed
    \item \textbf{Pain management:} appropriate analgesia, especially during dressing changes
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} for advanced ulcers, skin grafts or flap reconstruction may be considered
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy and mobilisation where possible to improve movement and reduce further pressure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cellulitis and soft-tissue infection
    \item Osteomyelitis -- infection of the underlying bone, which is difficult to treat
    \item Sepsis -- infection spreading through the bloodstream, a serious complication
    \item Chronic non-healing wounds that persist for months or years
    \item Amputation, in rare cases of severe deep infection
    \item Prolonged hospital stay, reduced independence, and increased care burden
    \item Psychological distress, pain, and reduced quality of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Early-stage pressure ulcers often heal within weeks with good repositioning and skin care, and many deeper ulcers improve with consistent treatment. Healing can be slow, particularly in older or frail people and those with poor nutrition or advanced illness. Recurrence is common without ongoing preventive care. Deep pressure ulcers, especially with bone infection or sepsis, are serious and may threaten life in frail patients. Prevention and early treatment are the keys to a good outcome.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Change position at least every two hours when in bed and every 30 minutes when seated; use lifts and helpers as needed
    \item Use pressure-relieving mattresses, heel protectors, and cushions for chairs and wheelchairs
    \item Inspect the skin daily, especially the sacrum, heels, and hips, looking for redness or early damage
    \item Keep the skin clean, dry, and moisturised; manage incontinence promptly
    \item Avoid dragging the skin when repositioning; lift rather than slide
    \item Maintain good nutrition and hydration, with adequate protein
    \item Keep moving and mobilise as much as possible; a carer should support regular activity
    \item Report any area of redness or broken skin to a nurse or doctor immediately
\end{itemize}
