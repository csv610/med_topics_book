\chapter{Health Problems Present from Birth}
\index{Health Problems Present from Birth}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Health problems present from birth, also called congenital conditions, are conditions that exist at the time a baby is born. They range from minor anatomical variations that need no treatment to serious conditions affecting the heart, brain, kidneys, or other organs. Congenital conditions can be caused by genetic changes, chromosomal abnormalities, infections during pregnancy, exposure to medicines or substances, or they can have no identifiable cause. Around 3--5\% of babies are born with a significant congenital condition. Some are detected before birth by antenatal screening, some are found at newborn checks, and others appear later. Advances in diagnosis and treatment mean many children with congenital conditions now live full, healthy lives.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Congenital conditions affect around 3--5\% of live births in many countries, making them a leading cause of infant illness and death. The most common significant types include congenital heart defects (around 1 in 100 births), neural tube defects such as spina bifida, cleft lip and palate, and chromosomal conditions such as Down syndrome (around 1 in 700 births). Congenital anomalies are a leading cause of infant death, but survival has improved dramatically, with more than 90\% of affected infants now surviving to adulthood with modern care.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Congenital conditions arise from genetic, environmental, or unknown factors acting during development.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chromosomal abnormalities:} changes in chromosome number or structure, such as Down syndrome and Turner syndrome
    \item \textbf{Single-gene disorders:} inherited or new mutations, such as cystic fibrosis and achondroplasia
    \item \textbf{Multifactorial conditions:} the interaction of multiple genes with environmental factors, such as cleft palate and many heart defects
    \item \textbf{Infections in pregnancy:} rubella, cytomegalovirus, and other infections can damage the developing baby
    \item \textbf{Medicines and substances:} alcohol (fetal alcohol spectrum disorder), some medicines, and illicit drugs
    \item \textbf{Maternal health:} poorly controlled diabetes, epilepsy, and other conditions increase risk
    \item \textbf{Unknown causes:} for many congenital conditions, no cause is identified
    \item \textbf{Timing:} most structural defects develop in the first trimester, when organs are forming
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features of congenital conditions depend on the organs affected.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Heart problems: murmurs, breathlessness, poor feeding, and blue colouration
    \item Brain and spine problems: developmental delay, seizures, and spina bifida
    \item Kidney and urinary problems: urinary infections, poor growth, and abnormal scans
    \item Gastrointestinal problems: feeding difficulties, vomiting, and bowel obstruction
    \item Limb and skeletal conditions: differences in limbs, fingers, or spine
    \item Facial differences, including cleft lip and palate
    \item Hearing and vision problems
    \item Metabolic conditions, which can cause lethargy, vomiting, and poor growth
    \item Many conditions are detected by antenatal ultrasound before symptoms appear
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Assessment distinguishes congenital conditions from other causes of symptoms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Acquired conditions that develop after birth, including infections and injury
    \item Developmental delay from environmental or other causes
    \item Prematurity-related complications
    \item Normal variations that are mistaken for anomalies
    \item Inherited versus new (de novo) genetic changes
    \item Conditions caused by birth-related events
    \item Functional conditions without a structural cause
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Antenatal care, newborn checks, and routine paediatric visits detect congenital conditions early. Parents should seek medical review for any concerns about feeding, growth, development, breathing, or colour in their baby.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A baby with difficulty breathing, blue colouration, or poor feeding
    \item A baby who is lethargic, difficult to wake, or having seizures
    \item Repeated vomiting or signs of bowel obstruction
    \item A baby with a fever or signs of serious illness
    \item Sudden collapse or loss of responsiveness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis may occur before or after birth.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antenatal screening:} ultrasound, combined screening, and non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) assess the risk of chromosomal and structural conditions
    \item \textbf{Prenatal diagnosis:} amniocentesis and chorionic villus sampling provide diagnostic genetic testing
    \item \textbf{Newborn examination:} a full physical check identifies many conditions
    \item \textbf{Newborn screening:} bloodspot testing detects a range of treatable metabolic and genetic conditions
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} ultrasound, echocardiography, and MRI assess structure and function
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} chromosome analysis and gene panels identify genetic causes
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} paediatricians and subspecialists manage the condition
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is tailored to the specific condition and its severity.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observation and monitoring:} many minor conditions need no treatment, only follow-up
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} corrects structural problems such as heart defects, cleft palate, and bowel obstruction
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} treat symptoms and complications, including infections and metabolic problems
    \item \textbf{Special diets:} essential for many metabolic conditions
    \item \textbf{Therapy:} physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and speech therapy support development
    \item \textbf{Education support:} early intervention and school assistance
    \item \textbf{Genetic counselling:} helps families understand the condition and its inheritance
    \item \textbf{Multidisciplinary care:} specialists, nurses, and allied health work together
    \item \textbf{Family support:} practical, emotional, and financial support services
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Complications of the condition itself, including organ failure and developmental delay
    \item Infections and other illnesses related to the condition
    \item Surgical complications
    \item Effects on growth, learning, and independence
    \item Psychological and financial burden on families
    \item Long-term health needs into adulthood
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for congenital conditions has improved enormously. Many conditions are fully correctable or manageable, and children with serious conditions such as congenital heart disease and Down syndrome now have much longer, healthier lives than in the past. Early detection, specialised treatment, and family support are the keys to the best outcomes. For families, genetic counselling, peer support, and clear information make a substantial difference.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take folic acid before conception and in early pregnancy to reduce neural tube defects
    \item Attend antenatal care and all recommended screening
    \item Avoid alcohol, tobacco, and harmful substances in pregnancy
    \item Manage chronic conditions such as diabetes before and during pregnancy
    \item Complete immunisations, including rubella and flu, before and during pregnancy
    \item Attend newborn checks and screening
    \item Seek early review of any concerns about your baby's health or development
    \item Connect with support organisations for your child's condition
    \item Seek genetic counselling if there is a family history of a congenital condition
\end{itemize}
