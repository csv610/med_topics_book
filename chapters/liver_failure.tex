\chapter{Liver failure}
\index{Liver failure}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Liver failure is a severe, life-threatening clinical syndrome resulting from the catastrophic impairment of hepatocyte function or massive hepatic necrosis. It occurs when the liver is no longer able to sustain its vital metabolic, synthetic, immunological, and excretory duties. Liver failure is classified into two distinct forms: acute liver failure (ALF) and chronic liver failure. Acute liver failure is defined as the rapid development of severe liver injury, coagulopathy (international normalised ratio [INR] $\ge 1.5$), and hepatic encephalopathy within 26 weeks in an individual without pre-existing cirrhosis. Chronic liver failure occurs as the terminal event of progressive, long-standing hepatic parenchymal disease (cirrhosis) and is characterised by episodic or persistent decompensation, portal hypertension, and multi-organ dysfunction.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Chronic liver failure is a major global healthcare burden, responsible for over one million deaths annually. Globally, the primary drivers are chronic hepatitis B and C infections, alcohol-related liver disease, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD, formerly known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease). In many countries, chronic liver failure is increasingly associated with MASLD and alcohol misuse, mirroring trends in other developed countries. In contrast, acute liver failure is rare, with an incidence of approximately 1 to 6 cases per million people annually. In many countries, the United Kingdom, and North America, accidental or intentional paracetamol overdose is the leading cause of ALF, accounting for over 50\% of cases, whereas viral hepatitis remains the predominant cause in developing regions.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The pathways leading to liver failure involve direct hepatocyte destruction, immunologically mediated damage, or severe vascular congestion. Key causes and pathophysiological factors include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Paracetamol toxicity:} The most common cause of acute liver failure. Excess paracetamol saturates normal conjugation pathways, leading to metabolism via cytochrome P450 into the toxic intermediate N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI), which depletes glutathione and causes oxidative necrosis of hepatocytes.
    \item \textbf{Viral hepatitis:} Infection with hepatotropic viruses (primarily hepatitis A, B, and E) causing severe, immune-mediated destruction of infected hepatocytes.
    \item \textbf{Alcohol-related liver disease:} Chronic alcohol consumption leads to steatosis, cellular injury, and fibrotic remodelling, which impairs blood flow through the hepatic sinusoids and leads to functional insufficiency.
    \item \textbf{Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH):} Lipotoxicity from chronic lipid accumulation drives inflammation and progressive fibrosis, culminating in decompensated cirrhosis.
    \item \textbf{Drug-induced liver injury (DILI):} Idiosyncratic reactions to prescription medications (such as anti-tuberculosis agents, antiepileptics, and certain antibiotics) or herbal and dietary supplements.
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune hepatitis:} An autoimmune disorder where T-cell-mediated destruction targets hepatocytes, causing progressive chronic failure or, rarely, acute presentation.
    \item \textbf{Budd-Chiari syndrome:} Hepatic venous outflow obstruction leading to severe post-sinusoidal portal hypertension and congested, ischaemic liver necrosis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features of liver failure reflect the loss of synthetic capacity, impaired detoxification, and the development of portal hypertension. The clinical features include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Jaundice:} Yellow discoloration of the skin, sclera, and mucous membranes due to hyperbilirubinaemia from impaired hepatic conjugation and excretion.
    \item \textbf{Hepatic encephalopathy:} Altered mental status ranging from mild cognitive impairment, personality changes, and asterixis (flapping tremor), to severe confusion, somnolence, and hepatic coma. This is primarily caused by blood-borne neurotoxins, particularly ammonia, bypassing the liver and crossing the blood-brain barrier.
    \item \textbf{Coagulopathy:} Easy bruising, petechiae, epistaxis, and prolonged bleeding from puncture sites due to the failure of hepatocyte synthesis of clotting factors (factors II, VII, IX, and X).
    \item \textbf{Ascites and peripheral oedema:} Accumulation of fluid within the peritoneal cavity and lower extremities, driven by portal hypertension, splanchnic vasodilation, and hypoalbuminaemia.
    \item \textbf{Hyperdynamic circulation and portal hypertension:} Hyperdynamic state (low systemic vascular resistance, tachycardia) combined with clinical signs of portal hypertension, including splenomegaly, caput medusae, and gastrointestinal haemorrhage from gastro-oesophageal varices.
    \item \textbf{Pruritus:} Intense, generalized itching due to the accumulation of bile acids and other pruritogens in the skin, typical in chronic biliary failure.
    \item \textbf{Fetor hepaticus:} A sweet, musty breath odour caused by the accumulation of volatile sulfur compounds (dimethyl sulfide) in the blood.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Liver failure must be differentiated from other systemic illnesses that present with altered consciousness, fluid overload, or jaundice:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Decompensated heart failure:} Severe right-sided heart failure causing hepatic congestion, hepatomegaly, and ascites, but distinguished by elevated jugular venous pressure, cardiomegaly, and absence of primary coagulopathy.
    \item \textbf{Sepsis with intrahepatic cholestasis:} Systemic infection causing hyperbilirubinaemia and altered mental state, but with preserved hepatic synthetic function (normal or near-normal INR and albumin).
    \item \textbf{Gilbert's syndrome:} A benign, inherited metabolic condition causing isolated unconjugated hyperbilirubinaemia, distinguished by completely normal liver enzymes, normal INR, and the absence of encephalopathy or ascites.
    \item \textbf{Biliary obstruction:} Choledocholithiasis or pancreatic neoplasm causing jaundice and pain, but characterised by biliary duct dilation on imaging and initially preserved synthetic parameters.
    \item \textbf{Uraemic encephalopathy:} Altered mental status in patients with severe renal failure, distinguished by elevated urea and creatinine levels and normal liver enzyme profiles.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients with a history of liver disease or those experiencing sudden-onset jaundice should seek immediate medical evaluation. Any suspected paracetamol overdose requires emergency medical attention, regardless of early symptoms.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Haematemesis (vomiting blood) or melaena (passing black, tarry stools).
    \item Acute onset of confusion, severe lethargy, or extreme drowsiness.
    \item Severe abdominal pain, particularly if accompanied by fever and chills.
    \item Difficulty breathing or rapid, shallow respiration.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Establishing the presence and severity of liver failure requires laboratory testing, imaging, and sometimes histopathological examination:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Liver function tests (LFTs):} Demonstrates hyperbilirubinaemia, hypoalbuminaemia, and elevated serum transaminases (AST and ALT), which are often extremely high ($>1000$ U/L) in acute liver injury but may be normal or low in end-stage chronic failure due to loss of hepatocyte mass.
    \item \textbf{Coagulation profile:} Demonstrates prolonged prothrombin time (PT) and elevated INR, reflecting deficient clotting factor synthesis.
    \item \textbf{Serum ammonia:} Elevated levels help support the diagnosis of hepatic encephalopathy and correlate with the risk of cerebral oedema in acute liver failure.
    \item \textbf{Arterial lactate:} Elevated levels reflect tissue hypoperfusion and anaerobic metabolism, serving as a strong prognostic indicator in acute liver failure.
    \item \textbf{Abdominal ultrasound with Doppler:} Evaluates liver size, nodularity, presence of ascites, and checks for patency or thrombosis in the portal and hepatic veins.
    \item \textbf{Screening for aetiology:} Toxicology screens (including serum paracetamol levels), viral hepatitis serologies (HAV IgM, HBsAg, HBcAb IgM, HCV RNA), and autoimmune markers (ANA, SMA, IgG).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management requires close monitoring, often in an intensive care unit (ICU), to address multi-system organ failure and manage the underlying cause:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Specific antidotes:} Intravenous N-acetylcysteine (NAC) is administered immediately for paracetamol overdose and has also shown benefits in early-stage non-paracetamol acute liver failure.
    \item \textbf{Ammonia-lowering therapies:} Lactulose administered orally or via nasogastric tube/enemas to trap ammonia in the bowel, combined with rifaximin to reduce urea-splitting gut flora.
    \item \textbf{Fluid and metabolic management:} Maintain haemodynamic stability with intravenous fluids, support blood glucose levels with continuous dextrose infusions (to prevent severe hypoglycaemia), and correct electrolyte abnormalities.
    \item \textbf{Management of portal hypertension and ascites:} Low-sodium diet, aldosterone antagonists (spironolactone) combined with loop diuretics (frusemide), and therapeutic paracentesis for symptomatic ascites.
    \item \textbf{Haemorrhage control:} Prophylactic vasoactive therapy (e.g. octreotide or terlipressin) and urgent endoscopic band ligation for gastro-oesophageal variceal bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Liver transplantation:} Immediate evaluation for emergency liver transplantation (using criteria such as the King's College Criteria) for patients with acute liver failure, or elective listing for decompensated chronic liver failure.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The complications of liver failure are systemic and carry high mortality:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cerebral oedema and intracranial hypertension:} Accumulation of fluid in the brain parenchyma in acute liver failure, leading to brain herniation and death.
    \item \textbf{Hepatorenal syndrome (HRS):} Acute kidney injury secondary to severe renal vasoconstriction triggered by splanchnic vasodilation and systemic haemodynamic changes.
    \item \textbf{Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP):} Bacterial translocation across the gut wall causing infection of ascites fluid, presenting with fever and abdominal pain.
    \item \textbf{Infections and sepsis:} Severe immune dysfunction leads to a high susceptibility to bacterial and fungal infections.
    \item \textbf{Severe coagulopathy and haemorrhage:} Life-threatening gastrointestinal variceal bleeding or intracranial haemorrhage.
    \item \textbf{Metabolic derangements:} Severe hypoglycaemia, lactic acidosis, and electrolyte disturbances (hyponatraemia, hypokalaemia).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of liver failure depends heavily on the speed of onset, the underlying cause, and eligibility for transplantation. Acute liver failure without transplantation has a poor prognosis, with historical mortality rates of 50--80\% depending on the aetiology; however, paracetamol-induced ALF has a transplant-free survival rate exceeding 60--70\% if N-acetylcysteine is initiated early. Chronic liver failure carries a progressive, guarded prognosis. Once a patient with cirrhosis experiences a decompensating event (e.g. ascites, variceal bleed, or encephalopathy), the median survival drops to approximately two years without a liver transplant. Successful liver transplantation provides an excellent long-term outcome, with 5-year survival rates exceeding 80--85\%.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventative actions and lifestyle measures are vital to protect the liver and prevent decompensation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Alcohol cessation:} Complete abstinence from alcohol is mandatory for patients with any form of liver disease to halt the progression of injury.
    \item \textbf{Safe medication usage:} Strictly adhere to dosing guidelines for paracetamol (maximum 4 grams per 24 hours in healthy adults; less in chronic liver disease) and avoid over-the-counter herbal remedies.
    \item \textbf{Immunisation:} Ensure vaccination against hepatitis A and hepatitis B, particularly for those with existing liver disease.
    \item \textbf{Metabolic health optimization:} Maintain a healthy body weight through a balanced, low-sodium diet and regular physical exercise to prevent and manage MASLD/MASH.
    \item \textbf{Avoidance of high-risk foods:} Avoid eating raw shellfish, which can carry hepatitis A or \textit{Vibrio vulnificus}, a bacterium that can cause rapid, fatal sepsis in patients with liver damage.
    \item \textbf{Regular clinical surveillance:} Attend scheduled ultrasounds and blood tests for early detection of hepatocellular carcinoma and oesophageal varices.
\end{itemize}
