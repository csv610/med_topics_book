\chapter{Hereditary Multiple Exostoses (HME)}
\index{Hereditary Multiple Exostoses (HME)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hereditary multiple exostoses (HME), also known as multiple hereditary osteochondromas or diaphyseal aclasis, is a rare inherited condition in which multiple benign bony growths called osteochondromas develop on the bones. These growths typically appear during childhood and continue to develop until the growth plates close in adolescence. Most osteochondromas cause no problems, but they can cause pain, deformity, restricted movement, and pressure on nerves, blood vessels, or tendons. There is a small lifelong risk that a growth becomes malignant. Treatment is mainly symptomatic, with surgery for troublesome lesions.

\section*{Epidemiology}
HME affects approximately one in 50,000 people. It is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern, so children of an affected parent have a 50\% chance of inheriting the condition, although about 10--20\% of cases arise from new mutations. It affects boys and girls equally but is more severe in males. The number of exostoses varies widely, from a few to dozens. The lifetime risk of malignant transformation of an osteochondroma to chondrosarcoma is around 1--3\%.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} mutations in the EXT1 or EXT2 genes, which are important for normal bone development
    \item \textbf{Inheritance:} autosomal dominant, with new mutations accounting for a minority of cases
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} the gene changes disrupt cartilage formation at the growth plate, causing cartilage-capped bony outgrowths
    \item \textbf{Growth pattern:} exostoses grow during childhood and stop growing when the growth plates close at skeletal maturity
    \item \textbf{Sites:} most common at the ends of the long bones of the arms and legs, and on the ribs and pelvis
    \item \textbf{Effects:} growths can deform bones, shorten limbs, and compress adjacent structures
    \item \textbf{Malignant change:} a growth that continues growing after skeletal maturity, or becomes painful, may indicate chondrosarcoma
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hard, painless lumps near the ends of bones, noticed in childhood
    \item The lumps are usually bilateral and multiple
    \item Pain, especially with activity or when a growth presses on a nerve
    \item Restricted joint movement when growths are near joints
    \item Limb deformity, including bowing of the forearm and short stature
    \item Pressure effects: nerve compression, blood vessel problems, or tendon irritation
    \item A growth that grows after puberty or becomes painful warrants urgent assessment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Solitary osteochondroma, which is not inherited and carries minimal risk
    \item Other benign bone tumours, such as enchondroma and osteoid osteoma
    \item Malignant tumours, including chondrosarcoma and osteosarcoma
    \item Other skeletal dysplasias causing deformity and short stature
    \item Imaging and biopsy distinguish these where needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Children with known or suspected HME should be followed by an orthopaedic specialist. Seek review for new pain, rapid growth of a lesion, neurological symptoms, or restricted movement.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe pain, swelling, or deformity near a growth
    \item Numbness, weakness, or loss of function in a limb
    \item Signs of a fracture through a growth
    \item Collapse or symptoms suggesting spinal cord compression
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} multiple characteristic bony lumps in a child with a family history
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} confirm the diagnosis and map the exostoses
    \item \textbf{MRI:} assesses cartilage caps and pressure on soft tissues
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} confirms the diagnosis and enables family counselling
    \item \textbf{Surveillance imaging:} for growths that change or cause symptoms
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} for suspected malignant change
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observation:} most exostoses need no treatment; regular review monitors growth
    \item \textbf{Pain management:} analgesia, activity modification, and physiotherapy
    \item \textbf{Surgical removal:} for painful lesions, nerve or vessel compression, deformity, or suspicious growth
    \item \textbf{Deformity correction:} osteotomy for significant angular deformity
    \item \textbf{Leg length management:} monitoring and treatment of length discrepancy
    \item \textbf{Malignancy surveillance:} lifelong awareness and prompt investigation of any change
    \item \textbf{Genetic counselling:} for affected families planning children
    \item \textbf{Support:} information and peer support for families
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Nerve compression with pain, numbness, and weakness
    \item Blood vessel compression, rarely causing ischaemia
    \item Joint restriction, tendon irritation, and bursitis
    \item Deformity, limb length discrepancy, and short stature
    \item Fracture through an exostosis
    \item Malignant transformation to chondrosarcoma in 1--3\%, most often in adulthood
    \item Psychological impact of visible lumps and functional limitations
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
HME is a lifelong condition, but most people function well. Many exostoses cause no symptoms, and surgery deals effectively with those that do. The growths stop growing at skeletal maturity, after which symptoms often stabilise. The main long-term concern is the small risk of malignant change, which is why ongoing surveillance is recommended. With orthopaedic follow-up and prompt attention to change, the outlook for most people with HME is good.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend orthopaedic follow-up in childhood and adolescence
    \item Report new pain, rapid growth, or neurological symptoms promptly
    \item Maintain muscle strength and fitness with physiotherapy support
    \item Use protective equipment for sports to reduce injury risk
    \item Monitor lesions in adulthood and seek review of any change
    \item Seek genetic counselling before having children
    \item Connect with support organisations for families affected by HME
    \item Keep medical records documenting the condition
\end{itemize}
