\chapter{Nipah virus}
\index{Nipah virus}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Nipah virus (NiV) is a highly pathogenic, zoonotic paramyxovirus that causes severe and frequently fatal disease in both humans and animals. The natural reservoir hosts of Nipah virus are fruit bats of the genus \textit{Pteropus} (commonly known as flying foxes). In humans, infection manifests across a broad clinical spectrum, ranging from asymptomatic infection and acute influenza-like respiratory illness to severe, necrotising encephalitis and acute respiratory distress syndrome. First identified in 1999 during a large outbreak among pig farmers in Malaysia and Singapore, Nipah virus is classified as a Biosafety Level 4 (BSL-4) agent. Due to its high case-fatality rate, potential for person-to-person transmission, and the absence of widely available vaccines or targeted therapeutic agents, it is recognised by the World Health Organization as a priority pathogen requiring urgent research and development.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Outbreaks of Nipah virus occur periodically in South and Southeast Asia, showing distinct geographic and transmission patterns. The initial outbreak in Malaysia and Singapore (1998--1999) involved over 265 human cases and was characterised by direct contact with infected agricultural pigs, carrying a case-fatality rate of approximately 40\%. In contrast, subsequent annual or biennial outbreaks in Bangladesh and seasonal outbreaks in eastern India (such as in Kerala) have shown significantly higher case-fatality rates, frequently ranging between 70\% and 100\%. In these South Asian regions, transmission is predominantly linked to the consumption of raw date palm sap contaminated with the saliva, urine, or faeces of infected fruit bats, alongside substantial person-to-person transmission within households and healthcare facilities. Although no cases have originated in many countries, native local \textit{Pteropus} species carry related henipaviruses (such as Hendra virus), making regional surveillance and strict quarantine measures essential for public health security.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Nipah virus is an enveloped, single-stranded, negative-sense RNA virus belonging to the genus Henipavirus within the family Paramyxoviridae. The pathogenic process of Nipah virus infection involves several key mechanisms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral entry and cell receptor binding:} The virus targets host cells by binding its attachment glycoprotein (G) to ephrin-B2 and ephrin-B3, which are highly conserved cell-surface proteins expressed on endothelial cells, neurons, and glial cells.
    \item \textbf{Systemic endothelial infection:} Following entry, the virus replicates within endothelial cells, leading to systemic vasculitis, endothelial cell syncytia formation, microvascular thrombosis, and subsequent ischaemic tissue necrosis in multiple organ systems.
    \item \textbf{Central nervous system invasion:} Neuroinvasion occurs via haematogenous dissemination across the compromised blood-brain barrier and through retrograde axonal transport along the olfactory nerve, causing diffuse encephalitis, perivascular cuffing, and extensive neuronal destruction.
    \item \textbf{Pulmonary pathology:} Direct infection of pulmonary endothelial and epithelial cells triggers severe inflammatory responses, leading to interstitial pneumonia, alveolar haemorrhage, and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).
    \item \textbf{Transmission dynamics:} Zoonotic transmission occurs through direct contact with infected pigs or other domestic animals, or through ingestion of bat-contaminated food products. Human-to-human transmission is mediated by direct contact with infectious respiratory droplets, secretions, or bodily fluids.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Following an incubation period of 4 to 14 days (which can occasionally extend up to 45 days), Nipah virus infection presents with a rapid progression of symptoms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prodromal symptoms:} Sudden onset of high fever, severe headache, myalgia, sore throat, dizziness, and profound fatigue.
    \item \textbf{Respiratory symptoms:} Non-productive cough, progressive dyspnoea, tachypnoea, and signs of severe respiratory distress.
    \item \textbf{Acute neurological features:} Altered mental status, acute confusion, disorientation, somnolence, and signs of meningeal irritation such as neck stiffness and photophobia.
    \item \textbf{Severe encephalitic signs:} Generalized or focal seizures, myoclonus, cerebellar ataxia, focal neurological deficits, and rapid progression to coma within 24 to 48 hours.
    \item \textbf{Autonomic dysfunction:} Fluctuations in blood pressure, sinus tachycardia, and hyperpyrexia, indicating brainstem involvement.
    \item \textbf{Late-onset and relapsing encephalitis:} Reappearance of acute neurological deficits or convulsions months or even years after recovery from the initial infection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The initial presentation of Nipah virus requires differentiation from other causes of acute encephalitis and severe respiratory illness in endemic and travel-related settings:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Japanese encephalitis:} A mosquito-borne viral encephalitis prevalent in Asia, differentiated by specific serological assays (IgM detection) and vector exposure history.
    \item \textbf{Hendra virus infection:} A closely related Henipavirus infection endemic to some countries, associated with close contact with infected horses rather than pigs or fruit bats.
    \item \textbf{Herpes simplex virus (HSV) encephalitis:} The most common cause of sporadic encephalitis globally, characterised by temporal lobe localization on neuroimaging and PCR of cerebrospinal fluid.
    \item \textbf{Rabies:} A lyssavirus infection presenting with distinct features of hydrophobia, aerophobia, and autonomic hyperactivity, almost always linked to a history of animal bites.
    \item \textbf{Bacterial meningitis:} Presents with acute fever and meningism, characterised by a purulent cerebrospinal fluid profile with elevated neutrophils, low glucose, and positive Gram stain or culture.
    \item \textbf{Cerebral malaria:} Caused by \textit{Plasmodium falciparum} infection, diagnosed through peripheral blood film examination showing parasitised red blood cells.
    \item \textbf{Melioidosis:} A bacterial infection caused by \textit{Burkholderia pseudomallei} that can manifest as severe pneumonia and systemic sepsis, diagnosed via bacterial culture or PCR.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Immediate medical assessment is required for any individual presenting with acute fever accompanied by respiratory distress or neurological symptoms, especially if they have travelled to endemic areas, had contact with ill pigs or bats, or consumed raw date palm sap.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, rapid breathing, or blue-coloured lips and face
    \item Confusion, severe drowsiness, or difficulty waking up
    \item Seizures, convulsions, or sudden loss of consciousness
    \item Rapidly deteriorating neurological state or sudden weakness in the limbs
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Due to the high risk of transmission and laboratory-acquired infection, diagnostic testing must be coordinated with public health authorities and processed in Biosafety Level 4 (BSL-4) facilities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Molecular testing (RT-PCR):} The primary diagnostic method during the acute phase, using throat or nasal swabs, urine, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), or blood to detect Nipah virus RNA.
    \item \textbf{Serology (ELISA):} Used to detect Nipah virus-specific IgM and IgG antibodies in serum or CSF, typically positive during the second week of illness or in recovering patients.
    \item \textbf{Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis:} Performed cautiously to rule out other pathogens; typically shows lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein levels, and normal glucose.
    \item \textbf{Neuroimaging (MRI):} Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain frequently demonstrates multiple, small, discrete, T2-weighted hyperintense lesions in the cerebral white matter, representing microinfarcts and areas of active vasculitis.
    \item \textbf{Viral isolation:} Attempted only in high-containment BSL-4 laboratories from blood, CSF, or tissue samples.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is primarily supportive and focused on intensive care, as there are currently no approved vaccines or specific antiviral therapies for humans:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} Standard intensive care support, including mechanical ventilation for respiratory failure, fluid and electrolyte management, and cardiovascular support for autonomic instability.
    \item \textbf{Anticonvulsant therapy:} Administration of anti-epileptic medications (e.g., levetiracetam, sodium valproate) to control seizures and prevent status epilepticus.
    \item \textbf{Monoclonal antibodies:} The monoclonal antibody m102.4, which targets the Henipavirus G glycoprotein, has been administered on a compassionate-use basis and is undergoing clinical evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Experimental antivirals:} Ribavirin has been used in some clinical settings with mixed outcomes, while the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase inhibitor remdesivir has shown efficacy in animal models.
    \item \textbf{Infection control and isolation:} Immediate isolation in negative-pressure rooms, using strict personal protective equipment (PPE) with airborne, droplet, and contact precautions to prevent healthcare-associated transmission.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Permanent neurological deficits, including cognitive impairment, personality changes, motor weakness, and cranial nerve palsies
    \item Relapsing or late-onset encephalitis, occurring months or years after the initial infection, which carries a very high mortality rate
    \item Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and severe pulmonary haemorrhage
    \item Secondary bacterial infections, such as ventilator-associated pneumonia and catheter-related septicaemia
    \item Persistent neuropsychiatric disorders, including clinical depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbances
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Nipah virus infection carries a high mortality rate, with case-fatality rates ranging between 40\% and 100\% depending on the viral strain, local healthcare infrastructure, and the speed of clinical intervention. Approximately 20\% to 30\% of survivors suffer from persistent neurological sequelae, such as seizure disorders, cognitive decline, or physical disability. A unique and concerning feature is the potential for late-onset or relapsing encephalitis, which can occur in patients who had mild or even asymptomatic initial infections, presenting suddenly and often proving fatal.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Avoid raw date palm sap:} Refrain from drinking raw date palm sap, as fruit bats feed on the sap and contaminate the collection containers.
    \item \textbf{Wash and peel fruit:} Meticulously wash and peel all fresh fruits before consumption, and discard any fruits that show signs of animal bites or scratches.
    \item \textbf{Avoid bat contact:} Stay away from areas where bats are known to roost, and avoid touching sick or dead bats or climbing trees frequented by bats.
    \item \textbf{Implement veterinary biosecurity:} Screen pig pens to prevent contact with bats, quarantine sick animals, and practice regular disinfection of farms.
    \item \textbf{Practise hand hygiene:} Regularly wash hands with soap and water or use alcohol-based hand sanitisers, particularly after handling livestock or visiting sick relatives.
    \item \textbf{Use personal protective equipment (PPE):} Healthcare workers and carers must wear full PPE, including N95/P2 respirators, eye protection, gowns, and gloves when handling suspected or confirmed cases.
\end{itemize}
