\chapter{Neutropenia}
\index{Neutropenia}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Educational information; seek professional advice for individual care.}

\section*{What it is}
Neutropenia means that the blood contains fewer neutrophils than expected. Neutrophils help fight infection, so the risk depends on how low the count is, how long it lasts, and the person’s other health conditions. Causes include medicines, cancer treatment, viral illness, nutritional deficiency, autoimmune disease, and bone-marrow disorders.

\section*{Symptoms and assessment}
Neutropenia itself may cause no symptoms. Fever, chills, sore throat, mouth ulcers, cough, breathlessness, diarrhoea, pain when passing urine, or redness around a wound may indicate infection. Assessment usually includes a full blood count with differential, repeat testing, medication review, examination, and targeted tests for the cause or an infection.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
If a person known to have significant neutropenia develops a fever or feels suddenly unwell, they should contact their treating team or emergency service immediately. Do not wait for symptoms to become severe or self-treat a fever without advice; infection can progress quickly even when few local signs are present.

\section*{Treatment}
Treatment depends on the cause and severity. It may include treating an infection promptly, changing a causative medicine, managing an underlying illness, nutritional replacement, or specialist treatment such as a growth factor. Antibiotics and other medicines should be selected by a clinician.

\section*{Reducing infection risk}
Follow the care team’s advice about hand hygiene, food safety, dental care, wounds, and contact with people who are unwell. Keep scheduled blood tests and carry information about the condition and medicines. Do not assume that avoiding all people or foods is necessary unless the treating team advises it.
