\chapter{Molluscum contagiosum}
\index{Molluscum contagiosum}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Molluscum contagiosum is a common, highly contagious viral skin infection caused by the molluscum contagiosum virus (MCV), a member of the Poxviridae family. The condition is characterised by the development of small, firm, dome-shaped papules on the skin that typically feature a central dimple or umbilication. While molluscum contagiosum is generally a benign, self-limiting condition that resolves spontaneously without leaving scars, it can cause mild pruritus, cosmetic concern, and secondary bacterial infection. Transmission occurs primarily through direct skin-to-skin contact, sexual contact, or contact with contaminated fomites (such as towels or bath toys).

\section*{Epidemiology}
Molluscum contagiosum is a cosmopolitan infection with a worldwide distribution, though it is most prevalent in warm, humid climates and areas with overcrowded living conditions. It primarily affects three populations: children under the age of 10 (with a peak incidence between 2 and 5 years), sexually active young adults (where it presents as a sexually transmitted infection), and immunocompromised individuals (particularly those with advanced HIV infection, who often present with giant, atypical, or treatment-resistant lesions). In many countries, it is a very common childhood skin disease, often circulating within primary schools, childcare centres, and public swimming pools.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The pathogenesis of molluscum contagiosum involves viral colonisation of the epidermis and evasion of the host immune response.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral pathogen:} Molluscum contagiosum virus (MCV) is a large, double-stranded DNA poxvirus, with MCV-1 being the most common subtype isolated in clinical cases, followed by MCV-2.
    \item \textbf{Direct contact transmission:} The virus enters the skin through minor microabrasions, often facilitated by scratching or wet skin (swimming).
    \item \textbf{Autoinoculation:} Individuals can spread the virus to other areas of their own body (autoinoculation) by scratching, rubbing, or shaving over active papules.
    \item \textbf{Epidermal hyperplasia:} The virus replicates within the cytoplasm of keratinocytes in the stratum spinosum, inducing rapid cellular division and the formation of characteristic intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies (Henderson-Paterson bodies).
    \item \textbf{Immune evasion:} MCV encodes proteins that inhibit the host's inflammatory response and interferon pathways, allowing the lesions to persist for months without triggering an immune reaction.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The infection is characterised by distinctive dermatological lesions that undergo evolutionary changes over several months.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Characteristic papules:} Firm, dome-shaped, pearly or flesh-coloured papules measuring 2--5 mm in diameter, containing a central umbilication from which a white, waxy core can be expressed.
    \item \textbf{Anatomical distribution:} In children, lesions are commonly found on the trunk, limbs, face, and axillae; in adults, they are typically located in the genital region, lower abdomen, and inner thighs.
    \item \textbf{Pruritus and eczema:} Mild itching is common, and a localised eczematous reaction (molluscum dermatitis) may develop around the lesions.
    \item \textbf{Erythematous changes:} The lesions may become red, swollen, and tender immediately prior to spontaneous resolution, a phenomenon known as the "beginning of the end" sign.
    \item \textbf{Atypical lesions:} Immunocompromised patients may present with giant lesions (greater than 10 mm in diameter) or hundreds of widespread papules.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Molluscum contagiosum must be differentiated from other benign and malignant cutaneous lesions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Verruca vulgaris:} Common warts which have a rough, hyperkeratotic surface and lack a central umbilication.
    \item \textbf{Varicella:} Chickenpox vesicles are typically fluid-filled, pruritic, and present with systemic symptoms like fever.
    \item \textbf{Condylomata acuminata:} Genital warts which have a cauliform, irregular surface, as opposed to smooth, umbilicated papules.
    \item \textbf{Folliculitis:} Inflammatory pustules centred around hair follicles, typically lacking the pearly appearance and central core of molluscum.
    \item \textbf{Milium:} Small, benign keratinous cysts commonly seen on the face, which are hard and lack umbilication.
    \item \textbf{Cutaneous fungal infections:} In immunocompromised patients, disseminated fungal infections (such as cryptococcosis) can produce cutaneous lesions that closely mimic molluscum.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
A medical consultation is advisable if the diagnosis is uncertain, if the lesions are extensive, causing significant cosmetic distress, or if there are signs of secondary infection.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of severe, systemic bacterial infection (septicaemia) complicating secondary cellulitis, such as high fever, confusion, rapid breathing, or a spreading red rash.
    \item Anaphylaxis or severe breathing difficulty if an allergic reaction to a topical treatment occurs.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is predominantly clinical, based on the classic appearance of the lesions, and investigations are rarely required.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} Visual inspection of the skin surface to identify the characteristic pearly, umbilicated papules.
    \item \textbf{Dermoscopy:} High-magnification skin inspection showing a central orifice with amorphous yellow-white structures and surrounding crown-like blood vessels.
    \item \textbf{Histopathological examination:} In atypical cases, a skin biopsy reveals hyperplastic lobules of squamous epithelium containing large, ovoid, intracytoplasmic Henderson-Paterson inclusion bodies.
    \item \textbf{Polymerase chain reaction (PCR):} Can be used to detect MCV DNA in research or highly specialised clinical contexts.
    \item \textbf{Sexual health screening:} Recommended for adults presenting with lesions in the anogenital region to rule out concurrent sexually transmitted infections.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
In healthy individuals, a watch-and-wait approach is often preferred. However, active treatment may be initiated to reduce transmission, alleviate discomfort, or resolve cosmetic concerns.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Expectant management:} Educating the patient and parents that the condition is self-limiting and will resolve naturally without intervention, usually within 6 to 18 months.
    \item \textbf{Physical destruction:} Cryotherapy (liquid nitrogen), curettage, or manual expression of the central waxy core under local anaesthetic.
    \item \textbf{Topical chemical agents:} Application of salicylic acid, potassium hydroxide, podophyllotoxin, or cantharidin (applied by a clinician) to induce localised irritation and immune activation.
    \item \textbf{Immunomodulators:} Topical imiquimod cream to stimulate the local immune response, particularly in older children or adults.
    \item \textbf{Secondary infection treatment:} Topical or oral antibiotics (e.g., flucloxacillin) to treat secondary bacterial cellulitis or impetiginisation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While molluscum contagiosum is generally benign, several complications can arise during the course of the infection.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Secondary bacterial infection:} Introduction of pathogens like Staphylococcus aureus or Streptococcus pyogenes due to scratching, leading to impetigo or cellulitis.
    \item \textbf{Severe scarring:} Pitting or depigmentation of the skin, which is more likely to occur if lesions are aggressively squeezed, scratched, or treated surgically.
    \item \textbf{Ocular complications:} Unilateral conjunctivitis or keratitis if lesions develop on the eyelids.
    \item \textbf{Disseminated disease:} Development of hundreds of giant lesions in immunocompromised individuals.
    \item \textbf{Psychosocial distress:} Anxiety, embarrassment, and social isolation due to the visible nature of the lesions, especially in teenagers and young adults.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The overall prognosis is excellent, as the infection is entirely self-limiting in immunocompetent individuals. Individual lesions persist for about 2 to 3 months, but because new lesions can develop via autoinoculation, the total duration of the disease typically ranges from 6 to 18 months, though some cases may persist for up to 3 years. Spontaneous clearance occurs once the host immune system successfully recognises the virus, marked by transient inflammation of the papules. Lesions generally heal without scarring unless secondary infection or excessive trauma has occurred. In patients with compromised immune systems, the prognosis is guarded, with lesions often persisting indefinitely unless the underlying immunodeficiency is corrected.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventative strategies focus on reducing the spread of the virus to other areas of the body and to other individuals.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Avoid scratching or picking:} Refrain from touching or squeezing the papules to prevent autoinoculation and secondary bacterial infection.
    \item \textbf{Keep lesions covered:} Cover visible papules with clothing or waterproof bandages, especially before participating in contact sports or swimming.
    \item \textbf{Good hand hygiene:} Wash hands thoroughly with soap and water after touching any affected areas of skin.
    \item \textbf{Do not share personal items:} Avoid sharing towels, clothing, hairbrushes, or bath toys with others.
    \item \textbf{Barrier precautions:} Use condoms during sexual activity if lesions are present in the genital region, although they may not cover all affected areas.
\end{itemize}
