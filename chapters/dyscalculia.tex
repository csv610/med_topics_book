\chapter{Dyscalculia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dyscalculia is a specific learning difficulty that affects a person's ability to understand, learn, and perform mathematics. It involves persistent difficulty with number concepts, arithmetic, estimation, and mathematical reasoning that is out of proportion to the person's overall intelligence and education. Dyscalculia is sometimes called "maths dyslexia." It is a lifelong condition, but with early identification, appropriate teaching strategies, and support, people with dyscalculia can learn to manage their difficulties and develop useful mathematical skills.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Dyscalculia affects an estimated 3 to 6 per cent of school-aged children, with some studies suggesting rates as high as 7 per cent. It is roughly as common as dyslexia, with which it frequently coexists, and it also commonly occurs alongside attention deficit hyperactivity disorder. Dyscalculia is often under-recognised, and many children and adults struggle for years without a diagnosis, attributing their difficulties to being "bad at maths."

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The cause of dyscalculia is not fully understood, but research points to differences in how the brain processes numbers.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} dyscalculia runs in families, suggesting a hereditary component
    \item \textbf{Brain differences:} imaging studies show altered activity and structure in brain regions involved in number processing, particularly the intraparietal sulcus
    \item \textbf{Coexisting conditions:} dyslexia, ADHD, and other learning difficulties commonly coexist
    \item \textbf{Environmental factors:} poor maths teaching and anxiety about mathematics can compound the difficulty, though they do not cause it
    \item \textbf{Working memory:} difficulties holding and manipulating numerical information in mind contribute to the problem
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The signs of dyscalculia vary with age but persist across the lifespan.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty counting, recognising numbers, and understanding what numbers mean
    \item Trouble learning and recalling number facts, such as times tables
    \item Difficulty with mental arithmetic and estimating quantities
    \item Confusing symbols such as plus, minus, and equals
    \item Trouble understanding place value and working with fractions and decimals
    \item Difficulty with telling the time, reading clocks, and handling money
    \item Problems with sequences, such as following recipes or directions
    \item Difficulty with measurement, distance, and judging speed
    \item Maths anxiety, avoidance of maths tasks, and low self-esteem
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Mathematical difficulties have several possible causes that should be considered.
\begin{itemize}
    \item General intellectual disability or global learning difficulties
    \item Dyslexia, which may affect word problems and instruction comprehension
    \item Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, affecting concentration during maths tasks
    \item Anxiety, particularly maths anxiety, which can impair performance
    \item School absence or poor teaching, which can leave gaps in knowledge
    \item Acquired acalculia, a loss of mathematical skill after brain injury or stroke
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents who notice persistent, significant difficulty with mathematics in their child should discuss their concerns with teachers, the school's learning support team, and a GP. Formal assessment by a psychologist or educational specialist is important to distinguish dyscalculia from other causes and to plan support.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden loss of mathematical skills or other new learning difficulties in an adult, which may indicate a stroke or other brain condition
    \item Sudden confusion, weakness, or speech problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis of dyscalculia is made through comprehensive assessment.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Educational history:} reports of progress, effort, and performance in mathematics
    \item \textbf{Cognitive assessment:} intelligence testing by a psychologist to identify the pattern of strengths and weaknesses
    \item \textbf{Standardised maths tests:} instruments that assess number sense, calculation, and mathematical reasoning
    \item \textbf{Screening for coexisting conditions:} assessment for dyslexia, ADHD, and anxiety
    \item \textbf{Hearing and vision checks:} to exclude sensory problems affecting learning
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is educational and psychological, focused on teaching strategies and building confidence.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Specialist teaching:} structured, multisensory maths programmes that build understanding step by step
    \item \textbf{Concrete materials:} using objects, visual aids, and real-life examples to make numbers tangible
    \item \textbf{Memory aids:} charts, calculators, and apps that reduce the burden of recalling number facts
    \item \textbf{Accommodations:} extra time in exams, and adjustments to maths tasks at school and university
    \item \textbf{Addressing maths anxiety:} counselling and gradual exposure to maths tasks in supportive settings
    \item \textbf{Practical skills training:} teaching money, time, and measurement skills relevant to daily life
    \item \textbf{Support for adults:} workplace adjustments and assistance with numeracy skills
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Low self-esteem, frustration, and avoidance of maths-related activities
    \item Reduced educational and career opportunities
    \item Difficulties with money management, budgeting, and everyday numeracy
    \item Anxiety and school refusal in severe cases
    \item Misattribution of difficulties to laziness or low intelligence
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Dyscalculia is a lifelong condition, but with early identification and appropriate teaching most people make meaningful progress and develop the numeracy skills they need for daily life. The goal of intervention is not necessarily to eliminate the difficulty but to equip the person with strategies, aids, and confidence. Many adults with dyscalculia lead successful lives, particularly when they receive understanding and practical support.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek assessment early if a child shows persistent difficulty with numbers
    \item Use practical, everyday maths: cooking, shopping, and budgeting build skills naturally
    \item Use calculators and apps without shame; they are legitimate tools
    \item Break maths tasks into small steps
    \item Advocate for school accommodations and support
    \item Focus on strengths and interests alongside the maths difficulty
    \item Seek psychological support for maths anxiety if it is severe
\end{itemize}
