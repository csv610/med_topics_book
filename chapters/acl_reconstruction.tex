\chapter{ACL Reconstruction}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
ACL reconstruction is an operation to rebuild a torn anterior cruciate ligament, the ligament in the middle of the knee that prevents the shin bone (tibia) from sliding too far forward on the thigh bone (femur). The damaged ligament is replaced with a graft: a piece of tendon taken from the patient's own body (an autograft, usually from the kneecap, hamstring, or quadriceps tendon) or, less commonly, from a deceased donor (an allograft).

The aim is to restore knee stability so that the knee no longer gives way and the person can return to an active life. Reconstruction is not needed for everyone with an ACL tear; the decision is made with the surgeon after weighing age, activity level, the degree of instability, and associated injuries such as meniscal tears.

\section*{Epidemiology}
ACL reconstruction is one of the most common orthopaedic operations, with hundreds of thousands performed worldwide each year. It is most often carried out in adolescents and young adults, because this group is most likely to return to high-demand sports such as football, netball, basketball, rugby, and skiing.

The rate of surgery has increased alongside greater participation in pivoting sports, and women, who have higher rates of ACL injury in these sports, make up a substantial share of those undergoing reconstruction. Around one-third of all ACL reconstructions occur in people under the age of 25, and re-injury rates remain a concern in this group.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The operation is performed because a torn ACL cannot heal usefully on its own. When the ligament ruptures, usually during a sudden stop, awkward landing, or twisting injury, the knee loses its main restraint against the tibia sliding forward. If the resulting instability persists, repeated giving way can damage the menisci and joint cartilage, increasing the risk of later osteoarthritis.

The graft acts as a scaffold, fixed into bone tunnels drilled at the ligament's attachment points. Over many months it becomes incorporated into the surrounding bone and develops its own blood supply in a process called ligamentisation. The graft is weakest a few months after surgery, before this process is complete, which is why rehabilitation must be carefully graduated and returning to sport delayed until healing has occurred.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Before surgery, there is usually a history of an ACL tear with the knee giving way or feeling unstable, sometimes with recurrent swelling and pain. Surgery is generally deferred until the knee has full movement, is no longer swollen, and the muscles have been conditioned through pre-operative rehabilitation.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Post-operative pain and swelling:} expected around the knee for the first few weeks
    \item \textbf{Bruising:} often spreads around the knee and down the upper calf
    \item \textbf{Crutches:} needed for the first weeks while weight-bearing is protected
    \item \textbf{Stiffness:} reduced range of motion, especially in fully straightening the knee
    \item \textbf{Muscle weakness:} the thigh muscles waste while the knee is protected
    \item \textbf{Recovery milestones:} a gradual return to weight-bearing and full movement over weeks to months
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
After surgery, a number of problems can be confused with the normal recovery process and need distinguishing.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Graft failure (re-tear) versus normal post-operative laxity or stiffness
    \item Wound infection versus the normal inflammatory response around the incisions
    \item Deep vein thrombosis versus ordinary calf pain and swelling
    \item Knee stiffness (arthrofibrosis) versus normal post-operative restriction of movement
    \item Pain from the graft harvest site versus pain from the reconstruction itself
    \item Meniscal or cartilage problems present before surgery versus new symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
After surgery, contact the surgeon or seek urgent review if there is heavy bleeding, a fever, spreading redness or pus from an incision, severe pain not controlled by the prescribed medicines, or a calf that is increasingly swollen, warm, or painful. Also report chest pain or breathlessness, which can rarely indicate a blood clot.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe chest pain or difficulty breathing
    \item A swollen, painful calf together with shortness of breath (possible blood clot)
    \item Heavy, uncontrolled bleeding from the surgical wounds
    \item Loss of consciousness or sudden collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative assessment:} examination of the knee, including stability tests such as the Lachman test and pivot-shift test
    \item \textbf{MRI:} confirms the ACL tear and shows any meniscal or other damage that needs attention at the same operation
    \item \textbf{X-ray:} checks the alignment of the knee and excludes fractures
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative rehabilitation:} conditioning the knee before surgery improves outcomes and speeds recovery
    \item \textbf{Post-operative review:} regular appointments to monitor healing, movement, and strength
    \item \textbf{Further imaging:} MRI may be repeated if graft failure, infection, or stiffness is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative preparation:} physiotherapy to regain full movement and strength before surgery
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} the graft is fixed in place, usually with keyhole (arthroscopic) techniques; meniscal tears are repaired at the same time where possible
    \item \textbf{Pain management:} pain-relieving medicines combined with local anaesthetic techniques around the knee
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} a structured physiotherapy programme in stages, from protecting the graft through regaining movement and strength, to sport-specific training
    \item \textbf{Return to sport:} usually permitted around 9 to 12 months after surgery, once strength, stability, and functional tests are passed
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} ongoing review with the surgeon and physiotherapist to guide each stage of recovery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Graft failure or re-tear, sometimes from returning to sport too early
    \item Infection within the joint or around the wounds
    \item Deep vein thrombosis, usually in the calf
    \item Stiffness of the knee (arthrofibrosis), sometimes requiring further treatment
    \item Pain or weakness at the graft harvest site, with temporary numbness around the knee
    \item An increased long-term risk of osteoarthritis of the knee
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people who have ACL reconstruction regain a stable knee and return to their previous level of sport, with around 9 in 10 reporting good or excellent results in the years after surgery. The best outcomes are seen in younger, active patients who complete their full rehabilitation programme.

Full recovery takes the best part of a year, and the quality of rehabilitation matters as much as the surgery itself in determining the result. A small number of people re-injure the graft or the opposite knee, particularly adolescents returning to sport. Even after a successful reconstruction, the knee carries a higher long-term risk of arthritis, so lifelong muscle conditioning and sensible activity levels are advised.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Complete the full rehabilitation programme before returning to sport
    \item Build and maintain strength in the thigh, hamstring, and hip muscles on both sides
    \item Practise safe jumping, landing, and cutting techniques during sport
    \item Attend all follow-up appointments and report new symptoms promptly
    \item Watch for signs of blood clots (calf swelling and pain) and of infection (fever, redness, discharge)
\end{itemize}
