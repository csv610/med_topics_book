\chapter{Epidural Injection for Chronic Pain Relief}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An epidural injection for chronic pain is a procedure in which medicine is injected into the epidural space around the spinal cord to relieve pain, most commonly pain radiating from the spine into the legs. The injection usually contains a corticosteroid to reduce inflammation and sometimes a small amount of local anaesthetic. It is used most often for pain caused by a herniated disc or spinal stenosis, and it is one option within a broader pain management plan. The injection is performed by a specialist and is usually done as a day procedure.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Spinal pain, including sciatica and pain from spinal stenosis, is extremely common and a leading cause of disability. Most people recover with conservative care, but a significant minority have persistent pain that affects their lives. Epidural steroid injections are among the most commonly performed procedures in pain medicine, and they provide meaningful relief for a substantial proportion of people with nerve-related back and leg pain.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The injection targets inflammation around the spinal nerves.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The cause of pain:} a herniated disc, bone spurs, or narrowing of the spinal canal can compress or irritate the spinal nerves, causing inflammation and pain radiating down the leg
    \item \textbf{The epidural space:} the space around the spinal cord and nerve roots, where the inflammation sits
    \item \textbf{Corticosteroids:} reduce inflammation around the affected nerve
    \item \textbf{Local anaesthetic:} provides immediate but temporary pain relief
    \item \textbf{How it helps:} reducing inflammation relieves the nerve irritation and allows the pain to settle
    \item \textbf{Not a cure:} the injection treats the inflammation but does not repair the disc, so it is used alongside physiotherapy and exercise
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Epidural injections are considered for particular patterns of pain.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain radiating from the lower back down one leg (sciatica)
    \item Pain from spinal stenosis, with numbness and weakness on walking
    \item Neck pain radiating into the arm
    \item Pain that has not improved after a period of conservative treatment
    \item The injection is less useful for isolated, mechanical back pain without nerve involvement
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of back and leg pain must be considered.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Muscular and ligamentous back strain
    \item Facet joint arthritis
    \item Sacroiliac joint pain
    \item Hip joint arthritis, which can mimic sciatica
    \item Peripheral nerve compression, such as in the leg
    \item Serious causes requiring urgent attention, including infection, fracture, and cauda equina syndrome
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with persistent nerve-related back pain should be assessed by a GP and referred to a pain specialist or musculoskeletal service. Urgent care is needed for any loss of bowel or bladder control, weakness in the legs, or sudden severe pain.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Loss of bladder or bowel control, or numbness in the saddle area, which may indicate cauda equina syndrome
    \item Sudden weakness or paralysis of the legs
    \item Severe back pain with fever
    \item After the injection: severe headache, new weakness, or fever
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Before an injection, the cause of the pain is confirmed.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} the pattern of pain, reflexes, and strength
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} MRI is the key test, showing disc herniation, stenosis, or other causes
    \item \textbf{Review of the whole picture:} the injection is appropriate when the pain matches the imaging
    \item \textbf{Pre-procedure checks:} medicines, allergies, and general health
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The injection is one part of a pain management plan.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The procedure:} performed with imaging guidance (X-ray or CT) to place the needle accurately; takes about 30 minutes
    \item \textbf{Sedation or local anaesthetic:} to keep the person comfortable
    \item \textbf{The medicine:} corticosteroid with or without local anaesthetic
    \item \textbf{Recovery:} observation for a period, then discharge the same day
    \item \textbf{Repeat injections:} a course of up to two or three injections over months if effective
    \item \textbf{Alongside care:} physiotherapy and exercise are essential for long-term benefit
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Temporary increase in pain after the injection
    \item Flushing, and in some people a brief rise in blood sugar or sleep disturbance
    \item Bleeding and infection, which are rare
    \item Nerve injury, which is very rare with imaging guidance
    \item Headache from dural puncture
    \item The effects of corticosteroids with repeated use
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Epidural injections provide significant short-term relief for many people with nerve-related back and leg pain, allowing them to participate in physiotherapy and activity. Long-term outcomes depend on the whole management plan, and the injection is most effective when combined with exercise and rehabilitation. Most people improve, and surgery is only needed for a minority.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Stay active and strengthen the core and back muscles
    \item Maintain a healthy weight
    \item Use good lifting techniques
    \item Follow the physiotherapy programme
    \item Report new weakness, numbness, or bladder problems urgently
    \item Discuss the benefits and risks of injections with your specialist
\end{itemize}
