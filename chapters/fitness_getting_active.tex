\chapter{Fitness: Getting Active}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Getting active is one of the most powerful things anyone can do for their health, yet many people struggle to start or maintain regular physical activity. The Australian guidelines recommend at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, each week, plus strength exercises on two or more days. The key to success is not heroic effort but starting small, choosing activities that are enjoyable and fit into daily life, and building up gradually. Every minute of activity counts, and the benefits begin with the very first steps. This chapter provides a practical guide to starting exercise, overcoming barriers, and building a habit that lasts.

\section*{Epidemiology}
A large proportion of Australians are insufficiently active: around 40--50\% of adults do not meet the physical activity guidelines, and inactivity rises with age and in some population groups. Physical inactivity is a leading risk factor for heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes, some cancers, and depression, and it is estimated to contribute to a substantial share of preventable deaths. Most people intend to be active but are stopped by time pressures, lack of motivation, cost, or uncertainty about how to start. Even modest increases in activity produce significant health benefits, and the largest gains come from moving from inactivity to some activity.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Understanding why activity helps makes starting easier.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular benefits:} regular activity strengthens the heart, lowers blood pressure, and improves cholesterol
    \item \textbf{Metabolic benefits:} activity improves blood sugar control, insulin sensitivity, and weight management
    \item \textbf{Musculoskeletal benefits:} strength and weight-bearing activity build muscle, protect bone, and prevent falls
    \item \textbf{Mental health:} activity releases endorphins, reduces stress hormones, improves sleep, and treats and prevents depression and anxiety
    \item \textbf{Moderate versus vigorous intensity:} moderate activity raises the heart rate and breathing (a brisk walk), while vigorous activity (running, fast cycling) produces sweating and harder breathing
    \item \textbf{The exercise dose:} 150 minutes of moderate activity, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, spread across the week, plus strength training twice weekly
    \item \textbf{Every bit counts:} benefits follow a dose-response curve; moving from zero to 30 minutes a day produces the largest gains
    \item \textbf{The habit loop:} exercise becomes sustainable through routine, enjoyment, and social connection, not willpower alone
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Starting an activity program has recognisable stages and features.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Choosing activities that suit fitness, interests, and circumstances, such as walking, swimming, cycling, dancing, or gym classes
    \item Starting small: 5--10 minute sessions and building gradually, rather than an all-or-nothing approach
    \item Moderate intensity: you can talk but not sing while exercising; vigorous means you can only say a few words
    \item Including strength exercises: bodyweight exercises, resistance bands, or weights, twice a week
    \item Scheduling activity like an appointment, with a specific plan for when and where
    \item Monitoring progress: steps, minutes, or distance can be tracked and celebrated
    \item Building social support: exercising with others improves adherence
    \item Preparing for setbacks: missed days are normal and do not mean failure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Barriers to activity need identifying and addressing individually.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Lack of time, which is managed by short sessions, incidental activity, and combining exercise with transport or chores
    \item Lack of motivation, which responds to small achievable goals, enjoyment, and routine
    \item Cost concerns, which are met by free activities such as walking, home exercise, and community programs
    \item Health conditions and pain, which require a plan tailored with a doctor or physiotherapist
    \item Fear of injury, which is reduced by gradual progression and proper technique
    \item Mental health barriers, which often improve markedly once activity starts, supported by structured plans
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most healthy adults can start moderate activity safely without medical review. People with heart disease, diabetes, or other chronic conditions, those with unexplained chest pain or dizziness on exertion, and anyone over 45 starting vigorous activity should check with a doctor first.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, pressure, or tightness during or after activity
    \item Severe breathlessness, dizziness, or collapse during exercise
    \item Palpitations that are fast, irregular, or associated with faintness
    \item Any emergency symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-exercise review:} a doctor's assessment for people with chronic conditions, including blood pressure, blood sugar, and heart risk
    \item \textbf{Activity readiness:} considering any symptoms such as chest pain, breathlessness, or joint problems
    \item \textbf{Setting a baseline:} current activity, fitness, and goals are assessed to plan progression
    \item \textbf{Specialist tests:} an exercise stress test may be recommended for people with heart disease before vigorous activity
    \item \textbf{Regular review:} tracking progress and adjusting the plan keeps it safe and effective
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Start small and build:} begin with 10-minute walks and add a few minutes each week
    \item \textbf{Choose enjoyable activities:} the best exercise is the one you will keep doing
    \item \textbf{Spread it across the week:} aim for 30 minutes on most days, and include a couple of strength sessions
    \item \textbf{Use incidental activity:} stairs instead of lifts, walking for transport, and active chores count
    \item \textbf{Plan and schedule:} put exercise in the calendar and prepare kit the night before
    \item \textbf{Track progress:} use steps, minutes, or distance to see gains and stay motivated
    \item \textbf{Build support:} exercise with friends, join classes, or use community programs
    \item \textbf{Expect and handle setbacks:} restart after illness or holidays without guilt
    \item \textbf{Warm up and cool down:} easy activity before and after sessions reduces injury risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Injuries from starting too hard, too fast, or without proper technique
    \item Giving up after overambitious goals that are not sustained
    \item Worsening of undiagnosed heart conditions during vigorous activity without medical review
    \item Falls in older adults without balance and strength training
    \item Heat-related illness when exercising in hot weather without precautions
    \item Missed health benefits from staying inactive
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The benefits of becoming active appear quickly: within weeks, fitness, mood, sleep, and energy improve, and blood pressure and blood sugar fall. Over months and years, regular activity substantially reduces the risk of heart disease, stroke, diabetes, cancer, and depression, and it extends life. People who build activity into their routine through enjoyment and social connection maintain it, while those who rely on willpower alone often lapse. The most reliable route to lasting fitness is a modest, enjoyable, gradually increasing plan, and the largest health gains come simply from moving more than before.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Work towards 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity
    \item Include strength exercises on two or more days
    \item Start with short, achievable sessions and increase gradually
    \item Choose activities you enjoy and vary them
    \item Schedule activity into your week like an appointment
    \item Use a step counter or activity app to track progress
    \item Exercise with others for support and accountability
    \item Seek medical advice before starting vigorous activity if you have health conditions
\end{itemize}
