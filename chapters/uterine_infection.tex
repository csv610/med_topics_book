\chapter{Uterine infection}
\index{Uterine infection}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A uterine infection is an infection of the inner lining and muscle of the uterus, known medically as endometritis and myometritis respectively. Infection may also spread to the fallopian tubes, ovaries, and surrounding pelvic tissues, in which case it is called pelvic inflammatory disease (PID). Uterine infection occurs in two main settings. Postpartum (or post-abortal) endometritis arises when bacteria enter the uterus after childbirth, miscarriage, or abortion. Pelvic inflammatory disease arises when sexually transmitted bacteria, usually chlamydia or gonorrhoea, ascend from the cervix into the upper genital tract. A less common cause is a foreign body, such as an intrauterine device (IUD), or instrumentation of the uterus. Uterine infection ranges from a mild, self-limiting irritation to a life-threatening systemic illness, and prompt treatment with antibiotics is important to protect fertility and long-term pelvic health. Both settings share the key message that early recognition and treatment prevent the most serious consequences.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Postpartum endometritis complicates about 1--3\% of vaginal deliveries and up to 5--10\% of caesarean deliveries, and the risk increases further with prolonged labour, prolonged rupture of membranes, and multiple vaginal examinations during labour. Pelvic inflammatory disease is diagnosed in approximately 1--2\% of women each year, and chlamydia and gonorrhoea are among the most commonly notified sexually transmitted infections in many countries, especially in people under 30. Untreated, a significant proportion of these infections ascend to the uterus and fallopian tubes. Endometritis related to an IUD is uncommon but does occur, particularly in the first weeks after insertion, and the risk is highest in women who have risk factors for sexually transmitted infections. Uterine infection is also seen after miscarriage management, surgical termination, and hysteroscopy if sterile technique is not maintained. Pelvic inflammatory disease affects the youngest age groups disproportionately, reflecting the higher rates of chlamydia and gonorrhoea in people under 30, and it is an important preventable cause of infertility.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The uterus normally has an effective defence against infection: the cervical mucus plug, the natural flora of the vagina, and the shedding of the lining each month. Infection occurs when these defences are breached.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Postpartum and post-abortal infection:} bacteria from the birth canal enter the uterus after delivery, often polymicrobial, involving both aerobic and anaerobic organisms including Group B streptococcus, \textit{Escherichia coli}, \textit{Staphylococcus aureus}, and anaerobes
    \item \textbf{Sexually transmitted infection:} \textit{Chlamydia trachomatis} and \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae} ascend through the cervix, most commonly in younger women and those with new or multiple partners
    \item \textbf{Other pelvic organisms:} \textit{Mycoplasma genitalium} and bacterial vaginosis-associated organisms can also ascend and cause infection
    \item \textbf{Foreign bodies and instrumentation:} an IUD, especially if inserted in the presence of undiagnosed infection, or procedures such as hysteroscopy, curettage, and insertion of instruments through the cervix
    \item \textbf{Retained products of conception:} fragments of placenta or membranes left after delivery or miscarriage provide a medium for bacterial growth, and the uterus is at its most vulnerable in the weeks after any pregnancy event
    \item \textbf{Chronic endometritis:} a low-grade, often silent inflammation of the lining increasingly recognised as a cause of abnormal bleeding and repeated implantation failure in fertility treatment
    \item \textbf{Rare causes:} tuberculosis and actinomycosis can infect the uterus in specific circumstances, and actinomycosis is occasionally associated with long-term IUD use
    \item \textbf{Compromised host defences:} anything that lowers the body's ability to fight infection, including poor diabetes control, immunosuppressive medicines, and anaemia, increases the risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms vary from subtle to severe, and some women have no symptoms at all. The presentation after childbirth, after a procedure, and with sexually acquired infection can look very similar, and the diagnosis is made on the combination of risk factors and findings.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Lower abdominal or pelvic pain, which may be dull and cramping
    \item Fever and chills, particularly after childbirth or procedures
    \item Abnormal or offensive-smelling vaginal discharge
    \item Bleeding after delivery, after an abortion, or between periods
    \item Pain during or after intercourse
    \item Pain when urinating or needing to urinate frequently
    \item General unwellness, feeling hot and sweaty, or shivering
    \item An enlarged, tender uterus on examination after delivery
    \item In severe cases, peritonitis, fainting, or signs of sepsis, including confusion and a racing heart
    \item Importantly, many women with chlamydia and gonorrhoea have no symptoms at all, which is why screening is recommended
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Normal postpartum pain and bleeding that does not indicate infection
    \item Urinary tract infection or kidney infection
    \item Appendicitis
    \item Ovarian cyst rupture or ovarian torsion
    \item Ectopic pregnancy
    \item Endometriosis or a flare of pelvic pain from other causes
    \item Gastroenteritis or bowel-related causes of pelvic pain
    \item In non-pregnant women, other causes of abnormal bleeding and pain such as fibroids or polyps
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with pelvic pain, abnormal discharge, fever after childbirth, a procedure, or an IUD insertion should seek medical review promptly, because early treatment protects fertility and prevents serious illness. Women who have been treated for chlamydia or gonorrhoea should also arrange a test of cure and repeat screening, and should be aware that repeat infection is common and carries the same risks as the first infection.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item High fever with severe pelvic pain after childbirth or a procedure
    \item Heavy bleeding together with fever, pain, and faintness
    \item Signs of sepsis, including confusion, rapid breathing, a racing heart, or feeling extremely unwell
    \item Collapse, fainting, or severe one-sided pelvic pain suggesting a ruptured abscess
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis combines clinical assessment with targeted tests, because the symptoms overlap with many other conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} recent delivery, miscarriage, abortion, procedure, IUD use, sexual history, and symptoms
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} temperature, heart rate, and blood pressure; gentle abdominal and pelvic examination to check for uterine tenderness, cervical motion tenderness, discharge, and masses
    \item \textbf{Swabs:} high vaginal and cervical swabs for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and other organisms, and swabs of any discharge; a first-void urine test can be used for chlamydia and gonorrhoea where swabs are not possible
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count and inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein; blood cultures in those who are severely unwell, so that antibiotics can be adjusted to the organism if the infection has spread
    \item \textbf{Urine test:} to exclude a urinary tract infection, which can mimic pelvic infection
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test:} to exclude pregnancy-related complications such as ectopic pregnancy or retained products of conception
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} to look for retained tissue, pelvic abscess, or free fluid, and to assess the lining of the uterus after delivery
    \item \textbf{Laparoscopy:} occasionally used to confirm pelvic inflammatory disease or to drain an abscess, though usually reserved for complex cases
    \item \textbf{Cervical screening test:} considered where discharge or bleeding suggests a cervical cause, and endometrial sampling where chronic endometritis is suspected after fertility problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Prompt treatment with antibiotics is the mainstay of management.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} broad-spectrum antibiotics covering chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and anaerobic organisms; oral treatment for mild cases and intravenous antibiotics in hospital for moderate to severe cases, because effective treatment requires antibiotics that reach the tissues of the upper genital tract
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} paracetamol and anti-inflammatory medicines
    \item \textbf{Rest and fluids:} rest at home and adequate hydration during recovery
    \item \textbf{Removal of the IUD:} usually advised if an IUD is present and infection develops, to allow the infection to clear
    \item \textbf{Drainage of abscess:} surgical or radiological drainage if a pelvic abscess forms, because antibiotics alone cannot clear a large collection of pus
    \item \textbf{Management of retained tissue:} if retained products of conception are present, they may need to be removed after antibiotics have been started
    \item \textbf{Partner treatment:} sexual partners of people with sexually transmitted infection need testing and treatment to prevent reinfection, and treatment is often offered presumptively
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} a review to ensure the infection has cleared, and repeat screening for sexually transmitted infections after treatment; people treated for gonorrhoea should be re-tested after a few weeks to confirm cure
    \item \textbf{Abstinence advice:} avoiding intercourse until the infection has resolved and symptoms have settled
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chronic pelvic pain:} persistent pain that affects work, sleep, and relationships, occurring in a minority of women after pelvic inflammatory disease
    \item Scarring and blockage of the fallopian tubes, causing subfertility and infertility
    \item Increased risk of ectopic pregnancy, because damaged tubes do not move eggs normally
    \item Pelvic abscess formation, which may require drainage or surgery
    \item Spread of infection to the bloodstream (sepsis), which can be life-threatening
    \item Recurrent infections
    \item Psychological distress related to pain and fertility concerns, which can persist long after the acute infection has been treated
    \item In pregnancy, untreated maternal infection can contribute to preterm birth and, rarely, infection of the newborn
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With early diagnosis and appropriate antibiotics, most uterine and pelvic infections resolve completely without long-term problems. The risk of complications rises with delay in treatment, so a low threshold for assessment is important. Untreated pelvic inflammatory disease is associated with a significant risk of tubal damage, and the risk of infertility increases with each episode: roughly one in eight women with a single episode of pelvic inflammatory disease, and a higher proportion with repeated episodes, experience tubal infertility. Postpartum endometritis responds quickly to intravenous antibiotics, and most women recover fully, especially when treatment starts before the infection has spread. Reinfection is prevented by treating sexual partners and using condoms. Women who have had pelvic inflammatory disease should be monitored, and those who have difficulty conceiving after such an infection should be assessed for tubal damage and referred for fertility care. With the treatments available today, the outlook for women who present early is very good, and fertility can often be preserved.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use condoms consistently to reduce the risk of sexually transmitted infections
    \item Have regular sexually transmitted infection screening if you have new or multiple partners
    \item Avoid douching, which disrupts the natural vaginal flora and can push bacteria upward
    \item Seek early testing and treatment for any unusual discharge or pelvic symptoms
    \item Treat bacterial vaginosis and any partner infections promptly to prevent reinfection
    \item Discuss IUD insertion with your clinician, including any risk factors for infection, and be reviewed if you develop pain, fever, or discharge after insertion
    \item In pregnancy, attend all antenatal appointments and report any group B streptococcus screening results
    \item After childbirth or a procedure, seek advice early if you develop fever, worsening pain, or offensive discharge
    \item Avoid intercourse until any infection has fully cleared, and until any antibiotics are finished
    \item Use barrier contraception with new or casual partners, and consider the HPV vaccine if you have not had it
\end{itemize}
