\chapter{Microbiome; Gut microbiome}
\index{Microbiome; Gut microbiome}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The human microbiome refers to the collective genomes of the trillions of microorganisms (including bacteria, archaea, fungi, viruses, and protozoa) that reside on and within the human body. The gut microbiome, situated predominantly in the distal large intestine (colon), represents the densest and most metabolically active community of these microbes. Rather than acting as passive commensal organisms, the gut microbiota functions as a complex, dynamic "forgotten organ" that plays a vital role in human physiology. It contributes to the fermentation of non-digestible dietary fibres, synthesises essential vitamins (such as vitamins K and B12), modulates host metabolism, maintains mucosal barrier integrity, and is fundamental to the development and regulation of the host immune system. A balanced gut microbiome (symbiosis) is crucial for health, whereas disturbances in its microbial composition or diversity (dysbiosis) are increasingly linked to gastrointestinal, metabolic, autoimmune, and neurological disorders.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The gut microbiome is highly variable among individuals, shaped by a multitude of genetic, lifestyle, and environmental factors. Epidemiological studies demonstrate that the human gut microbiome is established at birth, transitioning from a maternal-like profile to a stable, adult-like state by approximately three years of age. Modern lifestyle factors, particularly in industrialised nations like some countries, have significantly altered the epidemiology of the gut microbiome. The widespread consumption of Western diets (high in ultra-processed foods, refined sugars, and saturated fats, and low in dietary fibre) combined with high rates of antibiotic exposure has led to a progressive loss of ancestral microbial diversity. This decline in diversity is strongly correlated with the rising incidence of non-communicable diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), obesity, type 2 diabetes, asthma, and food allergies.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The composition and stability of the gut microbiome are influenced by host factors, environmental exposures, and dietary substrates.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary intake:} The primary driver of microbiome composition; diets rich in diverse plant fibres promote the growth of saccharolytic bacteria (e.g. Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus) that produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), while diets high in animal proteins select for proteolytic species.
    \item \textbf{Antibiotic exposure:} Broad-spectrum antibiotics cause rapid depletion of commensal bacterial populations, reducing diversity and creating niche environments for opportunistic pathogens like Clostridioides difficile.
    \item \textbf{Mode of delivery at birth:} Vaginal delivery inoculates the infant with maternal vaginal and faecal microbiota (e.g. Lactobacillus), whereas Caesarean section leads to colonisation by skin-like and hospital-associated microbes.
    \item \textbf{Host genetics and physiology:} Secretory IgA production, gastric acid secretion, and intestinal motility (peristalsis) actively shape the survival and distribution of microbial taxa.
    \item \textbf{Environmental hygiene:} The "hygiene hypothesis" suggests that reduced childhood exposure to diverse environmental microbes limits immune training, predisposing individuals to allergic and autoimmune conditions.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
While gut dysbiosis is not a disease in itself, it is characterised by localised gastrointestinal symptoms and systemic manifestations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal dysfunction:} Abdominal bloating, excessive flatulence, altered bowel habits (chronic diarrhoea or constipation), and abdominal discomfort.
    \item \textbf{Systemic inflammation:} Fatigue, skin issues (such as eczema or acne), and joint discomfort, often linked to increased intestinal permeability ("leaky gut") and circulating endotoxins (LPS).
    \item \textbf{Metabolic alterations:} Unexplained weight gain, difficulty losing weight, and fluctuations in appetite, driven by altered microbial energy extraction from food.
    \item \textbf{Neuropsychiatric symptoms:} Mood changes, brain fog, anxiety, or depressive symptoms, mediated via the bidirectional "gut-brain axis" and microbial neurotransmitter synthesis (e.g. serotonin).
    \item \textbf{Altered immune tolerance:} Increased susceptibility to food intolerances, environmental allergies, and recurrent mucosal infections.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Symptoms of gut dysbiosis overlap significantly with defined gastrointestinal and systemic disorders that must be diagnosed and managed appropriately.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS):} A functional gastrointestinal disorder presenting with recurrent abdominal pain and altered bowel habits, closely linked to dysbiosis but requiring clinical exclusion of red flags.
    \item \textbf{Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO):} An abnormal increase in bacterial populations in the small intestine, distinguished from colonic dysbiosis by hydrogen or methane breath tests.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD):} Chronic autoimmune diseases (Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis) presenting with bloody stools, weight loss, and mucosal ulceration, distinguished by colonoscopy and faecal calprotectin.
    \item \textbf{Coeliac disease:} An autoimmune reaction to gluten causing enteropathy, diagnosed by serology (tTG IgA) and duodenal biopsy while on a gluten-containing diet.
    \item \textbf{Food allergies and intolerances:} IgE-mediated reactions or non-immune intolerances (e.g. lactose intolerance due to lactase deficiency) presenting with overlapping bloating and diarrhoea.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Individuals experiencing persistent abdominal symptoms, bloating, or changes in bowel habits should consult a general practitioner or gastroenterologist. Immediate medical attention is required if warning signs of serious gastrointestinal pathology are present.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Severe gastrointestinal haemorrhage:} Passing large volumes of bright red blood or black, tarry stools (melena) accompanied by dizziness or fainting.
    \item \textbf{Acute abdomen:} Sudden, severe, constant abdominal pain accompanied by fever, rigidity, or rebound tenderness.
    \item \textbf{Severe dehydration:} Profound dizziness, confusion, or inability to pass urine due to intractable vomiting or diarrhoea.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
While commercial gut microbiome testing is widely available, its routine clinical utility is limited. Diagnostic workup focuses on ruling out organic pathology and identifying specific microbial overgrowths.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical evaluation:} Detailed dietary history, medication review (especially antibiotics and PPIs), and symptom assessment.
    \item \textbf{Faecal calprotectin:} A sensitive stool marker of mucosal inflammation, used to differentiate IBS from active IBD.
    \item \textbf{Breath testing:} Hydrogen and methane breath tests to evaluate for lactose malabsorption or small intestinal bacterial overgrowth.
    \item \textbf{Stool PCR and culture:} Indicated for acute, severe diarrhoea to rule out specific bacterial, viral, or parasitic pathogens.
    \item \textbf{Metagenomic stool analysis (next-generation sequencing):} Research or specialised clinical testing to map the relative abundance of phyla (e.g. Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes ratio) and functional metabolic pathways.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Therapeutic strategies focus on modifying the gut environment to support beneficial microbes, restore diversity, and resolve dysbiosis.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary modifications:} Consuming a high-fibre, plant-rich diet (aiming for 30+ different plants per week) containing prebiotic foods (e.g. garlic, onions, leeks, oats) to fuel beneficial bacteria.
    \item \textbf{Probiotic therapy:} Targeted administration of clinically validated bacterial strains (e.g. Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium) to manage specific symptoms or support recovery after antibiotic use.
    \item \textbf{Fermented foods:} Incorporating traditional fermented foods (e.g. kefir, yoghurt, kimchi, sauerkraut) containing live active cultures to transiently enrich the gut environment.
    \item \textbf{Judicious antibiotic use:} Limiting the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics to cases where they are strictly medically necessary.
    \item \textbf{Faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT):} The transfer of healthy donor stool into the colon of a patient, which is a highly effective, regulated treatment for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clostridioides difficile infection:} Severe pseudomembranous colitis resulting from opportunistic pathogen proliferation after antibiotic-induced depletion of commensal flora.
    \item \textbf{Chronic systemic metabolic inflammation:} Dysbiosis-induced endotoxaemia contributing to insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD).
    \item \textbf{Impaired nutrient absorption:} Micronutrient deficiencies resulting from altered microbial synthesis of vitamins and impaired mucosal absorption.
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune flare-ups:} Dysbiosis-driven immune dysregulation exacerbating symptoms in rheumatoid arthritis, multiple sclerosis, or IBD.
    \item \textbf{Chronic gastrointestinal distress:} Persistent bloating, pain, and food intolerances, leading to nutritional restrictions and reduced quality of life.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for restoring gut microbiome balance and resolving associated symptoms is generally very good. The gut microbiota is highly responsive to lifestyle interventions; significant changes in microbial composition can be observed within days of introducing dietary modifications. However, establishing a stable, resilient symbiotic state requires long-term adherence to a diverse, fibre-rich diet and healthy lifestyle habits. For specific infectious complications like Clostridioides difficile, treatments such as FMT have cure rates exceeding 90\%. Ongoing research into personalised nutrition and targeted microbial therapeutics continues to improve clinical outcomes for complex systemic diseases linked to gut dysbiosis.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Eat a diverse diet:} Aiming to consume a wide variety of vegetables, fruits, whole grains, nuts, seeds, and legumes.
    \item \textbf{Minimise ultra-processed foods:} Reducing intake of foods high in artificial additives, emulsifiers, and sweeteners, which can disrupt the mucosal barrier.
    \item \textbf{Manage stress levels:} Practising mindfulness, yoga, or exercise, as chronic stress can negatively alter gut motility and microbiota composition.
    \item \textbf{Prioritise sleep hygiene:} Ensuring consistent, restorative sleep to support the circadian rhythms of gut microbes.
    \item \textbf{Avoid unnecessary sanitation:} Balancing hygiene with contact with nature and soil to support microbial exposure.
\end{itemize}
