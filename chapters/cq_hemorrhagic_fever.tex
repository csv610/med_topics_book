\chapter{Crimean-Congo Haemorrhagic Fever}
\index{Crimean-Congo Haemorrhagic Fever}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever (CCHF) is a serious viral disease transmitted by tick bites or contact with blood or tissues of infected animals. It is caused by a virus spread mainly by Hyalomma ticks. Illness begins with fever, headache, and muscle aches and can progress to severe bleeding, liver failure, and death. It occurs in parts of Africa, the Balkans, the Middle East, and Asia. There is no widely available, consistently proven vaccine; treatment is mainly supportive, with specialist antiviral decisions. Prevention includes avoiding tick bites and handling livestock safely.

\section*{Epidemiology}
CCHF occurs across a wide geographic band, including Eastern Europe, the Middle East, central Asia, and Africa, wherever Hyalomma ticks are found. Outbreaks are most common among agricultural workers, shepherds, abattoir workers, and veterinarians. Human-to-human transmission can occur in healthcare settings through contact with infected blood, so strict infection control is essential. Travellers returning from endemic areas may develop illness after arrival elsewhere.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
CCHF virus infects humans through several routes.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tick bites:} the bite of an infected Hyalomma tick is the main route of transmission
    \item \textbf{Animal contact:} contact with blood or tissues of infected livestock, especially during slaughter
    \item \textbf{Nosocomial transmission:} healthcare workers can be infected through contact with patients' blood or secretions
    \item \textbf{Viral replication:} the virus multiplies in immune cells and the liver
    \item \textbf{Vascular damage:} the virus damages the lining of blood vessels, causing bleeding
    \item \textbf{Coagulopathy:} depletion of clotting factors and platelets leads to haemorrhage
    \item \textbf{Immune response:} the severity of disease reflects the extent of viral spread and immune activation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms appear after an incubation period of one to thirteen days.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden onset of fever, chills, and severe headache
    \item Muscle aches, back pain, and joint pain
    \item Dizziness, neck pain, and sensitivity to light
    \item Nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain
    \item Sore throat and red eyes in the early phase
    \item Bleeding manifestations from the fourth or fifth day: nosebleeds, bleeding gums, blood in the urine or stool, and bruising
    \item Rapid deterioration with liver failure and shock in severe cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The early symptoms resemble many other febrile illnesses, and diagnosis requires specific testing.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Other viral haemorrhagic fevers, such as Ebola, dengue haemorrhagic fever, and yellow fever
    \item Malaria and other tick-borne infections
    \item Bacterial sepsis and meningitis
    \item Typhoid fever and brucellosis
    \item Rickettsial diseases, such as typhus
    \item Hepatitis, with similar liver and gastrointestinal symptoms
    \item Leptospirosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who develops fever within two weeks of travel to an endemic area, a tick bite, or contact with livestock in an endemic region should seek urgent medical assessment and mention the exposure history.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding that is severe or from multiple sites
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or collapse
    \item Difficulty breathing
    \item A rapidly worsening fever with signs of shock
    \item Vomiting blood or passing blood in the stool
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis requires specialised laboratory testing, which must be performed under strict safety conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} travel to endemic areas, tick exposure, animal contact, and healthcare exposure
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count showing falling platelets and white cells, liver enzymes, and clotting studies
    \item \textbf{PCR testing:} detection of viral RNA in the blood, positive in the first week
    \item \textbf{Serology:} antibody tests, which become positive later in the illness
    \item \textbf{Virus isolation:} attempted only in high-containment laboratories
    \item \textbf{Public health notification:} CCHF is a notifiable disease in many countries
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Patients are managed in hospital with isolation and supportive care.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Isolation:} strict infection control precautions to prevent spread to staff and other patients
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} fluids, oxygen, and management of fever and pain
    \item \textbf{Blood products:} transfusions of platelets, plasma, and red cells for bleeding
    \item \textbf{Ribavirin:} the antiviral drug used for treatment, though evidence of benefit is limited
    \item \textbf{Organ support:} intensive care for liver failure, shock, and multi-organ failure
    \item \textbf{Contact tracing:} identification and monitoring of close contacts and healthcare workers
    \item \textbf{Recovery:} a prolonged convalescence, with close follow-up
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe bleeding and haemorrhagic shock
    \item Liver failure
    \item Kidney failure
    \item Multi-organ failure
    \item Disseminated intravascular coagulation
    \item Death in a significant proportion of cases
    \item Infection of healthcare workers and close contacts
    \item Prolonged fatigue and weakness during recovery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outcome depends on the speed of diagnosis, the availability of intensive care, and the severity of the initial illness. The case fatality rate is substantial, but patients who survive the first two weeks usually recover, although convalescence can be prolonged. Early supportive care and, where available, ribavirin may improve outcomes. Public health measures, including tick control, safe livestock handling, and hospital infection control, are the most effective ways to reduce deaths.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Avoid tick bites when in endemic areas: wear protective clothing, use repellents, and check for ticks
    \item Remove ticks promptly and safely with fine-tipped tweezers
    \item Use gloves when handling animal blood, tissues, or carcasses in endemic regions
    \item Follow strict infection control in healthcare settings
    \item Seek medical assessment promptly for fever after exposure
    \item Travellers to endemic areas should take insect precautions and avoid raw animal products
    \item Report suspected cases to public health authorities
\end{itemize}
