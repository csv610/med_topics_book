\chapter{Female Pattern Hair Loss}
\index{Female Pattern Hair Loss}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Female pattern hair loss, also called androgenetic alopecia, is the most common cause of progressive hair thinning in women. Unlike the dramatic balding seen in men, it usually presents as generalised thinning over the top of the scalp, with widening of the central parting, while the hairline at the front is preserved. It is a gradual, inherited condition driven by the effect of hormones on hair follicles that are genetically susceptible. Female pattern hair loss is not dangerous to health, but it can cause considerable distress and affect self-esteem and quality of life. Treatment exists that can slow or partly reverse the thinning, and starting it early gives the best results. This chapter explains the condition, its causes, and the options for management.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Female pattern hair loss is very common and becomes more frequent with age. It affects around 20--30\% of women by middle age and up to half of women over the age of 70, making it the leading cause of hair loss in women. It can begin at any age, including in the teens and twenties, but is most often noticed around or after menopause. The condition affects women of all ethnic backgrounds, though the pattern and prominence vary. Despite its frequency, fewer than half of affected women seek medical advice, often because they believe nothing can be done; in fact, effective treatment is available.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} an inherited susceptibility passed from either side of the family determines which hair follicles respond to normal hormone levels
    \item \textbf{Hormones:} androgens, including testosterone and its active metabolite dihydrotestosterone (DHT), act on susceptible follicles and shorten the growing (anagen) phase of the hair cycle
    \item \textbf{Follicle changes:} affected follicles produce finer, shorter, less pigmented hairs over time, a process called miniaturisation
    \item \textbf{Pattern:} thinning occurs mainly over the crown and top of the head, reflecting the sensitivity of follicles in these areas
    \item \textbf{Hormonal sensitivity:} unlike in men, most women with female pattern hair loss have normal blood androgen levels; the condition reflects increased sensitivity of the follicle rather than excess hormone
    \item \textbf{Other hormonal influences:} conditions of high androgen such as polycystic ovary syndrome can worsen the pattern, and menopause may accelerate it
    \item \textbf{Natural history:} the process is gradual, and because follicles do not die but miniaturise, the hair loss is potentially reversible with treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Thinning of hair over the crown and top of the scalp, with a widening central parting
    \item Preservation of the frontal hairline in most women
    \item A decrease in hair density and volume, with hair becoming finer and shorter
    \item Gradually increasing number of hairs seen in the brush, pillow, or shower, though shedding is usually not dramatic
    \item No scalp redness, scaling, or scarring
    \item The condition does not affect other body hair
    \item Onset is gradual over years, and the severity varies widely between women
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of hair loss in women must be distinguished, because treatments differ.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Telogen effluvium, a diffuse shedding weeks after stress, illness, childbirth, or rapid weight loss
    \item Iron deficiency and other nutritional deficiencies
    \item Thyroid disease, which can cause diffuse thinning
    \item Alopecia areata, which causes patchy round areas of loss
    \item Scarring (cicatricial) alopecia, with redness, scaling, and permanent loss
    \item Hair breakage from styling, chemicals, and traction
    \item Polycystic ovary syndrome and other hyperandrogen states, with signs such as acne, excess facial hair, and irregular periods
    \item Syphilis, which rarely causes a moth-eaten pattern of loss
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Women should see a doctor if hair loss is sudden, patchy, associated with redness or scaling, accompanied by other symptoms such as irregular periods or fatigue, or if it is causing significant distress. Early review matters because treatment works best when started promptly.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, dramatic hair loss with scalp pain, blisters, or widespread skin change, which may indicate an inflammatory emergency
    \item Hair loss with other signs of serious illness, such as high fever, weight loss, or confusion
    \item Any other emergency symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} onset and pace of loss, family history, medicines, diet, recent stress or illness, menstrual pattern, and signs of androgen excess
    \item \textbf{Examination:} assessment of the pattern of thinning, the scalp, and signs of other causes; a hair pull test may be performed
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} iron studies, ferritin, thyroid function, and in selected women, hormone levels including testosterone
    \item \textbf{Scalp biopsy:} rarely needed, but helps distinguish female pattern hair loss from scarring or inflammatory conditions
    \item \textbf{Dermatoscopy:} the dermatoscope shows the miniaturised hairs characteristic of the condition
    \item \textbf{Photography:} serial photos help document and measure progression
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Topical minoxidil:} the first-line treatment; applied to the scalp daily, it stimulates hair growth and slows loss, with results seen over 6--12 months of continuous use
    \item \textbf{Spironolactone:} an anti-androgen tablet that reduces the hormonal drive to hair loss, used when minoxidil alone is insufficient or as an alternative
    \item \textbf{Low-level laser therapy:} devices that emit light to stimulate follicles, used as an adjunct
    \item \textbf{Platelet-rich plasma (PRP):} injections of the patient's own concentrated platelets into the scalp, with variable but often useful results
    \item \textbf{Concealers and styling:} powders, sprays, and fibre products camouflage thinning effectively and improve confidence
    \item \textbf{Wigs and hairpieces:} for women who prefer a full cosmetic solution
    \item \textbf{Hair transplantation:} an option for selected women with stable, suitable patterns of loss
    \item \textbf{Treat underlying conditions:} iron replacement and thyroid treatment where deficiencies are found
    \item \textbf{Address the psychological impact:} the distress caused by hair loss deserves attention and support
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Psychological distress, reduced self-esteem, and impaired quality of life
    \item Social avoidance and anxiety in some women
    \item Side effects of treatment: scalp irritation with minoxidil, and breast tenderness, irregular periods, or dizziness with spironolactone
    \item Progression to more visible thinning if left untreated
    \item The potential for spironolactone to harm a developing fetus, so effective contraception is needed during use
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Female pattern hair loss is a chronic, gradually progressive condition, but treatment changes its course. With consistent use, minoxidil slows the loss and produces measurable regrowth in many women, and spironolactone adds benefit for others. The best outcomes occur when treatment begins early, before follicles have miniaturised extensively. The condition does not affect general health, and a woman's hair can be maintained for many years with treatment. For those who prefer not to use medicines, cosmetic concealment is highly effective, and most women find a combination of approaches that preserves their confidence.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Start treatment early if thinning is noticed, as response is better with earlier intervention
    \item Use minoxidil consistently; stopping it leads to reversal of gains within months
    \item Avoid harsh chemical treatments, tight hairstyles, and excessive heat styling that break hair
    \item Eat a balanced diet with adequate iron and protein
    \item Manage stress and get adequate sleep
    \item See a doctor to exclude treatable causes such as iron deficiency and thyroid disease
    \item Use cosmetic concealers or a good wig if preferred; these are effective and legitimate options
    \item Seek psychological support if the hair loss is causing distress
\end{itemize}
