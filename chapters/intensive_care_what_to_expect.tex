\chapter{Intensive Care: What to Expect}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Intensive care is the specialised care provided to people with life-threatening illness or injury in an intensive care unit (ICU). Patients in ICU receive constant monitoring of vital functions, close nursing supervision, and treatments that can include breathing support, blood pressure support, and dialysis. People are admitted to ICU after major surgery, serious accidents, severe infections, or sudden deterioration of chronic illness. For families, an ICU admission is frightening and unfamiliar, and knowing what to expect helps. This chapter explains how ICUs work, what happens during a stay, common equipment and procedures, and how families can be involved and supported.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Around 200,000 Australians are admitted to an intensive care unit each year. Admissions are roughly evenly divided between planned admissions after major surgery and emergency admissions for critical illness or injury. The average age of ICU patients has risen as care for older people has improved, and the most common reasons for admission include severe sepsis, respiratory failure, cardiac arrest, major trauma, and high-risk surgery. Most people survive their ICU stay, with overall in-hospital mortality around 10 per cent, and most survivors return home, although many need a period of rehabilitation.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
People require intensive care when one or more vital organs fail or are at risk of failing. Common problems include respiratory failure, in which the lungs cannot oxygenate the blood or remove carbon dioxide, requiring a ventilator; circulatory failure, in which the heart or blood pressure cannot sustain organ blood flow, requiring medicines to support blood pressure; and kidney, liver, or brain failure. These states arise from infection and sepsis, major trauma, heart attacks, strokes, severe asthma, and major surgery. The ICU provides time and support for the underlying condition to be treated while vital functions are maintained artificially. Even with full support, critical illness causes profound stress on the body, and recovery takes time.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item One-to-one nursing with continuous monitoring of heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen, and breathing
    \item Breathing support ranging from oxygen through a mask to a ventilator with a breathing tube
    \item Intravenous lines, arterial lines, and often a central line in the neck or groin
    \item Sedation and pain relief so the patient is comfortable and does not fight the equipment
    \item Tube feeding, urinary catheters, and regular blood tests
    \item Frequent observation by doctors, nurses, and allied health staff
    \item Visiting hours that may be flexible, with rest periods for the patient
    \item Families are kept informed and are encouraged to ask questions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The pattern of an ICU stay varies with the reason for admission and the person's health.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Planned (post-operative) admission:} after major surgery, usually brief and predictable
    \item \textbf{Respiratory failure:} from pneumonia, asthma, or chronic lung disease
    \item \textbf{Sepsis:} overwhelming infection causing organ failure
    \item \textbf{Cardiac conditions:} after cardiac arrest, heart attack, or heart surgery
    \item \textbf{Neurological conditions:} stroke, brain injury, or seizures
    \item \textbf{Trauma:} severe injuries requiring surgical and intensive care
    \item \textbf{High-dependency care:} some patients need close monitoring but not full intensive care
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Decisions about ICU admission are made by hospital medical teams, and admission is arranged by ambulance or within the hospital. Family members should seek information from the ICU team, ask questions, and speak with a doctor if they have concerns about the patient's care or comfort. If a loved one is at home and deteriorates suddenly, call an ambulance immediately rather than waiting.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cardiac arrest: start CPR and use a defibrillator while waiting
    \item Difficulty breathing, severe chest pain, or collapse
    \item Severe bleeding, serious injury, or unconsciousness
    \item Signs of stroke: face, arm, or leg weakness, or difficulty speaking
    \item Any sudden, severe deterioration at home
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Continuous monitoring:} heart rhythm, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, and breathing
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} frequent checks of oxygen, electrolytes, kidney and liver function, and infection markers
    \item \textbf{Arterial blood gases:} precise measurement of oxygen and carbon dioxide
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} portable chest X-rays, CT scans, and ultrasound
    \item \textbf{Echocardiography:} assessment of heart function
    \item \textbf{Microbiological tests:} cultures to identify infection
    \item \textbf{Specialist review:} input from intensivists, surgeons, and other specialists
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ventilation:} breathing support through a mask or a tube into the windpipe, with gradual weaning as the patient improves
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure support:} intravenous medicines to maintain organ blood flow
    \item \textbf{Treatment of the underlying condition:} antibiotics, surgery, or other specific therapy
    \item \textbf{Sedation and comfort:} medicines adjusted to keep the patient comfortable and pain-free
    \item \textbf{Nutrition:} feeding by tube or vein from an early stage
    \item \textbf{Prevention of complications:} pressure care, blood clot prevention, and infection control
    \item \textbf{Family communication:} regular updates, family meetings, and support
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy and occupational therapy in ICU and after discharge
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infections, including pneumonia and line-related bloodstream infections
    \item Delirium, confusion, and agitation during critical illness
    \item Weakness and muscle wasting from prolonged immobility
    \item Blood clots, pressure injuries, and bleeding
    \item Kidney failure requiring dialysis
    \item Psychological effects after discharge: anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress
    \item Post-intensive care syndrome, affecting physical, cognitive, and mental health
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people admitted to intensive care survive and leave hospital, but recovery is often gradual. Around one in ten patients does not survive the admission, and the outlook depends on age, the severity of illness, and pre-existing health. Survivors frequently need weeks to months to regain strength and function, and rehabilitation programs improve recovery. Families often provide essential support during this period. Knowing that recovery takes time helps set realistic expectations for patients and families alike.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item For families: ask questions and request regular updates from the ICU team
    \item Keep a diary or notes of the medical information provided
    \item Rest, eat, and look after yourself as a visitor, and accept support from others
    \item Bring familiar items such as photos and music, where the unit allows
    \item After discharge, attend follow-up and rehabilitation appointments
    \item Seek help for anxiety, depression, or poor sleep in the months after ICU
    \item Discuss any ongoing symptoms with the GP
    \item Take prescribed medicines and follow the discharge plan
\end{itemize}
