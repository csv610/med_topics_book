\chapter{Keratoconus}
\index{Keratoconus}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Keratoconus is a progressive eye condition in which the cornea, the clear front surface of the eye, becomes thin and bulges forward into a cone shape instead of remaining spherical. This distortion causes blurred and distorted vision that cannot be fully corrected with ordinary glasses. Keratoconus usually begins in the teenage years or early adulthood and progresses over time, often for 10 to 20 years before stabilising. Treatment ranges from glasses and contact lenses in early disease to corneal collagen cross-linking to halt progression, and, in advanced cases, corneal transplant. With modern treatment, most people with keratoconus retain good vision.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Keratoconus affects around 1 in 400 to 1 in 2000 people, with estimates varying by population, and it is slightly more common in males. It typically begins in adolescence and progresses through the 20s and 30s. Risk factors include a family history, which is present in around 10 per cent of cases, atopy and eczema, and vigorous eye rubbing, which is strongly associated with worsening. It occurs in all ethnic groups, with some studies showing higher rates in South Asian populations. It is one of the most common reasons for corneal transplant in younger people, although cross-linking has reduced the need.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The cornea is normally a smooth, dome-shaped structure that focuses light. In keratoconus, the cornea thins and steepens progressively, and the underlying cause is a weakness of the corneal structure, related to abnormalities in collagen fibres and the corneal stroma. The weakened cornea bulges forward under the pressure of the eye, and the irregular surface causes irregular astigmatism, scattering light and distorting vision. Eye rubbing, which is common in people with allergies, mechanically stresses the cornea and accelerates progression. Enzymes that break down corneal tissue appear to be overactive, and oxidative stress may contribute. The condition is typically progressive during the second and third decades of life.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Gradually worsening blurred and distorted vision
    \item Frequent changes to glasses prescriptions that stop giving clear vision
    \item Increased sensitivity to light and glare
    \item Difficulty driving at night
    \item Halos and ghosting around lights
    \item A need to squint to see clearly
    \item Itchy eyes and eye rubbing, common in people with allergies
    \item Progression usually stops in the 30s or 40s
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of progressive visual distortion should be considered.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High and irregular astigmatism:} without corneal thinning
    \item \textbf{Corneal scarring:} from infection or injury
    \item \textbf{Pellucid marginal degeneration:} a related corneal thinning condition
    \item \textbf{Cataracts:} gradual blurring in older adults
    \item \textbf{Refractive error:} myopia and astigmatism correctable with glasses
    \item \textbf{Other corneal dystrophies:} inherited corneal conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with progressive blurred vision, frequent glasses prescription changes, or visual distortion should have a comprehensive eye examination, including corneal imaging. People with a family history should be screened. Sudden worsening, severe pain, or a sudden drop in vision warrants urgent review, as does a very thin, bulging cornea (acute hydrops).

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe eye pain or a sudden drop in vision
    \item An eye injury
    \item Sudden redness with light sensitivity, which may indicate infection or acute hydrops
    \item A chemical splash to the eye
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Eye examination:} visual acuity and refraction
    \item \textbf{Corneal topography:} mapping of the corneal shape, the key diagnostic test
    \item \textbf{Corneal tomography:} measuring corneal thickness and the back surface
    \item \textbf{Slit-lamp examination:} looking for corneal thinning and signs
    \item \textbf{Pachymetry:} measurement of corneal thickness
    \item \textbf{Regular review:} monitoring for progression
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to an ophthalmologist for diagnosis and treatment planning
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Glasses:} for early, mild keratoconus
    \item \textbf{Contact lenses:} rigid gas-permeable, scleral, and hybrid lenses give the best vision by creating a smooth surface
    \item \textbf{Corneal collagen cross-linking:} the key treatment to halt progression, using ultraviolet light and riboflavin to stiffen the cornea
    \item \textbf{Intracorneal ring segments:} small implants to flatten the cornea in selected cases
    \item \textbf{Corneal transplant:} for advanced disease or scarring, with excellent success rates
    \item \textbf{Stopping eye rubbing:} essential to slow progression
    \item \textbf{Treatment of allergies:} to reduce itching and rubbing
    \item \textbf{Regular ophthalmology review:} monitoring and adjusting treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive visual loss if untreated
    \item Acute corneal hydrops: sudden fluid swelling of the cornea, causing pain and vision loss
    \item Corneal scarring from rubbing or hydrops
    \item The need for corneal transplant in advanced cases
    \item Complications of surgery, including rejection and infection
    \item Effects on driving, study, work, and quality of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for keratoconus has improved greatly. Cross-linking halts progression in the large majority of people when performed early, preserving vision and avoiding transplant. Contact lenses restore good vision for most, and corneal transplant succeeds in over 90 per cent of the small proportion who need it. Progression typically stops by middle age. With regular care, most people with keratoconus see well and live full, active lives.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Do not rub your eyes, and treat allergies to reduce itching
    \item Have regular eye examinations, including corneal imaging
    \item Attend all ophthalmology reviews and follow treatment
    \item Wear well-fitted contact lenses and follow hygiene and wear schedules
    \item Discuss cross-linking early to prevent progression
    \item Protect the eyes from the sun with UV-protective lenses
    \item Report sudden changes in vision promptly
    \item Seek genetic counselling if there is a family history
    \item Join support groups for information and reassurance
\end{itemize}
