\chapter{Lightning}
\index{Lightning}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Lightning injury is a rare but severe medical emergency caused by exposure to atmospheric electrical discharge. Although lightning strikes carry extremely high voltage (up to hundreds of millions of volts) and massive current (often exceeding 30,000 amperes), the duration of exposure is exceptionally brief (typically lasting milliseconds). Unlike domestic high-voltage electrical injuries that cause extensive deep tissue burns and muscle necrosis, lightning injuries primarily cause transient multisystem dysfunction due to massive electrical depolarisation, coupled with blast and thermal injuries. Immediate resuscitation is critical, and the management of victims requires specific trauma algorithms.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Worldwide, lightning causes thousands of deaths annually, with the highest incidence occurring in tropical regions and low-income countries where agricultural work is common and shelter is limited. In many countries, lightning strikes are relatively rare but represent a persistent hazard, leading to approximately 5 to 10 deaths and over 100 injuries each year. The majority of victims are young males engaged in outdoor recreational activities, such as hiking, camping, or golfing, or working in outdoor occupations like agriculture and construction. Incidents peak during summer months, corresponding with convective thunderstorm activity.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Lightning injuries occur through several distinct mechanisms of electrical and physical energy transfer.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Direct strike:} The electrical bolt directly strikes the individual, usually when they are in an open area, carrying the maximum amount of current.
    \item \textbf{Side flash (splash):} Lightning strikes a nearby tall object (like a tree) and then jumps to the victim, who presents a path of lesser resistance.
    \item \textbf{Contact injury:} The individual is touching an object (such as a metal fence or telephone line) that is directly struck or conducting current.
    \item \textbf{Ground current (step potential):} The current spreads through the ground from a nearby strike, entering one foot and exiting the other, which can affect multiple people simultaneously.
    \item \textbf{Upward streamer:} An incomplete electrical pathway rises from the ground through the individual to meet the descending lightning bolt, causing localised trauma.
    \item \textbf{Blast injury:} The rapid expansion of air heated by the lightning bolt creates a shock wave, causing blunt trauma, tympanic membrane rupture, or barotrauma.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of lightning injury is diverse, affecting multiple systems, with cardiorespiratory arrest being the most critical acute manifestation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cardiorespiratory arrest:} Simultaneous cardiac arrest (typically ventricular fibrillation or asystole) and respiratory arrest due to paralysis of the medullary respiratory centre.
    \item \textbf{Neurological dysfunction:} Transient paralysis of the lower limbs (keraunoparalysis), confusion, amnesia, loss of consciousness, and peripheral neuropathies.
    \item \textbf{Lichtenberg figures:} Pathognomonic transient, erythematous, fern-like or arborescent skin markings caused by static electricity passing through capillaries, appearing within hours and resolving within 24 hours.
    \item \textbf{Ocular and auditory damage:} Cataracts, retinal detachment, tympanic membrane rupture, and conductive or sensorineural hearing loss.
    \item \textbf{Thermal and mechanical injuries:} Superficial singe burns, linear burns from vaporised sweat, and fractures or soft tissue trauma from blast-induced falls.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
In an unresponsive patient found outdoors, lightning injury must be differentiated from other acute environmental, toxicological, or medical conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Domestic or occupational electrical injury:} Typically causes deeper, more extensive contact burns and severe rhabdomyolysis due to prolonged contact with alternating current (AC).
    \item \textbf{Traumatic brain injury or assault:} Blunt head trauma presenting with unconsciousness and external injuries, but lacking pathognomonic lightning signs.
    \item \textbf{Heatstroke:} Severe hyperthermia presenting with altered mental status and collapse, but without signs of electrical passage or blast injury.
    \item \textbf{Acute cardiovascular events:} Myocardial infarction or primary arrhythmia causing sudden collapse outdoors.
    \item \textbf{Envenomation:} Snakebite or insect stings causing rapid anaphylaxis, paralysis, or cardiac instability in outdoor environments.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any individual suspected of being struck by lightning requires immediate medical assessment, even if they appear uninjured or recover quickly.
\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unresponsiveness or lack of normal breathing in a lightning victim.
    \item Active chest pain, palpitations, or difficulty breathing.
    \item Severe burns, bleeding, or inability to move the limbs.
    \item Confusion, seizures, or loss of consciousness.
\end{itemize}
Resuscitative efforts (CPR) must be initiated immediately for anyone who is unresponsive or not breathing. Unlike standard multi-casualty triage where unresponsive patients are prioritised last, lightning triage dictates "reverse triage," where the apparently dead (apnoeic or pulseless) are resuscitated first, as respiratory arrest can outlast cardiac arrest and lead to secondary hypoxic cardiac arrest.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnostic evaluation focuses on identifying hidden visceral injuries, cardiac arrhythmias, and neurological complications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Electrocardiogram (ECG):} Performed immediately to check for arrhythmias, QT prolongation, or signs of myocardial ischaemia.
    \item \textbf{Cardiac enzymes (Troponin):} Measured to assess for myocardial injury or contusion.
    \item \textbf{Serum creatine kinase (CK):} Monitored to evaluate for muscle breakdown and rhabdomyolysis, although this is less common than in high-voltage electrical injuries.
    \item \textbf{CT scan of the head and spine:} Indicated if there is altered consciousness, focal neurological deficits, or suspected spinal trauma from blast-induced falls.
    \item \textbf{Otoscopy and ophthalmoscopy:} Performed to inspect for tympanic membrane perforation and assess for retinal damage or early cataract formation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of lightning injuries involves rapid field resuscitation, supportive hospital care, and long-term rehabilitation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reverse triage and CPR:} Initiating immediate cardiopulmonary resuscitation and artificial ventilation for apnoeic or pulseless victims, as prompt respiratory support can prevent secondary cardiac arrest.
    \item \textbf{Spinal immobilisation:} Maintaining cervical spine precautions during initial handling, as blast injuries or falls can cause spinal fractures.
    \item \textbf{Fluid resuscitation:} Administered carefully to maintain renal perfusion, guided by urine output; large-volume fluid resuscitation is rarely needed unless deep muscle damage is present.
    \item \textbf{Burn wound management:} Cleaning and dressing superficial thermal burns, and managing Lichtenberg figures conservatively as they resolve spontaneously.
    \item \textbf{Neurological and cardiac monitoring:} Continuous ECG monitoring and supportive care for neurological deficits, which often resolve within hours or days (e.g., keraunoparalysis).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Permanent neurological deficits:} Chronic neuropathic pain, cognitive impairment, memory loss, sleep disturbances, and peripheral nerve palsy.
    \item \textbf{Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD):} Long-term psychological distress, anxiety, depression, and fear of storms.
    \item \textbf{Cataracts:} Delayed visual impairment developing weeks or months after the strike.
    \item \textbf{Conductive or sensorineural hearing loss:} Caused by blast-induced tympanic membrane rupture or ossicular disruption.
    \item \textbf{Chronic pain syndromes:} Refractory musculoskeletal or neuropathic pain.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for lightning strike survivors is generally favourable, with an overall mortality rate of approximately 10\%. About 70\% to 80\% of survivors experience long-term morbidity, which is predominantly neurological or psychological rather than physical. Cardiorespiratory arrest at the scene is the primary cause of death. Survivors who do not suffer cardiac arrest or hypoxic brain injury have high recovery rates, although chronic conditions like neuropathic pain, memory deficits, and sleep issues require ongoing multi-disciplinary management.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Follow the 30-30 rule: seek shelter if the time between lightning and thunder is less than 30 seconds, and remain indoors for 30 minutes after the last clap of thunder.
    \item Seek safe shelter immediately inside a fully enclosed building or a hard-topped metal vehicle; avoid open sheds, picnic shelters, or tents.
    \item Avoid standing near tall objects, such as trees, utility poles, or towers, and stay away from open bodies of water.
    \item If caught in an open field with no shelter, assume the lightning crouch: squat low, ball up with feet together, and tuck your head to minimise ground contact and height.
    \item Avoid using corded landline phones, electrical appliances, or plumbing fixtures indoors during a storm, as metal pipes and wires can conduct lightning.
\end{itemize}
