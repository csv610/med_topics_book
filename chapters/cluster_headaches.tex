\chapter{Cluster Headaches}
\index{Cluster Headaches}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cluster headache is a primary headache disorder and one of the most severe and disabling types of headache known to medicine. It is characterised by recurrent, strictly one-sided attacks of excruciating pain, usually centred around the eye or temple, that recur with remarkable regularity and are accompanied by autonomic symptoms on the same side of the face. The term ``cluster'' refers to the distinctive pattern of the condition: bouts of frequent attacks lasting weeks to months, known as cluster periods, are separated by complete or near-complete remissions that can last months or even years. Attacks often strike at the same time of day, frequently at night, and are so intense that patients describe them as the worst pain they have ever experienced. Although the condition is uncommon, it is highly recognisable, and effective acute and preventive treatments exist, so prompt diagnosis dramatically improves quality of life.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cluster headache is uncommon, with a lifetime prevalence of roughly 1 in 1,000 people, making it considerably rarer than migraine or tension-type headache. It shows a marked male predominance, affecting men three to four times more often than women, although the sex gap has narrowed in recent decades, possibly reflecting changing smoking patterns. The disorder most often begins in the twenties or thirties, but onset can occur at any age, and it is unusual to start after the age of 60. Smoking is strongly associated with the condition: the vast majority of people affected are current or former smokers. Alcohol is a potent trigger during active cluster periods but rarely provokes attacks during remissions. Cluster headache is lifelong for most affected individuals, but the frequency and severity of attacks often diminish with advancing age, and many patients enjoy long spontaneous remissions.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The precise cause of cluster headache remains incompletely understood, but the evidence points to a disorder of brain networks centred on the hypothalamus, the region that regulates the body's internal clock and the sleep--wake cycle.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Trigeminal--autonomic reflex:} activation of the trigeminal nerve produces the intense periorbital pain, while reflex activation of the parasympathetic system accounts for the ipsilateral autonomic features such as tearing, nasal congestion, and eyelid drooping.
    \item \textbf{Hypothalamic involvement:} the striking circadian and circannual periodicity of attacks--often occurring at the same hour each night and at the same season each year--implicates the hypothalamic suprachiasmatic nucleus, and functional imaging shows activation of this region during attacks.
    \item \textbf{Triggers:} alcohol, strong smells, nitroglycerin, high altitude, heat, exertion, and daytime napping can precipitate attacks during an active cluster period.
    \item \textbf{Smoking:} tobacco smoking and a history of smoking are consistently associated with cluster headache, though the mechanism linking the two remains unclear.
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} a family history is present in up to 10\% of cases, and genome-wide studies have identified susceptibility loci, indicating a modest hereditary component.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical picture of cluster headache is so stereotyped that experienced clinicians can often make the diagnosis from the history alone.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pain:} excruciating, sharp, boring, or burning pain on one side of the head, almost always around, behind, or above the eye, or in the temple.
    \item \textbf{Duration:} each attack lasts 15 minutes to 3 hours if untreated, with a rapid onset peaking within minutes.
    \item \textbf{Frequency:} attacks occur from once every other day up to eight times per day, commonly at the same time of day and often waking the patient 1--2 hours after falling asleep.
    \item \textbf{Restlessness:} unlike migraine, where patients prefer to lie still in a dark room, cluster patients pace, rock, or press their head, and may bang the head against a wall because the pain is so severe.
    \item \textbf{Autonomic features:} on the affected side there is reddening and watering of the eye, drooping of the eyelid, constriction of the pupil, nasal blockage or runny nose, and facial sweating or flushing.
    \item \textbf{Pattern:} cluster periods of weeks to months, during which attacks occur with clockwork regularity, are separated by remissions; a minority of patients have chronic cluster headache without meaningful remission.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the pain is among the most severe encountered in medicine, serious secondary causes must be considered before the diagnosis is accepted.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Migraine, particularly attacks with prominent autonomic symptoms (so-called migraine with autonomic features).
    \item Trigeminal neuralgia, which causes brief, electric-shock-like facial pain and can coexist with cluster headache.
    \item Paroxysmal hemicrania and hemicrania continua, rare one-sided headache disorders that respond dramatically to indomethacin.
    \item Giant cell arteritis in older patients, with scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, and elevated inflammatory markers.
    \item Acute angle-closure glaucoma, acute sinusitis, dental disease, or temporomandibular disorders causing unilateral facial pain.
    \item Secondary cluster-like headache due to structural lesions such as pituitary tumours, arteriovenous malformations, or aneurysms, which can produce an almost identical clinical picture.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone experiencing a first-ever severe one-sided headache, headaches with autonomic features, or attacks that wake them from sleep should be assessed to confirm the diagnosis and exclude more serious causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek medical care for any new, very severe headache that interrupts sleep or normal activity.
    \item Seek care if headaches begin after the age of 50 or are accompanied by fever, weight loss, scalp tenderness, or jaw pain.
    \item Consult your doctor promptly if you are in an active cluster period, as effective acute and preventive treatments are available.
    \item Seek advice if attacks change in character, frequency, or pattern, as this may indicate a new problem.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A sudden ``thunderclap'' headache of explosive onset--the worst headache of your life.
    \item Headache associated with fever and a stiff neck, which may suggest meningitis.
    \item Headache with weakness, numbness, confusion, difficulty speaking, or visual loss.
    \item Headache that follows a head injury, or any headache accompanied by collapse, seizures, or loss of consciousness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Cluster headache is a clinical diagnosis based on the history, because the pattern of attacks is so characteristic. There is no confirmatory test, but investigation is used to exclude secondary causes and to plan treatment safely.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detailed history:} the site, severity, duration, frequency, and timing of attacks, together with autonomic features, restlessness, and the presence of cluster periods and remissions.
    \item \textbf{Neurological examination:} usually entirely normal between attacks; careful examination during an attack can demonstrate the autonomic changes and excludes other focal neurology.
    \item \textbf{Headache diary:} documenting attacks, triggers, and medication use over several weeks to confirm the diagnosis and guide management.
    \item \textbf{Brain MRI:} indicated when the pattern is atypical, when there are persistent abnormal neurological signs, or when a secondary cause is suspected; it may include specific pituitary imaging.
    \item \textbf{Cardiac evaluation:} baseline electrocardiography and blood pressure measurement before starting verapamil, because this drug can affect cardiac conduction.
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a neurologist or specialist headache clinic for confirmation of the diagnosis and optimisation of treatment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management has two complementary aims: aborting individual attacks quickly, and suppressing attacks throughout a cluster period. Both are essential, because acute medication alone cannot prevent the relentless recurrence that characterises the condition.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute attack treatment:} inhaled high-flow oxygen (12--15 L/min via a non-rebreathing mask for 15--20 minutes), which can abort an attack in around 15 minutes and is safe and free of side effects.
    \item \textbf{Abortive medication:} subcutaneous sumatriptan 6 mg, or sumatriptan or zolmitriptan nasal spray, gives rapid relief; it is limited by the maximum daily dose and is unsuitable for frequent attacks.
    \item \textbf{Preventive medication:} verapamil taken daily is the mainstay of prevention, titrated upwards with electrocardiographic monitoring, because it can cause heart block or bradycardia.
    \item \textbf{Steroid bridge:} short courses of prednisolone can terminate attacks rapidly at the start of a cluster period while the preventive drug is being titrated.
    \item \textbf{Occipital nerve block:} an injection of local anaesthetic and steroid around the greater occipital nerve can suppress attacks for several weeks and is useful when medications are poorly tolerated.
    \item \textbf{Neuromodulation:} devices such as vagus nerve stimulation and sphenopalatine ganglion stimulation offer options for people with chronic cluster headache who do not respond to medication.
    \item \textbf{Surgical options:} for severely disabled, medically refractory patients, procedures targeting the sphenopalatine ganglion or hypothalamus are undertaken in specialist centres.
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} avoidance of alcohol and other triggers during cluster periods, and maintenance of a regular sleep--wake schedule.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Both the disorder itself and its treatments can give rise to complications, and the psychological burden is substantial.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe impairment of quality of life, work performance, and relationships because of the frequency and intensity of attacks.
    \item Chronic sleep deprivation, since attacks so often wake patients during the night.
    \item Depression, anxiety, and a recognised increased risk of suicidal thoughts during active periods; this risk should be actively explored.
    \item Side effects of verapamil, including hypotension, ankle swelling, constipation, and cardiac conduction disturbances requiring regular electrocardiography.
    \item Medication-overuse headache if simple analgesics or triptans are used excessively between attacks.
    \item Physical injury during attacks from falls, or head-banging and self-inflicted trauma during agitation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Cluster headache is a lifelong condition for the majority of patients, but its natural history is generally favourable: attacks and cluster periods frequently become less severe, shorter, and less frequent with advancing age. In around one in five patients the disorder follows a chronic course, with remissions lasting less than one year. Most patients respond to a combination of acute and preventive therapy, and many achieve good control that allows them to work and live normally. Early diagnosis, patient education, and ongoing specialist support are the strongest predictors of a good outcome, and effective treatments mean that no patient should be left to endure attacks untreated.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Identify and strictly avoid personal triggers, particularly alcohol, throughout any cluster period.
    \item Maintain a regular sleep schedule, avoid daytime napping, and keep shift patterns as consistent as possible when at risk of attacks.
    \item Take preventive medication exactly as prescribed for the entire cluster period, even when attack-free.
    \item Use acute treatment early in an attack, as soon as pain begins, for the best chance of aborting it.
    \item Keep oxygen equipment and rescue medication available at home, at work, and when travelling.
    \item Keep a headache diary to help your specialist fine-tune preventive therapy and recognise patterns.
    \item Seek emotional and psychological support for the burden of the condition, including urgent professional help if thoughts of self-harm arise.
\end{itemize}
