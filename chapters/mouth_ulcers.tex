\chapter{Mouth Ulcers}
\index{Mouth Ulcers}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Mouth ulcers, also known as aphthae or oral ulcerations, are painful mucosal lesions characterised by a breach in the epithelial integrity of the oral cavity. They present as shallow, round or ovoid depressions with a greyish-white or yellow necrotising centre and a surrounding erythematous border. Mouth ulcers can occur as solitary or multiple lesions, and while the majority are benign and self-limiting (such as recurrent aphthous stomatitis or traumatic ulcers), chronic or persistent ulcerations can be a manifestation of systemic inflammatory diseases, infections, drug reactions, or oral malignancies.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Mouth ulcers are extremely common, affecting up to 20--25\% of the global population at some stage of life. Recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS) is the most frequent form, typically starting in childhood or adolescence, peaking in young adults, and gradually decreasing in frequency and severity with age. There is a slight female predisposition. In many countries, surveys indicate that recurrent mouth ulcers represent a major source of minor oral discomfort, leading to frequent self-medication and pharmacy consultations. While minor aphthae account for approximately 80--85\% of RAS cases, major aphthae represent 10--15\%, and herpetiform ulceration represents less than 5\% of cases.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The pathogenesis of mouth ulcers is multifactorial, involving localised trauma, immunologically mediated damage, nutritional deficiencies, and genetic predisposition.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Local trauma:} Mechanical injury to the oral mucosa caused by accidental biting, sharp teeth, orthodontic appliances, hard food, or aggressive toothbrushing.
    \item \textbf{Immunological mechanisms:} A cell-mediated immune response where T-lymphocytes and cytokines (such as TNF-alpha) target and destroy oral epithelial cells, leading to localised tissue necrosis.
    \item \textbf{Nutritional deficiencies:} Inadequate levels of iron, folate, vitamin B12, and vitamin B6, which impair mucosal cell regeneration and increase susceptibility to ulceration.
    \item \textbf{Genetic predisposition:} A strong hereditary link is present, with up to 40\% of patients with recurrent aphthous stomatitis having a family history of the condition.
    \item \textbf{Systemic inflammatory diseases:} Conditions such as coeliac disease, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, and Behçet's disease, which trigger mucosal inflammation throughout the gastrointestinal tract.
    \item \textbf{Chemical and drug irritation:} Contact hypersensitivity to dental products containing sodium lauryl sulphate (SLS) or systemic medications like non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and nicorandil.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Mouth ulcers present with localised pain, mucosal destruction, and varying patterns of recurrence.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Minor aphthous ulcers:} Small (less than 10 mm in diameter), shallow, painful ulcers that heal without scarring within 7 to 14 days, commonly located on non-keratinised mucosa (such as the inner lips, cheeks, or floor of the mouth).
    \item \textbf{Major aphthous ulcers:} Large (greater than 10 mm), deep, extremely painful ulcers that can last for several weeks to months and often heal with scarring, occasionally occurring on keratinised surfaces like the palate or tongue.
    \item \textbf{Herpetiform ulcers:} Multiple pinhead-sized (1--2 mm) crops of numerous tiny ulcers that can coalesce to form larger irregular erosions, resolving in 10--30 days.
    \item \textbf{Localised pain and discomfort:} Sharp, burning pain that is exacerbated by eating, drinking, speaking, or oral hygiene activities.
    \item \textbf{Systemic signs:} Occasional low-grade fever, lymphadenopathy, and fatigue, particularly during the initial eruptive phase of major or herpetiform ulcers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
It is essential to distinguish benign aphthae from ulcers caused by specific infections, autoimmune disorders, or malignancy.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Oral squamous cell carcinoma:} Characterised by a single, chronic, painless (initially) ulcer with raised, rolled, indurated borders that persists for more than 3 weeks, particularly in high-risk areas like the lateral tongue or floor of the mouth.
    \item \textbf{Herpes simplex virus (HSV) stomatitis:} Presents with widespread, painful fluid-filled vesicles that quickly rupture into multiple ulcers, typically involving the keratinised gingiva and hard palate, and accompanied by high fever.
    \item \textbf{Coeliac disease and Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD):} Recurrent mouth ulcers may be the presenting symptom of these conditions, distinguished by systemic features such as chronic diarrhoea, weight loss, and abdominal pain.
    \item \textbf{Erythema multiforme:} An acute hypersensitivity reaction causing extensive targetoid skin lesions and painful, bleeding haemorrhagic crusting of the lips and oral mucosa.
    \item \textbf{Behçet's disease:} A systemic vasculitis characterised by recurrent, severe oral ulcers, genital ulcers, and ocular inflammation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should seek medical or dental evaluation for mouth ulcers that do not heal within two weeks, are unusually large or painful, recur frequently, or are accompanied by systemic symptoms like fever or difficulty swallowing.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe airway compromise or difficulty breathing due to rapid, massive swelling of the tongue or throat.
    \item Signs of severe systemic infection or sepsis (such as high fever, confusion, or rapid heart rate) stemming from a secondary bacterial infection of an ulcer.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is primarily clinical, with investigations focused on identifying underlying systemic causes or ruling out malignancy.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Biopsy of the lesion:} Indicated for any solitary, non-healing ulcer persisting for more than 14 days to exclude oral squamous cell carcinoma.
    \item \textbf{Haematological screening:} Full blood count (to screen for anaemia or neutropenia), iron studies, serum vitamin B12, and red cell folate.
    \item \textbf{Coeliac disease serology:} Tissue transglutaminase IgA antibodies to screen for coeliac-induced aphthae.
    \item \textbf{Microbiological swabs:} Swabbing for HSV-1/2 PCR or fungal culture if an atypical infection is suspected.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment focuses on relieving pain, accelerating healing, and reducing the frequency of recurrences.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Topical corticosteroids:} The primary medical treatment, utilising triamcinolone acetoide in oral paste, clobetasol gel, or hydrocortisone lozenges applied directly to the ulcer.
    \item \textbf{Analgesics and anaesthetics:} Topical application of lidocaine gel, benzydamine mouthwash, or choline salicylate gel to numb the area and reduce pain during meals.
    \item \textbf{Protective barrier pastes:} Over-the-counter mucosal patches or paste formulations (such as Orabase) that cover the ulcer, protecting it from irritation.
    \item \textbf{Nutritional supplementation:} Oral replacement of iron, folate, or vitamin B12 in patients with documented deficiencies.
    \item \textbf{Systemic therapies:} Reserved for severe, treatment-resistant major aphthae or Behçet's disease, including oral prednisolone, colchicine, or immunomodulators.
    \item \textbf{Avoidance of irritants:} Using sodium lauryl sulphate-free toothpaste and avoiding sharp, spicy, acidic, or salty foods.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Although most ulcers are benign, severe or chronic ulceration can lead to physical and psychological complications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Secondary bacterial infection:} Opportunistic infection of the open ulcer by oral bacteria, leading to increased pain, swelling, and delayed healing.
    \item \textbf{Scarring and tissue deformity:} Deep major aphthous ulcers can cause significant tissue destruction, leading to fibrous scarring that restricts mouth opening or tongue mobility.
    \item \textbf{Weight loss and dehydration:} Severe pain leading to inadequate intake of food and fluids, particularly in children or elderly patients.
    \item \textbf{Delayed diagnosis of malignancy:} Mistaking a persistent oral cancer for a benign aphthous ulcer, resulting in advanced-stage presentation and poorer outcomes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for the vast majority of mouth ulcers is excellent. Minor aphthous ulcers resolve spontaneously within 10 to 14 days without scarring. While recurrent aphthous stomatitis can be frustrating and painful, it is a self-limiting condition that tends to burn out as patients reach middle age. Major ulcers can take several weeks or months to heal, but respond well to potent topical or systemic corticosteroids. When mouth ulcers are secondary to a systemic condition (such as coeliac disease), treating the underlying disease typically leads to complete resolution of the oral lesions.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Self-care measures can help prevent mouth ulcers and minimise the severity of outbreaks.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Choose SLS-free toothpaste:} Use toothpastes that do not contain sodium lauryl sulphate, a detergent that can irritate the oral mucosa.
    \item \textbf{Practise gentle oral hygiene:} Use a soft-bristled toothbrush and avoid rapid, aggressive brushing movements to prevent accidental cuts.
    \item \textbf{Avoid triggering foods:} Eliminate very sharp, crunchy, spicy, acidic, or hot foods and beverages from the diet if they trigger outbreaks.
    \item \textbf{Manage stress levels:} Incorporate stress-reduction techniques such as regular exercise, meditation, or breathing exercises, as stress is a common trigger for recurrent ulcers.
    \item \textbf{Address dental issues:} Visit a dentist to smooth sharp tooth edges, repair broken restorations, or adjust ill-fitting dentures and orthodontic brackets.
\end{itemize}
