\chapter{Cervical Screening Test}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The cervical screening test is a test that looks for infection with the human papillomavirus (HPV) in the cells of the cervix, the lower part of the uterus that opens into the vagina. High-risk types of HPV cause almost all cervical cancers, so the test is designed to find women at risk of developing the disease long before cancer appears.

In Australia, the test replaced the older Pap smear and involves taking a small sample of cells from the cervix, either by a clinician or by the woman herself using a self-collection swab. The sample is tested for HPV, and if high-risk HPV is found, the same sample is checked for abnormal cell changes. This allows pre-cancerous changes to be detected and treated early, making cervical cancer one of the most preventable cancers.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cervical screening is one of the most effective cancer prevention measures ever developed. In Australia, the organised screening program, together with HPV vaccination, has reduced cervical cancer incidence and death rates dramatically, and Australia is on track to become one of the first countries to eliminate cervical cancer as a public health problem.

Routine screening is recommended every five years from age 25 to 74 for people with a cervix who have ever been sexually active. About 1 in 12 screening tests detects HPV, and most of these women do not have cancer; the test simply flags the need for closer monitoring or further investigation. Participation in screening is the single biggest factor that determines whether cervical cancer develops.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The cervical screening test detects the cause of cervical cancer rather than the cancer itself:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HPV infection:} a common sexually transmitted infection, usually cleared by the immune system within one to two years
    \item \textbf{High-risk types:} HPV 16 and 18 cause most cervical cancers; other high-risk types cause the remainder
    \item \textbf{Persistent infection:} when the virus is not cleared, it can drive pre-cancerous cell changes
    \item \textbf{Progression:} over many years, low-grade changes can progress to high-grade changes and eventually invasive cancer
    \item \textbf{Role of screening:} testing for HPV identifies who needs follow-up, and looking for cell changes finds pre-cancer before it becomes invasive
\end{itemize}

Because the progression from HPV infection to cancer takes many years, regular five-yearly screening reliably detects problems within a window in which they can still be treated simply.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The cervical screening test itself is a screening test performed on women without symptoms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A sample of cells is collected from the cervix during a speculum examination, or by the woman using a self-collection vaginal swab
    \item It is usually quick and causes only minor, brief discomfort
    \item Results take one to two weeks to return
    \item A negative result means no HPV was found and the next test is due in five years
    \item A positive result means high-risk HPV was found, and the sample is checked for abnormal cells
    \item An abnormal result does not mean cancer; most abnormal results are followed up and resolve or are treated before cancer develops
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
An abnormal screening result raises the question of what the underlying change is:

\begin{itemize}
    \item HPV infection without cell changes, which often clears on its own
    \item Low-grade pre-cancerous changes, which may resolve without treatment
    \item High-grade pre-cancerous changes, which warrant treatment
    \item Invasive cervical cancer, which requires full staging and treatment
    \item Benign conditions that can cause false-positive appearances, such as inflammation, infection, or cervical ectropion
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Take part in cervical screening every five years from age 25 to 74, and attend any follow-up recommended after an abnormal result. See a doctor promptly if you have bleeding between periods, after sex, or after the menopause, an unusual discharge, or pain during intercourse.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:} very heavy vaginal bleeding that soaks through pads rapidly, severe pelvic pain, or fever with heavy bleeding, which require urgent assessment.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary HPV test:} the first test in the screening pathway; if no high-risk HPV is found, no further testing is needed for five years
    \item \textbf{Liquid-based cytology:} examination of cells under the microscope, performed automatically when HPV is detected
    \item \textbf{Self-collection:} a vaginal swab that the woman collects herself, now an option for most women and as accurate as a clinician-collected sample
    \item \textbf{Colposcopy:} a magnified examination of the cervix arranged after an abnormal screening result
    \item \textbf{Cervical biopsy:} small tissue samples taken at colposcopy to confirm the severity of any changes
    \item \textbf{Follow-up testing:} repeated HPV tests at intervals depending on the initial result, to monitor whether infection clears
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Negative result:} no treatment needed; the next test is due in five years
    \item \textbf{HPV with no cell changes:} repeat testing in twelve months, since the infection often clears
    \item \textbf{Low-grade changes:} usually monitored, as many regress without treatment
    \item \textbf{High-grade changes:} treatment to remove the abnormal area, such as loop excision (LLETZ), laser, or freezing
    \item \textbf{Colposcopy:} the key step that determines whether treatment is needed
    \item \textbf{HPV vaccination:} recommended separately from screening; vaccination does not replace screening
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} repeat screening at the recommended interval after any abnormality, and continued routine screening after treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Missing screening appointments leaves pre-cancerous change undetected and raises the risk of developing cervical cancer
    \item Anxiety and worry associated with an abnormal result, even though most do not indicate cancer
    \item Small risks of the screening test itself, such as minor discomfort or bleeding during sampling
    \item Small risks of treatment for high-grade changes, including bleeding, infection, and a small effect on future pregnancy
    \item False reassurance if symptoms develop between screening tests; screening is not a test for current symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for women who take part in screening is excellent. Pre-cancerous changes are almost always detected and treated before they become cancer, and the great majority of women with a positive result never develop cervical cancer. When cervical cancer does occur, it is usually in women who have never been screened or who have not kept up with follow-up. Regular five-yearly screening, combined with HPV vaccination, offers the highest level of protection available against this largely preventable disease.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Have the HPV vaccine, ideally before sexual debut, whether or not you have been vaccinated
    \item Book and attend cervical screening every five years from age 25 to 74
    \item Continue screening even if you have had the HPV vaccine, because the vaccine does not cover every cancer-causing type
    \item Ask about self-collection if you find speculum examinations difficult
    \item Do not smoke, as smoking increases the risk of HPV persisting
    \item Use condoms to reduce the risk of HPV and other sexually transmitted infections
    \item Report unusual bleeding or discharge promptly rather than waiting for the next screening test
    \item Attend all recommended follow-up after an abnormal result
\end{itemize}
