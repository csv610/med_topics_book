\chapter{Hemorrhoids}
\index{Hemorrhoids}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hemorrhoids, also spelled haemorrhoids and commonly called piles, are swollen blood vessels in the lower rectum and anus. They are part of the normal anatomy of the anal canal, but when they become enlarged, they can bleed, itch, hurt, and prolapse through the anus. Hemorrhoids are extremely common and are usually linked to straining, constipation, pregnancy, and prolonged sitting. They are generally not serious, do not cause cancer, and most can be managed with diet, fluids, and simple self-care. Persistent or severe hemorrhoids can be treated with banding, other office procedures, or surgery.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hemorrhoids affect a large proportion of the population. Studies suggest that up to 50--75% of people will have hemorrhoids at some point in their lives, and around 4--5% of the population has symptomatic hemorrhoids at any time. The prevalence increases with age, peaking between 45 and 65 years, and risk is higher in people with chronic constipation, obesity, and pregnancy. Men and women are affected similarly. Many people self-treat and never see a doctor, so the true figure is likely higher.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Hemorrhoids form when the vascular cushions in the anal canal enlarge under pressure.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal cushions:} the anal canal contains cushions of blood vessels that help keep the anus closed
    \item \textbf{Pressure and strain:} chronic constipation, straining at stool, and prolonged sitting increase pressure in the anal veins
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} the growing uterus and hormone changes increase pelvic pressure
    \item \textbf{Obesity and diet:} low-fibre diets and excess weight contribute
    \item \textbf{Internal hemorrhoids:} form above the dentate line, typically bleeding and prolapsing without pain
    \item \textbf{External hemorrhoids:} form below it, causing pain and itching, and can thrombose (clot)
    \item \textbf{Progression:} internal hemorrhoids are graded by how far they prolapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms of hemorrhoids are familiar to most people.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Bright red blood on toilet paper, in the bowl, or on the stool
    \item Itching and irritation around the anus
    \item A lump or swelling at the anus
    \item Discomfort, soreness, and a feeling of incomplete emptying
    \item Prolapse: tissue that protrudes and may need to be pushed back
    \item Sudden, severe pain with a tender lump from a thrombosed external hemorrhoid
    \item Mucus discharge and soilage
    \item Symptoms that come and go, often worse with constipation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Anal symptoms and bleeding can have other, more serious causes.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Anal fissure, which causes sharp pain and bleeding with bowel movements
    \item Anal fistula and perianal abscess
    \item Colorectal cancer and polyps, which can bleed
    \item Inflammatory bowel disease
    \item Rectal prolapse
    \item Anal warts and skin tags
    \item Anal itching from dermatitis, infections, or hygiene issues
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for any rectal bleeding, persistent anal symptoms, or a lump that does not go away. Rectal bleeding should always be investigated, especially in people over 50 or with a family history of bowel cancer.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heavy or continuous rectal bleeding
    \item Severe sudden anal pain with a tender, swollen lump
    \item Fever with anal pain or swelling, which may indicate infection
    \item An inability to pass stool or urine with severe pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is usually straightforward, with tests to exclude other causes when needed.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} symptoms and risk factors
    \item \textbf{Examination:} inspection of the anus and digital rectal examination
    \item \textbf{Anoscopy or proctoscopy:} internal inspection of the anal canal
    \item \textbf{Colonoscopy:} for bleeding, especially in people over 50
    \item \textbf{Blood count:} if there has been significant bleeding
    \item \textbf{Staging:} internal hemorrhoids are graded 1--4
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Most hemorrhoids respond to simple measures; treatment escalates as needed.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fibre and fluids:} a high-fibre diet and plenty of water soften stools and reduce straining
    \item \textbf{Regular exercise:} promotes normal bowel function
    \item \textbf{Topical treatments:} creams, ointments, and suppositories for pain and itching
    \item \textbf{Warm baths:} soaks relieve discomfort
    \item \textbf{Rubber band ligation:} the most common office procedure for internal hemorrhoids
    \item \textbf{Sclerotherapy and coagulation:} alternatives for small internal hemorrhoids
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} hemorrhoidectomy for large, prolapsing, or recurrent hemorrhoids
    \item \textbf{Managing contributing factors:} weight, constipation, and sitting habits
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Anemia from chronic bleeding (uncommon)
    \item Thrombosed external hemorrhoids with severe pain
    \item Strangulated prolapsed hemorrhoids requiring urgent treatment
    \item Infection (rare)
    \item Complications of treatment, including pain, bleeding, and recurrence
    \item Masking of more serious conditions when bleeding is not investigated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Hemorrhoids are a benign condition with a good outlook. Most improve with dietary and lifestyle measures, and office procedures resolve most persistent internal hemorrhoids. Surgery cures severe cases with low recurrence when lifestyle measures continue. Hemorrhoids do not cause cancer and do not increase cancer risk, but the bleeding they cause can mimic bowel cancer, so any unexplained bleeding should be investigated, particularly in older people.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Eat plenty of fibre-rich foods and drink adequate water
    \item Exercise regularly
    \item Do not strain or sit on the toilet for long periods
    \item Respond to the urge to open your bowels promptly
    \item Maintain a healthy weight
    \item Take regular breaks from prolonged sitting
    \item Keep the anal area clean, using gentle washing rather than vigorous wiping
    \item See a doctor for bleeding or persistent symptoms
    \item If you have diabetes or take blood thinners, discuss any bleeding with your doctor
\end{itemize}
