\chapter{Feeding Your Newborn Baby}
\index{Feeding Your Newborn Baby}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Feeding a newborn baby is one of the most important and frequent tasks of early parenting, and getting it right lays the foundation for growth, development, and bonding. In the first months, a baby needs either breast milk or formula, fed frequently, in amounts that meet their rapidly increasing needs. Breastfeeding is recommended as the ideal nutrition for babies, providing antibodies, the right balance of nutrients, and long-term health benefits for both mother and baby. However, formula feeding is a safe and healthy alternative when breastfeeding is not possible or not chosen. This chapter explains how newborn feeding works, how to tell if a baby is getting enough, and how to manage common feeding challenges.

\section*{Epidemiology}
In many countries, more than 90\% of mothers initiate breastfeeding, and the majority breastfeed for at least a few months. Exclusive breastfeeding, with no other food or fluid, is recommended for the first 6 months, and continued breastfeeding alongside solids for up to 12 months and beyond. In practice, however, exclusive breastfeeding at 6 months is achieved by only around 15--25\% of local babies, with many mothers stopping earlier than they intended because of difficulties such as latch problems, pain, and perceived low milk supply. Formula feeding is used by a large proportion of families, either exclusively or alongside breast milk. Support from trained professionals significantly improves breastfeeding duration and success.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Understanding how feeding works helps parents respond to their baby's cues.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Breast milk production:} milk is produced on a supply-and-demand basis; frequent and effective feeding stimulates the hormone prolactin, which drives milk production
    \item \textbf{Milk release:} the hormone oxytocin causes the milk ejection (let-down) reflex, so milk flows when the baby suckles
    \item \textbf{Colostrum:} the thick, yellowish first milk is rich in antibodies and perfectly matched to the newborn's needs in the first days
    \item \textbf{Stomach capacity:} a newborn's stomach is tiny, about the size of a cherry at birth, which is why feeds are frequent and small
    \item \textbf{Growth spurts:} babies cluster-feed during growth spurts around 2--3 weeks, 6 weeks, and 3 months, temporarily increasing demand
    \item \textbf{Formula:} modern formulas are carefully designed to resemble breast milk, with iron, vitamins, and essential fatty acids added
    \item \textbf{Feeding cues:} rooting, sucking on hands, and mouthing indicate hunger, while crying is a late hunger cue
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Normal newborn feeding has recognisable patterns.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Feeding 8--12 times in 24 hours in the early weeks, on demand rather than on a strict schedule
    \item Each feed lasting 10--40 minutes at the breast, or 60--90 ml of formula per feed in the first month, increasing over time
    \item Swallowing heard during breastfeeds and milk visible at the corner of the mouth
    \item The baby settles and appears content after most feeds
    \item Wet nappies: at least 6--8 a day after the first week, with pale urine
    \item Stools: meconium in the first days, then frequent soft yellow stools in breastfed babies
    \item Weight gain along the growth centile, regaining birth weight by around 2 weeks
    \item Contentment between feeds and normal waking and sleeping patterns
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When feeding is not going well, several issues are common.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Poor latch or positioning, the most common cause of painful and ineffective feeding
    \item Perceived low milk supply, which is frequently a misunderstanding of normal cluster feeding rather than a true shortfall
    \item Tongue-tie, which can interfere with effective attachment in some babies
    \item Nipple pain, thrush, or mastitis in the mother
    \item Inadequate weight gain, which may reflect underfeeding, illness, or a metabolic problem
    \item Jaundice or dehydration in a baby who is not feeding well
    \item Reflux and excessive possetting, which worry parents but are usually benign
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents should seek help early with feeding difficulties, sore nipples, concerns about milk supply, or questions about the baby's weight. A midwife, lactation consultant, or child health nurse can provide expert support.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A newborn who is floppy, unresponsive, or hard to wake
    \item Signs of dehydration: no wet nappies for 6--8 hours, a sunken soft spot, or dry mouth in a young baby
    \item Breathing difficulty, unusual grunting, or a blue tinge to the skin
    \item A baby refusing all feeds for more than a few hours with signs of illness
    \item Seizures or fitting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Weight checks:} regular weighing, ideally at the same time of day, plotted on a growth chart; the key is the trend rather than any single number
    \item \textbf{Wet and dirty nappy count:} a reliable daily indicator of adequate intake
    \item \textbf{Observation of a feed:} a midwife or lactation consultant watching a feed identifies latch, positioning, and swallowing problems
    \item \textbf{Baby checks:} examination for tongue-tie, jaundice, and general wellbeing
    \item \textbf{Mother checks:} examination of the breasts for engorgement, blocked ducts, mastitis, or nipple damage
    \item \textbf{Milk supply assessment:} occasionally by test weighing before and after a feed, or expressing to estimate volume
    \item \textbf{Medical tests:} only when illness is suspected, such as infection or metabolic disorders
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Feed on demand:} respond to early hunger cues, allowing the baby to feed as often and as long as they need
    \item \textbf{Positioning and latch:} skin-to-skin contact, a comfortable supported position, and a deep, asymmetric latch reduce pain and improve milk transfer
    \item \textbf{Breast care:} treat engorgement with frequent feeding and warmth before feeds; treat sore nipples by correcting the latch and using expressed milk or lanolin
    \item \textbf{Mastitis:} continue feeding from the affected breast, rest, and seek medical care for antibiotics if needed
    \item \textbf{Formula feeding:} prepare formula strictly according to instructions using cooled boiled water, feed responsively, and discard unused formula
    \item \textbf{Supplements:} vitamin D drops for breastfed babies and iron-rich solids from 6 months
    \item \textbf{Professional support:} lactation consultants, child health nurses, and GPs can resolve most feeding difficulties
    \item \textbf{Expressing:} pumping can relieve engorgement, support return to work, and allow partners to share feeds
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Poor weight gain and growth faltering from inadequate intake
    \item Dehydration and neonatal jaundice in the first days if feeding is ineffective
    \item Mastitis and breast abscess from untreated blocked ducts
    \item Sore, cracked nipples that can discourage breastfeeding
    \item Maternal stress and postnatal depression associated with feeding difficulties
    \item Risk of contamination or incorrect concentration with improperly prepared formula
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With support, most feeding difficulties resolve within the first few weeks, and the vast majority of babies grow and thrive whether they are breastfed or formula-fed. Breastfeeding confers short- and long-term benefits, including fewer infections in infancy and lower rates of some chronic diseases later, and it supports bonding and maternal recovery. These benefits are greatest with exclusive breastfeeding but are seen with any amount of breast milk, and they are modest in comparison with the love and care of a responsive parent. The best feeding plan is the one that works for each family, and parents should be supported without judgement in their choices.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Learn about feeding before the birth, including skin-to-skin contact in the first hour
    \item Ask for help early, in the maternity ward, with positioning and latch
    \item Feed in response to the baby's cues, not the clock, in the early weeks
    \item Keep the baby close and feed frequently to establish milk supply
    \item Look after yourself: rest when the baby sleeps, eat well, and drink plenty of fluid
    \item If formula feeding, follow preparation instructions exactly and never water down formula
    \item Accept help from family, friends, and health professionals, and know where to find support if breastfeeding is hard
\end{itemize}
