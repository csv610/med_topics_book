\section*{Definition and Overview}
Constipation in children means having difficult, painful, or infrequent bowel movements. It is defined roughly as fewer than three stools a week, passing large, hard stools, or soiling because a mass of hard stool has built up in the bowel. It is very common and usually has no serious underlying cause, but it can become a long-term problem if it is not managed early. Treatment focuses on emptying the bowel, softening stools, and establishing comfortable toilet habits.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Constipation affects roughly 1 in 10 children at some time and is one of the most common reasons children see a GP. It is most frequent in the toddler and preschool years, particularly around toilet training, and again around school entry. It is more common in boys than girls in early childhood. Many children first develop constipation after a painful bowel movement, an illness, or a change in routine such as starting school or travelling.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Functional constipation:} the great majority of cases, with no physical cause found
    \item \textbf{Pain avoidance:} a child withholds stool after a painful passage, so stool hardens and is even more painful, creating a vicious cycle
    \item \textbf{Toilet training and routine:} delaying using unfamiliar toilets, or being too busy to go
    \item \textbf{Diet:} low fibre, insufficient fluids, and excessive cow's milk
    \item \textbf{Behavioural factors:} stress, family changes, or anxiety about using the toilet
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} some medicines can slow the bowel
    \item \textbf{Rare physical causes:} Hirschsprung's disease, thyroid problems, and disorders of the nerves or muscles of the bowel
\end{itemize}

When stool is withheld, the rectum stretches and becomes less sensitive, so the urge to pass stool is felt less and less. The result is a large, packed bowel with hard stool that leaks liquid stool around it (overflow soiling).

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infrequent bowel movements: fewer than three a week
    \item Large, hard, or pellet-like stools that are painful to pass
    \item Straining and pain with bowel movements
    \item Abdominal pain and bloating
    \item Soiling or smearing of underclothes (overflow soiling)
    \item Poor appetite and occasional vomiting
    \item A child hiding, squatting, or rocking to hold in a stool
    \item Irritability and behaviour change around toileting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hirschsprung's disease, especially with delayed first poo in a newborn and failure to thrive
    \item Hypothyroidism and other metabolic conditions
    \item Coeliac disease, with poor growth and bloating
    \item Anatomical problems such as anal stenosis or a tight anal sphincter
    \item Spinal cord problems causing altered leg function or bladder symptoms
    \item Lead poisoning or other rarities, depending on history
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if constipation is severe, does not improve with simple measures, or you are worried about your child. A newborn who has not passed a first stool (meconium) within 48 hours needs review.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services or seek urgent care if the child:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Has severe abdominal pain with vomiting or a swollen, hard tummy
    \item Passes a lot of blood, or blood with a painful stool in a baby
    \item Appears limp, unwell, or has a fever with the constipation
    \item Has weakness or numbness in the legs, or trouble with passing urine
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} stool frequency and consistency, soiling, pain, diet, fluid intake, toilet habits, and family changes
    \item \textbf{Examination:} growth (height and weight) plotted on growth charts, abdominal examination, and inspection of the back and anal area
    \item \textbf{Rectal examination:} generally avoided in children unless another cause is suspected
    \item \textbf{Abdominal X-ray:} occasionally used to show the amount of stool in the bowel
    \item \textbf{Blood tests and biopsy:} only when a physical cause is suspected, e.g.\ thyroid tests or a rectal biopsy for Hirschsprung's disease
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a paediatrician if symptoms are severe, persistent, or unusual
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Disimpaction first:} a doctor may prescribe a course of laxatives to clear the packed bowel, which is essential before prevention can work
    \item \textbf{Maintenance laxatives:} continued for several months even after symptoms settle, to keep stools soft while habits are retrained
    \item \textbf{Diet and fluids:} plenty of water, fruits, vegetables, and wholegrains; avoid excessive cow's milk
    \item \textbf{Toilet routine:} regular, unhurried sitting on the toilet after meals, with rewards and praise for sitting (not just for producing a stool)
    \item \textbf{No punishment:} avoid blame or punishment for soiling; this reduces anxiety and withholding
    \item \textbf{Physical activity:} daily movement encourages bowel activity
    \item \textbf{Review:} regular check-ups to adjust laxative doses and support the family
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Faecal impaction with severe abdominal pain
    \item Overflow soiling that causes embarrassment and social difficulties
    \item Anal fissures and bleeding from passing hard stools
    \item Chronic retention of stool and reduced sensation of needing to go
    \item Loss of appetite and poor growth if untreated
    \item Behavioural and emotional distress, including toilet avoidance
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most children with functional constipation recover fully with consistent treatment, though improvement can take months because the stretched bowel needs time to regain normal tone. Up to a third of children may have symptoms that persist or recur over several years, so ongoing follow-up matters. Relapses are common when laxatives are stopped too early. With reassurance, diet, laxatives used as directed, and positive toilet habits, the large majority of children achieve normal bowel function.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Encourage a fibre-rich diet and regular water intake from an early age
    \item Set a daily toilet routine, especially after meals
    \item Make toileting relaxed, unhurried, and free of pressure
    \item Respond to a child's need to go promptly, including at school
    \item Maintain regular physical activity
    \item Use positive reinforcement and avoid punishing or shaming soiling
    \item Seek advice early if bowel movements become painful or infrequent
\end{itemize}
