\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cold sores are small, painful blisters that usually appear on or around the lips, and sometimes on the nose or chin. They are caused by the herpes simplex virus (HSV), most commonly type 1 (HSV-1), and are extremely common. After the first infection the virus stays in the body for life, hiding in nerve cells, and can reactivate from time to time to produce new sores. Cold sores are contagious and are spread by direct contact with the blister, saliva, or shared items such as cups and towels. Although they are harmless for most people, they can be more serious in newborn babies and in people with weakened immune systems.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cold sores are one of the most common infections worldwide. Most people are infected with HSV-1 in childhood, often without obvious symptoms. It is estimated that around two-thirds of the world's population carry the virus, although many never develop visible cold sores. Among those who do, outbreaks tend to become less frequent with age. Recurrences are triggered by factors such as stress, illness, fatigue, sun exposure, cold weather, and hormonal changes such as menstruation. Most people have fewer than three outbreaks per year, but some have them monthly.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Cold sores are caused by the herpes simplex virus. The first infection usually occurs in childhood through contact with an infected person, and may cause a mild illness with mouth ulcers and sore gums, or no symptoms at all. After this primary infection:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The virus travels along nerve fibres and becomes dormant (inactive) in clusters of nerve cells near the site of infection
    \item On reactivation, the virus travels back down the nerve to the skin, where it reproduces and forms a blister
    \item Reactivation is triggered by events that stress the body, such as fever, infection, sunburn, fatigue, emotional stress, or hormonal changes
    \item The immune system usually controls the virus within a few days, and the sore heals
    \item The virus does not leave the body, so new outbreaks are possible at any time
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Cold sores typically develop in predictable stages:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tingling:} itching, burning, or tingling on the lip several hours before the sore appears
    \item \textbf{Blisters:} small, fluid-filled blisters form, often in a cluster
    \item \textbf{Ulceration:} the blisters burst or weep, leaving a shallow, tender sore
    \item \textbf{Crusting:} a yellow or brown crust forms over the sore
    \item \textbf{Healing:} the crust falls off, usually leaving normal skin behind
    \item Associated features can include mild swelling of the lip, and in some people a low-grade fever or swollen lymph glands, especially with the first infection
\end{itemize}

The whole process usually takes about one to two weeks.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Impetigo, a bacterial skin infection that can also cause crusted sores around the mouth
    \item Mouth ulcers (aphthous ulcers), which occur inside the mouth and are not caused by the herpes virus
    \item Herpangina or hand, foot, and mouth disease, viral infections causing mouth sores in children
    \item Angular cheilitis, cracked and sore corners of the mouth, usually fungal or due to deficiency
    \item Allergic or irritant reactions to lip products
    \item A cold sore can occasionally be mistaken for an early shingles blister when it appears on the face
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most cold sores do not need medical treatment. See a doctor or pharmacist for advice if:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The sore is very large, painful, or does not heal within two weeks
    \item Outbreaks are frequent or severe
    \item The infection spreads near the eyes
    \item You have a weakened immune system, or you are undergoing chemotherapy or taking immune-suppressing medicines
    \item The person affected is a newborn baby or young infant
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:} signs of a serious infection, such as a very high fever, confusion, or difficulty breathing, or if a baby with cold sores becomes drowsy, floppy, or seriously unwell.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} cold sores have a characteristic appearance, and a diagnosis is usually made on sight
    \item \textbf{History:} asking about previous outbreaks, triggers, and exposure to others with cold sores
    \item \textbf{Viral swab:} a swab of fluid from a blister can confirm the herpes virus, mainly used when the diagnosis is unclear
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} antibody tests can show past exposure to the virus but are rarely needed for diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to an eye specialist if the infection involves or is close to the eye
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antiviral creams:} applied at the first sign of tingling can shorten the outbreak; available over the counter or on prescription
    \item \textbf{Oral antiviral tablets:} for frequent, severe, or spreading outbreaks, prescribed by a doctor
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple pain relievers such as paracetamol, and numbing creams or gels
    \item \textbf{Home care:} keep the area clean, apply a cold pack for comfort, and keep the lip moist with a plain balm
    \item \textbf{Prevent spread:} avoid kissing, sharing cups, cutlery, towels, or lip products while the sore is active
    \item \textbf{Hand hygiene:} wash hands after touching the sore, and avoid touching the eyes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Spread of the virus to other parts of the body, such as the fingers, through touching the sore
    \item Infection of the eye, which can threaten sight and requires urgent specialist care
    \item Secondary bacterial infection of the sore
    \item Severe, widespread infection in people with weakened immune systems
    \item Severe infection in newborns, which can be serious and requires hospital care
    \item Eczema herpeticum, a rapidly spreading infection over the skin of people with eczema
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For most people, cold sores are an occasional nuisance that clears within one to two weeks and causes no lasting problems. Antiviral treatment started early can shorten and ease outbreaks. The frequency of recurrences often falls with age. Because the virus remains in the body for life, there is no permanent cure, but most people manage outbreaks well with self-care and, when needed, antiviral treatment. People with weakened immune systems or eye involvement need medical care, as complications can be more serious.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Apply sunscreen or lip balm with SPF to the lips before sun exposure
    \item Protect against cold, wind, and extreme temperatures
    \item Keep stress levels down and get enough sleep
    \item Try to avoid or manage known triggers such as illness, fatigue, or hormonal changes
    \item Start antiviral treatment at the first sign of tingling
    \item Avoid close contact, kissing, and sharing personal items during an outbreak
    \item Wash hands regularly, especially after touching the face or the sore
\end{itemize}
