\chapter{Anti-Inflammatory Medicines}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Anti-inflammatory medicines are drugs that reduce inflammation in the body. Inflammation is the normal protective response to injury, infection, or irritation, and is marked by pain, redness, swelling, and heat. When inflammation becomes excessive, prolonged, or directed against the body's own tissues, medicines are used to dampen it down.

The main groups are the \textbf{non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)}, such as ibuprofen, naproxen, and diclofenac, and the \textbf{corticosteroids}, which work differently and are far more powerful. Some newer \textbf{biologic medicines} target specific inflammatory chemicals and are used for conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease. Paracetamol relieves pain and fever but is not a true anti-inflammatory medicine.

\section*{Epidemiology}
NSAIDs are among the most commonly used medicines in the world, and several are available over the counter without a prescription. They are used by people of all ages for headaches, musculoskeletal pain, arthritis, menstrual pain, and after injury or surgery. Corticosteroids are prescribed across many specialties for inflammatory and autoimmune conditions, from asthma and eczema to inflammatory bowel disease and arthritis.

Use of anti-inflammatory medicines increases with age, reflecting the rising burden of osteoarthritis and chronic pain in older adults. This matters because the same medicines are also less well tolerated with age, and the risk of stomach, kidney, and cardiovascular complications rises sharply in older users.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Inflammation is driven by chemical signals in the body, including \textbf{prostaglandins}, which are produced by enzymes called cyclo-oxygenase (COX). COX-1 protects the stomach lining and supports normal kidney function, whereas COX-2 is produced mainly at sites of inflammation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{NSAIDs} block the COX enzymes, reducing prostaglandin production and thereby relieving pain and swelling; blocking COX-1 also explains why NSAIDs can irritate the stomach
    \item \textbf{Corticosteroids} act inside cells to switch off many inflammatory genes at once, making them far more powerful but with more widespread effects
    \item \textbf{Biologics} neutralise specific inflammatory proteins, such as tumour necrosis factor (TNF), that drive disease in autoimmune conditions
\end{itemize}

The choice of medicine therefore balances effectiveness against the particular risks of each class.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical picture reflects both the intended benefit and the adverse effects of the medicines:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intended effect:} reduced pain, swelling, stiffness, and redness in the affected joint, muscle, or tissue
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal effects:} indigestion, heartburn, nausea, and stomach ulcers or bleeding, especially with NSAIDs
    \item \textbf{Kidney and fluid effects:} ankle swelling, fluid retention, and a rise in blood pressure
    \item \textbf{Corticosteroid effects:} weight gain, increased appetite, insomnia, mood changes, and raised blood sugar
    \item \textbf{Allergic reactions:} rash, wheezing, or rarely anaphylaxis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When symptoms develop or fail to improve while taking an anti-inflammatory medicine, the following should be considered:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Poor response due to the wrong diagnosis versus an inadequate dose or duration of treatment
    \item A flare of the underlying disease versus a side effect of the medicine
    \item Stomach ulcer or bleeding versus another cause of abdominal pain
    \item New or worsening fluid retention versus underlying kidney or heart disease
    \item A drug interaction versus an independent new condition
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek urgent medical advice if:

\begin{itemize}
    \item You vomit blood or pass black, tarry stools, or have severe upper abdominal pain
    \item You develop sudden shortness of breath or rapid swelling of the legs
    \item You pass little urine, or develop new confusion, especially while taking high doses
    \item You have taken too much medicine -- contact a poisons information line or emergency department
    \item You are taking corticosteroids and develop a fever or a serious infection
    \item You are pregnant or planning a pregnancy and have been taking these medicines
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, swelling of the face or throat, or collapse (possible severe allergic reaction)
    \item Vomiting blood, passing black or tarry stools, or fainting
    \item Severe chest pain or sudden breathlessness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Medication review:} a check of all medicines, including over-the-counter products, to detect double-ups and interactions
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} kidney function, liver function, and full blood count, especially with long-term use
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure monitoring:} NSAIDs and corticosteroids can both raise blood pressure
    \item \textbf{Endoscopy:} if a stomach ulcer or gastrointestinal bleeding is suspected
    \item \textbf{Bone density testing:} for people taking long-term corticosteroids
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lowest effective dose for the shortest time:} the guiding principle for all anti-inflammatory medicines
    \item \textbf{Take NSAIDs with food} to reduce stomach irritation, and use topical gels or creams for localised pain
    \item \textbf{Paracetamol:} a gentler alternative for simple pain where inflammation is not prominent
    \item \textbf{Stomach protection:} a proton pump inhibitor may be added for people at higher risk of bleeding
    \item \textbf{Corticosteroids:} use the lowest dose that controls symptoms, and taper the dose rather than stopping suddenly
    \item \textbf{Non-drug measures:} rest, ice, physiotherapy, and exercise are often as important as medicines
    \item \textbf{Specialist review:} biologic therapy is initiated and monitored by a specialist team
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Stomach ulcers and gastrointestinal bleeding with NSAIDs
    \item Kidney injury and fluid retention, particularly in older people and those with existing kidney disease
    \item An increased risk of heart attack and stroke with some NSAIDs at high doses or with long-term use
    \item Corticosteroid complications: osteoporosis, diabetes, weight gain, and adrenal suppression
    \item Interactions with blood-thinning medicines that raise the risk of bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people use anti-inflammatory medicines safely for short courses of pain relief. Side effects become more likely with long-term use, higher doses, older age, and pre-existing stomach, kidney, or heart disease. Symptoms such as indigestion or fluid retention usually settle once the dose is adjusted or the medicine is stopped.

For chronic inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis or inflammatory bowel disease, the outlook depends on the underlying disease and on how well treatment is monitored; biologic therapies have dramatically improved outcomes for many of these conditions, but they require specialist supervision.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use the lowest dose that works, for the shortest time needed
    \item Never take two anti-inflammatory medicines at once, and check combination pain-relief products for hidden NSAID ingredients
    \item Take medicines with food, and report indigestion, dark stools, or stomach pain early
    \item Avoid NSAIDs in pregnancy and in severe kidney, liver, or stomach disease unless advised otherwise by a doctor
    \item Have blood pressure, kidney, and bone checks if you take these medicines long term
    \item Ask a doctor or pharmacist how your medicines interact, especially with blood thinners
\end{itemize}
