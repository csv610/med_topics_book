\chapter{Basal Cell Carcinoma}
\index{Basal Cell Carcinoma}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) is the most common form of skin cancer. It arises from the basal cells of the epidermis, the deepest layer of the outer skin. BCC grows slowly and almost never spreads to other parts of the body, but it can invade and destroy surrounding skin and tissue if left untreated. Because it grows locally rather than metastasising, it is often described as a semi-malignant tumour. Early detection and treatment give an excellent outcome, and it is rarely life-threatening.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Basal cell carcinoma is the most common cancer in fair-skinned populations worldwide, and some countries have one of the highest rates on Earth. Roughly two in three people of European ancestry living in sunny regions will develop a BCC at some point. The risk rises steeply with age, with most cases diagnosed after 50 years, although younger people are increasingly affected. Men are affected slightly more often than women. Cumulative and intermittent sun exposure are the dominant risk factors, so rates are highest where sun exposure and fair skin coincide.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{UV radiation:} ultraviolet light from the sun (and solariums) damages the DNA of skin cells; the damage is usually repaired, but over time mutations accumulate in the basal layer
    \item \textbf{Hedgehog pathway:} most BCCs involve mutations in genes of the hedgehog signalling pathway, which normally controls cell growth
    \item \textbf{Skin type:} fair skin that burns easily, light eyes and hair, and a tendency to freckle increase susceptibility
    \item \textbf{Other risk factors:} older age, male sex, immunosuppression (for example, after organ transplant), previous skin cancer, arsenic exposure, and rare genetic conditions such as Gorlin syndrome
    \item \textbf{Growth pattern:} BCC cells grow slowly and locally, invading the dermis and deeper tissue but rarely spreading through blood or lymphatics
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A pearly or translucent bump on the skin, often with visible small blood vessels (telangiectasia)
    \item A flat, scaly red patch that may itch or crust
    \item A sore that bleeds, crusts, and never quite heals, then recurs
    \item A white or waxy scar-like area without a definite border
    \item A nodule that may become ulcerated in the centre (rodent ulcer), with rolled edges
    \item Most often found on sun-exposed sites: face, ears, scalp, neck, shoulders, and back
    \item Slow growth over months to years
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Squamous cell carcinoma:} a more aggressive skin cancer
    \item \textbf{Melanoma:} the most dangerous skin cancer, including pigmented variants
    \item \textbf{Seborrhoeic keratosis:} a common benign, warty skin growth
    \item \textbf{Intradermal naevus} or other benign mole
    \item \textbf{Actinic keratosis:} a pre-cancerous scaly patch
    \item \textbf{Benign skin conditions} such as psoriasis, eczema, or a dermatofibroma that resemble a non-healing patch
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for any new or changing skin lesion, especially one that bleeds, crusts, or fails to heal within four to six weeks, or that grows steadily. People with many moles, fair skin, or a history of skin cancer should have regular skin checks.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A lesion that bleeds heavily or repeatedly
    \item Rapid growth, ulceration, or an obvious lump that is changing quickly
    \item A lesion near the eye, nose, or ear that is causing functional problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Skin examination:} a doctor examines the lesion and the whole skin surface, including the scalp and behind the ears
    \item \textbf{Dermatoscopy:} examination with a magnifying device and special light to help distinguish BCC from other lesions
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} a small sample (shave, punch, or excision) is taken and examined under a microscope to confirm the diagnosis and subtype
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} rarely needed; in advanced or deeply invasive cases, imaging such as MRI may assess how far the tumour has spread
    \item \textbf{Staging:} BCC almost never spreads, so staging investigations are only performed in exceptional cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgical excision:} the standard treatment; the lesion is removed with a margin of normal skin and the wound closed
    \item \textbf{Micrographic (Mohs) surgery:} for BCCs on the face or with indistinct edges; thin layers are removed and examined one by one to spare healthy tissue
    \item \textbf{Curettage and cautery:} scraping the tumour and sealing the base, used for small, low-risk lesions
    \item \textbf{Cryotherapy:} freezing superficial BCCs with liquid nitrogen
    \item \textbf{Topical creams:} imiquimod or fluorouracil cream for very superficial, low-risk lesions
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} for people who cannot have surgery, or for lesions in difficult locations
    \item \textbf{Targeted therapy:} hedgehog-inhibitor tablets (for example, vismodegib) for advanced or metastatic BCC
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular skin checks, since people with one BCC are at higher risk of developing others
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Local tissue destruction if untreated, including damage to the eye, ear, nose, or bone
    \item Cosmetic disfigurement, especially after treatment of facial lesions
    \item Recurrence of the tumour at the treatment site, particularly after non-surgical treatment
    \item Development of further skin cancers elsewhere, including squamous cell carcinoma and melanoma
    \item Metastasis to lymph nodes or organs is extremely rare but can occur in neglected or unusual cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis is excellent. With complete surgical removal, more than 95 out of 100 BCCs are cured, and the small number that recur can usually be treated again successfully. Metastasis is very rare. Most people are cured by a single minor procedure. The main ongoing risk is the development of new skin cancers, so long-term skin surveillance and sun protection are important. Death from BCC is very unusual.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use broad-spectrum sunscreen (SPF 50+), applied generously and re-applied every two hours
    \item Wear a wide-brimmed hat, sunglasses, and protective clothing, especially between 10 am and 4 pm
    \item Seek shade and avoid sunburn at all ages
    \item Never use solariums
    \item Check your skin regularly, or have a partner check it, looking for new or changing lesions
    \item See a doctor early for any non-healing or suspicious spot
    \item Attend regular skin checks if you have a history of skin cancer or are at high risk
\end{itemize}
