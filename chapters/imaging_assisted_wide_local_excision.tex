\chapter{Imaging-Assisted Wide Local Excision}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Wide local excision (WLE) is a surgical procedure that removes a cancer together with a margin of healthy tissue around it, so that the entire tumour is taken out in one piece. Imaging-assisted wide local excision uses imaging techniques, such as ultrasound, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or X-ray-guided wire or seed localisation, to precisely locate the tumour before and during surgery. This approach is most commonly used for breast cancer, where the lesion is often impalpable (cannot be felt) and imaging guides the surgeon to the exact site. It is also used for some sarcomas and other soft tissue tumours. The aim is complete removal of the cancer with clear margins while removing as little healthy tissue as possible.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Wide local excision is one of the most common operations for early breast cancer, and the majority of newly diagnosed breast cancers in Australia are now treated with breast-conserving surgery of this type rather than mastectomy. Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in Australian women, with around 20,000 new cases each year, and an increasing proportion of these are detected by screening mammography while still impalpable. Imaging-guided localisation is therefore required in a substantial proportion of breast-conserving operations, and the technique has become a standard part of the breast cancer treatment pathway.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The principle behind wide local excision is that cancer cells extend microscopically beyond the edge of the visible or palpable tumour, and removing a cuff of surrounding tissue reduces the risk of leaving disease behind. For impalpable lesions, imaging provides the "roadmap" for surgery. Several localisation methods exist: a wire is inserted into the tumour under ultrasound or X-ray guidance on the day of surgery; a radioactive seed or magnetic marker can be placed days in advance; or intraoperative ultrasound is used to scan the breast during the operation. The excised specimen is X-rayed to confirm the lesion is within it and to assess margin distance. This is usually followed by radiotherapy to the remaining breast tissue to treat any microscopic residual disease.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Recommended for early breast cancer and some other localised tumours
    \item Used when the lesion is impalpable, detected by mammography, ultrasound, or MRI
    \item The operation removes the tumour with a margin of healthy tissue, typically 1--2 cm in breast surgery
    \item The breast is preserved rather than removed, with a scar and usually some change in shape
    \item Sentinel node biopsy is often performed at the same time to check the lymph nodes
    \item Post-operative radiotherapy is usually required to reduce the risk of recurrence
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The decision between wide local excision and alternative treatments depends on the tumour and the patient.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mastectomy:} removal of the whole breast, considered for large or multiple tumours, or where radiotherapy is not feasible
    \item \textbf{Neoadjuvant therapy:} chemotherapy or hormone therapy before surgery to shrink the tumour, sometimes allowing a smaller excision
    \item \textbf{Excisional biopsy alone:} where a benign or low-risk lesion has been completely removed and no further surgery is needed
    \item \textbf{Alternative localisation:} ultrasound-guided excision, wire localisation, seed localisation, or specimen radiography
    \item \textbf{Oncoplastic techniques:} combined with reshaping of the breast for larger excisions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone diagnosed with a lesion requiring wide local excision will have the procedure planned in detail by their surgical team. Contact the team before the procedure with any new symptoms, such as fever or infection, and follow all pre-operative instructions. After surgery, report severe pain, heavy bleeding, spreading redness, or discharge from the wound.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain or difficulty breathing after surgery (possible blood clot or anaesthetic complication)
    \item Sudden severe swelling, redness, or pain in the operated area
    \item Heavy bleeding that does not stop with pressure
    \item Fever with shaking chills
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} mammography, ultrasound, and MRI to characterise the lesion, its extent, and the presence of other disease
    \item \textbf{Core biopsy:} needle biopsy to confirm the diagnosis before surgery
    \item \textbf{Hormone receptor and HER2 testing:} on biopsy tissue to guide adjuvant treatment
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative localisation:} wire, seed, or marker placement under imaging guidance
    \item \textbf{Intraoperative specimen radiography:} X-ray of the removed tissue to confirm the lesion is contained within it
    \item \textbf{Histopathology:} microscopic examination of margins, tumour size, grade, and lymph node status
    \item \textbf{Further imaging:} sentinel node biopsy and staging tests where indicated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} imaging-assisted wide local excision under general anaesthesia, typically as a day procedure or with one overnight stay
    \item \textbf{Sentinel node biopsy:} removal and testing of the first draining lymph node(s) to detect spread
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} whole-breast or partial-breast radiation over several weeks, started after wound healing
    \item \textbf{Adjuvant systemic therapy:} hormone therapy, chemotherapy, or targeted therapy depending on tumour features
    \item \textbf{Re-excision:} a second, smaller operation if the margins are found to contain cancer cells
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular clinical review and surveillance mammography
    \item \textbf{Oncoplastic reconstruction:} techniques to optimise the cosmetic result where larger volumes are removed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wound complications: infection, bruising, bleeding, and fluid collections (seroma or haematoma)
    \item Poor cosmetic outcome, including breast deformity or asymmetry
    \item Numbness or altered sensation in the breast, armpit, or upper arm
    \item Positive margins requiring re-excision or mastectomy in a minority of cases
    \item Lymphoedema after node surgery
    \item Effects of radiotherapy: skin irritation, fatigue, and long-term changes to the breast
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Breast-conserving wide local excision with clear margins followed by radiotherapy achieves survival outcomes equivalent to mastectomy for early breast cancer. The main goal is complete removal of the tumour with clear margins, and the procedure is successful in the large majority of cases. The risk of local recurrence is low, especially with modern adjuvant therapy, and regular follow-up detects any recurrence early. Outcomes for other tumour types treated with this technique depend on the underlying disease.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend breast cancer screening as recommended
    \item Rest and follow the surgeon's instructions for wound care and lifting after the operation
    \item Wear the support garment or bra as advised to reduce swelling
    \item Report any changes in the wound or breast promptly
    \item Complete the full course of radiotherapy and any prescribed medicines
    \item Attend all follow-up appointments and surveillance imaging
    \item Seek support from breast care nurses and support organisations if needed
\end{itemize}
