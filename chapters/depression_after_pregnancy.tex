\chapter{Depression After Pregnancy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Depression after pregnancy, also called postnatal or postpartum depression, is a depressive illness that begins during pregnancy or within the first year after giving birth. It is more than the "baby blues", which affect many new mothers in the first days and settle quickly; postnatal depression is a significant illness with persistent low mood, loss of pleasure, and difficulty coping, and it can affect the mother's health and her bond with the baby. It is common, treatable, and not a sign of weakness or a failing as a mother. This chapter covers the features, causes, and treatment of depression after pregnancy.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Around one in seven to one in ten women experience depression during pregnancy or in the first year after birth, making it one of the most common complications of childbirth. It can begin at any point in the first year, and it is also experienced by some fathers and partners. Many cases go unrecognised because symptoms overlap with the exhaustion and changes of new parenthood. Risk factors include a previous history of depression, stressful life events, lack of support, pregnancy complications, and difficulty breastfeeding. Left untreated, it can persist and affect the whole family.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hormonal changes:} rapid shifts in oestrogen, progesterone, and other hormones after birth may affect mood
    \item \textbf{Sleep deprivation:} broken sleep and exhaustion contribute to low mood
    \item \textbf{History of depression:} previous depression, including in pregnancy, is the strongest risk factor
    \item \textbf{Life stress:} financial pressure, relationship difficulties, and lack of support increase risk
    \item \textbf{Birth experiences:} difficult or traumatic birth and health problems for mother or baby contribute
    \item \textbf{Biological vulnerability:} genetic factors and thyroid changes may play a role
    \item \textbf{Adjustment:} the demands of caring for a new baby, changes in identity, and isolation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent sadness, tearfulness, or irritability lasting more than two weeks
    \item Loss of interest or pleasure in things normally enjoyed
    \item Overwhelming fatigue and difficulty sleeping, even when the baby sleeps
    \item Difficulty bonding with the baby or feeling distant from them
    \item Anxiety, panic, excessive worry about the baby, and intrusive thoughts
    \item Changes in appetite, poor concentration, and feelings of guilt or worthlessness
    \item Withdrawal from family and friends
    \item Thoughts of harming oneself, or in rare cases the baby, require immediate help
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The "baby blues", which affect up to 80\% of new mothers, peak around day 3--5 and resolve within two weeks
    \item Postnatal anxiety, which can occur alone or with depression
    \item Postpartum psychosis, a rare emergency with delusions, hallucinations, and confusion
    \item Thyroid disorders, including postpartum thyroiditis, which can cause mood symptoms
    \item Anaemia and other physical causes of fatigue
    \item Sleep deprivation, which can mimic depressive symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any new parent with persistent low mood, loss of pleasure, anxiety, or difficulty coping should see a doctor, midwife, or maternal and child health nurse. Screening for depression is routine at postnatal checks, and women should be honest about how they are feeling. Treatment is highly effective, and seeking help early is a sign of strength.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Thoughts of harming yourself or the baby
    \item Confusion, hallucinations, or bizarre behaviour (possible postpartum psychosis)
    \item Inability to care for oneself or the baby
    \item A sudden deterioration in mental state
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Support lines:} PANDA (Perinatal Anxiety and Depression Australia) on 1300 726 306, and Lifeline (13 11 14).

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Screening:} the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) is used routinely to identify at-risk mothers
    \item \textbf{Clinical interview:} assessment of mood, anxiety, sleep, bonding, and risk
    \item \textbf{Physical assessment:} blood tests for thyroid function, anaemia, and vitamin levels where indicated
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} careful enquiry about thoughts of self-harm or harm to the baby
    \item \textbf{Partner screening:} depression in partners should also be considered
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychological therapy:} cognitive-behavioural therapy and interpersonal therapy are highly effective
    \item \textbf{Antidepressant medication:} safe options are available for breastfeeding mothers; treatment is guided by the doctor
    \item \textbf{Practical support:} help with the baby, sleep, and household tasks from family, friends, and services
    \item \textbf{Peer support:} mother's groups and perinatal support organisations reduce isolation
    \item \textbf{Self-care:} rest, nutrition, gentle activity, and time for oneself
    \item \textbf{Treating physical causes:} thyroid or anaemia treatment where relevant
    \item \textbf{Specialist care:} referral to perinatal mental health services for severe illness
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular review until recovery, as postnatal depression is treatable and outcomes are good
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent depression and chronic mental illness if untreated
    \item Difficulty bonding, which can affect the baby's development
    \item Effects on breastfeeding and caregiving
    \item Relationship difficulties and family stress
    \item Postpartum psychosis in a small minority
    \item Risk of self-harm and, rarely, harm to the baby
    \item Recurrence in subsequent pregnancies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With treatment, postnatal depression resolves fully in the majority of women within months. Psychological therapy and medication are both effective, and many women recover completely and go on to enjoy motherhood. Early detection through routine screening and prompt treatment give the best outcomes for mothers, babies, and families. Women who have had postnatal depression have a higher risk in future pregnancies and should plan early support.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend routine postnatal screening and answer honestly
    \item Rest when the baby sleeps and accept offers of help
    \item Eat well and stay hydrated
    \item Get outdoors and take gentle exercise
    \item Stay connected with friends, family, and other parents
    \item Talk about how you feel rather than hiding it
    \item Ask about support groups such as PANDA and local maternal and child health services
    \item Plan early support if you have a history of depression or previous postnatal illness
\end{itemize}
