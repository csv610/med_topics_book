\chapter{Endometrial Biopsy}
\index{Endometrial Biopsy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An endometrial biopsy is a procedure in which a small sample of the lining of the uterus, called the endometrium, is removed and examined under a microscope. It is performed to investigate abnormal uterine bleeding, particularly bleeding after the menopause, and to check for endometrial hyperplasia and endometrial cancer. The biopsy is usually done in a clinic with a thin tube passed through the cervix, takes only a few minutes, and requires no anaesthetic or only a local one. The results guide further investigation and treatment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Abnormal uterine bleeding is one of the most common reasons for gynaecological referral, and endometrial biopsy is a standard investigation for it. The risk of endometrial cancer rises with age and with conditions such as obesity, diabetes, and the use of unopposed oestrogen, and post-menopausal bleeding is the classic warning sign. Most biopsies show benign changes, but the test is essential because cancer is curable when detected early.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
An endometrial biopsy examines the tissue that lines the uterus, which changes through the menstrual cycle and with hormone levels.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal endometrium:} the lining thickens under oestrogen and is shed each month with menstruation
    \item \textbf{Hyperplasia:} excessive growth of the lining, usually from prolonged oestrogen exposure, which can progress to cancer in some cases
    \item \textbf{Cancer:} endometrial cancer, the most common gynaecological cancer in many countries, usually presents with bleeding
    \item \textbf{Polyps and other findings:} benign growths and other changes are also detected
    \item \textbf{Why biopsy:} the only way to know what is causing abnormal bleeding is to examine the tissue directly
    \item \textbf{The procedure:} a thin sampling device is passed through the cervix to collect the tissue
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Endometrial biopsy is performed for specific indications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Post-menopausal bleeding of any amount
    \item Heavy, prolonged, or irregular bleeding in women of reproductive age
    \item Bleeding between periods
    \item A thickened endometrium seen on ultrasound
    \item Surveillance in women taking hormone therapy
    \item The procedure itself may cause mild cramping and light spotting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Biopsy findings are interpreted alongside the clinical picture.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Normal cyclical changes in the lining
    \item Benign polyps, fibroids, and adenomyosis
    \item Endometrial hyperplasia with or without atypia
    \item Endometrial cancer
    \item Bleeding from other sites, including the cervix and vagina
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Women with post-menopausal bleeding, very heavy periods, or other abnormal bleeding should be assessed by a GP and gynaecologist. Urgent care is needed for heavy bleeding with faintness, and for any suspicion of a pregnancy complication.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Very heavy vaginal bleeding with faintness or dizziness
    \item Severe pelvic pain
    \item Bleeding in pregnancy with pain
    \item Fever with pelvic pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Endometrial biopsy is one part of the assessment of abnormal bleeding.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the bleeding pattern, risk factors, and pelvic examination
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} transvaginal ultrasound measures the endometrial thickness and looks for other findings
    \item \textbf{The biopsy:} performed in clinic, with a thin tube through the cervix; mild cramping is normal
    \item \textbf{Alternative procedures:} hysteroscopy with targeted biopsy, and dilation and curettage under anaesthetic, when the clinic biopsy is not possible or the sample is inadequate
    \item \textbf{Histology:} the sample is examined by a pathologist
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management follows the biopsy result.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal result:} reassurance, with management of the bleeding by other means if needed
    \item \textbf{Benign hyperplasia:} monitoring, hormones, or a progesterone-releasing IUD
    \item \textbf{Hyperplasia with atypia:} usually treated with removal of the uterus (hysterectomy) because of the risk of progression
    \item \textbf{Cancer:} specialist cancer care, usually involving surgery, with radiotherapy or hormone treatment as needed
    \item \textbf{Further sampling:} repeat biopsy or hysteroscopy if bleeding continues
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Mild cramping and spotting, which settle quickly
    \item Infection, which is rare
    \item An inadequate sample, requiring the test to be repeated
    \item A small risk of uterine perforation with the sampling device
    \item The risk of a false reassurance if the sample is not representative
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Endometrial biopsy is a safe, quick procedure that provides a definitive diagnosis for most women with abnormal bleeding. Early detection of endometrial cancer, when it is curable, is the most important benefit of the test. Most results are benign, and women with abnormal results are managed effectively according to the findings.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Report any post-menopausal bleeding promptly
    \item Seek assessment for heavy or irregular bleeding rather than accepting it as normal
    \item Maintain a healthy weight, which reduces the risk of endometrial cancer
    \item Discuss hormone therapy and its risks with a doctor
    \item Attend follow-up appointments after a biopsy
    \item Manage any pain after the procedure with simple analgesia
\end{itemize}
