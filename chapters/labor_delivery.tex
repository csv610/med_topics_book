\chapter{Labour and Delivery}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Labour is the process by which a baby is born, beginning with regular uterine contractions and progressive opening (dilatation) of the cervix, and ending with delivery of the baby and the placenta. Labour is divided into three stages: the first stage (from the onset of established contractions to full dilatation), the second stage (pushing and birth of the baby), and the third stage (delivery of the placenta). In Australia, most women give birth in hospital under the care of midwives and doctors, though home birth and birth-centre care are options for some. This chapter explains what happens in labour, pain relief options, and how to prepare for birth.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Almost all births in Australia occur in hospitals, with midwives involved in the great majority. Around one-third of first-time mothers have a caesarean section, and a similar proportion use epidural analgesia. Induction of labour occurs in roughly one in three pregnancies. Most women go into labour spontaneously between 37 and 42 weeks of pregnancy; before 37 weeks the birth is preterm, and after 42 weeks it is post-term. The vast majority of Australian babies are born healthy, with one of the lowest infant mortality rates in the world.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Onset of labour:} triggered by complex hormonal signals, including a fall in progesterone and rises in oestrogen, oxytocin, and prostaglandins, which cause coordinated uterine contractions
    \item \textbf{First stage:} contractions efface (thin) and dilate the cervix from 0 to 10 cm; divided into latent and active phases
    \item \textbf{Second stage:} full dilatation is reached and the woman pushes as contractions expel the baby through the birth canal
    \item \textbf{Third stage:} uterine contraction shears the placenta from the uterine wall and it is delivered, either spontaneously or with active management using medication
    \item \textbf{The presenting part:} the baby usually descends head first; breech, transverse, or face presentations occur in a minority of births
    \item \textbf{Fetal adaptations:} the baby's head moulds and rotates as it negotiates the pelvis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Regular, increasingly strong and frequent contractions
    \item A "show": loss of the mucus plug, often with a little blood
    \item Rupture of membranes ("waters breaking"), with a gush or trickle of fluid
    \item Progressive cervical dilatation, assessed on vaginal examination
    \item Low back pain, cramping, and an urge to push in the second stage
    \item Contractions of the uterus that can be felt as tightening of the abdomen
    \item Delivery of the baby, followed within minutes by the placenta
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Braxton Hicks contractions:} irregular, painless practice contractions that do not dilate the cervix
    \item \textbf{Preterm labour:} regular contractions before 37 weeks, requiring urgent assessment
    \item \textbf{Rupture of membranes without labour:} waters may break before contractions begin
    \item \textbf{Other causes of abdominal pain in late pregnancy:} placental abruption, uterine rupture, or other acute conditions
    \item \textbf{False labour:} irregular contractions that settle, more common in first pregnancies
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Women should contact their maternity unit or midwife when they have regular contractions, their waters break, or they have any concern. Attend the birth suite when contractions are regular and strong (commonly every three to four minutes), when waters break, or when advised by the maternity team.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heavy vaginal bleeding at any time in pregnancy
    \item Sudden severe abdominal pain or a rigid, painful abdomen
    \item Signs of pre-eclampsia: severe headache, visual disturbance, or upper abdominal pain
    \item Reduced fetal movements
    \item The baby's cord, arm, or leg presenting before the head
    \item Contractions before 37 weeks with any bleeding, fluid loss, or concern
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vaginal examination:} to assess cervical dilatation, effacement, and the baby's position
    \item \textbf{Cardiotocography (CTG):} continuous monitoring of the baby's heart rate and uterine contractions
    \item \textbf{Fetal scalp monitoring:} sometimes used for more accurate heart rate tracing
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure and urine testing:} to monitor for pre-eclampsia during labour
    \item \textbf{Assessment of liquor:} checking the colour and volume of the amniotic fluid when membranes rupture
    \item \textbf{Birth plan discussion:} not a test, but an important part of preparation covering preferences, pain relief, and contingencies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First stage:} support from a midwife and birth partner, mobility, breathing techniques, warm showers, and pain relief as desired
    \item \textbf{Pain relief options:} gas (nitrous oxide), opioid injections such as pethidine or morphine, and epidural analgesia; non-drug measures such as massage, water immersion, and acupressure
    \item \textbf{Second stage:} guided pushing, positioning to aid descent, and delivery; assisted birth with forceps or vacuum is used when needed
    \item \textbf{Third stage:} active management with an oxytocin injection reduces the risk of heavy bleeding; physiological (natural) third stage is an alternative
    \item \textbf{Induction of labour:} using prostaglandins, a balloon catheter, or synthetic oxytocin when labour does not start or for medical reasons
    \item \textbf{Caesarean section:} planned or emergency surgery when vaginal birth is not safe or progresses poorly
    \item \textbf{Fetal monitoring and obstetric review:} continuous assessment to identify and manage complications promptly
    \item \textbf{Immediate newborn care:} skin-to-skin contact, delayed cord clamping, and early breastfeeding are encouraged
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Postpartum haemorrhage: heavy bleeding after birth, more common after long labour, multiple pregnancy, or previous bleeding
    \item Perineal tears, which are common and range from minor grazes to more extensive tears requiring surgical repair
    \item Fetal distress, requiring assisted or caesarean birth
    \item Shoulder dystocia: difficulty delivering the baby's shoulders, an emergency
    \item Umbilical cord prolapse or placental abruption
    \item Infection, including of the uterus or urinary tract
    \item Pre-eclampsia and eclampsia, which can complicate labour
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for labour and delivery in Australia is excellent. The great majority of women have a safe vaginal birth or a planned caesarean with good outcomes for mother and baby. Labour length varies widely, especially for first babies, and a slow start does not predict a difficult birth. Most women recover well within six weeks, with appropriate support for healing, breastfeeding, and mental health. Complications are usually managed successfully when care is timely and skilled.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend antenatal classes and write a birth plan, but stay flexible
    \item Keep active and fit in pregnancy under guidance
    \item Practise breathing, relaxation, and coping techniques with a birth partner
    \item Eat well, rest, and stay hydrated in early labour at home
    \item Know the warning signs: bleeding, reduced movements, severe headache, or sudden pain
    \item Plan transport and an overnight bag well before the due date
    \item Seek postnatal support for healing, feeding, and emotional wellbeing
\end{itemize}
