\chapter{Hysterectomy}
\index{Hysterectomy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A hysterectomy is a surgical operation to remove the uterus (womb). It is one of the most common gynaecological operations, performed for conditions such as fibroids, heavy menstrual bleeding, endometriosis, prolapse, and cancers of the uterus, cervix, or ovaries. A hysterectomy may remove the uterus alone or also the cervix, and the ovaries and fallopian tubes are sometimes removed as well. It is major surgery with significant implications, including the end of menstruation and fertility, so the decision is made carefully after other options have been considered. Most women recover well and their symptoms resolve.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Around 25,000--30,000 hysterectomies are performed in many countries each year, and about one in three women will have a hysterectomy by age 60, though rates are declining as less invasive treatments improve. The most common indications are fibroids and heavy menstrual bleeding. Hysterectomy rates are higher in women over 40 and in some socioeconomic groups. With modern minimally invasive techniques, recovery is faster and complications fewer than in the past.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fibroids:} benign uterine tumours causing bleeding and pain
    \item \textbf{Heavy menstrual bleeding:} when medical and conservative treatments fail
    \item \textbf{Endometriosis and adenomyosis:} severe pelvic pain and bleeding
    \item \textbf{Uterine prolapse:} descent of the uterus causing pressure and incontinence
    \item \textbf{Cancers:} uterine, cervical, and ovarian cancer
    \item \textbf{Types of hysterectomy:} total (uterus and cervix), subtotal (uterus only), and radical (with surrounding tissues for cancer)
    \item \textbf{Routes:} abdominal, laparoscopic (keyhole), or vaginal
    \item \textbf{Oophorectomy:} removal of the ovaries, sometimes performed with hysterectomy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The operation ends menstruation and fertility
    \item If the ovaries are removed before natural menopause, surgical menopause follows
    \item Recovery takes 4--6 weeks for abdominal surgery and 2--3 weeks for keyhole
    \item Symptoms of the underlying condition resolve after surgery
    \item Hormone therapy may be needed after ovarian removal
    \item The decision follows discussion of alternatives, risks, and benefits
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Alternatives include medical treatment, hormonal options, myomectomy, and endometrial ablation
    \item The choice of route depends on the indication, uterine size, and surgeon expertise
    \item Whether to remove the ovaries is individualised
    \item Fertility wishes are central to the decision
    \item A second opinion is reasonable for major surgery
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Women considering hysterectomy should discuss all options with a gynaecologist. Seek urgent care for heavy bleeding, severe pain, or symptoms that may indicate cancer.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heavy vaginal bleeding with faintness or dizziness
    \item Severe pelvic or abdominal pain
    \item Heavy bleeding in pregnancy
    \item Fever and pelvic pain after surgery
    \item Chest pain, breathlessness, or a swollen painful leg after surgery (possible blood clot)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gynaecological examination and ultrasound:} identify the underlying condition
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} MRI for complex cases
    \item \textbf{Endometrial biopsy:} for abnormal bleeding
    \item \textbf{Preoperative assessment:} fitness for surgery and anaesthesia
    \item \textbf{Counselling:} discussion of effects, alternatives, and expectations
    \item \textbf{Pathology:} examination of removed tissue
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Abdominal hysterectomy:} an incision in the lower abdomen; used for large uteri and cancer
    \item \textbf{Laparoscopic hysterectomy:} keyhole surgery with faster recovery
    \item \textbf{Vaginal hysterectomy:} removal through the vagina, for prolapse and small uteri
    \item \textbf{Robotic surgery:} a laparoscopic variant
    \item \textbf{Recovery:} pain relief, early mobilisation, and graduated activity
    \item \textbf{Hormone management:} HRT after ovarian removal before menopause
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review of recovery and pathology results
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding, infection, and damage to nearby organs
    \item Blood clots in the legs or lungs
    \item Anaesthesia complications
    \item Surgical menopause if the ovaries are removed
    \item Psychological effects, including grief and changes in body image
    \item Pelvic floor problems in some women
    \item Long-term effects vary with whether the ovaries are removed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most women have excellent outcomes after hysterectomy: the symptoms that led to surgery resolve, and quality of life improves substantially. Recovery is usually complete within weeks, and most women return to full activity. The operation is very safe in appropriate candidates, and complications are uncommon. Outcomes depend on the underlying condition, the route of surgery, and careful postoperative care.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discuss all treatment options before choosing surgery
    \item Seek a second opinion if unsure
    \item Consider fertility wishes and ovarian conservation with your surgeon
    \item Prepare for recovery: arrange help at home
    \item Follow postoperative care: rest, wound care, and graduated activity
    \item Manage surgical menopause with HRT where appropriate
    \item Attend follow-up and pathology review
    \item Seek support for emotional adjustment
\end{itemize}
