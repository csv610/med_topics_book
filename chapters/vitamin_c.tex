\chapter{Vitamin c}
\index{Vitamin c}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Vitamin C, chemically identified as L-ascorbic acid or ascorbate, is a water-soluble micronutrient that is fundamental to human physiology. Unlike the vast majority of mammals, which can synthesize ascorbic acid from glucose in the liver or kidneys, humans, other anthropoid primates, guinea pigs, some species of bats, and teleost fish cannot produce it. This inability is due to a prehistoric evolutionary mutation in the gene encoding L-gulonolactone oxidase (GULO), the terminal enzyme in the biosynthetic pathway of ascorbic acid. Consequently, vitamin C is classified as an essential dietary nutrient that must be regularly consumed to prevent deficiency.

Physiologically, vitamin C is a potent reducing agent and an essential cofactor for numerous enzymatic reactions. It acts as an electron donor for at least eight enzymes in humans, including those involved in collagen synthesis, carnitine biosynthesis, catecholamine synthesis, and peptide hormone activation. Structurally, L-ascorbic acid is a six-carbon lactone ring with an enediol group that undergoes reversible oxidation to form monodehydroascorbate (an ascorbyl radical) and subsequently dehydroascorbic acid (DHA). Dehydroascorbic acid is transported into cells via glucose transporters (specifically GLUT1, GLUT3, and GLUT4), where it is rapidly reduced back to active ascorbate by glutathione- and NADPH-dependent enzymes. Ascorbate itself is transported across cell membranes via sodium-dependent vitamin C transporters SVCT1 and SVCT2, which are distributed across various tissues.

Beyond its role as an enzymatic cofactor, vitamin C is one of the most prominent water-soluble antioxidants in the human body. It scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS) and reactive nitrogen species (RNS), thereby protecting lipids, proteins, and DNA from oxidative damage. It also plays a key role in regenerating other essential antioxidants, such as vitamin E, back to their active, reduced states. Additionally, vitamin C enhances the intestinal absorption of non-heme iron by reducing ferric iron to the more soluble ferrous state in the duodenum.

Historically, the severe deficiency of vitamin C, known as scurvy, was a devastating disease that claimed the lives of millions of sailors during the Age of Sail. The identification of citrus fruits as a treatment by British Royal Navy surgeon Dr. James Lind in 1747, during one of the first controlled clinical trials in medical history, eventually led to the eradication of scurvy on long voyages and highlighted the critical importance of micronutrients in human health. In modern clinical practice, while clinical scurvy is rare, subclinical deficiency and marginal vitamin C status remain prevalent in specific risk groups, making vitamin C a focus of ongoing nutritional and medical interest.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of vitamin C deficiency varies significantly between developed and developing nations, reflecting differences in socioeconomic status, dietary habits, and food security. In industrialized nations, clinical scurvy is uncommon, but subclinical deficiency remains a notable public health concern. Data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) in the United States suggest that approximately 6\% to 7\% of the population has clinical vitamin C deficiency, defined as a serum or plasma ascorbate concentration of less than 11.4 $\mu$mol/L (0.2 mg/dL). In contrast, in resource-limited countries and regions facing severe food insecurity, the prevalence of deficiency can exceed 30\% in vulnerable populations, particularly during dry seasons or periods of famine when fresh fruits and vegetables are unavailable.

Epidemiological studies consistently demonstrate that vitamin C deficiency is more common in males than in females. This disparity is primarily attributed to poorer dietary habits, lower consumption of fruits and vegetables, and higher rates of tobacco use among men. Age is another critical epidemiological factor; elderly individuals, especially those living in isolation, nursing homes, or assisted-living facilities, exhibit a high prevalence of deficiency due to limited access to fresh food, poor dentition, and general cognitive or physical decline.

Certain demographic and behavioral cohorts exhibit a substantially increased risk of vitamin C deficiency:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cigarette Smokers}: Tobacco smoke induces significant oxidative stress, leading to a rapid depletion of ascorbate pools. Smokers have plasma vitamin C levels that are, on average, 40\% lower than those of non-smokers with similar dietary intakes.
    \item \textbf{Socioeconomically Disadvantaged Populations}: Individuals living in food deserts or experiencing low income often rely on highly processed, shelf-stable foods that lack vitamin C, resulting in a higher incidence of subclinical deficiency.
    \item \textbf{Individuals with Alcohol Use Disorder}: Alcoholics frequently suffer from severe malnutrition, impaired intestinal absorption, and increased urinary excretion of vitamin C.
    \item \textbf{Patients with Chronic Illnesses}: Individuals undergoing long-term hemodialysis or those with chronic inflammatory conditions have accelerated loss or utilization of the vitamin.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The primary aetiology of vitamin C deficiency is an inadequate dietary intake of fresh fruits and vegetables. Because the human body cannot synthesize ascorbate and has a limited storage capacity—with total body pools typically depleted within 30 to 90 days of complete dietary deprivation—regular intake is required to maintain physiological homeostasis. Malabsorption syndromes and conditions that increase the metabolic demand for vitamin C also contribute to the development of deficiency.

The pathophysiology of vitamin C deficiency is characterized by the impairment of several enzymatic pathways:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Defective Collagen Hydroxylation}: Collagen is the primary structural protein of the extracellular matrix in connective tissues, including blood vessels, skin, tendons, ligaments, and bone. The synthesis of stable collagen requires the post-translational hydroxylation of proline and lysine residues in procollagen. The enzymes responsible, prolyl hydroxylase and lysyl hydroxylase, require ferrous iron ($Fe^{2+}$) at their active sites. During the hydroxylation reaction, the iron is oxidized to ferric iron ($Fe^{3+}$), inactivating the enzyme. Vitamin C acts as a specific reducing agent that donates electrons to reduce the iron back to the active ferrous ($Fe^{2+}$) state. Without vitamin C, collagen remains under-hydroxylated. This structurally unstable collagen cannot form a stable triple helix, leading to blood vessel fragility, poor wound healing, capillary leakage, and structural weakness in skin, gums, and bones.
    \item \textbf{Impaired Carnitine Biosynthesis}: Carnitine is a quaternary ammonium compound essential for transporting long-chain fatty acids into the mitochondria, where they undergo beta-oxidation to produce adenosine triphosphate (ATP). Vitamin C is a required cofactor for two enzymes in the carnitine biosynthetic pathway: epsilon-trimethyllysine hydroxylase and gamma-butyrobetaine hydroxylase. A deficiency in vitamin C leads to reduced carnitine synthesis, impairing mitochondrial energy production and resulting in the profound fatigue, muscle weakness, and lethargy characteristic of early-stage scurvy.
    \item \textbf{Altered Norepinephrine Synthesis}: Vitamin C is a cofactor for dopamine beta-hydroxylase, which converts dopamine to norepinephrine in the adrenal medulla and nervous system. Inadequate norepinephrine synthesis contributes to the vasomotor instability, orthostatic hypotension, and neuropsychiatric symptoms (such as depression and apathy) observed in deficient individuals.
    \item \textbf{Loss of Cellular Antioxidant Defense}: As a primary intracellular and extracellular scavenger of free radicals, vitamin C protects cell membranes and DNA from oxidative stress. When ascorbate levels fall, cellular components are exposed to unchecked reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to cellular damage, lipid peroxidation, and accelerated inflammatory pathways.
    \item \textbf{Impaired Non-Heme Iron Absorption}: Vitamin C facilitates the absorption of non-heme iron in the small intestine by reducing it from the ferric ($Fe^{3+}$) to the more soluble ferrous ($Fe^{2+}$) state and forming a stable, absorbable chelate. A lack of vitamin C reduces iron bioavailability, contributing to the development of microcytic anemia, which is often exacerbated by blood loss from fragile capillaries.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of vitamin C deficiency develops progressively over several weeks to months of inadequate intake. Historically and clinically, the progression of scurvy is categorized into stages that reflect the gradual depletion of tissue ascorbate stores.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Phase 1 (Early Constitutional Symptoms)}: The earliest clinical manifestations are non-specific and typically appear after 4 to 6 weeks of severe deficiency, when plasma levels drop below 15 $\mu$mol/L. Patients report progressive fatigue, malaise, generalized weakness, irritability, and diffuse myalgias and arthralgias. These symptoms are primarily related to impaired carnitine synthesis and altered catecholamine pathways.
    \item \textbf{Phase 2 (Dermatological and Follicular Changes)}: As tissue stores deplete further, characteristic dermatological signs emerge. Perifollicular hyperkeratosis develops, particularly on the extensor surfaces of the upper arms and thighs. Hair follicles become plugged with keratin, and the hairs grow in an abnormal, coiled shape, known as ``corkscrew hairs'' or ``swan-neck hairs.'' Capillary walls weaken, leading to rupture and the formation of perifollicular hemorrhages—small, reddish-purple macules centered around hair follicles, most commonly on the lower limbs.
    \item \textbf{Phase 3 (Gingival and Oral Pathology)}: Severe gum changes are a classic feature of scurvy. The gingivae become swollen, spongy, bluish-purple, and highly friable, bleeding easily with minimal manipulation or chewing. The periodontal ligament, which secures teeth to the alveolar bone, degenerates, leading to tooth mobility, pain, and eventual tooth loss. Secondary bacterial infections of the gums, including ulcerative gingivitis, may occur. Notably, these oral manifestations are absent in edentulous patients, as they lack the periodontal structures.
    \item \textbf{Phase 4 (Hemorrhagic and Musculoskeletal Signs)}: Capillary fragility leads to widespread bleeding. Petechiae, ecchymoses, and large subcutaneous hematomas can occur spontaneously or after minor trauma. Bleeding into the joint spaces (hemarthrosis) causes severe pain, swelling, and restricted movement, particularly in the knees and ankles. Subperiosteal hemorrhages are highly painful and can cause pseudoparalysis in pediatric patients (Barlow's disease), who may lie with their legs flexed and abducted (the ``frog-leg'' position) due to pain. Splinter hemorrhages under the fingernails and poor wound healing, including the breakdown of previously healed scars, also occur.
    \item \textbf{Phase 5 (Late-Stage Systemic Complications)}: In advanced, untreated scurvy, patients develop generalized edema (anasarca) and pleural effusions due to increased capillary permeability and hypoalbuminemia. Severe normocytic or microcytic anemia develops from chronic hemorrhage and impaired iron absorption. Vasomotor instability can lead to orthostatic hypotension, dyspnea, and high-output heart failure. Jaundice, convulsions, and sudden death from internal hemorrhage, myocardial ischemia, or cardiac tamponade (secondary to hemopericardium) represent the terminal phase of the disease.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because vitamin C deficiency presents with multi-system symptoms—including bleeding, joint pain, skin lesions, and fatigue—it can mimic several other medical conditions. A careful differential diagnosis is necessary to ensure correct treatment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Thrombocytopenia and Platelet Disorders}: Conditions such as Immune Thrombocytopenic Purpura (ITP), drug-induced thrombocytopenia, or leukemia can present with petechiae, purpura, and mucosal bleeding. However, these disorders are characterized by a low platelet count on a complete blood count, whereas scurvy typically presents with a normal platelet count.
    \item \textbf{Coagulation Factor Deficiencies}: Hemophilia A and B, Von Willebrand disease, and acquired coagulopathies (such as liver disease or disseminated intravascular coagulation) present with bruising and hemarthrosis. These can be differentiated from scurvy by an abnormal coagulation profile, such as a prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) or prothrombin time (PT/INR), which remain normal in isolated vitamin C deficiency.
    \item \textbf{Systemic Vasculitis}: Conditions like Henoch-Sch\"{o}nlein Purpura (HSP) or cutaneous small-vessel vasculitis present with palpable purpura and arthralgias. Scurvy can be distinguished by the presence of non-palpable purpura, perifollicular localization of hemorrhages, corkscrew hairs, and severe gingival hypertrophy, which are not typical of vasculitis.
    \item \textbf{Other Nutritional Deficiencies}:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Vitamin K Deficiency}: Causes bleeding and bruising due to impaired synthesis of clotting factors (factors II, VII, IX, and X), presenting with a prolonged PT/INR.
            \item \textbf{Zinc Deficiency}: Presents with skin lesions, impaired wound healing, and hair loss. However, zinc deficiency is characterized by periorificial and acral dermatitis, and does not cause the perifollicular hemorrhages or bleeding gums typical of scurvy.
            \item \textbf{Folate and Vitamin B12 Deficiencies}: Present with fatigue and macrocytic anemia, which must be differentiated from the normocytic or microcytic anemia commonly associated with scurvy.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rheumatological Disorders}: Rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and polymyalgia rheumatica share symptoms of fatigue, joint pain, and effusions. These can be ruled out by testing for immunological markers (e.g., rheumatoid factor, anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibodies, antinuclear antibodies) and the absence of hemorrhagic dermatological signs.
    \item \textbf{Infectious Diseases}: Acute infections such as meningococcemia, infective endocarditis, or rickettsial diseases (e.g., Rocky Mountain spotted fever) cause petechiae and purpura. These are accompanied by high-grade fever, toxic appearance, leukocytosis, and positive blood cultures, which are absent in chronic scurvy.
    \item \textbf{Non-Accidental Injury (Child Abuse)}: In infants and children, the presence of unexplained bruising, subperiosteal hematomas, and bone pain can be mistaken for physical abuse. Detailed nutritional history, skeletal surveys showing metabolic bone changes, and rapid response to vitamin C supplementation help establish the correct diagnosis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should consult a general practitioner if they experience persistent fatigue, unexplained bruising, bleeding gums when brushing or eating, or chronic joint and muscle pain, especially if their diet is low in fresh fruits and vegetables.

Immediate emergency medical evaluation is critical for life-threatening symptoms associated with advanced vitamin C deficiency. Call emergency services (such as **local emergency services** in many countries, **911** in the United States and Canada, or **999** in the United Kingdom) if the patient exhibits any of the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, unexplained bleeding or rapidly expanding, large bruises (hematomas).
    \item Sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, or a rapid, pounding heart rate, which may indicate high-output heart failure or bleeding into the chest cavity or pericardium.
    \item Severe, sudden joint swelling that prevents weight-bearing or walking.
    \item Altered mental status, severe confusion, lethargy, or seizures.
    \item A rapid, widespread purple skin rash accompanied by a high fever.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of vitamin C deficiency is primarily clinical, based on a history of inadequate dietary intake, risk factors, and characteristic physical findings. Laboratory and radiological investigations are used to confirm the diagnosis and rule out alternative conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Serum/Plasma Ascorbic Acid Levels}: This is the standard diagnostic test. Fasting samples are preferred because plasma levels fluctuate with recent dietary intake.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Normal}: $>$ 28.4 $\mu$mol/L (0.5 mg/dL).
            \item \textbf{Marginal Deficiency (Subclinical)}: 11.4 to 28.4 $\mu$mol/L (0.2 to 0.5 mg/dL).
            \item \textbf{Severe Deficiency (Clinical Scurvy)}: $<$ 11.4 $\mu$mol/L (0.2 mg/dL).
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Leukocyte Vitamin C Concentration}: This measurement reflects tissue stores more accurately than plasma levels because leukocytes maintain high concentrations of ascorbate. A level of less than 0.1 mg/10$^{8}$ cells indicates deficiency. However, this test is technically demanding, expensive, and not widely available in clinical settings.
    \item \textbf{Complete Blood Count (CBC) and Anemia Panel}: Anemia is present in up to 80\% of patients with clinical scurvy. It is typically normocytic and normochromic due to bleeding, but can be microcytic (due to concurrent iron deficiency) or macrocytic (due to impaired folate metabolism).
    \item \textbf{Coagulation Profile}: Prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), and platelet count are typically normal, which helps rule out primary coagulopathies and thrombocytopenia.
    \item \textbf{Skeletal Radiography (Pediatrics)}: X-rays of long bones, particularly the knees and wrists, are useful in children and reveal characteristic skeletal changes:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Wimberger Ring Sign}: A thin, sharp circular radiopaque cortex surrounding a radiolucent center in the epiphyseal ossification center.
            \item \textbf{White Line of Fraenkel}: A dense, radiopaque band at the metaphysis, representing a thickened zone of provisional calcification.
            \textbf{Tr\"{u}mmerfeld Zone (Scurvy Zone)}: A radiolucent band proximal to the white line of Fraenkel, representing microfractures and osteopenia.
            \item \textbf{Pelkan Spur}: Lateral bony spurs projecting from the metaphysis, caused by outward displacement of the calcified zone.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Therapeutic Diagnostic Trial}: In cases where diagnostic testing is unavailable, the rapid resolution of symptoms (such as gum bleeding and bone pain) within 24 to 72 hours of initiating vitamin C supplementation confirms the diagnosis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of vitamin C deficiency focuses on rapid replenishment of tissue stores and addressing the underlying causes of the deficiency.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High-Dose Ascorbic Acid Supplementation}:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Adult Regimen}: The standard therapy is 300 mg to 1000 mg of oral ascorbic acid daily in divided doses. This high dose is continued for one to two weeks to saturate tissue stores, followed by a maintenance dose of 100 mg daily for several weeks until clinical recovery is complete.
            \item \textbf{Pediatric Regimen}: Children are typically treated with 100 mg to 300 mg of oral vitamin C daily in divided doses, followed by a maintenance dose of 30 mg to 50 mg daily.
            \item \textbf{Route of Administration}: The oral route is preferred due to excellent bioavailability. Intravenous or intramuscular administration is reserved for patients with severe malabsorption, persistent vomiting, or critical illness.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary Modification and Education}: Patients should work with a registered dietitian to incorporate vitamin C-rich foods into their daily diet. High-priority foods include citrus fruits (oranges, lemons, limes, grapefruits), kiwi, strawberries, papaya, red and green bell peppers, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, spinach, and tomatoes.
    \item \textbf{Addressing Underlying Risk Factors}:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Provide smoking cessation support, as tobacco users have higher requirements.
            \item Treat alcohol use disorder and refer to support services.
            \item Manage eating disorders (such as anorexia nervosa or avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder) with multidisciplinary psychiatric and nutritional therapy.
            \item Treat underlying malabsorptive disorders, such as celiac disease or Crohn's disease.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Correction of Coexisting Deficiencies}: Scurvy often coexists with other nutritional deficiencies. Patients should be evaluated and treated for deficiencies in iron, folate, vitamin D, vitamin B12, and zinc. Iron supplementation is indicated if severe anemia is present.
    \item \textbf{Clinical Monitoring}: Patients should be monitored for the resolution of clinical features. Plasma vitamin C levels can be repeated after 2 to 4 weeks of therapy to confirm adequacy of replacement.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While vitamin C deficiency is easily treated, untreated scurvy or excessive supplementation can lead to complications.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Complications of Untreated Scurvy}:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Life-Threatening Hemorrhage}: Severe internal bleeding can occur, including hemopericardium (leading to cardiac tamponade), intracranial hemorrhage, and massive gastrointestinal or pulmonary bleeding.
            \item \textbf{High-Output Heart Failure}: Caused by severe anemia and myocardial ischemia due to inadequate oxygen delivery.
            \item \textbf{Pathological Fractures and Bone Deformities}: In children, impaired bone matrix formation leads to fractures, painful subperiosteal hematomas, and growth disturbances.
            \item \textbf{Irreversible Dental Damage}: Loss of multiple teeth due to degeneration of the periodontal ligament and resorption of alveolar bone.
            \item \textbf{Severe Infections}: Impaired immune cell function increases susceptibility to severe bacterial infections and sepsis.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Complications of Vitamin C Excess (Toxicity)}:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal Distress}: Intakes exceeding the Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) of 2000 mg/day can cause osmotic diarrhea, abdominal cramping, bloating, and nausea.
            \item \textbf{Nephrolithiasis (Kidney Stones)}: Vitamin C is metabolized to oxalate, which is excreted in the urine. High doses (especially above 1000 to 2000 mg/day) can lead to hyperoxaluria, increasing the risk of calcium oxalate kidney stone formation, particularly in individuals with pre-existing renal impairment or a history of stones.
            \item \textbf{Hemolysis in G6PD Deficiency}: High intravenous doses of vitamin C can induce oxidative stress, triggering acute intravascular hemolysis in individuals with Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency. This is a medical emergency.
            \item \textbf{Iron Overload}: Because vitamin C enhances non-heme iron absorption, chronic megadose supplementation can exacerbate iron overload in patients with hereditary hemochromatosis, thalassemia, or sideroblastic anemia, potentially leading to organ damage (hemosiderosis).
        \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for patients diagnosed with vitamin C deficiency is excellent, provided treatment is initiated before the onset of catastrophic cardiovascular or hemorrhagic events.

Response to treatment is remarkably rapid:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain, fatigue, and constitutional symptoms begin to improve within 24 hours of starting supplementation.
    \item Spontaneous bleeding, bruising, and petechiae typically resolve within 24 to 48 hours.
    \item Gingival swelling and bleeding begin to heal within 2 to 3 days, although complete recovery of gum tissue and tooth stability may take several weeks.
    \item Muscle and bone pain improve within a few days, and subperiosteal hematomas gradually resorb over weeks.
    \item Anemia, skin lesions (including corkscrew hairs), and wound healing normalize over 2 to 4 weeks as new collagen is synthesized and iron absorption improves.
\end{itemize}

If untreated, scurvy is uniformly fatal. Death occurs due to sudden internal hemorrhage, heart failure, or severe secondary infection. The outcome is determined by the patient's adherence to dietary modifications and supplementation, and the successful management of any pre-existing conditions that caused the deficiency.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventing vitamin C deficiency is highly achievable through simple dietary habits and awareness of individual nutritional needs.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adherence to Recommended Dietary Allowances (RDA)}:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Adult males: 90 mg/day.
            \item Adult females: 75 mg/day.
            \item Pregnant individuals: 85 mg/day.
            \item Lactating individuals: 120 mg/day.
            \item Active cigarette smokers: require an additional 35 mg/day (totaling 125 mg/day for men and 110 mg/day for women) to compensate for increased oxidative depletion.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary Optimization}: Consume at least 5 servings of fruit and vegetables daily. Citrus fruits (oranges, grapefruits, mandarins), kiwi, strawberries, papaya, bell peppers (particularly yellow and red), broccoli, Brussels sprouts, spinach, and kale are rich sources of vitamin C.
    \item \textbf{Appropriate Food Storage and Cooking Methods}: Vitamin C is highly sensitive to heat, light, and water. Extended cooking, boiling, or baking can destroy up to 50\% or more of the vitamin C content in food. To preserve its nutritional value, foods should be eaten fresh when possible, or cooked using quick, low-water methods like steaming, microwaving, or stir-frying.
    \item \textbf{Mindful Supplementation}: For individuals at risk of deficiency (such as those with restricted eating patterns or gastrointestinal malabsorption), a daily multivitamin containing vitamin C (typically 60 to 90 mg) or a low-dose ascorbic acid supplement is highly effective. Avoid routine megadose supplementation (above 2000 mg/day) to prevent gastrointestinal and renal complications.
    \item \textbf{Social and Environmental Interventions}: Families and caregivers of elderly, isolated, or mentally ill individuals should monitor food intake to ensure access to fresh fruits and vegetables. Regular clinical assessments are recommended for patients with chronic malabsorptive disorders.
\end{itemize}
