\chapter{Haemoptysis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Haemoptysis is the coughing up of blood or blood-stained mucus from the lungs or airways. It ranges from small streaks of blood in the sputum to large amounts of blood, and it always warrants medical assessment. The most common causes are respiratory infections such as bronchitis and pneumonia, but haemoptysis can also be a sign of more serious conditions, including tuberculosis, lung cancer, bronchiectasis, and pulmonary embolism. In most people the cause is benign and the bleeding settles, but the investigation of haemoptysis is important to exclude serious disease. Massive haemoptysis is a medical emergency.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Haemoptysis accounts for around 1% of visits to respiratory specialists and a smaller proportion of GP consultations. It is more common in men and in people who smoke, and its incidence increases with age. The underlying cause varies by setting: in Australia, infections, bronchiectasis, and lung cancer are leading causes, while tuberculosis is the dominant cause in many developing countries. Around 5--10% of people with haemoptysis are found to have lung cancer, and the risk is highest in older smokers. Massive haemoptysis is rare but carries a high mortality.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Blood in the sputum comes from bleeding within the airways or lung tissue.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bronchitis:} inflammation of the airways, most often from infection, irritates and damages the lining, causing streaks of blood
    \item \textbf{Pneumonia:} infection and inflammation of the lung tissue can cause bleeding
    \item \textbf{Bronchiectasis:} permanently damaged, dilated airways bleed easily
    \item \textbf{Lung cancer:} tumours can erode into blood vessels and bleed
    \item \textbf{Tuberculosis:} infection can cause bleeding, and cavitation can damage vessels
    \item \textbf{Pulmonary embolism:} blood clots in the lung can cause bleeding with infarction
    \item \textbf{Other causes:} heart failure, bleeding disorders, anticoagulant medicines, foreign bodies, and trauma
    \item \textbf{Massive haemoptysis:} bleeding from large bronchial arteries can rapidly compromise the airway and cause death
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The appearance and context of the blood provide important clues.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Bright red blood or blood-streaked sputum with coughing
    \item Frothy, pink-tinged sputum, which can indicate pulmonary oedema
    \item Frank blood in larger amounts
    \item Associated cough, fever, and purulent sputum in infection
    \item Unexplained weight loss, fatigue, or chest pain in possible malignancy
    \item Night sweats in tuberculosis
    \item Shortness of breath and pleuritic chest pain in pulmonary embolism
    \item It is important to distinguish haemoptysis from blood from the nose, mouth, or stomach
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other sources of blood must be distinguished from true haemoptysis.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Epistaxis (nosebleeds) with blood running into the throat
    \item Bleeding from the mouth, gums, or throat
    \item Haematemesis (vomiting blood) from the stomach, which is usually dark or like coffee grounds
    \item Bronchitis and pneumonia
    \item Bronchiectasis
    \item Lung cancer
    \item Tuberculosis
    \item Pulmonary embolism
    \item Heart failure with pulmonary oedema
    \item Bleeding disorders and anticoagulant therapy
    \item Foreign bodies, especially in children
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any episode of coughing up blood should be reported to a doctor, and people with risk factors such as smoking, weight loss, or persistent symptoms should be assessed promptly. Even small amounts of blood should not be ignored.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Coughing up large amounts of blood or blood that does not settle
    \item Coughing up blood with difficulty breathing or chest pain
    \item Coughing up blood with dizziness, faintness, or collapse
    \item Blood in the sputum with signs of a blood clot, such as sudden breathlessness and calf swelling
    \item Massive haemoptysis, which is a life-threatening emergency
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The cause of haemoptysis is found by a combination of history, examination, and tests.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} character of the blood, associated symptoms, smoking history, and medicines
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} the first imaging test, looking for pneumonia, masses, and other abnormalities
    \item \textbf{Sputum tests:} culture and cytology for infection and abnormal cells
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} detailed imaging of the chest to detect tumours, bronchiectasis, and emboli
    \item \textbf{Bronchoscopy:} direct inspection of the airways with a camera, which can identify and sometimes treat bleeding sources
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, clotting studies, and inflammatory markers
    \item \textbf{Other tests:} echocardiography and ventilation-perfusion scanning as indicated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on the cause and the severity of the bleeding.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infection:} antibiotics for bronchitis and pneumonia
    \item \textbf{Bronchiectasis:} airway clearance and treatment of infections
    \item \textbf{Lung cancer:} surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or other treatment as appropriate
    \item \textbf{Tuberculosis:} a full course of antituberculous antibiotics
    \item \textbf{Pulmonary embolism:} anticoagulant treatment
    \item \textbf{Bleeding disorders and anticoagulants:} management of the clotting problem
    \item \textbf{Massive haemoptysis:} urgent hospital care with airway protection, and interventions such as embolisation of the bleeding vessel or surgery
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} rest and observation for minor bleeding
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} repeat imaging and review to confirm resolution
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Massive haemoptysis causing airway obstruction, asphyxia, and death
    \item Anaemia from significant blood loss
    \item Aspiration of blood into the lungs
    \item Delayed diagnosis of lung cancer or tuberculosis when haemoptysis is dismissed
    \item Recurrence of bleeding, especially in bronchiectasis
    \item Complications of bronchoscopy and angiography
    \item Anxiety about the significance of the symptom
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis depends on the underlying cause. Haemoptysis from acute bronchitis or pneumonia settles as the infection resolves and has an excellent outlook. Bronchiectasis causes recurrent bleeding that is usually manageable. Lung cancer, when found, has a much better outlook if diagnosed early. Massive haemoptysis is life-threatening but is treatable with rapid specialist care. The most important message is that haemoptysis always warrants investigation, because it can be the first sign of serious, treatable disease.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Do not smoke, and quit if you do, as smoking is the main risk factor for lung cancer and bronchitis
    \item Report any episode of coughing up blood to a doctor, even if small
    \item Complete all recommended investigations
    \item Treat respiratory infections promptly and completely
    \item Attend follow-up appointments and repeat imaging as advised
    \item If on anticoagulants, attend regular blood monitoring and report bleeding promptly
    \item Practise good hygiene to prevent respiratory infections
    \item Seek urgent care for large amounts of blood or bleeding with breathlessness
\end{itemize}
