\chapter{Vitamin d}
\index{Vitamin d}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Vitamin D is a fat-soluble secosteroid hormone that plays a fundamental role in human physiology, primarily known for its critical function in maintaining calcium and phosphate homeostasis, promoting bone mineralization, and supporting skeletal integrity. Unlike classic vitamins, which must be obtained entirely from dietary sources, vitamin D can be synthesized endogenously by the human body when the skin is exposed to ultraviolet B (UVB) radiation from sunlight. Consequently, it is more accurately classified as a prohormone rather than a strict micronutrient.

The term ``vitamin D'' collectively refers to a group of closely related secosteroid molecules, the two major forms of which are vitamin D$_{2}$ (ergocalciferol) and vitamin D$_{3}$ (cholecalciferol). Ergocalciferol is primarily synthesized by plants, fungi, and yeasts in response to UVB irradiation, whereas cholecalciferol is synthesized by animals, including humans, when 7-dehydrocholesterol in the epidermal layer of the skin undergoes photochemical conversion by solar UVB rays. Once synthesized or absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, both forms undergo identical multi-step metabolic activation pathways. They are first hydroxylated in the liver to form 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D], the primary circulating form and clinical marker of vitamin D status. Subsequently, a second hydroxylation occurs, primarily in the kidneys, to produce 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D [1,25(OH)$_{2}$D], the biologically active hormone (also known as calcitriol).

The significance of vitamin D extends far beyond skeletal health. The discovery of the vitamin D receptor (VDR) in nearly every tissue of the human body---including immune cells, cardiac myocytes, vascular endothelial cells, pancreatic beta cells, and neurons---has highlighted its pleiotropic systemic actions. Vitamin D regulates the transcription of hundreds of genes, influencing cellular proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, immune modulation, insulin secretion, and cardiovascular function. A deficiency in this essential hormone is associated not only with classic bone diseases such as rickets and osteomalacia but also with an increased risk of chronic diseases, including osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease, autoimmune disorders, and respiratory infections. Thus, understanding vitamin D physiology, recognizing deficiency, and ensuring adequacy are vital components of modern preventive medicine and clinical practice.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Vitamin D deficiency and insufficiency represent a global public health pandemic, affecting an estimated one billion people worldwide across all age groups, ethnicities, and socioeconomic classes. Epidemiological studies demonstrate that the prevalence of suboptimal vitamin D levels varies significantly based on geographic latitude, season, cultural practices, skin pigmentation, and dietary habits. In many parts of the world, including regions of the Middle East, South Asia, East Asia, and Europe, more than 50\% of the population has circulating 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels below 50 nmol/L (20 ng/mL), which is the widely accepted threshold for deficiency.

Demographic variations are highly pronounced in the epidemiology of vitamin D deficiency:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Age groups:} Infants who are exclusively breastfed without vitamin D supplementation are at extremely high risk, as breast milk contains negligible amounts of the hormone. The elderly population also exhibits a disproportionately high prevalence of deficiency due to several physiological changes, including a reduced concentration of 7-dehydrocholesterol in the aging skin, decreased outdoor activity, and declining renal function, which limits the conversion to the active form.
    \item \textbf{Geographic latitude and seasonal variation:} Populations living at latitudes greater than 37 degrees north or south experience a ``vitamin D winter'' during which the solar zenith angle prevents UVB rays from effectively penetrating the atmosphere, rendering cutaneous synthesis impossible for several months of the year. Consequently, serum 25(OH)D levels typically peak in late summer and nadir in late winter.
    \item \textbf{Race and ethnicity:} Darker skin pigmentation, characterized by higher concentrations of melanin in the epidermis, acts as a natural sunscreen by absorbing UVB photons. As a result, individuals with darker skin require significantly longer sun exposure than those with lighter skin to synthesize equivalent amounts of vitamin D. In multicultural nations, immigrant populations from Africa, South Asia, and the Middle East often display the highest rates of severe deficiency.
    \item \textbf{Cultural and lifestyle factors:} The rise of urbanization, indoor occupations, and the use of sun protection (such as sunscreens and protective clothing) have dramatically reduced cutaneous synthesis. Furthermore, in cultures where traditional clothing covers the majority of the body, such as the wearing of hijabs, abayas, or burqas, deficiency rates among women can exceed 80\% to 90\%, regardless of the geographic location's solar intensity.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The development of vitamin D deficiency is multifactorial, stemming from insufficient cutaneous synthesis, inadequate dietary intake, impaired intestinal absorption, defective metabolic activation, or increased excretion. The underlying pathophysiology involves a disruption in the delicate balance of calcium and phosphorus homeostasis, leading to secondary hyperparathyroidism and compromised skeletal mineralization.

The primary causes and pathways include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inadequate exposure to sunlight:} This is the most common cause globally. Cutaneous synthesis of vitamin D$_{3}$ is dependent on solar UVB radiation (wavelengths of 290 to 315 nm). Factors that block UVB penetration---such as high-SPF sunscreen, heavy air pollution, glass windows, clothing that covers the entire body, and high geographic latitudes---prevent the photolysis of 7-dehydrocholesterol to pre-vitamin D$_{3}$, initiating a cascade of systemic deficiency.
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal malabsorption disorders:} Because vitamin D is fat-soluble, its absorption in the small intestine requires bile acids and pancreatic enzymes. Inflammatory bowel diseases (such as Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis), celiac disease, cystic fibrosis, pancreatic insufficiency, short bowel syndrome, and history of bariatric surgery (such as Roux-en-Y gastric bypass) impair lipid digestion and severely reduce the bioavailability of dietary and supplemental vitamin D.
    \item \textbf{Impaired renal or hepatic activation:} Chronic liver diseases (such as cirrhosis or non-alcoholic steatohepatitis) can impair the initial 25-hydroxylation step. More critically, chronic kidney disease (CKD) restricts the activity of the enzyme 1-alpha-hydroxylase, which converts 25(OH)D to the active calcitriol. In advanced kidney failure, active vitamin D production is profoundly blunted, leading to severe renal osteodystrophy.
    \item \textbf{Increased metabolic clearance and medication interactions:} Certain pharmacological agents induce the hepatic cytochrome P450 enzyme system, specifically CYP3A4, which catabolizes 25(OH)D and 1,25(OH)$_{2}$D into inactive metabolites (such as calcitroic acid). Key medications causing this include anticonvulsants (such as phenytoin, carbamazepine, and phenobarbital), rifampicin, glucocorticoids, and certain highly active antiretroviral therapies (HAART).
    \item \textbf{Obesity and adipose tissue sequestration:} Vitamin D is highly lipophilic. In individuals with high body mass indexes (BMI $\ge$ 30 kg/m$^{2}$), excess subcutaneous fat acts as a physiological sink, sequestering synthesized or ingested vitamin D within adipocytes and preventing its release into the systemic circulation.
\end{itemize}

When circulating 25(OH)D levels fall, the pathological cascade begins. Active calcitriol levels eventually decline, causing a significant reduction in the intestinal absorption of dietary calcium and phosphorus (decreasing calcium absorption from a normal 30\%--40\% down to less than 10\%--15\%). The resulting transient hypocalcaemia triggers the calcium-sensing receptors on the parathyroid glands, stimulating the secretion of parathyroid hormone (PTH). This state of secondary hyperparathyroidism attempts to normalize serum calcium through three primary mechanisms: increasing renal calcium reabsorption in the distal convoluted tubule; upregulating renal 1-alpha-hydroxylase to maximize calcitriol conversion; and stimulating osteoclastogenesis, which mobilizes calcium and phosphate directly from the skeleton by promoting bone resorption.

Additionally, elevated PTH levels inhibit renal phosphate reabsorption in the proximal tubule, causing phosphaturia. The combination of low serum phosphate and inadequate calcium prevents the proper deposition of calcium hydroxyapatite crystals into the osteoid matrix. Consequently, newly formed bone matrix remains unmineralized, leading to the clinical manifestations of rickets in children (prior to epiphyseal fusion) and osteomalacia in adults.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of vitamin D deficiency ranges from asymptomatic subclinical states to severe, debilitating bone and muscle disease. In many adults, the symptoms are subtle, non-specific, and slowly progressive, often leading to delayed recognition and diagnosis.

Key clinical features include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diffuse musculoskeletal pain:} Patients frequently complain of dull, aching, and generalized pain localized to the bones and muscles. The pain is characteristically worse in weight-bearing joints, the lower back, pelvis, hips, and lower extremities. Bone pain is often described as deep and throbbing, and it can be elicited clinically by applying gentle pressure over the sternum, anterior tibia, or greater trochanters.
    \item \textbf{Proximal muscle weakness:} Vitamin D is critical for skeletal muscle cell function. Deficiency leads to atrophy of Type II (fast-twitch) muscle fibers. Affected individuals experience weakness in the proximal muscle groups, particularly the pelvic girdle and thighs. This manifests as difficulty rising from a chair without using the arms, trouble climbing stairs, and a characteristic waddling gait.
    \item \textbf{Fatigue and generalized malaise:} Chronic, unexplained fatigue and low energy levels are common complaints. This fatigue is often disproportionate to daily activities and is partially attributed to the direct cellular effects of vitamin D deficiency on mitochondrial function and skeletal muscle energy metabolism.
    \item \textbf{Increased risk of falls and fractures:} Due to the combination of muscle weakness, impaired balance, and reduced bone mineral density, individuals with vitamin D deficiency have a significantly elevated risk of falls. This increases the incidence of fragility fractures, particularly of the femoral neck, distal radius, and vertebral bodies.
    \item \textbf{Skeletal deformities in children (Rickets):} In growing children, the lack of mineralization at the growth plates causes severe structural deformities. Clinical signs include bowing of the femurs and tibias (genu varum), knock-knees (genu valgum), enlargement of the wrists and ankles, widening of the costochondral junctions (known as the ``rachitic rosary''), delayed closure of the cranial fontanelles (craniotabes), and frontal bossing of the skull. Children may also present with delayed dentition, short stature, and motor milestone delays.
    \item \textbf{Hypocalcaemic symptoms:} In severe, acute cases, particularly in neonates and young infants, hypocalcaemia can manifest as neuromuscular irritability. Signs include perioral paresthesia, muscle spasms, tetany, carpopedal spasms, laryngospasm, and generalized seizures.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the clinical features of vitamin D deficiency---such as generalized pain, muscle weakness, and fatigue---are highly non-specific, it is critical to distinguish it from other conditions that present with similar musculoskeletal and systemic manifestations.

The differential diagnoses to consider include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fibromyalgia:} Characterized by widespread musculoskeletal pain, fatigue, sleep disturbances, and localized tender points. While fibromyalgia does not cause abnormalities in bone biochemistry or metabolic bone markers, vitamin D deficiency must be ruled out or corrected, as the two conditions can coexist and exacerbate pain symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Polymyalgia Rheumatica (PMR):} An inflammatory disorder occurring in older adults that presents with severe pain and stiffness in the shoulder and pelvic girdles. Unlike vitamin D deficiency, PMR is characterized by markedly elevated inflammatory markers, such as the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP), and normal bone biochemistry.
    \item \textbf{Primary Hyperparathyroidism:} Caused by an autonomous parathyroid adenoma or hyperplasia. While both primary and secondary hyperparathyroidism exhibit elevated PTH levels, primary hyperparathyroidism is distinguished by elevated serum calcium (hypercalcaemia) and normal or elevated vitamin D levels, whereas secondary hyperparathyroidism due to vitamin D deficiency presents with low-normal or low serum calcium.
    \item \textbf{Osteoporosis:} While closely linked to vitamin D deficiency, osteoporosis is a primary disorder of bone microarchitecture leading to decreased bone mass, but the mineral-to-collagen ratio remains normal. In contrast, osteomalacia is specifically a defect in osteoid mineralization. Bone biopsies or specific radiological findings help differentiate the two, though they frequently co-occur in elderly patients.
    \item \textbf{Hypophosphatasia and Renal Tubular Acidosis:} Inborn errors of metabolism or acquired renal disorders that impair mineralization. Renal tubular acidosis presents with metabolic acidosis and hypokalaemia, which are absent in uncomplicated vitamin D deficiency. Hypophosphatasia is characterized by low alkaline phosphatase activity, whereas vitamin D deficiency typically causes elevated alkaline phosphatase.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory Myopathies (Polymyositis/Dermatomyositis):} Present with proximal muscle weakness. However, these autoimmune conditions are accompanied by significantly elevated muscle enzymes (such as creatine kinase) and inflammatory changes on electromyography or muscle biopsy, which are absent in vitamin D-induced myopathy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most individuals with mild to moderate vitamin D deficiency do not require emergency medical intervention and can be managed through routine outpatient clinical appointments. However, severe deficiency can precipitate acute, life-threatening metabolic crises that demand immediate medical attention.

Patients should contact a primary care physician if they experience persistent, unexplained bone pain, progressive muscle weakness that impairs mobility, chronic fatigue, or a history of multiple low-impact fractures. A medical consultation is also warranted for individuals with known risk factors, such as malabsorption syndromes or those taking medications that interfere with vitamin D metabolism.

Immediate emergency medical care must be sought if symptoms of severe hypocalcaemia develop. These symptoms indicate critical neuromuscular instability and require urgent intravenous stabilization. Emergency services---such as **local emergency services** in many countries, **911** in the United States, or **999** in the United Kingdom---should be contacted immediately if a patient exhibits:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Generalized seizures or sudden loss of consciousness.
    \item Laryngospasm, causing severe difficulty breathing, high-pitched stridor, or choking.
    \item Tetany, characterized by painful, uncontrollable muscle spasms, particularly in the hands and feet (carpopedal spasm) or facial muscles.
    \item Cardiorespiratory instability, including chest pain, palpitations, or arrhythmias, which may be associated with hypocalcaemia-induced prolongation of the QT interval on an electrocardiogram.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of vitamin D status is established through a combination of clinical evaluation, biochemical testing, and, when indicated, radiological imaging. Self-diagnosis is discouraged, as symptoms are non-specific and excessive self-supplementation can lead to toxicity.

The diagnostic workup typically involves:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] assay:} This is the gold standard test for assessing vitamin D stores. It reflects both cutaneous synthesis and dietary intake. The serum levels are interpreted as follows:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Deficiency:} Serum 25(OH)D levels less than 50 nmol/L (20 ng/mL).
        \item \textbf{Insufficiency:} Serum 25(OH)D levels between 50 and 75 nmol/L (20--30 ng/mL).
        \item \textbf{Sufficiency:} Serum 25(OH)D levels greater than 75 nmol/L (30 ng/mL), with optimal levels generally targeted between 75 and 125 nmol/L.
        \item \textbf{Toxicity:} Serum 25(OH)D levels greater than 250 nmol/L (100 ng/mL), often accompanied by hypercalcaemia.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Secondary Biochemical Markers:} To assess the physiological impact of deficiency, clinicians evaluate:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Serum Calcium and Phosphate:} Often low-normal or low in moderate-to-severe deficiency.
        \item \textbf{Parathyroid Hormone (PTH):} Elevated (secondary hyperparathyroidism) in response to falling calcium levels.
        \item \textbf{Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP):} Elevated, reflecting increased bone turnover and osteoblastic activity trying to repair the unmineralized osteoid.
        \item \textbf{24-Hour Urine Calcium:} Low, as the kidneys actively reabsorb calcium under the influence of elevated PTH.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Radiological Imaging:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Plain X-rays:} In children with rickets, X-rays of the wrists or knees show characteristic cupping, fraying, and widening of the metaphyses. In adults with osteomalacia, radiographs may reveal cortical thinning and ``Looser's zones'' (pseudofractures), which are radiolucent bands perpendicular to the bone cortex, commonly found in the pelvis, femur, and scapula.
        \item \textbf{Dual-Energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA):} Used to measure bone mineral density (BMD) at the lumbar spine and hip, helping to quantify the severity of osteopenia or osteoporosis.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The primary objective of managing vitamin D deficiency is to restore circulating 25(OH)D levels to a safe, sufficient range, reverse skeletal and neuromuscular symptoms, and normalize calcium and phosphorus metabolism. The treatment regimen depends on the severity of the deficiency, patient age, underlying comorbidities, and the presence of malabsorption.

The management protocol typically includes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological Supplementation:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Loading Therapy (for Severe Deficiency):} Adults with 25(OH)D levels less than 50 nmol/L are typically treated with high-dose cholecalciferol (Vitamin D$_{3}$). Dosing strategies include 50,000 IU orally once weekly for 8 weeks, or 3,000 to 5,000 IU orally daily for 6 to 8 weeks. Cholecalciferol is preferred over ergocalciferol (D$_{2}$) because it is more effective at raising and maintaining serum 25(OH)D levels.
        \item \textbf{Maintenance Therapy:} Once serum levels reach sufficiency ($>$75 nmol/L), maintenance dosing is initiated, typically ranging from 800 to 2,000 IU orally daily, depending on individual risk factors and sun exposure.
        \item \textbf{Active Vitamin D Analogues:} In patients with chronic kidney disease (stage 4 or 5) or hypoparathyroidism, active calcitriol (1,25(OH)$_{2}$D) or analogues like alfacalcidol are prescribed, as these patients cannot hydroxylate standard vitamin D.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Calcium Co-supplementation:} To prevent ``hungry bone syndrome'' and ensure adequate substrate for bone mineralization, patients should maintain a daily calcium intake of 1,000 to 1,200 mg. If dietary intake is insufficient, calcium carbonate or calcium citrate supplements are co-prescribed.
    \item \textbf{Management of Malabsorption States:} Patients with celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, or a history of gastric bypass require significantly higher doses of oral vitamin D (often 10,000 to 50,000 IU daily) or may benefit from ultraviolet B phototherapy or intramuscular vitamin D injections where available.
    \item \textbf{Clinical Monitoring:} Serum 25(OH)D, calcium, and renal function should be reassessed 3 months after initiating loading therapy to ensure target levels are achieved and to screen for hypercalcaemia. Once stabilized, annual monitoring is generally sufficient.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated, prolonged vitamin D deficiency can lead to severe skeletal, neuromuscular, and systemic complications. Conversely, inappropriate, excessive supplementation can result in vitamin D toxicity.

Potential complications include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Osteoporosis and Fragility Fractures:} Accelerated bone loss increases skeletal fragility. Minor falls can result in debilitating hip, wrist, or vertebral fractures, which are associated with high morbidity and mortality in elderly populations.
    \item \textbf{Permanent Skeletal Deformities:} In children, untreated rickets can lead to permanent bowing of the legs, spinal deformities (scoliosis or kyphosis), pelvic narrowing, and short stature. Pelvic deformities in females can lead to obstetric complications (obstructed labor) later in life.
    \item \textbf{Sarcopenia and Mobility Impairment:} Severe muscle weakness and muscle wasting (sarcopenia) increase the likelihood of frequent falls, loss of independence, and chronic disability in older adults.
    \item \textbf{Vitamin D Toxicity (Hypervitaminosis D):} Occurs almost exclusively due to excessive intake of high-dose supplements (usually long-term intake exceeding 10,000 IU/day). This causes hypercalcaemia and hypercalciuria.
    \item \textbf{Nephrolithiasis and Nephrocalcinosis:} Elevated calcium excretion in the urine promotes the formation of calcium oxalate kidney stones (nephrolithiasis) and can lead to calcium deposition within the kidney parenchyma (nephrocalcinosis), potentially causing chronic kidney damage.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals with vitamin D deficiency is generally excellent, provided the condition is diagnosed early and managed appropriately.

With adequate supplementation, biochemical parameters respond rapidly. Secondary hyperparathyroidism resolves within weeks, and serum alkaline phosphatase levels normalize over several months. Muskeletal pain and proximal muscle weakness typically begin to improve within 2 to 4 weeks of initiating therapy, restoring physical function and reducing the risk of falls.

In adults with osteomalacia, bone mineralization is gradually restored, and pseudofractures fully heal, leaving no long-term structural deficits. In children with rickets, early treatment leads to the correction of active skeletal lesions and allows normal bone growth to resume. However, if treatment is delayed until after the growth plates have fused, structural bone deformities (such as severe bowing of the legs or pelvic narrowing) may become permanent, requiring surgical correction (osteotomy) and physical rehabilitation.

For individuals with underlying chronic illnesses, such as chronic kidney disease or severe malabsorption, the prognosis is linked to the management of the primary disease. In these cases, close, lifelong clinical monitoring is essential to adapt treatment doses and prevent metabolic bone disease recurrence.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventing vitamin D deficiency involves maintaining a balance between safe sun exposure, a nutrient-rich diet, and targeted supplementation, tailored to individual risk factors.

Practical self-care and preventive measures include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Safe Sunlight Exposure:} For most people, brief, daily exposure of the arms and legs to sunlight is sufficient. During summer, exposing the skin for 5 to 15 minutes (depending on skin type) mid-morning or mid-afternoon is generally safe and effective. In winter, or for individuals with dark skin, longer exposure is required. Sun safety guidelines must be followed to minimize the risk of skin cancer; prolonged exposure without sunscreen is not recommended.
    \item \textbf{Dietary Intake:} Increase consumption of vitamin D-rich foods. Natural sources include oily fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines, and herring), cod liver oil, beef liver, egg yolks, and sun-exposed mushrooms. Additionally, fortified foods---such as milk, orange juice, cereals, and plant-based milk alternatives---can contribute significantly to daily intake.
    \item \textbf{Routine Supplementation for High-Risk Groups:} The Dietary Reference Intake (DRI) recommended by the Institute of Medicine is 600 IU/day for individuals aged 1 to 70 years, and 800 IU/day for adults over 70. Exclusively breastfed infants should receive 400 IU/day of liquid vitamin D drops starting from birth.
    \item \textbf{Regular Physical Activity:} Engaging in weight-bearing exercises (such as walking, jogging, resistance training, and balance exercises) helps maintain bone density and muscle strength, synergizing with vitamin D to prevent falls and fractures.
    \item \textbf{Avoidance of High-Dose Self-Supplementation:} Consumers should not take mega-doses of vitamin D (e.g., above 4,000 IU/day) without medical supervision, as this increases the risk of toxicity and hypercalcaemia.
\end{itemize}
