\chapter{Magnesium}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Magnesium is an essential mineral and the second most abundant intracellular divalent cation in the human body. It acts as a cofactor in more than 300 enzymatic reactions, regulating diverse physiological processes including protein synthesis, muscle and nerve function, blood glucose control, and blood pressure regulation. Clinically, magnesium is utilised as a nutritional supplement, a pharmacological agent for treating acute hypomagnesaemia, an anticonvulsant in pre-eclampsia and eclampsia, a tocolytic, and an antiarrhythmic agent for specific cardiac conditions such as Torsades de Pointes.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hypomagnesaemia (low magnesium) is a common electrolyte disorder, affecting approximately 2\% to 15\% of the general population and up to 60\% to 65\% of critically ill patients in intensive care units. Dietary surveys in Australia and other developed nations suggest that a substantial proportion of the population does not meet the recommended daily intake of magnesium due to high consumption of processed foods. In contrast, hypermagnesaemia (elevated magnesium) is rare and typically occurs in patients with advanced chronic kidney disease who ingest magnesium-containing antacids, laxatives, or supplements.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Magnesium homeostasis is tightly regulated by intestinal absorption, bone storage, and renal excretion. Disturbances in these mechanisms lead to clinical deficiency or toxicity.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal absorption:} Absorbed primarily in the small intestine (jejunum and ileum) via passive paracellular transport and active transcellular transport mediated by TRPM6 and TRPM7 channels.
    \item \textbf{Renal regulation:} The kidneys are the principal regulators of magnesium balance; approximately 70\% of ultrafiltrable magnesium is reabsorbed in the thick ascending limb of the loop of Henle via paracellular pathways.
    \item \textbf{Causes of hypomagnesaemia:} Chronic dietary deficiency, alcoholism (which impairs renal reabsorption), chronic diarrhoea, malabsorption syndromes, and medications such as proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and loop diuretics.
    \item \textbf{Causes of hypermagnesaemia:} Advanced renal impairment (GFR less than 30 mL/min) combined with exogenous magnesium intake (e.g. magnesium hydroxide laxatives or intravenous infusions).
    \item \textbf{Neuromuscular irritability:} Magnesium acts as a natural calcium antagonist; low levels increase acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction, leading to muscle hyperexcitability, tetany, and seizures.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The manifestations of magnesium disorders primarily affect the neuromuscular, cardiovascular, and metabolic systems.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hypomagnesaemia - Neuromuscular:} Muscle cramps, spasms, tremors, hyperreflexia, tetany (positive Chvostek and Trousseau signs), and generalised seizures.
    \item \textbf{Hypomagnesaemia - Cardiovascular:} ECG changes (prolonged PR and QT intervals, widened QRS), ventricular arrhythmias, Torsades de Pointes, and increased sensitivity to digoxin toxicity.
    \item \textbf{Hypomagnesaemia - Metabolic:} Refractory hypokalaemia and hypocalcaemia, as magnesium is required for potassium retention and parathyroid hormone (PTH) secretion and action.
    \item \textbf{Hypermagnesaemia - Mild to Moderate:} Nausea, vomiting, flushing, headache, lethargy, and loss of deep tendon reflexes.
    \item \textbf{Hypermagnesaemia - Severe:} Hypotension, bradycardia, respiratory depression, muscle paralysis, and cardiac arrest.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Magnesium imbalances must be distinguished from other electrolyte anomalies and neurological conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hypocalcaemia:} Presents with similar neuromuscular irritability (tetany, spasms, seizures) and must be differentiated, although it frequently coexists with hypomagnesaemia.
    \item \textbf{Hypokalaemia:} Causes muscle weakness, cramps, and arrhythmias; refractory cases are often secondary to unrecognised magnesium depletion.
    \item \textbf{Essential tremor or dystonia:} Chronic movement disorders that cause tremors and muscle contractions but present with normal serum electrolyte levels.
    \item \textbf{Uraemia:} In patients with renal failure, symptoms of lethargy, confusion, and neuromuscular signs can mimic hypermagnesaemia.
    \item \textbf{Guillain-Barré syndrome:} Presents with progressive weakness and loss of deep tendon reflexes, mimicking moderate-to-severe hypermagnesaemia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients taking magnesium supplements or those with chronic kidney disease who develop unexplained muscle weakness, lethargy, or persistent muscle cramps should seek medical advice.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seizures, sudden muscle paralysis, or severe difficulty breathing
    \item Palpitations, chest pain, or irregular heartbeats
    \item Severe drowsiness, confusion, or loss of consciousness
    \item Accidental or intentional overdose of intravenous or high-dose oral magnesium
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis of magnesium disorders relies on laboratory serum measurements and clinical assessments of predisposing factors.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Serum magnesium test:} The standard diagnostic tool, with a normal reference range typically between 0.70 and 1.10 mmol/L; however, serum levels may not reflect total body magnesium stores as most is intracellular.
    \item \textbf{Fractional excretion of magnesium (FEMg):} A 24-hour urine collection to differentiate renal magnesium wasting (FEMg greater than 2\% to 3\%) from extrarenal causes (FEMg less than 1\%).
    \item \textbf{Electrocardiogram (ECG):} Essential in both deficiency and excess to monitor for life-threatening conduction abnormalities and arrhythmias.
    \item \textbf{Co-electrolyte panels:} Checking serum calcium, potassium, phosphate, and renal function (urea, creatinine, and GFR) to identify concurrent abnormalities.
    \item \textbf{Parathyroid hormone (PTH) level:} Checked in chronic hypomagnesaemia with hypocalcaemia to assess for functional hypoparathyroidism.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment involves correcting the underlying cause, replenishing magnesium in deficiency, and enhancing elimination in toxicity.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Oral supplementation:} Magnesium oxide, carbonate, or chloride tablets for mild, chronic, or dietary deficiency; higher doses can cause diarrhoea due to osmotic effects.
    \item \textbf{Intravenous magnesium therapy:} Intravenous magnesium sulfate (MgSO4) for severe, symptomatic hypomagnesaemia, pre-eclampsia/eclampsia, or Torsades de Pointes, administered with continuous ECG monitoring.
    \item \textbf{Management of hypermagnesaemia:} Discontinue all exogenous magnesium sources immediately and initiate intravenous fluids to promote renal clearance.
    \item \textbf{Intravenous calcium gluconate:} Administered in severe hypermagnesaemia to antagonise the neuromuscular and cardiotoxic effects of magnesium.
    \item \textbf{Haemodialysis:} Indicated in patients with severe hypermagnesaemia and advanced renal failure to rapidly remove magnesium from circulation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias, including ventricular fibrillation and Torsades de Pointes.
    \item Severe respiratory failure and skeletal muscle paralysis secondary to hypermagnesaemia.
    \item Intractable hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, leading to severe tetany or seizures.
    \item Chronic bowel disturbances, such as diarrhoea and abdominal pain from oral supplement overuse.
    \item Cardiac arrest and death from unrecognised magnesium toxicity, especially in renal impairment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for magnesium disorders is generally excellent with timely diagnosis and appropriate therapy. Hypomagnesaemia is easily corrected with oral or intravenous supplementation, leading to rapid resolution of symptoms and normalisation of dependent electrolytes. Similarly, hypermagnesaemia responds well to discontinuation of magnesium and supportive care, provided renal function is intact. Long-term outcomes depend primarily on the treatment and control of the underlying causative conditions, such as renal disease, malabsorption, or dietary habits.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary intake:} Consume magnesium-rich foods, including leafy green vegetables (spinach, kale), nuts, seeds, legumes, and whole grains.
    \item \textbf{Supplement caution:} Avoid high-dose over-the-counter magnesium supplements without medical advice, particularly if you have kidney disease.
    \item \textbf{Monitor with diuretics:} Have serum electrolytes checked regularly if taking loop or thiazide diuretics or long-term proton pump inhibitors.
    \item \textbf{Recognise early signs:} Seek medical evaluation if you experience persistent muscle twitching, cramps, or unexplained fatigue.
    \item \textbf{Restrict laxative use:} Limit the use of magnesium-containing laxatives or antacids to short-term relief.
\end{itemize}
