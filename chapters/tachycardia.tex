\section*{Definition and Overview}
Tachycardia simply means a heart rate that is faster than normal. In adults, a resting heart rate above 100 beats per minute is generally considered tachycardic, although the normal range varies with age, fitness, and situation: a newborn's heart naturally beats far faster than an adult's, and a well-trained athlete may have a resting heart rate well below 60. Tachycardia is not a disease in itself but a sign, and its meaning ranges from completely normal to life-threatening. A heart racing during exercise, fear, or fever is a healthy response; a heart racing while sitting still, for no reason, may indicate a problem that deserves investigation.

The term covers two very different situations. Sinus tachycardia is a normal speeding-up of the heart's natural pacemaker, the sinus node, in response to exercise, stress, illness, or other demands on the body. Arrhythmic tachycardia is a disturbance of the heart's electrical system in which the heart beats rapidly because of an abnormal rhythm, such as atrial fibrillation, supraventricular tachycardia, or, at the dangerous end of the spectrum, ventricular tachycardia. Distinguishing these is the central task of assessment, because the treatment and the risk are entirely different.

This chapter explains what makes the heart speed up, how the different types of tachycardia present, which ones are harmless and which are dangerous, and what to do when a fast heartbeat is felt. It also covers the tests used to catch an intermittent rhythm problem and the treatments that slow the heart, restore normal rhythm, and prevent the complications of persistent rapid heartbeats.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Tachycardia in its simplest form, sinus tachycardia, is universal: everyone's heart speeds up with exercise, stress, and fever, and it is the most common "abnormal heart rate" finding in general practice. Arrhythmic tachycardia is also common, and its frequency rises steeply with age. Atrial fibrillation, the commonest significant tachyarrhythmia, affects a small percentage of people over 60 and becomes progressively more common with each decade, and its rates are rising in many countries and worldwide as the population ages. Supraventricular tachycardia typically begins in younger adults, often in the twenties or thirties, and affects women and men fairly equally. Ventricular tachycardia is much rarer and occurs mostly in people with heart disease, previous heart attacks, or inherited heart conditions.

There are also important patterns by setting. Tachycardia in hospital is most often sinus tachycardia in response to pain, fever, dehydration, or bleeding. In the community, palpitations are one of the most common reasons people see a GP, and in the large majority of cases no significant heart disease is found. These figures reassure: a racing heart is common, usually benign, and only sometimes a marker of something serious that needs treatment.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The heart rate is set by the sinus node, the heart's natural pacemaker, which normally fires about sixty to one hundred times a minute in a resting adult. The sinus node is controlled by the nervous system: the sympathetic ("fight or flight") system speeds it up and the parasympathetic system slows it down, so any state that activates the sympathetic system, such as exercise, anxiety, fever, dehydration, blood loss, or an overactive thyroid, causes sinus tachycardia. This is a normal, useful response that increases oxygen delivery to the body.

Arrhythmic tachycardias have different mechanisms. In atrial fibrillation, many rapid, disorganised electrical signals fire across the atria, making the upper chambers quiver and producing a fast, irregular pulse. In supraventricular tachycardia, an extra electrical pathway in or near the upper chambers forms a short-circuit that drives a sudden, very regular racing rhythm. In ventricular tachycardia, the rapid rhythm arises in the ventricles, the heart's main pumping chambers, and is dangerous because it can collapse blood pressure or degenerate into cardiac arrest, particularly in hearts already damaged by coronary disease or scarring.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sinus tachycardia:} exercise, stress, anxiety, pain, fever, dehydration, blood loss, anaemia, low blood oxygen, caffeine, alcohol, and an overactive thyroid
    \item \textbf{Atrial fibrillation:} high blood pressure, heart valve disease, heart failure, lung disease, alcohol, and age, sometimes with no cause found
    \item \textbf{Supraventricular tachycardia:} an extra electrical pathway present from birth that acts as a short-circuit
    \item \textbf{Ventricular tachycardia:} coronary artery disease, previous heart attack, heart failure, cardiomyopathy, and inherited conditions such as long QT syndrome
    \item \textbf{Drugs and substances:} stimulants, some cold and asthma medicines, energy drinks, and illegal drugs can all speed the heart
    \item \textbf{Electrolyte and hormonal imbalance:} abnormal potassium or magnesium, and thyroid disease, disturb the heart's electrical stability
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features of tachycardia depend on the underlying rhythm and how fast the heart is beating, and they range from a subtle awareness of the heartbeat to collapse.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Palpitations:} an awareness of the heart beating fast, fluttering, or racing, sometimes described as a "flip-flop" in the chest
    \item \textbf{Rapid pulse:} a pulse that may be regular, as in sinus tachycardia and supraventricular tachycardia, or irregular, as in atrial fibrillation
    \item \textbf{Dizziness and light-headedness:} common when blood pressure falls during a fast episode
    \item \textbf{Shortness of breath:} especially with effort, or at rest during an episode
    \item \textbf{Chest discomfort:} tightness, pressure, or pain that may accompany the rapid rhythm
    \item \textbf{Fainting or near-fainting:} a sudden, sustained fast rhythm can reduce blood flow to the brain enough to cause collapse
    \item \textbf{Sweating and anxiety:} the racing heart often triggers a sense of dread or panic
    \item \textbf{Sudden onset and offset:} arrhythmic tachycardias typically start and stop abruptly, whereas sinus tachycardia builds and fades gradually
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The most important diagnostic task is to separate the fast rhythms from each other and from conditions that mimic them, because the treatment differs so much.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sinus tachycardia:} the heart's normal response to a trigger; the rate rises and falls smoothly and the rhythm is regular
    \item \textbf{Atrial fibrillation:} fast and irregular; confirmed on the ECG and important to treat because of stroke risk
    \item \textbf{Supraventricular tachycardia:} sudden, very regular fast rhythm in young people, usually not dangerous but unpleasant
    \item \textbf{Ventricular tachycardia:} a fast, broad-complex rhythm that is a medical emergency in those with heart disease
    \item \textbf{Panic attack and anxiety:} anxiety causes sinus tachycardia and palpitations, and can coexist with genuine arrhythmias
    \item \textbf{Hyperthyroidism:} an overactive thyroid causes persistent sinus tachycardia and palpitations, and is detected by blood tests
    \item \textbf{Anaemia and dehydration:} both speed the heart as the body compensates, and are identified by examination and blood tests
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Occasional palpitations with no other symptoms, especially when related to stress, caffeine, or exercise, can be discussed at a routine GP visit. See your GP if the racing heart is frequent, prolonged, or associated with dizziness, or if you have heart disease, heart failure, or a family history of sudden cardiac death, since these change the risk. An irregular heartbeat, especially in someone over 60, warrants prompt review because of the risk of atrial fibrillation.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, pressure, or tightness with a fast or racing heartbeat
    \item Fainting, collapse, or near-fainting during an episode
    \item Severe shortness of breath or breathlessness at rest
    \item A very fast heartbeat that does not settle, with sweating, confusion, or feeling cold and clammy
    \item A known heart condition with a new or very fast rhythm
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The aim of investigation is to capture the rhythm on a recording, identify its type, and look for the cause. Because many tachycardias come and go, several strategies are used to catch them.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pulse and examination:} the rate, regularity, and character of the pulse, blood pressure, and signs of thyroid or heart disease
    \item \textbf{Resting ECG:} a twelve-lead electrocardiogram records the heart's electrical activity during the visit and is the key test
    \item \textbf{Holter monitor:} a wearable recorder that captures the rhythm continuously for 24 to 48 hours
    \item \textbf{Event monitor:} a device worn for weeks that the person activates when symptoms occur
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, thyroid function, and electrolytes check for anaemia, thyroid disease, and mineral imbalance
    \item \textbf{Echocardiogram:} an ultrasound of the heart checks valve and pump function and looks for structural disease
    \item \textbf{Exercise stress test:} a treadmill ECG can provoke rhythm problems that appear only with activity
    \item \textbf{Electrophysiology study:} an invasive test that maps the heart's electrical pathways and can treat some arrhythmias at the same time
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is directed at the underlying cause and the specific rhythm, and ranges from simple lifestyle advice to emergency electrical treatment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Treat the cause:} correcting dehydration, fever, anaemia, or thyroid disease resolves sinus tachycardia on its own
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} reducing caffeine, alcohol, and stimulants, and managing stress, settles many benign palpitations
    \item \textbf{Vagal manoeuvres:} holding the breath and straining, or splashing cold water on the face, can terminate supraventricular tachycardia
    \item \textbf{Beta-blockers and rate-controlling drugs:} slow the heart rate in atrial fibrillation and other fast rhythms
    \item \textbf{Rhythm-control medicines:} antiarrhythmic drugs are used to restore and maintain normal rhythm in selected cases
    \item \textbf{Cardioversion:} a controlled electric shock restores normal rhythm in atrial fibrillation, and is used as an emergency in ventricular tachycardia
    \item \textbf{Ablation:} a catheter procedure that destroys the abnormal electrical pathway, offering a potential cure for supraventricular tachycardia and many cases of atrial fibrillation
    \item \textbf{Anticoagulants:} blood-thinning medicines prevent stroke in people with atrial fibrillation
    \item \textbf{Implantable defibrillator:} a device that detects and treats dangerous ventricular rhythms in high-risk patients
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Most tachycardias are harmless, but the persistent or dangerous rhythms can cause serious complications if left untreated.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stroke:} atrial fibrillation allows clots to form in the heart, which can travel to the brain
    \item \textbf{Heart failure:} a chronically fast or irregular heartbeat weakens the heart over time, a state called tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy
    \item \textbf{Fainting and injury:} sudden drops in blood pressure cause falls and accidents
    \item \textbf{Cardiac arrest:} ventricular tachycardia can degenerate into a life-threatening rhythm in people with heart disease
    \item \textbf{Impaired exercise tolerance:} persistent rapid rhythm reduces the heart's efficiency and capacity
    \item \textbf{Anxiety and worry:} recurrent palpitations often cause significant fear, even when the rhythm is benign
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for most people with tachycardia is excellent. Sinus tachycardia is a normal response that resolves when the trigger is corrected, and supraventricular tachycardia, though distressing, does not damage the heart and can often be cured with ablation. Atrial fibrillation is very manageable with modern treatments, and stroke risk is dramatically reduced with appropriate anticoagulation. Even ventricular tachycardia can be controlled in most people with medicines, ablation, or a defibrillator.

The single most important factor in prognosis is the type of rhythm and the health of the underlying heart. A person with a structurally normal heart and a benign rhythm can expect a normal lifespan, whereas a person with heart disease and ventricular arrhythmias needs specialist care. For everyone, the take-home message is the same: a racing heart deserves an ECG, and once the rhythm is identified, most tachycardias can be controlled or cured so that they no longer limit life or cause fear.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Limit caffeine, energy drinks, alcohol, and over-the-counter stimulant cold medicines
    \item Manage stress with relaxation, breathing exercises, and regular activity
    \item Keep well hydrated, especially in hot weather and during illness
    \item Maintain good control of blood pressure, diabetes, and other heart risk factors
    \item Stay physically active, building fitness gradually, which lowers the resting heart rate
    \item Take prescribed heart medicines exactly as directed, and do not stop them without advice
    \item Learn to take your own pulse, and keep a simple diary of palpitations, with timing and triggers, to show your GP
\end{itemize}
