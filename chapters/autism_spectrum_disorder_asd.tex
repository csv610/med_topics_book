\chapter{Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is the formal medical diagnosis given to people who show persistent differences in social communication and interaction together with restricted, repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests or activities. These differences begin in early childhood, are present across different settings, and cause meaningful difficulty in daily life. ASD replaced older labels such as autistic disorder, Asperger's syndrome and pervasive developmental disorder, which are now grouped together under one diagnosis with levels of support. The diagnosis is made by experienced clinicians using standardised tools and describes a pattern of development, not a person's worth or potential.

\section*{Epidemiology}
ASD is one of the most commonly diagnosed neurodevelopmental conditions, affecting roughly one in 70 to one in 100 people in Australia, and it is diagnosed about three to four times more often in boys than in girls. Girls may be under-diagnosed because their presentation is sometimes less obvious, for example through better copying of social behaviour or quieter, less disruptive interests. ASD occurs across all cultures and backgrounds. Many people with ASD also have an intellectual disability, and many have coexisting ADHD, anxiety, depression, epilepsy or sleep disorders. Reported rates have risen over recent decades, largely because of broader criteria and better recognition.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The causes of ASD are complex and still not fully understood. The evidence strongly indicates that genetic factors play a central role, and that no single cause applies to everyone.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} many genes contribute; some are inherited, some arise spontaneously, and some are linked to known syndromes
    \item \textbf{Brain development:} research shows differences in how brain regions grow, connect and communicate
    \item \textbf{Prenatal factors:} certain events in pregnancy, such as some infections or medicines, slightly raise risk
    \item \textbf{Parental age:} older parental age is associated with a small increase in risk
    \item \textbf{No link to vaccines:} large studies have found no connection between vaccination and ASD
    \item \textbf{Not caused by parenting style:} ASD is present from early development and is not a result of how a child is raised
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
To meet the diagnosis, a person has difficulties in both core areas, with the specific features varying hugely between individuals.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Social communication:} reduced sharing of interests and emotions, difficulty with back-and-forth conversation, and less use of eye contact, facial expression and gesture
    \item \textbf{Social relationships:} difficulty developing and keeping friendships appropriate to age
    \item \textbf{Understanding others:} trouble reading tone of voice, sarcasm, jokes and unwritten social rules
    \item \textbf{Restricted interests:} intense focus on particular topics, objects or activities
    \item \textbf{Repetitive behaviour:} hand-flapping, rocking, spinning, lining up objects or repeating words and phrases
    \item \textbf{Routine and sameness:} distress at small changes, and insistence on doing things a certain way
    \item \textbf{Sensory differences:} unusually strong or weak reactions to sound, light, touch, taste, smell or movement
    \item \textbf{Development in early life:} symptoms are present in childhood, though they may only become obvious later
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because some features of ASD overlap with other conditions, a careful assessment is needed to tell them apart, especially in young children.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Global developmental delay and intellectual disability:} slow development without the specific social pattern of ASD
    \item \textbf{Language disorder:} delayed or disordered speech with otherwise typical social interest
    \item \textbf{ADHD:} inattention, restlessness and impulsivity, which can coexist with or mimic ASD
    \item \textbf{Social anxiety disorder:} social avoidance due to fear rather than a difference in understanding
    \item \textbf{Selective mutism:} not speaking in some settings while communicating normally in others
    \item \textbf{Hearing loss:} poor response to sound or speech that can look like social withdrawal
    \item \textbf{Reactive attachment or trauma:} early adversity can affect social development
    \item \textbf{Obsessive-compulsive disorder:} repetitive thoughts and behaviours that are not part of a wider social difference
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Talk to a GP or child health nurse early if a child does not respond to their name by 12 months, does not babble or point by 12 to 14 months, has no single words by 16 months or no two-word phrases by 24 months, loses speech or social skills, or does not play with others in typical ways.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A seizure or convulsion
    \item A child or adult who has wandered and is missing or in danger
    \item Serious injury, or signs of a medical emergency such as difficulty breathing
    \item A mental health crisis involving immediate risk to safety
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
There is no blood test or scan that diagnoses ASD. Diagnosis relies on a comprehensive developmental assessment by a team of experienced professionals.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical history:} a detailed account of early development, behaviour, communication and family history
    \item \textbf{Structured observation:} standardised play- and interview-based assessments (such as the ADOS)
    \item \textbf{Developmental questionnaires:} parent and teacher rating scales that screen for autistic features
    \item \textbf{Hearing and vision checks:} to exclude sensory causes of communication difficulties
    \item \textbf{Cognitive and language assessment:} to identify strengths and coexisting delays
    \item \textbf{Medical assessment:} by a paediatrician, child psychiatrist or clinical psychologist
    \item \textbf{Occasional tests:} genetic testing or other investigations if a specific syndrome is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management of ASD aims to build skills, reduce distress and support participation, tailored to each person's needs and strengths.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Early intervention:} programmes that support communication, play, self-care and social engagement from a young age
    \item \textbf{Behavioural approaches:} positive, structured strategies to teach skills and manage difficult behaviour
    \item \textbf{Speech and language therapy:} for spoken communication, understanding and alternative communication
    \item \textbf{Occupational therapy:} for sensory processing, fine motor skills and everyday routines
    \item \textbf{Psychology:} to help with anxiety, emotion regulation and social understanding
    \item \textbf{Education planning:} adjustments, support staff and individual plans at school
    \item \textbf{Medication:} not for ASD itself, but may be used for severe coexisting problems such as significant irritability, anxiety or ADHD
    \item \textbf{NDIS support:} funding for therapy and supports for many Australians with a diagnosis of ASD
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
ASD is a lifelong pattern rather than a disease, but it carries an increased risk of certain difficulties that deserve attention.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Anxiety disorders and depression, particularly in adolescence and adulthood
    \item Social isolation, bullying and difficulty finding and keeping employment
    \item Epilepsy, affecting a minority of people with ASD
    \item Sleep problems, feeding difficulties and constipation in childhood
    \item Self-injury or challenging behaviour when communication or sensory needs are unmet
    \item Wandering, which can put children and adults at risk of injury
    \item Family stress, and financial burden from the cost of therapies
    \item Harm from unproven or unsafe "therapies" marketed to families
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for people with ASD is highly variable and shaped more by support, coexisting conditions and environment than by the diagnosis alone. Many children who receive early, appropriate support go on to live independently, study and work. Outcomes are generally better in people with good language and intellectual ability, while those with intellectual disability or significant communication difficulties may need lifelong support. Early recognition, family support and inclusive schools are strongly associated with better wellbeing. Many adults with ASD lead rich and fulfilling lives and consider their autism part of who they are.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
ASD cannot be prevented, since its foundations are laid early in development. The most helpful actions are early recognition and good support.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Know the early signs and seek assessment promptly if concerned
    \item Build predictable routines and give clear warnings about change
    \item Support sensory comfort at home, school and work
    \item Encourage the person's interests and strengths
    \item Use visual supports and simple, concrete language where helpful
    \item Address sleep, feeding and constipation early, as these affect wellbeing
    \item Support parents and carers, including respite and peer groups
    \item Work with reputable professionals and be wary of unproven treatments
\end{itemize}
