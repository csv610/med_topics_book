\chapter{Multiple Myeloma Treatment}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Multiple myeloma treatment refers to the multimodal therapeutic approach aimed at managing clonal plasma cell proliferation within the bone marrow. Multiple myeloma is a haematological malignancy characterised by bone marrow infiltration of abnormal plasma cells, monoclonal protein (M-protein) production, and clinical signs of end-organ damage, commonly summarised by the CRAB criteria: hypercalcaemia, renal impairment, anaemia, and bone lesions. Treatment is highly individualised, determined by patient age, comorbidities, transplant eligibility, and disease risk profile. The primary therapeutic objectives are to achieve deep, durable remissions, prolong overall survival, and preserve or improve the patient's quality of life. The therapeutic landscape has evolved from conventional chemotherapy to a stratified approach utilizing immunomodulatory drugs (IMiDs), proteasome inhibitors (PIs), monoclonal antibodies, autologous stem cell transplantation, and emerging cellular therapies.

\section*{Epidemiology}
In Australia, multiple myeloma accounts for approximately 1.5\% of all newly diagnosed cancers and around 10\% of haematological malignancies. The median age at diagnosis is approximately 70 years, and the disease is rare in individuals under the age of 40. There is a slight male predominance, and incidence rates increase progressively with age. Globally, there are approximately 176,000 new cases diagnosed annually, with the highest incidence rates observed in North America, Western Europe, and Australasia. Risk is also significantly higher in people of African descent compared to Caucasian and Asian populations, pointing to an underlying genetic predisposition.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Multiple myeloma arises from the malignant transformation of post-germinal centre B-lineage plasma cells, typically progressing through premalignant stages.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Premalignant progression:} Virtually all cases are preceded by monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS), which progresses to smouldering multiple myeloma (SMM) and eventually active multiple myeloma at a rate determined by clonal biology.
    \item \textbf{Genetic and cytogenetic abnormalities:} Primary genetic events include immunoglobulin heavy chain (IGH) translocations (e.g., t(4;14), t(11;14), t(14;16)) and hyperdiploidy. Secondary events, such as del(17p) and TP53 mutations, drive disease progression and drug resistance.
    \item \textbf{Microenvironmental interactions:} Malignant plasma cells interact closely with the bone marrow microenvironment, stimulating osteoclast activity and suppressing osteoblast function through cytokines like RANKL and DKK1, leading to osteolysis.
    \item \textbf{M-protein production:} Clonal plasma cells produce excessive amounts of monoclonal immunoglobulins or free light chains, leading to serum hyperviscosity, renal tubular injury, and amyloid deposition.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of multiple myeloma reflects the consequences of clonal plasma cell accumulation and M-protein production.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bone pain and skeletal instability:} Typically presenting as persistent back or rib pain, aggravated by movement, caused by osteolytic lesions and pathological fractures.
    \item \textbf{Anaemia and fatigue:} Resulting from bone marrow infiltration and renal failure, causing pallor, lethargy, and dyspnoea on exertion.
    \item \textbf{Renal impairment:} Characterised by elevated creatinine levels, caused by light chain cast nephropathy, hypercalcaemia, dehydration, or amyloidosis.
    \item \textbf{Hypercalcaemia:} Presenting with symptoms such as nausea, constipation, polyuria, polydipsia, confusion, and generalised muscle weakness.
    \item \textbf{Recurrent infections:} Caused by functional hypogammaglobulinaemia and neutropenia, commonly manifesting as bacterial pneumonias or urinary tract infections.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Distinguishing multiple myeloma from other conditions presenting with monoclonal gammopathy or skeletal lesions is critical.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS):} Characterised by monoclonal protein < 30 g/L, < 10\% bone marrow plasma cells, and the absence of end-organ damage (CRAB criteria).
    \item \textbf{Smouldering multiple myeloma (SMM):} Defined by monoclonal protein $\ge$ 30 g/L and/or 10--60\% bone marrow plasma cells without CRAB criteria or myeloma-defining events.
    \item \textbf{AL amyloidosis:} Characterised by monoclonal light chain deposition in organs (kidney, heart, nerves), presenting with nephrotic syndrome, cardiomyopathy, or neuropathy, without necessarily meeting myeloma cell thresholds.
    \item \textbf{Waldenstrom macroglobulinaemia:} A lymphoplasmacytic lymphoma producing monoclonal IgM, presenting with lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, and hyperviscosity, rather than osteolytic bone lesions.
    \item \textbf{Metastatic carcinoma:} Solid malignancies (e.g., breast, prostate, lung) presenting with osteolytic bone lesions and hypercalcaemia, but without monoclonal plasma cells or paraproteinaemia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients diagnosed with multiple myeloma or undergoing treatment require regular haematological monitoring, but immediate emergency care is essential for acute, life-threatening complications.
\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden onset of severe back pain, lower limb weakness, numbness, or loss of bowel/bladder control, which are signs of spinal cord compression.
    \item High fever (temperature $\ge$ 38$^\circ$C), rigors, chills, or rapid heart rate, indicating systemic infection or neutropenic sepsis.
    \item Confusion, severe drowsiness, stupor, or altered mental status, suggesting severe hypercalcaemia or hyperviscosity.
    \item Chest pain, sudden shortness of breath, or leg swelling, which may indicate venous thromboembolism or pulmonary embolism.
    \item Uncontrolled bleeding or bruising.
\end{itemize}
Patients should contact their haematology team or general practitioner for other less urgent concerns, such as progressive numbness or tingling in the hands and feet, worsening fatigue, persistent bone pain, or a reduction in urine output.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A comprehensive laboratory and radiological workup is required to confirm the diagnosis, stage the disease, and assess organ function prior to treatment initiation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Serum and urine protein electrophoresis (EPG/IEPG):} Identifies and quantifies the monoclonal paraprotein (M-protein spike) in the serum and urine (Bence Jones protein).
    \item \textbf{Serum free light chain (SFLC) assay:} Measures the kappa and lambda light chains, calculating the ratio to detect clonal light chain excess.
    \item \textbf{Bone marrow aspirate and trephine biopsy:} Essential to quantify the percentage of clonal plasma cells (typically $\ge$ 10\% for diagnosis) and obtain tissue for cytogenetic profiling.
    \item \textbf{Cytogenetics (FISH):} Performed on bone marrow cells to identify high-risk abnormalities, such as del(17p), t(4;14), and t(14;16), which guide prognosis and therapy selection.
    \item \textbf{Whole-body low-dose CT or PET-CT:} Preferred imaging modalities to detect osteolytic lesions, bone destruction, or extramedullary plasmacytomas.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment for multiple myeloma is determined by patient age, performance status, and eligibility for autologous stem cell transplantation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Induction therapy:} Combination therapy typically consisting of a proteasome inhibitor (e.g., bortezomib), an immunomodulatory drug (e.g., lenalidomide), and a corticosteroid (e.g., dexamethasone), designed to rapidly reduce tumor burden.
    \item \textbf{Autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT):} High-dose melphalan chemotherapy followed by stem cell rescue, recommended as consolidation for eligible, fit patients (typically aged under 65--70 years).
    \item \textbf{Maintenance therapy:} Long-term, low-dose treatment (most commonly lenalidomide) to sustain remission and prolong progression-free survival post-transplant or after initial induction.
    \item \textbf{Relapsed and refractory therapy:} Management of relapsed disease incorporates second-generation PIs (e.g., carfilzomib), IMiDs (e.g., pomalidomide), monoclonal antibodies (e.g., daratumumab, elotuzumab), or CAR-T cell therapy.
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} Includes bisphosphonates (e.g., zoledronic acid) to prevent skeletal events, erythropoietin for anaemia, antiviral prophylaxis (e.g., aciclovir for bortezomib users), and thromboprophylaxis (e.g., aspirin or low-molecular-weight heparin).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The complications of multiple myeloma and its treatments can be chronic and debilitating, requiring proactive management.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Renal failure:} Light chain cast nephropathy can lead to acute kidney injury and end-stage renal failure requiring dialysis.
    \item \textbf{Skeletal morbidity:} Pathological fractures of long bones or vertebrae, spinal cord compression, and severe bone pain requiring radiotherapy or surgical stabilisation.
    \item \textbf{Peripheral neuropathy:} Frequently induced by proteasome inhibitors (e.g., bortezomib) or immunomodulatory drugs (e.g., thalidomide), presenting as painful paresthesias.
    \item \textbf{Venous thromboembolism:} Increased risk of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, especially when taking immunomodulatory drugs combined with high-dose dexamethasone.
    \item \textbf{Infection risk:} Recurrent bacterial and viral infections (e.g., shingles) due to disease-induced immunosuppression and treatment-related cytopenias.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for patients with multiple myeloma has improved significantly over the past two decades due to the introduction of novel agents, targeted therapies, and improved supportive care. Median survival for transplant-eligible patients now exceeds 8 to 10 years, while older or less fit patients have a median survival of approximately 5 years. Prognosis is heavily dependent on staging (using the Revised International Staging System, R-ISS) and cytogenetic risk factors. Although multiple myeloma remains largely incurable, it is increasingly managed as a chronic relapsing-remitting illness, with patients undergoing multiple sequential lines of therapy over many years.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Patients can adopt specific measures to manage treatment side effects and minimise the impact of the disease.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adequate hydration:} Consuming at least 2--3 litres of water daily to help prevent renal damage from light chain clearance and to manage hypercalcaemia.
    \item \textbf{Infection control measures:} Regular hand hygiene, avoiding contact with sick individuals, keeping vaccinations up to date (influenza, pneumococcal, COVID-19), and taking prophylactic anti-infectives as prescribed.
    \item \textbf{Avoiding nephrotoxins:} Strict avoidance of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen, which can precipitate acute kidney injury.
    \item \textbf{Gentle physical activity:} Engaging in low-impact exercises like walking to help maintain bone density and muscle strength while avoiding high-impact activities that increase fracture risk.
    \item \textbf{Neuropathy monitoring:} Inspecting hands and feet daily for sensory changes, skin integrity, or injury, and reporting symptoms immediately to adjust drug dosing.
\end{itemize}
