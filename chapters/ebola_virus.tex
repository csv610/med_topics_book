\chapter{Ebola Virus}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The Ebola virus is a rare but dangerous virus that causes Ebola virus disease, a severe illness with fever, vomiting, diarrhoea, and bleeding. It belongs to a family of viruses called filoviruses, which also includes the Marburg virus. The virus lives in animal reservoirs, particularly fruit bats, and can spread to humans and then between people through contact with infected body fluids. This chapter focuses on the virus itself: what it is, how it is transmitted, the disease it causes, and the vaccines and treatments developed to control it.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Ebola virus is found mainly in sub-Saharan Africa, where sporadic outbreaks have occurred since it was first identified in 1976. The largest outbreak, in West Africa from 2014 to 2016, infected more than 28,000 people and killed more than 11,000. Outbreaks occur when the virus jumps from its animal reservoir to humans, then spreads within communities and healthcare facilities. The virus is a serious public health threat because of its high fatality rate and potential to spread, but outbreaks have been controlled successfully through detection, isolation, and vaccination.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Ebola is caused by several species of the Ebola virus, which differ in severity.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Species:} Zaire ebolavirus is the most common and lethal; others include Sudan, Bundibugyo, and Taï Forest species, and the related Reston virus, which does not cause disease in humans
    \item \textbf{Reservoir and spillover:} fruit bats are the likely natural reservoir, and infection occurs through contact with bats, primates, or bushmeat
    \item \textbf{Human transmission:} through direct contact with blood, vomit, faeces, saliva, semen, or contaminated surfaces and needles
    \item \textbf{Not airborne:} the virus does not spread through coughing or sneezing
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} the virus enters cells, replicates rapidly, and causes widespread inflammation, damage to blood vessels, and impaired blood clotting
    \item \textbf{Incubation and infectiousness:} symptoms appear within 21 days, and transmission occurs once symptoms start
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The disease follows a characteristic course in those who become ill.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Abrupt onset of fever, severe headache, muscle aches, and fatigue
    \item Sore throat and weakness
    \item Vomiting, diarrhoea, and abdominal pain within days
    \item Rash in some people
    \item Bleeding from the gums, nose, or other sites in severe disease
    \item Organ failure, shock, and death in severe cases
    \item Survivors may experience joint pain, eye problems, and fatigue for months
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The early symptoms of Ebola are shared with many other diseases.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Malaria, which is endemic in the same regions
    \item Typhoid fever
    \item Dengue and other viral fevers
    \item Other viral haemorrhagic fevers, including Marburg and Lassa viruses
    \item Bacterial sepsis and gastroenteritis
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with fever and a history of travel to an outbreak area, or contact with a case, must seek immediate medical attention and disclose this history. Early diagnosis allows isolation, treatment, and the protection of others.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe bleeding, collapse, or shock
    \item Difficulty breathing
    \item Confusion, seizures, or unconsciousness
    \item Severe dehydration
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis of Ebola requires laboratory confirmation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PCR testing:} detects viral genetic material in blood and is the primary diagnostic test
    \item \textbf{Antigen tests:} rapid detection of viral proteins
    \item \textbf{Serology:} detection of antibodies in later stages
    \item \textbf{Supportive blood tests:} blood counts, electrolytes, and liver and kidney function
    \item \textbf{Safe specimen handling:} all testing occurs under strict biosafety conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment combines supportive care with specific therapies.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intensive supportive care:} intravenous fluids, oxygen, and treatment of symptoms and complications
    \item \textbf{Monoclonal antibodies:} treatments such as the antibody combination used in recent outbreaks significantly reduce mortality
    \item \textbf{Experimental and supportive therapies:} used under outbreak protocols
    \item \textbf{Infection prevention and control:} isolation and protective equipment for staff
    \item \textbf{Community engagement:} safe burials and contact tracing to interrupt transmission
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe dehydration and shock
    \item Multiple organ failure
    \item Disseminated intravascular coagulation and severe bleeding
    \item Secondary bacterial infections
    \item Long-term complications in survivors, including vision and joint problems
    \item Psychological trauma for patients, families, and communities
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for Ebola has improved substantially with modern treatment. Where intensive care and monoclonal antibodies are available, a majority of patients now survive. The virus remains a serious threat in areas with limited healthcare infrastructure, where fatality rates are higher. Control measures, including vaccination of contacts, have proven effective at ending outbreaks.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vaccination: the rVSV-ZEBOV vaccine protects against the Zaire species and is used in outbreak response
    \item Avoid travel to active outbreak areas unless essential
    \item Avoid contact with blood and body fluids and practise rigorous hand hygiene
    \item Avoid handling bats, primates, and bushmeat
    \item Follow public health guidance and disclose travel history when seeking care
\end{itemize}
