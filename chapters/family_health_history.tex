\chapter{Family Health History}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Family health history is the record of illnesses in a person's close relatives, including parents, siblings, children, and grandparents. Many common conditions run in families, including heart disease, diabetes, some cancers, and mental health conditions, and knowing the family history helps doctors assess risk and recommend screening and prevention. A family history can also reveal inherited genetic conditions that affect other family members. Recording and sharing your family health history is one of the simplest and most valuable things you can do for your health and your family's health.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Most people have at least one close relative with a chronic disease, and family history is an important risk factor for conditions such as heart disease, stroke, diabetes, and breast, bowel, and ovarian cancer. A significant proportion of people do not know their family health history in detail, and many never discuss it with their doctor. Genetic conditions, from common predispositions to rare inherited diseases, affect families across the community.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Family history reflects genes, shared environment, and shared lifestyle.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genes:} inherited genetic variations increase the risk of many conditions
    \item \textbf{Inherited conditions:} single-gene conditions such as cystic fibrosis, Huntington's disease, and familial cancers are directly inherited
    \item \textbf{Shared environment:} families share diet, lifestyle, and exposures
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} a strong family history, early onset, and multiple affected relatives indicate higher risk
    \item \textbf{Polygenic risk:} most common diseases arise from the interaction of many genes and lifestyle
    \item \textbf{Why it matters:} knowing the history allows targeted screening, prevention, and genetic testing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Gathering a family history involves collecting specific information.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Recording the illnesses of parents, siblings, and children
    \item Including grandparents and other close relatives where known
    \item Noting the age at which relatives were diagnosed
    \item Noting the cause and age of death of relatives
    \item Asking about conditions such as heart disease, diabetes, cancer, and mental illness
    \item Asking about inherited conditions and genetic testing in the family
    \item Updating the record as the family changes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A family history is interpreted carefully.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A family history may reflect shared lifestyle rather than genes
    \item The absence of a family history does not rule out risk
    \item Some inherited conditions skip generations or affect relatives variably
    \item The doctor interprets the significance of the history
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People should share their family history with their GP, particularly when starting screening or when planning a family. People with a strong family history of cancer, heart disease, or genetic conditions should discuss genetic counselling and testing.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute symptoms in a family member that are emergencies
    \item Signs of stroke or heart attack
    \item Any urgent medical situation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A family history is used to guide care.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History taking:} the GP records and reviews the family history
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} tools estimate the risk of conditions such as heart disease and breast cancer
    \item \textbf{Screening:} earlier or more frequent screening may be recommended
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} arranged through genetic services when a specific inherited condition is suspected
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to genetic counselling for complex histories
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The family history informs prevention and screening.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Earlier screening:} mammograms, bowel screening, and heart checks may start earlier
    \item \textbf{Prevention:} stronger focus on blood pressure, cholesterol, weight, and lifestyle
    \item \textbf{Genetic counselling:} for inherited conditions and family planning
    \item \textbf{Testing:} predictive testing for family members at risk
    \item \textbf{Communication:} sharing important information with relatives
    \item \textbf{Ongoing review:} updating the history at health checks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Missed opportunities for prevention when the history is not known
    \item Late diagnosis of conditions that could have been screened for
    \item Anxiety about risk, which is eased by accurate information and counselling
    \item The impact of genetic findings on family members
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Knowing the family health history leads to earlier detection and prevention, improving outcomes for conditions such as cancer and heart disease. Genetic testing can clarify risk and guide decisions. The information is a powerful tool that benefits the whole family across generations.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ask relatives about their health history and record it
    \item Bring the record to appointments with your GP
    \item Update the history when family circumstances change
    \item Discuss screening and prevention with your doctor
    \item Share important genetic information with relatives
    \item Consider genetic counselling if there is a strong family history
    \item Do not let family history cause undue worry; use it to act
\end{itemize}
