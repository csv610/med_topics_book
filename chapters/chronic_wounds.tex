\section*{Definition and Overview}
A chronic wound is a break in the skin or underlying tissue that fails to heal within the expected time -- usually defined as not healing within four to six weeks, or not showing steady progress toward healing within that period. Acute wounds, such as a clean cut or surgical incision, normally heal through a predictable sequence of inflammation, new tissue formation, and remodelling. Chronic wounds get stuck in this process, often because of an underlying problem such as poor blood flow, diabetes, pressure, or infection.

The most common chronic wounds are leg ulcers (usually venous), diabetic foot ulcers, pressure injuries (bedsores), and wounds on limbs affected by poor arterial blood supply. They can be painful, odorous, disabling, and costly to treat, and they require ongoing, structured care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Chronic wounds are common, particularly in older adults and people with long-term conditions. Venous leg ulcers affect roughly one to three in a hundred older adults, and their prevalence rises with age. Diabetic foot ulcers occur in a substantial proportion of people with diabetes over their lifetime -- estimates suggest around a quarter -- and are a leading cause of amputation.

Pressure injuries are most common in people who are immobile, such as hospital inpatients and residents of aged-care facilities. Men and women are affected at similar rates once age and risk factors are taken into account. Chronic wounds are more common in people with diabetes, poor circulation, kidney disease, obesity, and malnutrition.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Chronic wounds develop and persist when the normal healing process is interrupted. Common contributing factors include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Venous disease:} damaged leg veins cause blood to pool, raising pressure in the skin and leading to venous leg ulcers
    \item \textbf{Arterial disease:} narrowed arteries reduce blood supply (ischaemia), so tissue cannot heal; this commonly causes ulcers on the toes and feet
    \item \textbf{Diabetes:} high blood sugar damages nerves (neuropathy, so injuries are not felt) and blood vessels (reduced healing), making diabetic foot ulcers common
    \item \textbf{Pressure:} constant pressure over bony areas (heels, sacrum, elbows) in immobile people cuts off blood flow, causing pressure injuries
    \item \textbf{Infection:} wounds can become colonised or infected, delaying healing
    \item \textbf{Other factors:} smoking, poor nutrition, kidney disease, obesity, and some medicines (e.g.\ corticosteroids) slow healing
\end{itemize}

Whatever the cause, the wound remains in an inflamed state with poor new tissue formation. Chronic wounds often contain raised levels of bacteria and a thickened layer of dead tissue, and their edges fail to close.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Chronic wounds may present in different ways depending on the type:

\begin{itemize}
    \item An open sore that has not healed after several weeks
    \item \textbf{Venous ulcers:} usually around the ankle and lower leg, shallow, often weeping, with surrounding skin that is brown, dry, and itchy
    \item \textbf{Arterial ulcers:} on the toes, feet, or heels, often painful, with a punched-out appearance and pale or black edges
    \item \textbf{Diabetic foot ulcers:} on pressure areas of the foot, often painless because of neuropathy, sometimes deep with infection
    \item \textbf{Pressure injuries:} over bony areas, ranging from persistent redness to deep open sores
    \item Pain, which varies by wound type -- venous ulcers may be uncomfortable, arterial ulcers often very painful, diabetic ulcers frequently painless
    \item Discharge (clear, yellow, or cloudy), odour, and surrounding redness or swelling
    \item Dead tissue (slough) or black, hard tissue (eschar) in the wound
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When a wound fails to heal, doctors consider other causes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection of the wound or underlying bone (osteomyelitis)
    \item Underlying arterial disease or deep vein problems
    \item Skin conditions that mimic ulcers, such as vasculitis, pyoderma gangrenosum, or skin cancer (including squamous cell carcinoma)
    \item Inflammatory conditions (e.g.\ rheumatoid arthritis, lupus) affecting the skin
    \item Severe anaemia or malnutrition impairing healing
    \item Medication effects (e.g.\ corticosteroids, some blood-thinning or chemotherapy drugs)
    \item Factitious injury (a wound deliberately worsened or reopened)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any wound that has not healed or significantly improved within four to six weeks should be assessed by a doctor or nurse. Early assessment is especially important for people with diabetes, poor circulation, or reduced sensation, because they may not feel the wound worsening.

\textbf{Seek urgent care for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Spreading redness, warmth, swelling, or fever -- signs of infection
    \item Increasing pain, foul odour, or discharge
    \item Black, dead tissue or a wound that is suddenly larger or deeper
    \item A cold, pale, or blue limb with a wound
    \item For people with diabetes, any foot wound, even a small one, should be assessed promptly
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment looks beyond the wound itself to find why it is not healing:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} how long the wound has been present, how it started, and any history of diabetes, vein or artery problems, or previous ulcers
    \item \textbf{Wound assessment:} size, depth, colour, discharge, odour, and condition of the surrounding skin, recorded over time
    \item \textbf{Leg and foot examination:} checking pulses, skin temperature, and sensation; this distinguishes venous from arterial ulcers
    \item \textbf{Ankle--brachial pressure index (ABPI):} a comparison of blood pressure in the ankle and arm to detect arterial disease
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, blood glucose (for diabetes), inflammatory markers, kidney function, and nutrition markers
    \item \textbf{Swabs:} taken for culture if infection is suspected
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} ultrasound to assess veins; X-ray or MRI if bone infection (osteomyelitis) is possible
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} of non-healing or unusual wounds, to exclude skin cancer
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Chronic wounds need coordinated, ongoing care, often involving nurses, GPs, and specialist clinics:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Wound care:} cleaning, removal of dead tissue (debridement), and appropriate dressings applied by trained staff
    \item \textbf{Treating the underlying cause:} for example, compression bandaging for venous ulcers, revascularisation (restoring blood flow) for arterial disease, and offloading (relieving pressure) for diabetic foot ulcers
    \item \textbf{Pressure relief:} repositioning, specialist mattresses and cushions for pressure injuries
    \item \textbf{Infection treatment:} antibiotics when there is evidence of infection; topical antiseptics as part of wound care
    \item \textbf{Diabetes management:} good blood glucose control to promote healing
    \item \textbf{Improving nutrition:} adequate protein, vitamins, and minerals, often with dietitian input
    \item \textbf{Smoking cessation:} quitting smoking substantially improves healing
    \item \textbf{Pain management:} appropriate analgesia, often with dressings chosen to reduce pain
    \item \textbf{Advanced treatments:} some wounds benefit from negative-pressure wound therapy, skin grafts, or growth factor dressings, decided case by case
    \item \textbf{Podiatry:} foot care and offloading for people with diabetic foot ulcers
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wound infection, including cellulitis (infection of the deeper skin)
    \item Infection of underlying bone (osteomyelitis)
    \item Gangrene and tissue death, which can require amputation
    \item Sepsis in severe cases
    \item Chronic pain, odour, and discharge affecting quality of life
    \item Restricted mobility and loss of independence
    \item Depression and social isolation
    \item Recurrence, which is common once a wound has healed
    \item Treatment-related problems, such as skin damage from dressings or bandages
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With good care, most chronic wounds eventually heal, but healing often takes weeks to months. Venous leg ulcers, for example, heal in the majority of cases with sustained compression therapy, although recurrence is common without ongoing prevention. Diabetic foot ulcers heal in most people but require careful pressure offloading and control of diabetes.

Arterial ulcers and deep pressure injuries heal more slowly and have a worse outlook, sometimes requiring surgery. Early, consistent, multidisciplinary treatment gives the best chance of healing and of avoiding amputation. A healed wound always needs continued skin care and prevention measures to reduce the chance of it returning.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Many chronic wounds can be prevented, especially in people at risk:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Manage diabetes carefully, including daily inspection of the feet
    \item Check feet daily for cuts, blisters, redness, or calluses, especially if you have reduced sensation
    \item Wear well-fitting shoes and seek podiatry care regularly
    \item For people with venous disease, use compression stockings as recommended and keep legs raised when resting
    \item For people who are immobile, reposition regularly and use pressure-relieving surfaces
    \item Keep skin clean, dry, and moisturised
    \item Eat a nutritious diet with adequate protein
    \item Do not smoke
    \item Protect skin from injury and treat minor wounds early
    \item Seek prompt professional assessment for any wound that is not healing
\end{itemize}
