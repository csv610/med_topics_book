\chapter{HPV Test}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The HPV test detects the presence of high-risk types of human papillomavirus (HPV) in cells taken from the cervix. Since 2017, the National Cervical Screening Program in Australia has used the HPV test as the primary screening test for cervical cancer, replacing the Pap smear. The test looks for the DNA of the high-risk HPV types that cause cervical cancer; if none are found, the risk of developing cervical cancer in the next five years is very low. If high-risk HPV is found, the sample is examined for cell changes to decide the next step. The HPV test is accurate, safe, and has made cervical screening more effective.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Around one in ten women of screening age has a positive HPV test at any given time, with the highest rates in women in their twenties. Most positive tests clear without treatment. Since HPV-based screening began in 2017, more than 3 million Australian women have been screened, and the program is on track to virtually eliminate cervical cancer in Australia by 2035. The HPV test detects more pre-cancerous changes earlier than the Pap smear and allows longer, safer screening intervals.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{What it detects:} DNA of 14 high-risk HPV types, including 16 and 18, which cause most cervical cancers
    \item \textbf{How it works:} the laboratory tests the cervical sample for viral DNA
    \item \textbf{HPV 16/18:} types with the highest cancer risk are reported separately
    \item \textbf{Other high-risk types:} positive results are managed by checking for cell changes
    \item \textbf{Negative test:} absence of high-risk HPV means the risk of cancer in the next 5 years is extremely low
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} persistent infection with high-risk HPV drives cervical cell changes; the test detects the virus years before cancer could develop
    \item \textbf{Self-collection:} women can now collect their own sample, which has increased participation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The test is done at cervical screening, every 5 years from age 25 to 74
    \item A negative result: screening repeats in 5 years
    \item HPV 16/18 positive: referral for colposcopy
    \item Other high-risk HPV positive: the cell sample is examined; low-grade changes are followed up
    \item The test is also used to follow up after treatment for cell changes
    \item The test causes no symptoms; it is a screening investigation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A positive HPV test does not mean cancer; most infections clear on their own
    \item Cell changes are graded separately from the HPV result
    \item HPV types differ in risk, which the test distinguishes
    \item Follow-up depends on the combination of HPV result and cell findings
    \item Vaccination does not affect the test result for the types tested
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Women should attend cervical screening every 5 years from age 25, including those who have been vaccinated. Anyone with abnormal bleeding, pelvic pain, or an abnormal result needs follow-up as advised.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heavy or persistent abnormal bleeding with dizziness
    \item Severe pelvic pain
    \item Bleeding in pregnancy with collapse
    \item Any emergency symptoms; the HPV test itself causes no acute illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Screening sample:} collected by a health professional, or self-collected vaginal swab
    \item \textbf{HPV testing:} detection of high-risk viral DNA
    \item \textbf{Liquid-based cytology:} review of cells when HPV is positive
    \item \textbf{Colposcopy:} examination of the cervix for HPV-positive or abnormal results
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} for definitive diagnosis of cell changes
    \item \textbf{Follow-up testing:} repeat HPV tests at intervals to confirm clearance
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Negative result:} reassure and re-screen in 5 years
    \item \textbf{HPV positive with normal cells:} repeat testing in 12 months
    \item \textbf{HPV 16/18 or abnormal cells:} colposcopy referral
    \item \textbf{Treatment:} loop excision or other procedures for higher-grade changes
    \item \textbf{Test of cure:} HPV testing after treatment confirms the virus has cleared
    \item \textbf{Communication:} results are explained clearly, and anxiety is addressed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Anxiety about positive results, which are common and usually resolve
    \item Missed screening, which is the main risk of cervical cancer
    \item Over-treatment of changes that would have resolved, reduced by current protocols
    \item False reassurance if screening intervals are not followed
    \item Incomplete follow-up after abnormal results
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The HPV test has transformed cervical screening: it detects the cause of cervical cancer years before disease develops, and a negative test provides five years of very high reassurance. Most positive results clear without treatment, and cell changes that persist are treated effectively. Australia's screening program, together with vaccination, is on track to virtually eliminate cervical cancer. Regular screening remains the single most important preventive step.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend cervical screening every 5 years from age 25 to 74
    \item Do not skip screening because you have been vaccinated
    \item Accept self-collection if offered and preferred
    \item Follow up positive results as advised
    \item Attend colposcopy promptly when referred
    \item Complete the test of cure after treatment
    \item Limit sexual partners and use condoms to reduce HPV exposure
\end{itemize}
