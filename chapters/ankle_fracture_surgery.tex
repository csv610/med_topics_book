\chapter{Ankle Fracture Surgery}
\index{Ankle Fracture Surgery}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Ankle fracture surgery is an operation to repair one or more broken bones around the ankle joint. It is usually recommended when a fracture is displaced (the bone ends no longer line up), unstable, involves the joint surface, or breaks through the skin. The aim is to restore the bones to their normal anatomical position and hold them there while they heal, using plates, screws, wires, or rods.

The operation is technically known as open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF). It is chosen because a stable, well-aligned ankle heals with a smooth joint surface, allowing normal movement and reducing the long-term risk of arthritis. Not every ankle fracture needs surgery: many minor, undisplaced fractures heal perfectly well in a plaster cast or walking boot.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Ankle fractures are among the most common fractures seen in emergency departments. They show two distinct peaks: young men, usually with high-energy injuries such as sport or road traffic accidents, and older women, usually with low-energy falls, often in the presence of osteoporosis. The incidence rises with age, and the number of fractures has grown as the population ages and levels of sporting activity increase.

Approximately half of significant ankle fractures are considered unstable and require surgery. Ankle fractures also occur frequently in children, where injury to the growth plate (physis) needs special attention because it can affect future bone growth.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
An ankle fracture is almost always caused by a twisting, rolling, or direct impact force on the foot and ankle:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Twisting injuries:} the classic rolling of the ankle that stresses the joint beyond its normal range of movement
    \item \textbf{Falls:} a common cause, particularly in older adults with reduced bone strength
    \item \textbf{High-energy trauma:} road traffic accidents or falls from a height, which can shatter the bone or injure the overlying skin
    \item \textbf{Weakened bone:} osteoporosis and other conditions that reduce bone density make fractures more likely with relatively minor force
\end{itemize}

The ankle joint is formed by the tibia and fibula of the lower leg meeting the talus bone of the foot. The tibia and fibula are bound together by a strong ligament complex, the syndesmosis. When the bony ring of the ankle is broken in more than one place, the joint becomes unstable and the talus can shift out of position, damaging the joint surface. The collateral ligaments are often torn at the same time. Surgery restores the shape of the joint so that the cartilage survives and post-traumatic arthritis is less likely to develop later.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms of an ankle fracture vary with the severity of the injury:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe pain around the ankle, often worse with any attempt to bear weight
    \item Swelling, which can be marked and develops within hours
    \item Bruising that may spread up the leg or down into the foot
    \item Deformity or an obvious change in the shape of the ankle
    \item Difficulty or inability to walk or put weight on the foot
    \item If the skin is broken near the fracture, the injury is an open (compound) fracture with a high risk of infection
    \item Numbness, tingling, or a pale, cool foot if nerves or blood vessels are involved
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other injuries can look very similar to an ankle fracture:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ankle sprain:} ligament injury with swelling and pain but intact bones
    \item \textbf{Tendon injury or rupture:} for example, the peroneal tendons behind the outer ankle
    \item \textbf{Fracture of other foot bones:} such as the talus, calcaneus, or metatarsals
    \item \textbf{Growth plate injury:} in children, injury to the physis may be significant even when no obvious break is visible
    \item \textbf{Syndesmosis injury:} tearing of the ligament holding the tibia and fibula together, which can mimic a fracture and may itself require surgery
    \item \textbf{Bone contusion:} injury to bone without a frank fracture, seen as bone bruising on imaging
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any injury causing significant ankle pain, swelling, or inability to bear weight should be assessed promptly, ideally on the same day. A missed or undertreated fracture can displace over time and lead to long-term problems.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services or attend an emergency department for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item An ankle that looks deformed or obviously out of place
    \item The skin broken near the injury (open fracture)
    \item Numbness, tingling, or a cold, pale, blue foot below the ankle
    \item Pain that is severe or rapidly worsening
    \item Inability to move the foot or toes
    \item A high-energy mechanism, such as a car accident or a fall from a height
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment begins with a careful history of how the injury happened and a physical examination of the ankle, foot, and lower leg, including checking the pulses, sensation, and condition of the skin:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} the main test, taken from several angles to identify the fracture and assess displacement
    \item \textbf{Stress views:} X-rays taken while the ankle is gently stressed to test for ligament or syndesmosis instability
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} used for complex or comminuted fractures to plan surgery in detail
    \item \textbf{MRI:} occasionally used when associated ligament or cartilage damage is suspected
    \item \textbf{Soft-tissue assessment:} careful inspection for open wounds, blisters, or severe swelling, which affects the timing of surgery
    \item \textbf{Vascular and neurological assessment:} checking pulses, capillary return, and sensation if there is concern about blood flow or nerves
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Surgery is generally performed within days of the injury, once the swelling has settled sufficiently, to reduce the risk of wound complications:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF):} the standard operation, in which the bone is repositioned and held with plates and screws
    \item \textbf{Intramedullary fixation:} a rod or nail used in some fibula fractures
    \item \textbf{Syndesmosis fixation:} if the tibia and fibula are pulled apart, screws or suture devices hold them together while the ligament heals
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} given for open fractures to prevent wound and bone infection
    \item \textbf{Immobilisation:} a plaster cast or walking boot worn for several weeks while the bone heals
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} regular simple analgesics after the operation, often with a short course of stronger medication
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy, progressive weight-bearing, and exercises to restore movement, strength, and balance
    \item \textbf{Metalware removal:} screws are sometimes removed months later if they cause discomfort, although this is not routinely necessary
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Wound infection:} more common after open fractures, in smokers, and in people with diabetes
    \item \textbf{Delayed union or non-union:} the bone heals slowly or fails to unite, sometimes requiring further surgery
    \item \textbf{Malunion:} the bone heals in a slightly abnormal position, altering the mechanics of the joint
    \item \textbf{Post-traumatic arthritis:} long-term stiffness and pain, more likely after injuries that damaged the joint surface
    \item \textbf{Nerve injury:} numbness or altered sensation, usually over the top or side of the foot
    \item \textbf{Deep vein thrombosis:} a blood clot in the leg, reduced by early movement and sometimes blood-thinning medication
    \item \textbf{Hardware problems:} prominent or loosening screws and plates that cause discomfort
    \item \textbf{Complex regional pain syndrome:} an uncommon condition of persistent pain, swelling, and skin changes after injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most ankle fractures treated with surgery heal well, and the majority of people regain good function. Weight-bearing is typically protected for several weeks and then built up gradually, with most people returning to normal daily activities within three months and to sport or heavy work by six to twelve months. Some stiffness, swelling, or aching may persist for many months.

Outcomes are generally better when the fracture is simple, the joint surface is restored accurately, and the rehabilitation programme is followed. Post-traumatic arthritis can develop years later, especially after severe injuries that damaged the cartilage, and a minority of people are left with long-term stiffness or pain.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Strengthen the muscles around the ankle and practise balance exercises regularly
    \item Wear appropriate, well-fitted footwear for the activity and surface
    \item Remove home hazards such as loose rugs, poor lighting, and slippery surfaces, especially for older adults
    \item Manage osteoporosis with a bone-healthy diet, vitamin D, weight-bearing exercise, and prescribed treatment where advised
    \item Stop smoking before surgery, since it significantly impairs bone and wound healing
    \item Follow the surgeon's instructions on weight-bearing and wear the boot or plaster as directed
    \item Attend physiotherapy and continue the prescribed exercises even after the pain settles
    \item Report any wound redness, discharge, or unexpected pain promptly after surgery
\end{itemize}
