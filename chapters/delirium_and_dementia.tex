\chapter{Delirium and Dementia}
\index{Delirium and Dementia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Delirium and dementia are two distinct but commonly confused conditions that affect cognition, particularly in older people. Delirium is an acute, fluctuating state of confusion that develops over hours to days, usually caused by an underlying medical problem such as infection, dehydration, or medication, and it is potentially reversible. Dementia is a progressive, long-term decline in memory, thinking, and function caused by diseases of the brain such as Alzheimer's disease. Delirium can occur on top of dementia, and both require careful assessment and management. This chapter explains the differences and how each is recognised and treated.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Delirium is very common in hospitalised older people, affecting up to one-third of older patients in general wards and over half of those in intensive care or after major surgery. It is associated with longer hospital stays, functional decline, and increased mortality. Dementia affects around one in ten people over 65 and nearly one in three over 85, with Alzheimer's disease the most common cause. The number of people living with dementia is growing with population ageing and is projected to exceed 500,000 in coming decades.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Delirium causes:} infection, dehydration, electrolyte disturbance, medications, surgery, pain, urinary retention, constipation, sleep deprivation, and sensory impairment
    \item \textbf{Dementia causes:} Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia, Lewy body dementia, frontotemporal dementia, and other less common conditions
    \item \textbf{Brain vulnerability:} older brains and brains already affected by dementia are more vulnerable to the insults that trigger delirium
    \item \textbf{Neurotransmitter changes:} both conditions involve disruption of brain chemical systems; delirium also involves acute inflammation and metabolic disturbance
    \item \textbf{Protein accumulation:} in Alzheimer's disease, abnormal proteins (amyloid and tau) build up, damaging nerve cells
    \item \textbf{Vascular damage:} in vascular dementia, reduced blood flow to the brain causes areas of damage
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Delirium:} sudden onset over hours to days, fluctuating alertness, inattention, confusion, hallucinations, agitation or withdrawal, and disturbed sleep-wake cycle
    \item \textbf{Dementia:} gradual onset over months to years, progressive memory loss, difficulty with language, problem-solving, and daily tasks, personality and mood changes
    \item \textbf{Delirium on dementia:} a person with dementia becomes suddenly more confused, drowsy, or agitated, suggesting an acute illness
    \item Disorientation to time, place, or person
    \item Reduced ability to care for oneself, and safety concerns such as wandering
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Delirium versus dementia, distinguished by onset, course, and reversibility
    \item Depression, which can mimic dementia ("pseudodementia") with poor concentration and memory
    \item Medication side effects, which can cause confusion
    \item Psychiatric conditions such as psychosis or severe anxiety
    \item Stroke, brain injury, or a brain tumour
    \item Hearing or vision impairment, which can make a person appear confused
    \item Thyroid disease, vitamin B12 deficiency, and other reversible causes of cognitive change
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
A sudden change in alertness, thinking, or behaviour in an older person is a medical emergency and requires urgent assessment, because delirium often signals a serious underlying illness. People with progressive memory decline affecting daily life should see their GP for assessment and referral. Anyone noticing sudden neurological symptoms such as weakness, speech difficulty, or facial droop should seek emergency care.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden confusion with fever, drowsiness, or difficulty waking
    \item Sudden weakness, facial droop, or speech difficulty (possible stroke)
    \item A person with dementia who is suddenly very agitated, violent, or unable to be settled
    \item Seizures or loss of consciousness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cognitive assessment:} standardised tests such as the Mini-Mental State Examination or Montreal Cognitive Assessment
    \item \textbf{Delirium screening:} tools such as the Confusion Assessment Method
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} including checking for infection, dehydration, and neurological signs
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, electrolytes, kidney and liver function, glucose, thyroid function, and vitamin B12
    \item \textbf{Urine tests:} to detect urinary tract infection, a common cause of delirium
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT or MRI of the brain for stroke, bleeding, or other structural causes
    \item \textbf{ECG and chest X-ray:} where heart or lung problems are suspected
    \item \textbf{Medication review:} identifying drugs that can cause or worsen confusion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Delirium:} treat the underlying cause, provide reassurance and orientation, encourage fluids and nutrition, involve family, and reduce unnecessary medications and monitoring that disturb sleep
    \item \textbf{Dementia:} multidisciplinary care with memory clinics, medications that may slow progression in Alzheimer's disease, and management of behavioural and psychological symptoms
    \item \textbf{Non-pharmacological approaches:} structured routines, meaningful activity, environmental cues, and support for daily tasks
    \item \textbf{Medication:} antipsychotics are used sparingly and only for severe agitation or psychosis in dementia, because of significant risks
    \item \textbf{Carer support:} education, respite care, and services to support family carers
    \item \textbf{Planning:} advance care planning, legal and financial arrangements, and planning for future care needs
    \item \textbf{Safety measures:} falls prevention, supervision of driving, and home modifications
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Falls, fractures, and injuries during confusion
    \item Prolonged hospital stays and functional decline after delirium
    \item Worsening of dementia from superimposed delirium
    \item Carer stress and burnout
    \item Malnutrition, dehydration, and pressure injuries in advanced dementia
    \item Complications of medication used for behavioural symptoms
    \item Premature death, particularly in frail older people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Delirium is potentially reversible, and most people recover fully once the underlying cause is treated, although recovery can take weeks and some people do not return to their previous level of function. Dementia is progressive, but the rate varies; some people live with mild cognitive change for many years, and some treatments slow progression. Good support, early diagnosis, and comprehensive care improve quality of life for people with dementia and their carers. Both conditions require coordinated care, and outcomes are better when delirium is prevented and dementia is managed proactively.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Prevent delirium: stay hydrated, well-nourished, and mobile; keep glasses and hearing aids in use; avoid unnecessary medication changes; and maintain a good sleep routine
    \item Ask hospital staff about delirium prevention strategies during admission
    \item Keep the brain active with social engagement, reading, and learning
    \item Manage vascular risk factors such as blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, and smoking
    \item Maintain physical activity and a healthy diet
    \item Seek early assessment for memory concerns
    \item Carers: seek support, respite, and education from dementia services
\end{itemize}
