\chapter{Ovarian Vein Thrombosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Ovarian vein thrombosis (OVT) is a rare but serious vaso-occlusive disorder characterised by the formation of a thrombus within the lumen of one or both ovarian veins. It occurs most frequently in postpartum patients (historically termed puerperal ovarian vein thrombophlebitis), but can also arise in association with pelvic surgery, malignancy, pelvic inflammatory disease, or hypercoagulable states. OVT represents a subset of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and is predominantly right-sided. Left-sided or bilateral involvement is less common. Pathologically, it is driven by the components of Virchow's triad: venous stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability. Clinical recognition is critical as it can mimic other acute pelvic pathologies and carries risks of pulmonary embolism and septic pelvic thrombophlebitis.

\section*{Epidemiology}
OVT is a relatively uncommon condition, complicating approximately 1 in 600 to 1 in 2000 deliveries in the postpartum period, with the incidence rising to up to 1--2\% following caesarean deliveries or twin gestations. In the non-puerperal setting, the exact incidence is unknown but is strongly linked to pelvic malignancies (such as ovarian, endometrial, or cervical cancers), pelvic inflammatory disease, and major pelvic surgeries. There is a marked right-sided predominance, with approximately 80--90\% of cases occurring in the right ovarian vein. This is attributed to the anatomical differences between the two sides: the right ovarian vein is longer, enters the inferior vena cava (IVC) at an acute angle, has incompetent valves, and is subjected to dextrorotation of the gravid uterus, which compresses the vein. In contrast, the left ovarian vein enters the left renal vein at a right angle.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The development of OVT is explained by Virchow's triad of venous stasis, vascular endothelial damage, and a systemic hypercoagulable state.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy and the postpartum state:} The physiological changes of pregnancy create a hypercoagulable state with increased clotting factors and decreased fibrinolysis. Following delivery, rapid uterine involution and the decompression of pelvic vessels lead to venous stasis, which is further exacerbated by uterine dextrorotation.
    \item \textbf{Endothelial injury:} Vascular trauma sustained during vaginal or caesarean delivery, pelvic surgery (such as hysterectomy or oophorectomy), or pelvic instrumentation can disrupt the venous intima, triggering the coagulation cascade.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic infection:} Pelvic inflammatory disease, endometritis, or chorioamnionitis can cause contiguous inflammatory changes in the adjacent pelvic veins, promoting thrombus formation (septic thrombophlebitis).
    \item \textbf{Malignancy:} Pelvic and abdominal cancers induce a prothrombotic state and can cause direct mechanical compression or invasion of the ovarian veins.
    \item \textbf{Inherited and acquired thrombophilias:} Genetic predispositions (such as Factor V Leiden mutation, prothrombin gene mutation, protein C or S deficiency, or antithrombin deficiency) and acquired disorders (like antiphospholipid syndrome) significantly increase the risk of OVT.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of OVT typically occurs within the first 2 to 10 days postpartum, or following pelvic surgery/infection, and is characterised by a combination of pelvic pain and systemic symptoms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Lower abdominal or pelvic pain, which is typically unilateral (most commonly right-sided), constant, dull or stabbing, and may radiate to the flank or groin.
    \item Pyrexia (fever), which is often persistent or spiking, especially in septic pelvic thrombophlebitis, and may not respond to initial broad-spectrum antibiotics.
    \item Palpable pelvic mass, representing the thrombosed, tender, and dilated ovarian vein, which may be felt as a sausage-like structure in the adnexa.
    \item Nausea, vomiting, and abdominal distension, mimicking acute appendicitis or small bowel obstruction.
    \item Tachycardia and tachypnoea, which may indicate systemic inflammation or, critically, a complicating pulmonary embolism.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
OVT presents with symptoms that overlap with many common acute abdominal and pelvic pathologies, necessitating careful differentiation.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute appendicitis:} Presents with migrating right lower quadrant pain and fever; often the primary misdiagnosis in right-sided OVT.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic inflammatory disease or endometritis:} Characterised by pelvic pain, fever, and purulent vaginal discharge, which often coexist with or precede OVT, but lack the focal venous thrombosis on imaging.
    \item \textbf{Pyelonephritis or ureteric colic:} Presents with flank pain, fever, and costovertebral tenderness, differentiated by urinalysis and renal ultrasound.
    \item \textbf{Ovarian torsion:} Sudden, severe, colicky unilateral pain, differentiated by characteristic ultrasound findings showing compromised ovarian arterial/venous flow.
    \item \textbf{Postpartum uterine infection or pelvic abscess:} Differentiated by pelvic imaging (CT or MRI) which localises the fluid collection or endometritis without thrombus in the ovarian vein.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
OVT is a serious medical condition requiring prompt diagnosis and hospitalisation. Any postpartum patient or individual who has recently undergone pelvic surgery or treatment for pelvic infection and experiences new, worsening pelvic or flank pain should seek immediate medical evaluation.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe, or worsening lower abdominal, pelvic, or flank pain.
    \item High, spiking fever associated with chills or rigors, particularly in the postpartum period.
    \item Sudden onset of shortness of breath, chest pain (pleuritic), or coughing up blood (haemoptysis), suggesting a pulmonary embolism.
    \item Rapid heart rate, dizziness, confusion, or cold, clammy skin.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is rarely made on clinical examination alone and relies heavily on cross-sectional pelvic imaging and laboratory workup.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Contrast-enhanced Computed Tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis:} The diagnostic modality of choice due to its high sensitivity (up to 100\%) and specificity. It classically demonstrates a dilated ovarian vein with a low-attenuation (dark) intraluminal filling defect, surrounded by a thick, enhancing vessel wall.
    \item \textbf{Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):} Highly sensitive and specific, useful for patients in whom contrast-medium or radiation exposure must be avoided (e.g., during pregnancy or renal impairment).
    \item \textbf{Duplex Doppler Ultrasound:} Often the initial screening tool. It may show a non-compressible, echogenic mass within the ovarian vein with absent or reduced flow on Doppler, though it is often limited by overlying bowel gas.
    \item \textbf{Full blood count (FBC) and inflammatory markers:} FBC typically reveals leukocytosis. CRP and ESR are commonly elevated, reflecting systemic inflammation.
    \item \textbf{Thrombophilia screen:} Indicated in non-puerperal cases, recurrent thrombosis, or patients with a strong family history of venous thromboembolism, to test for factor V Leiden, antiphospholipid antibodies, and other coagulation abnormalities.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The cornerstone of OVT management is anticoagulation therapy combined with broad-spectrum antibiotics if an infectious aetiology (septic pelvic thrombophlebitis) is suspected.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anticoagulation therapy:} Initial therapy with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) or unfractionated heparin, followed by oral anticoagulants (such as warfarin or direct oral anticoagulants like apixaban or rivaroxaban) for a duration of 3 to 6 months, or longer if a persistent hypercoagulable state is present.
    \item \textbf{Intravenous antibiotics:} Indicated if septic thrombophlebitis is suspected (characterised by persistent fever despite anticoagulation). Broad-spectrum antibiotics covering aerobes and anaerobes (such as ampicillin, gentamicin, and metronidazole) are administered.
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} Intravenous fluids, bed rest, adequate analgesia (with paracetamol or opioids; NSAIDs are used with caution if anticoagulated), and close monitoring for signs of embolisation.
    \item \textbf{Inferior Vena Cava (IVC) filter placement:} Rarely indicated; reserved for patients with contraindications to anticoagulation or those who develop recurrent pulmonary emboli despite therapeutic anticoagulation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pulmonary embolism (PE), occurring in up to 13\% of untreated cases, resulting from the thrombus propagating into the IVC or renal veins.
    \item Septic pelvic thrombophlebitis (SPT) and pelvic abscess, where the thrombus becomes infected, potentially leading to systemic sepsis and septic shock.
    \item Extension of the thrombus into the inferior vena cava (from the right ovarian vein) or the left renal vein (from the left ovarian vein).
    \item Ovarian infarction (rare), due to complete venous outflow obstruction.
    \item Chronic pelvic congestion syndrome, characterised by persistent pelvic pain due to venous valvular incompetence.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With early diagnosis and the initiation of anticoagulation (and antibiotics if indicated), the prognosis for patients with OVT is excellent. Resolution of thrombi and clinical symptoms occurs in the vast majority of cases within weeks. The mortality rate is low when treated, but untreated OVT associated with sepsis or pulmonary embolism carries significant risk. Recurrence of OVT in subsequent pregnancies is estimated at 3--5\%, warranting consideration of prophylactic anticoagulation in future postpartum periods, particularly in patients with confirmed underlying thrombophilias.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Engage in early mobilisation after childbirth (especially caesarean delivery) or pelvic surgery to reduce venous stasis.
    \item Utilise mechanical thromboprophylaxis (such as sequential compression devices or compression stockings) during and immediately after hospitalisation.
    \item Administer pharmacological thromboprophylaxis (such as prophylactic LMWH) in high-risk patients, including those with a history of venous thromboembolism or known thrombophilia.
    \item Ensure adequate hydration to prevent haemoconcentration and minimise the risk of thrombus formation.
    \item Adhere strictly to the prescribed anticoagulation regimen and attend regular follow-up appointments for blood monitoring (if taking warfarin) and imaging.
\end{itemize}
