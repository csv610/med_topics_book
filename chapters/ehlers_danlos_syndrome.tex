\chapter{Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS) is the name given to a group of inherited disorders of connective tissue. Connective tissue is the "glue" that holds the body together, providing strength and elasticity to the skin, joints, blood vessels, and organs. In EDS, defects in collagen and related proteins make this tissue abnormally weak, producing loose joints, stretchy skin, and fragile tissues. The syndrome is classified into several types with different genetic causes and severities, and management is tailored to the type and the individual's symptoms.

\section*{Epidemiology}
EDS is rare overall, with the hypermobile type being the most common and the other types much rarer. It affects people of all ages, sexes, and ethnicities, and diagnosis may occur at any point from childhood to adulthood. Because joint hypermobility is common in the general population, and because many people with milder forms have no significant problems, the true prevalence is difficult to measure, and the condition is often diagnosed later than ideal.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
EDS results from genetic changes that affect the production or processing of collagen.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hyper-mobile EDS:} caused by changes in genes involved in connective tissue proteins, including the gene COL5A1 and others, and diagnosed mainly on clinical criteria
    \item \textbf{Classical EDS:} caused by mutations in the collagen type V genes, COL5A1 and COL5A2
    \item \textbf{Vascular EDS:} caused by mutations in the COL3A1 gene, which encodes type III collagen, important in blood vessels and organs
    \item \textbf{Other types:} kyphoscoliotic, arthrochalasia, and dermatosparaxis EDS are very rare
    \item \textbf{Inheritance:} most types are autosomal dominant, with a 50 per cent chance of passing the condition to children
    \item \textbf{Consequences:} weakened collagen leads to lax joints, fragile and hyperextensible skin, easy bruising, and, in vascular EDS, fragile arteries and hollow organs
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms differ between the types but share common themes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Joint hypermobility and instability, with dislocations and subluxations
    \item Chronic joint, muscle, and body pain
    \item Hyperextensible, soft, velvety skin
    \item Easy bruising and abnormal scarring
    \item Fatigue, which is a common and underappreciated symptom
    \item Autonomic symptoms, including dizziness and palpitations on standing
    \item Digestive symptoms such as reflux and abdominal pain
    \item In vascular EDS: thin, translucent skin, easy bruising, and a risk of sudden rupture of arteries or organs
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The features of EDS overlap with other conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Generalized joint hypermobility without a connective tissue disorder
    \item Marfan syndrome, which shares tall stature and aortic involvement
    \item Loeys-Dietz syndrome and other aortic disorders
    \item Fibromyalgia and chronic pain syndromes
    \item Other causes of easy bruising, including blood disorders and medication
    \item Hypermobility spectrum disorders
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Assessment is appropriate for people with significant joint instability, recurrent dislocations, stretchy or fragile skin, or a family history of EDS. A GP can arrange referral to a rheumatologist or clinical geneticist.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe chest, abdominal, or back pain, which may indicate arterial or organ rupture in vascular EDS
    \item Sudden severe headache, weakness, or stroke symptoms
    \item Collapse or severe dizziness
    \item Unusual severe bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis combines clinical evaluation with genetic confirmation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical criteria:} the international classification criteria for each type, including the Beighton score for hypermobility and skin features
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} identifies the causative gene in the classical, vascular, and other types
    \item \textbf{Echocardiography:} assessment of the aorta and heart valves, relevant in several types
    \item \textbf{Skin biopsy:} rarely used, for some historical diagnoses
    \item \textbf{Specialist assessments:} physiotherapy, pain, and cardiology review as needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is individualised and multidisciplinary.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} strengthening and proprioception training to stabilise joints and reduce dislocations
    \item \textbf{Pain management:} a combination of exercise, pacing, psychology, and medication as needed
    \item \textbf{Joint protection:} bracing, taping, and aids where useful
    \item \textbf{Skin and wound care:} careful handling of wounds, appropriate dressings, and scar management
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular care:} blood pressure control and monitoring for people at risk of aortic problems
    \item \textbf{Managing dysautonomia:} fluids, salt, and counter-pressure manoeuvres
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} addressing the emotional impact of chronic pain and disability
    \item \textbf{Genetic counselling:} for affected people and their families
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic pain and disability
    \item Recurrent dislocations and early osteoarthritis
    \item Poor wound healing and disfiguring scars
    \item Chronic fatigue and functional limitation
    \item Anxiety and depression related to chronic illness
    \item In vascular EDS: life-threatening rupture of blood vessels or the bowel
    \item Complications in pregnancy, requiring specialist care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis varies widely by type. People with hypermobile and classical EDS generally have a normal lifespan, and with good physiotherapy most manage their symptoms effectively, though chronic pain and fatigue may persist. Vascular EDS is the most serious type, with a reduced life expectancy due to the risk of vessel and organ rupture, but specialist monitoring and management improve outcomes. A correct diagnosis is essential for appropriate care and family planning.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Build and maintain muscle strength around vulnerable joints
    \item Exercise regularly with low-impact activities
    \item Protect the skin from sun, trauma, and friction
    \item Learn to recognise and pace around fatigue
    \item Seek prompt treatment of injuries
    \item Connect with support groups and education programs
    \item Attend scheduled specialist reviews
\end{itemize}
