\section*{Definition and Overview}
Treatment of conjunctivitis depends on its cause, because viral, bacterial, and allergic conjunctivitis respond to different approaches. For most people, supportive self-care is all that is needed: the eye settles on its own within one to two weeks. Antibiotics are not always required and are often unnecessary. The goals of treatment are to relieve discomfort, control the cause where possible, and prevent spread to others or damage to the eye.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Because conjunctivitis is one of the most common eye problems in the community, treatment choices affect many people. Viral conjunctivitis, the most common type in adults, usually resolves without specific therapy. Bacterial conjunctivitis is more frequent in children and is the form most likely to be treated with antibiotic drops. Allergic conjunctivitis is common in people with other allergic conditions and responds mainly to allergen avoidance and anti-allergy drops. Unnecessary antibiotic use is common and contributes to antibiotic resistance.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Treatment is guided by the mechanism of the inflammation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral conjunctivitis:} caused by adenoviruses and others; no specific antiviral is needed for routine cases and the immune system clears the infection
    \item \textbf{Bacterial conjunctivitis:} caused by bacteria colonising the conjunctiva; topical antibiotics can shorten the illness in selected cases
    \item \textbf{Allergic conjunctivitis:} an immune response releasing histamine and other mediators; treated with antihistamines and mast-cell stabilisers
    \item \textbf{Irritant conjunctivitis:} triggered by chemicals or foreign material; treatment is removal of the irritant and supportive care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features that guide treatment include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Red, gritty, itchy eyes, with or without discharge
    \item Watery discharge, suggesting viral or allergic disease
    \item Thick, yellow, or crusting discharge, suggesting bacterial disease
    \item Itching out of proportion to redness, suggesting allergy, often with sneezing or a runny nose
    \item Eyelids stuck together on waking
    \item No change in vision in uncomplicated cases
    \item A contact lens wearer, which raises the risk of serious infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before starting treatment it is important to distinguish conjunctivitis from conditions that need different or urgent care:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keratitis (corneal infection or inflammation) requiring urgent ophthalmic review
    \item Acute angle-closure glaucoma, a medical emergency
    \item Iritis or uveitis (inflammation inside the eye)
    \item Corneal abrasion or a retained foreign body
    \item Subconjunctival haemorrhage (a painless red patch from a broken vessel)
    \item Dry eye syndrome, which causes chronic irritation
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor or optometrist if the eye is painful, vision is affected, symptoms persist beyond a few days, or you wear contact lenses.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services or attend an emergency department for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe eye pain or sudden loss of vision
    \item Marked sensitivity to light
    \item A chemical splash to the eye
    \item Redness in a newborn baby
    \item Symptoms that worsen quickly despite treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} onset, discharge type, contact lens use, cold symptoms, allergies, and exposure to others with red eyes
    \item \textbf{Examination:} inspection of the conjunctiva, cornea, and pupils, ideally with a slit lamp
    \item \textbf{Visual acuity:} to confirm the vision is unaffected
    \item \textbf{Fluorescein dye:} to detect corneal abrasions or ulcers
    \item \textbf{Microbial swabs:} reserved for severe, recurrent, or neonatal cases to guide antibiotic choice
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to an ophthalmologist when the diagnosis is unclear or the condition does not respond
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} cool compresses, lubricating (artificial tear) drops, and gentle cleaning of the eyelids with cooled boiled water
    \item \textbf{Viral conjunctivitis:} usually no treatment beyond support; symptoms settle in one to two weeks
    \item \textbf{Bacterial conjunctivitis:} often self-limiting, but antibiotic eye drops (e.g.\ chloramphenicol) may be prescribed for severe, prolonged, or high-risk cases
    \item \textbf{Allergic conjunctivitis:} allergen avoidance, cool compresses, and antihistamine or mast-cell stabiliser eye drops; occasionally a short course of steroid drops under specialist supervision
    \item \textbf{Contact lens wearers:} remove lenses until fully better; if symptoms are severe or worsening, urgent review because infection can be serious
    \item \textbf{Infection control:} frequent hand washing and separate towels to avoid spreading infection
    \item \textbf{Review:} recheck if there is no improvement after several days of treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Spread of infection to family members, classmates, or colleagues
    \item Corneal involvement that can threaten vision if treatment is delayed
    \item Skin irritation from antibiotic drops or ointments
    \item Allergic reaction to eye drops (burning, redness, or swelling)
    \item Development of antibiotic resistance from unnecessary antibiotic use
    \item Superinfection when the eye remains irritated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With correct treatment, most cases of conjunctivitis resolve fully without lasting eye damage. Viral cases settle within one to two weeks, bacterial cases usually within days of starting antibiotics, and allergic cases respond to avoidance and anti-allergy drops. Outcomes are very good when red-flag symptoms such as pain, reduced vision, or light sensitivity prompt early review. Chronic allergic conjunctivitis may need long-term management but is generally well controlled.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wash hands before and after touching the eyes
    \item Do not share towels, face cloths, pillows, or eye makeup
    \item Replace eye makeup and contact lens storage cases after an infection
    \item Take antibiotic drops exactly as prescribed, even if the eye feels better
    \item Avoid self-medicating with eye drops that contain steroids
    \item For allergic conjunctivitis, identify and avoid triggers such as pollens and dust
    \item Seek advice before using any drops if you wear contact lenses
\end{itemize}
