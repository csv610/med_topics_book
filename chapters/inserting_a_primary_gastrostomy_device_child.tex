\chapter{Inserting a Gastrostomy Device (Child)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A gastrostomy is a surgical opening through the skin of the abdomen directly into the stomach, through which a feeding tube is placed to provide nutrition to a child who cannot eat enough by mouth. The procedure, called a gastrostomy insertion, is performed under general anaesthesia, either by endoscopy, keyhole surgery, or open surgery, and the tube is used for liquid feeds, fluids, and medicines. Gastrostomy feeding is used for children with feeding difficulties, unsafe swallowing, severe growth failure, or chronic illness. It is a safe and routine procedure that transforms the care of many children, allowing reliable nutrition and freeing families from the stress of inadequate oral intake.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Gastrostomy feeding is used in a small but significant number of children with a wide range of conditions. The most common indications are neurological conditions causing feeding and swallowing difficulties, such as cerebral palsy; congenital syndromes and developmental delay; severe gastro-oesophageal reflux or aspiration; and chronic conditions such as cystic fibrosis, cardiac disease, and cancer requiring supplementary feeding. Prematurity and its complications also lead to gastrostomy in some children. The procedure is performed in paediatric surgical centres, and most children do well, with the tube typically remaining in place for years and being replaced by a low-profile device once healed.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Children may need gastrostomy feeding when they cannot take in enough nutrition safely or effectively by mouth. Swallowing problems, from neurological conditions or anatomical abnormalities, risk choking and aspiration of food into the lungs; poor appetite and high energy needs from chronic illness cause growth failure; and severe reflux or vomiting prevents adequate intake. A gastrostomy bypasses these problems by delivering feed directly into the stomach through a tube held in place by a small balloon or by healed tissue. The procedure creates a controlled opening (stoma) between the stomach and the skin, through which feeding, fluids, and medicines are given reliably.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Indications: poor weight gain or growth, unsafe swallowing with aspiration, and chronic illness with high nutritional needs
    \item The tube is placed under general anaesthesia, with the child in hospital for a few days
    \item Initial tube is usually a balloon device; it may later be changed to a low-profile button
    \item Feeds are given by gravity or a pump over set times, including overnight
    \item The stoma site needs regular cleaning and care
    \item Mild site irritation is common; redness, pain, or discharge may indicate infection
    \item The tube allows medicines to be given reliably
    \item Some children still eat orally for enjoyment and therapy alongside tube feeds
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The decision to place a gastrostomy follows careful assessment of the feeding problem.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Swallowing difficulties:} assessed by speech pathology and modified swallow studies
    \item \textbf{Reflux and aspiration:} which may require treatment or fundoplication at the same time
    \item \textbf{Growth failure:} from undernutrition, disease, or metabolic causes
    \item \textbf{Feeding refusal and behavioural feeding problems:} which may respond to therapy
    \item \textbf{High energy needs:} in cardiac, respiratory, and malignant disease
    \item \textbf{Alternatives:} nasogastric tube feeding, which is short-term, or oral supplements
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
The decision for gastrostomy is made by the child's medical team with the family, after full assessment of feeding, growth, and the underlying condition. Parents should seek review for any problems with the tube, including blockage, displacement, or leakage, and for signs of infection at the site. Tube problems can usually be fixed in clinic or by the community team.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The tube coming out soon after insertion or within the first weeks, before the track is healed
    \item Severe pain, swelling, or signs of peritonitis: a rigid, tender abdomen
    \item Significant bleeding from the site
    \item Inability to feed or give medicines when the tube is blocked or displaced
    \item Breathing difficulty or choking during feeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Feeding and swallowing assessment:} speech pathology, dietetic review, and modified barium swallow
    \item \textbf{Growth monitoring:} weight and length tracking over time
    \item \textbf{Assessment of reflux:} pH studies or endoscopy where relevant
    \item \textbf{Medical work-up:} assessment of the underlying condition and nutritional status
    \item \textbf{Preoperative assessment:} anaesthetic review and blood tests
    \item \textbf{Post-procedure imaging:} a contrast study or X-ray to confirm tube position before feeding begins
    \item \textbf{Ongoing review:} dietetic and surgical follow-up to manage the tube and nutrition
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Insertion techniques:} percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG), laparoscopic, or open placement
    \item \textbf{Feeding plan:} developed by the dietitian with volumes, rates, and schedules
    \item \textbf{Site care:} daily cleaning with saline, and keeping the site dry
    \item \textbf{Tube care:} flushing before and after feeds and medicines
    \item \textbf{Replacement:} the initial tube may be changed to a low-profile button once healed
    \item \textbf{Managing problems:} unblocking, repositioning, and treating infection
    \item \textbf{Oral skills therapy:} continuing speech and feeding therapy alongside tube feeding
    \item \textbf{Family support:} training and 24-hour support for home feeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection or leakage at the stoma site
    \item Blockage of the tube
    \item Displacement of the tube, with the tract closing quickly if it occurs early
    \item Granulation tissue, an overgrowth of skin at the site
    \item Reflux and aspiration in some children
    \item Rarely, perforation or bleeding during placement
    \item Skin irritation and excoriation around the site
    \item Psychological and social adjustment for the child and family
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Gastrostomy feeding is safe and highly effective. Most children gain weight and grow, have fewer chest infections from aspiration, and are easier to care for, with feeding times reduced and stress relieved. Serious complications are uncommon. Some children are able to transition to oral feeding if their underlying condition improves, while others use the tube for many years or throughout life. With good dietetic and nursing support, children with gastrostomies participate fully in school, sport, and family life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Follow the feeding plan developed by the dietitian
    \item Clean and dry the site daily and change dressings as advised
    \item Flush the tube with water before and after feeds and medicines
    \item Give medicines in liquid or crushed form as advised, never blocking the tube
    \item Attend all scheduled reviews and replacements
    \item Learn how to unblock the tube and what to do if it comes out
    \item Support the child's oral skills with therapy and safe oral foods
    \item Keep emergency numbers handy and seek help early for problems
    \item Connect with support groups for families of children with feeding tubes
\end{itemize}
