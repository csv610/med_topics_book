\chapter{The Physical Process of Dying}
\index{The Physical Process of Dying}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{This chapter is for general education and support. A person’s dying process varies, and the treating or palliative-care team can explain what is happening.}

\section*{What may happen}
In the last days or hours of life, a person may sleep more, become less interested in food and drink, speak less, or become less responsive. Breathing may become irregular, with pauses or noisy secretions. Hands and feet may become cool or mottled, and urine may decrease. These changes are common, but not every person has all of them or in the same order.

\section*{Comfort care}
The care team can treat pain, breathlessness, anxiety, nausea, agitation, and other distress. Gentle mouth and lip care, repositioning, a calm environment, and the presence of familiar people may help. Do not force food, drink, or medicines when swallowing is unsafe; ask the team about alternative routes and what the person would have wanted.

\section*{Supporting the person and family}
Hearing may persist even when the person cannot respond, so speak calmly and explain care. Ask about the goals of care, who to call, advance decisions, cultural or spiritual needs, and what to expect after death. Bereavement support is appropriate before and after death.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the treating or palliative-care team for new distress, uncontrolled pain, severe breathlessness, agitation, bleeding, seizures, or uncertainty about medicines. Call emergency services if death was unexpected, the person is in immediate danger, or a documented end-of-life plan does not cover the situation.
