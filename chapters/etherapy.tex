\chapter{E-therapy}
\index{E-therapy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
E-therapy, also called online therapy or digital mental health treatment, is the delivery of psychological therapy through the internet. It can take many forms, including video consultations with a psychologist, structured online therapy programs, smartphone apps, and text-based counselling. E-therapy makes mental health care more accessible and convenient, and there is strong evidence that online cognitive behavioural therapy is effective for conditions such as depression and anxiety. It is not suitable for every situation, but for many people it is an excellent alternative or addition to face-to-face care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Mental health conditions affect a large proportion of people, and most people do not access professional help, often because of cost, distance, stigma, or waiting times. E-therapy has grown rapidly in response, accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic, and online mental health services are now widely used. Digital tools are especially valuable for people in rural and remote areas, people with mobility or time constraints, and young people who are comfortable with technology.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
E-therapy works through the same principles as face-to-face therapy.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cognitive behavioural therapy:} the most common and best-evidenced online therapy, teaching skills to identify and change unhelpful thoughts and behaviours
    \item \textbf{Video consultations:} sessions with a therapist conducted in real time
    \item \textbf{Structured programs:} modules completed online, often with therapist support
    \item \textbf{Apps:} self-guided tools for mood tracking, relaxation, and skills practice
    \item \textbf{How it works:} the same therapeutic techniques are delivered through technology, and many programs are based on the proven components of face-to-face CBT
    \item \textbf{Evidence:} research shows online CBT is effective for depression, anxiety, and other conditions, particularly with guidance
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
E-therapy suits particular needs and situations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Mild to moderate depression and anxiety
    \item Difficulty accessing a therapist in person
    \item Preference for privacy or flexibility
    \item Long waiting lists for face-to-face care
    \item Need for ongoing support between sessions
    \item Follow-up after a course of face-to-face therapy
    \item Self-management for mild symptoms while waiting for other care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
E-therapy is not appropriate for everyone or every situation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe mental illness, including psychosis, severe depression with risk, and bipolar disorder, usually needs face-to-face care
    \item Crisis situations require immediate help, not online programs
    \item People in immediate danger need emergency services
    \item Children and some older people may need different formats
    \item The choice depends on the condition, the person, and the level of support needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People considering e-therapy should discuss it with a GP, who can recommend suitable services and arrange referral where needed. Anyone experiencing thoughts of self-harm, psychosis, or severe symptoms should seek immediate professional help.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Thoughts or plans of suicide
    \item Self-harm
    \item Severe agitation or psychosis
    \item Any situation where someone is in immediate danger
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Choosing the right e-therapy involves matching the service to the need.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Assessment:} a GP or psychologist assesses the condition and its severity
    \item \textbf{Evidence-based programs:} choose services based on proven approaches such as CBT
    \item \textbf{Level of support:} decide whether self-guided or therapist-supported therapy is appropriate
    \item \textbf{Crisis suitability:} confirm the service has clear processes for urgent problems
    \item \textbf{Review:} monitor progress and adjust the approach
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
E-therapy is used within an overall care plan.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Online CBT programs:} structured courses, such as those offered through government and clinical services
    \item \textbf{Video and telephone therapy:} regular sessions with a psychologist
    \item \textbf{Text-based support:} counselling services for immediate needs
    \item \textbf{Apps:} mood tracking, mindfulness, and skills tools
    \item \textbf{Combining with other care:} medicines, GP reviews, and face-to-face therapy as needed
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular review of progress and safety
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Inappropriate use for severe illness, delaying effective care
    \item Technical problems and interrupted sessions
    \item Privacy and security concerns
    \item Less suitable for people who prefer or need face-to-face contact
    \item The risk that a person in crisis does not receive urgent help
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
E-therapy is effective for many people with common mental health conditions, with outcomes comparable to face-to-face therapy for mild to moderate illness, especially when therapist-supported. It reduces barriers to care and supports self-management. The best outcomes come from choosing evidence-based services and combining digital therapy with appropriate professional review.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discuss e-therapy options with your GP
    \item Choose programs from reputable, evidence-based providers
    \item Set aside regular time for the therapy
    \item Complete the program rather than dipping in and out
    \item Keep the crisis numbers handy in case of urgent need
    \item Combine e-therapy with sleep, activity, and social connection
    \item Review progress with a professional regularly
\end{itemize}
