\chapter{MERS (coronavirus)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Middle East Respiratory Syndrome (MERS) is a severe, acute zoonotic respiratory disease caused by the Middle East Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus (MERS-CoV). MERS-CoV is a single-stranded, positive-sense RNA betacoronavirus first identified in Saudi Arabia in 2012. The clinical spectrum of MERS-CoV infection ranges from asymptomatic cases or mild upper respiratory tract symptoms to rapidly progressive, life-threatening viral pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and multi-organ failure. Dromedary camels serve as the primary animal reservoir host from which the virus is transmitted to humans, while human-to-human transmission occurs predominantly in healthcare settings through close contact and aerosol-generating procedures.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Since its discovery in 2012, more than 2,600 laboratory-confirmed cases of MERS have been reported to the World Health Organization (WHO), resulting in over 900 deaths. The disease is characterised by a high case fatality rate of approximately 35\%. The vast majority of cases have occurred in the Arabian Peninsula, particularly in Saudi Arabia. However, travel-associated cases have been imported to countries globally, including a major hospital-acquired outbreak in South Korea in 2015. MERS remains rare in Australia, with no locally acquired cases reported; however, international travel from endemic regions presents a continuous risk of importation. The disease predominantly affects males and individuals with pre-existing chronic health conditions.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The pathogenesis of MERS involves zoonotic spillover followed by viral invasion of host tissues via specific cellular receptors:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Zoonotic transmission:} Human infection occurs through direct contact with infected camels, camel secretions, or ingestion of unpasteurised camel milk or undercooked camel meat.
    \item \textbf{Human-to-human transmission:} Transmission occurs through respiratory droplets or fine-particle aerosols during close, unprotected contact, particularly in healthcare settings lacking adequate infection control.
    \item \textbf{Receptor binding:} The spike glycoprotein of MERS-CoV binds specifically to the dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP4, also known as CD26) receptor, which is highly expressed on non-ciliated bronchial epithelial cells, alveolar type II cells, renal tubular cells, and small intestinal cells.
    \item \textbf{Severe lung injury and systemic inflammation:} Viral replication in the lower respiratory tract triggers a dysregulated immune response (cytokine storm), leading to diffuse alveolar damage, pulmonary vascular leakage, and systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The incubation period for MERS is typically 2 to 14 days, with symptoms progressing from mild systemic complaints to severe respiratory compromise:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute respiratory symptoms:} High fever, chills, a dry or productive cough, progressive dyspnoea (shortness of breath), and clinical signs of pneumonia.
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal symptoms:} Watery diarrhoea, abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting, which can precede or accompany respiratory symptoms in up to one-third of patients.
    \item \textbf{Systemic manifestations:} Severe myalgia (muscle aches), headache, sore throat, and profound fatigue.
    \item \textbf{Respiratory failure signs:} Tachypnoea, accessory muscle use, central cyanosis, and severe arterial oxygen desaturation.
    \item \textbf{Renal dysfunction signs:} Oliguria (decreased urine output) progressing to anuria, indicating acute kidney injury.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the clinical features of MERS mimic other severe respiratory pathogens, molecular testing is essential to differentiate it from:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2):} Presents with similar respiratory and gastrointestinal symptoms but has a significantly lower case fatality rate and higher community transmission.
    \item \textbf{SARS (SARS-CoV):} Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome, which caused outbreaks in 2002-2003, presenting with severe pneumonia but currently considered eradicated.
    \item \textbf{Severe influenza:} Seasonal influenza (type A or B) or zoonotic avian influenza (e.g., H5N1 or H7N9), which can present with rapidly progressive viral pneumonia and ARDS.
    \item \textbf{Legionnaires' disease:} An atypical bacterial pneumonia caused by \textit{Legionella} species, presenting with high fever, cough, and prominent gastrointestinal symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Severe community-acquired pneumonia:} Bacterial pneumonias (e.g., \textit{Streptococcus pneumoniae}) presenting with fever, chest pain, and productive cough.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Travellers returning from the Middle East (or those who have had close contact with a confirmed case) who develop fever, cough, or shortness of breath within 14 days of return should seek immediate medical advice.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Severe shortness of breath:} Difficulty breathing, gasping for air, or rapid chest movement.
    \item \textbf{Cyanosis:} Bluish discolouration of the lips, gums, tongue, or face, indicating severe hypoxia.
    \item \textbf{Septic shock:} Dizziness, confusion, cold and clammy skin, rapid heart rate, and severe lethargy.
    \item \textbf{Altered mental state:} Acute confusion, disorientation, or inability to remain awake.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A suspected case of MERS requires urgent laboratory confirmation under biosafety guidelines:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reverse Transcription PCR (RT-PCR):} The diagnostic standard; testing must be performed on lower respiratory tract specimens (sputum, tracheal aspirate, or bronchoalveolar lavage) as they have a higher viral load than upper respiratory swabs.
    \item \textbf{Chest radiography and CT scan:} Imaging showing bilateral, patchy ground-glass opacities, consolidation, and pleural effusions, characteristically affecting the lower lung zones.
    \item \textbf{Serological assays:} Immunofluorescence or ELISA to detect MERS-CoV antibodies in acute and convalescent serum samples (at least 14 days apart).
    \item \textbf{Full blood count and biochemistry:} Characterised by marked lymphopenia, thrombocytopenia, elevated transaminases (AST and ALT), and elevated urea and creatinine.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
No specific antiviral treatment is currently approved for MERS; management is supportive, focusing on oxygenation and organ protection:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Advanced respiratory support:} Escalation from supplemental oxygen to non-invasive ventilation, mechanical ventilation, or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) in patients with severe ARDS.
    \item \textbf{Fluid management:} Maintaining a conservative fluid balance strategy in patients with ARDS to prevent worsening pulmonary oedema while maintaining renal perfusion.
    \item \textbf{Strict isolation and infection control:} Immediate isolation of suspected patients in negative-pressure rooms, utilising airborne, droplet, and contact precautions (including N95 respirators, eye protection, and gowns).
    \item \textbf{Experimental pharmacotherapy:} Under clinical trial protocols, therapies such as combinations of interferon-beta, ribavirin, remdesivir, or monoclonal antibodies may be considered.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Severe MERS-CoV infection is associated with several life-threatening complications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS):} Diffuse alveolar damage leading to profound, refractory hypoxia and respiratory failure.
    \item \textbf{Acute Kidney Injury (AKI):} Renal failure requiring haemodialysis, which occurs in up to 50\% of ICU patients due to direct viral invasion of renal cells via DPP4.
    \item \textbf{Septic shock and multi-organ failure:} Refractory hypotension, metabolic acidosis, and cardiovascular collapse.
    \item \textbf{Secondary bacterial infections:} Ventilator-associated pneumonia or catheter-related septicaemia, which increase hospital mortality.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for MERS is poor in patients who require hospitalisation for lower respiratory tract disease. The mortality rate is high at 35\%, rising to over 60\% in older adults (aged 60 and above) and individuals with chronic co-morbidities such as diabetes, renal failure, chronic lung disease, and immunosuppression. Patients who survive severe MERS face a prolonged recovery period, with many experiencing long-term pulmonary impairment (pulmonary fibrosis), chronic kidney disease, and persistent physical and psychological deconditioning.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
In the absence of a licensed human vaccine, prevention relies on avoiding exposure to the virus and adhering to hygiene practices:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Avoid contact with camels:} Refraining from visiting camel farms, markets, or contact with camels when travelling in endemic regions.
    \item \textbf{Food hygiene:} Avoiding the consumption of raw camel milk, camel urine, or undercooked camel meat.
    \item \textbf{General hygiene measures:} Washing hands regularly with soap and water or using alcohol-based hand sanitiser, especially after contact with animals.
    \item \textbf{Meticulous PPE use:} Strict adherence by healthcare workers to personal protective equipment guidelines during aerosol-generating procedures.
\end{itemize}
