\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bacterial vaginosis (BV) is the most common cause of abnormal vaginal discharge in women of reproductive age. It is a disturbance of the normal balance of bacteria in the vagina, in which the usual protective organisms, particularly \textit{Lactobacillus} species, are reduced and are replaced by an overgrowth of other bacteria such as \textit{Gardnerella vaginalis}, \textit{Atopobium vaginae}, and various anaerobes. BV is not a classic sexually transmitted infection, because it can occur in women who have never been sexually active, but sexual activity is a strong risk factor. The characteristic feature is a thin, greyish-white vaginal discharge with a noticeable fishy smell, especially after intercourse or around menstruation. Many affected women have no symptoms at all. Although BV is readily treated with antibiotics, it tends to recur, and it carries important health implications, including an increased risk of complications in pregnancy and of acquiring other infections. This chapter explains what BV is, who gets it, how it is diagnosed, and how it is treated and prevented.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bacterial vaginosis is extremely common. Studies from around the world report a prevalence of between 10 and 30 percent in unselected women of reproductive age, and it is the leading cause of vaginitis worldwide, accounting for around half of all cases of abnormal vaginal discharge. Prevalence varies widely with population, being higher in some groups such as women attending sexual health clinics, women who use intrauterine devices, and women of African descent. In many countries, BV is estimated to affect roughly one in five women of reproductive age at any given time. It is more common in women who have had multiple sexual partners, in those who are sexually active with female partners, and in women who use douching practices. The condition peaks in the reproductive years and is uncommon before puberty and after the menopause, when oestrogen levels and the natural lactobacillus population are low. Because up to half of cases produce no symptoms, many women are unaware they have BV.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The vagina normally maintains a carefully balanced ecosystem dominated by \textit{Lactobacillus} species, which produce lactic acid and hydrogen peroxide, keeping the environment acidic with a pH below about 4.5. This acidity suppresses the growth of other bacteria. Bacterial vaginosis occurs when this protective balance is disrupted and lactobacilli are displaced by an overgrowth of a mixed population of anaerobic and facultative bacteria. This overgrowth raises the vaginal pH, produces amines (chemicals that cause the characteristic fishy smell), and creates a thin, greyish discharge.

Factors that disturb the vaginal ecosystem include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sexual activity:} new or multiple partners, and unprotected sex, which can introduce organisms and alter the bacterial balance
    \item \textbf{Sex with female partners:} BV is strongly associated with same-sex activity between women
    \item \textbf{Douching:} washing out the vagina disrupts the natural flora and is strongly linked to BV
    \item \textbf{Intrauterine devices:} some studies show a higher risk of BV in IUD users
    \item \textbf{Hormonal changes:} menstruation, pregnancy, and the menstrual cycle can all shift the vaginal environment
    \item \textbf{Cigarette smoking:} smokers have higher rates of BV
    \item \textbf{Antibiotic use:} broad-spectrum antibiotics can temporarily wipe out protective lactobacilli
\end{itemize}

Bacterial vaginosis is not a single organism infection; it is a polymicrobial imbalance, which is one reason it is prone to recurrence.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms of bacterial vaginosis vary from none at all to very noticeable, and include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Thin, greyish-white vaginal discharge:} the classic sign, often described as coating the vaginal walls
    \item \textbf{A fishy odour:} most obvious after sexual intercourse, because semen temporarily raises the vaginal pH, and around menstruation
    \item \textbf{Little or no itching, soreness, or irritation:} BV usually does not cause the intense itch of thrush, and the vaginal area is typically not inflamed
    \item \textbf{No pain on urination:} unlike a urinary tract infection, and usually no pain with intercourse, although some women do report mild discomfort
    \item \textbf{An asymptomatic course:} up to half of affected women notice nothing at all and are diagnosed on screening or during examination for another reason
\end{itemize}

If a woman experiences itching, burning, pain, or bleeding with her discharge, an alternative diagnosis should be considered, because BV alone typically produces discharge and smell without marked tissue inflammation.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A range of conditions produce vaginal discharge or vulval irritation, and distinguishing them matters because the treatments differ completely.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vulvovaginal candidiasis (thrush):} thick, white, curd-like discharge with intense itching and redness; caused by overgrowth of \textit{Candida} species
    \item \textbf{Trichomoniasis:} a sexually transmitted infection causing a frothy, yellow-green, often foul-smelling discharge with itching and soreness
    \item \textbf{Chlamydia and gonorrhoea:} STIs that can cause discharge, pelvic pain, and bleeding but may also be silent
    \item \textbf{Cervical ectopy or cervicitis:} inflammation of the cervix, which can produce a mucoid discharge
    \item \textbf{Physiological discharge:} a normal increase in clear or whitish mucus around ovulation and in pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Hormonal changes:} vaginal atrophy after menopause or during breastfeeding causing dryness and discharge changes
    \item \textbf{Foreign body:} a retained tampon or condom, which produces a foul, often blood-stained discharge
\end{itemize}

Because symptoms overlap, laboratory testing rather than appearance alone is the reliable way to confirm BV.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most cases of bacterial vaginosis can be managed through a general practitioner, sexual health clinic, or women's health service. A consultation is appropriate if a woman notices a new, unusual, or persistent discharge, an unpleasant smell, or any vulval itching or soreness, especially if she is pregnant, at risk of an STI, or concerned about symptoms. Pregnant women with symptoms should be assessed and treated, because BV is associated with an increased risk of preterm birth.

Seek medical care promptly, and the same day if possible, for:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discharge accompanied by fever, abdominal pain, or pelvic pain
    \item Discharge with bleeding between periods or after intercourse
    \item Sores, blisters, or intense itching
    \item Symptoms that suggest an STI, such as frothy discharge or pain on passing urine
    \item Symptoms during pregnancy
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services} for fever with severe pelvic pain, heavy vaginal bleeding, or collapse, although these are not typical of BV itself and suggest a more serious condition such as pelvic inflammatory disease.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Bacterial vaginosis is diagnosed by a combination of clinical examination and simple tests. A clinician will take a careful history and perform a speculum examination to assess the discharge. The classic findings are the thin grey-white discharge, an elevated vaginal pH above 4.5, and a positive "whiff test", in which a drop of potassium hydroxide is added to a sample of discharge and produces the characteristic fishy smell. Under the microscope, vaginal fluid shows "clue cells", which are vaginal epithelial cells dotted with adherent bacteria, and a relative absence of lactobacilli. These findings form the Amsel criteria, of which three of four are required for the diagnosis.

In many local settings, particularly at sexual health services, nucleic acid amplification tests (NAAT) are now used to detect the organisms associated with BV with high accuracy. If there is any possibility of a sexually transmitted infection, swabs are also tested for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and trichomoniasis. Women with recurrent BV may be investigated further for contributing factors, but most cases are diagnosed and treated on the first visit.

\section*{Management}
Bacterial vaginosis is readily treated with antibiotics that target the anaerobic organisms while preserving the normal vaginal flora as much as possible.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Metronidazole:} the first-line treatment, given as oral tablets or as a vaginal gel. Oral metronidazole is usually taken twice daily for five to seven days. Alcohol must be avoided during and for a day or two after treatment, because it causes a severe flushing reaction
    \item \textbf{Clindamycin:} a vaginal cream or ovule, used when oral metronidazole is not suitable or as an alternative
    \item \textbf{Treatment during pregnancy:} oral or topical antibiotics are safe and are recommended, because BV in pregnancy is linked to preterm birth and other complications
    \item \textbf{Symptom control:} the discharge and odour usually resolve within days of starting treatment, even though the full course should be completed
    \item \textbf{Partner treatment:} routine treatment of male sexual partners is not recommended, because it does not reduce recurrence, although women with female partners may be advised to be treated together
    \item \textbf{Treatment of recurrence:} a longer course, repeated courses, or maintenance therapy may be recommended for women who relapse, and specialist review is helpful
    \item \textbf{Avoid douching:} douching is not part of treatment and makes matters worse
\end{itemize}

Probiotics, particularly lactobacillus-containing vaginal preparations, are sometimes suggested to help restore the vaginal flora, but evidence for benefit is still mixed and they should not replace antibiotic treatment.

\section*{Complications}
Although bacterial vaginosis is a relatively benign infection for most women, it is not trivial. It is associated with a number of important complications, particularly when it is recurrent or untreated.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Increased risk of STIs:} including HIV, chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and herpes, partly because the loss of protective lactobacilli reduces the vagina's natural defences
    \item \textbf{Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID):} BV organisms can ascend into the upper genital tract, particularly after procedures such as insertion of an intrauterine device or cervical surgery, and can cause pelvic infection and tubal damage
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy complications:} BV has been linked to an increased risk of preterm birth, low birth weight, and, in some studies, miscarriage and infection of the amniotic fluid
    \item \textbf{Complications after gynaecological surgery:} including infection after hysterectomy or termination of pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Recurrence:} the majority of women experience a recurrence within a year, and some women develop a chronic, relapsing pattern that is frustrating to live with
    \item \textbf{Psychological impact:} persistent discharge and odour can cause embarrassment, distress, and avoidance of intimacy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For most women, bacterial vaginosis resolves quickly and completely with a single course of treatment, and symptoms settle within days. The condition is, however, notoriously prone to recurrence, with up to half of women relapsing within six to twelve months. Recurrent BV is frustrating but manageable, and strategies including longer treatment courses, maintenance therapy, and attention to lifestyle factors improve control even if complete cure is not always achieved. Pregnancy-associated BV responds to treatment, and managing it is part of standard antenatal care. There is no long-term damage to the vagina itself, and BV does not affect a woman's fertility directly, although the associated pelvic infection can do so if it ascends and damages the fallopian tubes. The overall outlook is excellent, with symptoms usually easy to suppress even when the underlying tendency to recurrence remains.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Do not douche or use vaginal deodorants, which disrupt the normal vaginal flora
    \item Use condoms with new or casual partners to reduce the introduction of new bacteria
    \item Consider the contraceptive choices that suit your body, and discuss any concerns about IUDs with your clinician
    \item Wear breathable cotton underwear and avoid tight synthetic clothing, though the evidence that these factors matter is limited
    \item Avoid scented soaps, bubble baths, and intimate hygiene products around the vagina; plain water is enough
    \item Quit smoking, as smokers have higher rates of BV
    \item If using antibiotics for another reason, be aware that a course may trigger BV
    \item See a doctor early for any persistent or unusual discharge, particularly in pregnancy or before planned gynaecological procedures
    \item If recurrence is a problem, attend the follow-up arrangements offered, rather than repeatedly self-treating
\end{itemize}
