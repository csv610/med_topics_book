\chapter{Underweight}
\index{Underweight}
\section*{Definition and Overview}
Being underweight means weighing less than is healthy for an individual's age, height, body composition, and circumstances. In adults, BMI below 18.5 kg/m$^2$ is a screening threshold, not a diagnosis. In children and teenagers, growth charts and trajectory are more useful than an adult BMI cut-off.
\section*{Epidemiology}
Underweight may reflect inadequate food access, illness, malabsorption, mental-health problems, or normal body build. Risk and appropriate thresholds vary by age, pregnancy, ethnicity, and medical context.
\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Causes include inadequate intake, swallowing or dental problems, nausea, chronic infection, cancer, inflammatory bowel or coeliac disease, hyperthyroidism, diabetes, chronic lung or heart disease, medication effects, depression, eating disorders, and social or financial barriers to food. Low intake can cause loss of muscle, fat, vitamins, and immune and reproductive function.
\section*{Clinical Features}
Features may include unintentional weight loss, fatigue, weakness, dizziness, feeling cold, reduced concentration, menstrual changes, recurrent illness, poor wound healing, or loss of muscle. In children, crossing down growth centiles or delayed development is concerning.
\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Consider inadequate intake, gastrointestinal disease, endocrine disease, infection, malignancy, medication effects, depression, anxiety, substance use, anorexia nervosa, ARFID, and food insecurity.
\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Arrange medical assessment for unintentional or continuing weight loss, fainting, vomiting, diarrhoea, blood in stool, difficulty swallowing, severe weakness, dehydration, pregnancy with poor intake, or suspected eating disorder. Emergency care is needed for collapse, confusion, severe dehydration, chest pain, or suicidal intent.
\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment includes weight history, growth chart where relevant, diet and food access, physical examination, medicines, mental health, and functional status. Blood tests, stool tests, imaging, swallowing assessment, or dietetic assessment are selected according to findings.
\section*{Management}
Treat the cause. A dietitian may recommend energy- and protein-dense meals, snacks, oral supplements, or texture modification. Address dental, swallowing, mental-health, social, and medication barriers. Avoid rapid unsupervised refeeding after prolonged severe restriction because refeeding syndrome can be dangerous.
\section*{Complications}
Complications include malnutrition, muscle loss, falls, anaemia, infection, impaired fertility, poor wound healing, osteoporosis, and refeeding syndrome.
\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Outcome depends on the cause and how quickly it is treated. Weight restoration should be gradual and monitored, with attention to strength, function, and nutritional deficiencies rather than weight alone.
\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Eat regular balanced meals, seek help early for appetite or swallowing problems, maintain safe strength activity, monitor children on their growth chart, and seek support if food access or body-image concerns affect eating.
