\chapter{Hair Problems}
\index{Hair Problems}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hair problems encompass a wide range of conditions affecting the scalp and hair, including hair loss (alopecia), excessive hair growth, hair thinning, scalp conditions, and changes in hair texture. Hair loss is the most common concern and can be caused by genetics, hormonal changes, stress, illness, medicines, and nutritional factors. Many hair problems are temporary and reversible, while others, such as male and female pattern hair loss, are progressive. Because the causes are varied, assessment by a doctor is important, and treatment ranges from addressing underlying causes to medicines, procedures, and cosmetic approaches.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hair problems are very common. Around 50% of men experience male pattern hair loss by age 50, and it affects most men by old age. Female pattern hair loss affects up to 40% of women by menopause. Telogen effluvium, a temporary shedding of hair after stress, illness, or childbirth, is one of the most common reasons for GP visits about hair. Around 2% of the population experiences alopecia areata, an autoimmune cause of patchy hair loss. Excessive hair growth (hirsutism) affects around 5--10% of women.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The causes of hair problems are diverse and act through different mechanisms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic hair loss:} androgenetic alopecia (male and female pattern hair loss) is driven by genetics and hormones, particularly the effect of dihydrotestosterone on hair follicles
    \item \textbf{Telogen effluvium:} stress, illness, surgery, childbirth, rapid weight loss, and some medicines cause many hair follicles to enter the resting phase, leading to shedding two to three months later
    \item \textbf{Alopecia areata:} an autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks hair follicles, causing patchy loss
    \item \textbf{Hormonal causes:} thyroid disorders, polycystic ovary syndrome, and menopause
    \item \textbf{Nutritional causes:} iron deficiency, and deficiencies of zinc, protein, and B vitamins
    \item \textbf{Scalp conditions:} seborrhoeic dermatitis, psoriasis, and fungal infections
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} chemotherapy, some blood pressure and mood medicines, and others
    \item \textbf{Hair practices:} tight hairstyles, chemical treatments, and heat damage (traction alopecia)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The pattern of hair loss and associated symptoms point to the cause.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Gradual thinning on the top of the head in men, or widening of the part in women
    \item Sudden, diffuse shedding of hair, often seen on pillows and in the shower
    \item Patchy, circular bald spots (alopecia areata)
    \item Complete loss of scalp hair (alopecia totalis) or body hair (alopecia universalis)
    \item Thinning eyebrows and eyelashes
    \item Redness, scaling, or itching of the scalp
    \item Excessive facial or body hair growth in women, with other signs of hormone excess such as acne and irregular periods
    \item Brittle, breaking, or greasy hair
    \item A history of a recent trigger, such as illness, stress, or childbirth
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The many causes of hair problems must be distinguished.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Male and female pattern hair loss
    \item Telogen effluvium
    \item Alopecia areata and its variants
    \item Thyroid disease, including hypo- and hyperthyroidism
    \item Iron deficiency and other nutritional deficiencies
    \item Polycystic ovary syndrome and other hormonal disorders
    \item Scalp infections, including ringworm (tinea capitis)
    \item Seborrhoeic dermatitis and psoriasis
    \item Traction alopecia and trichotillomania (hair pulling)
    \item Medicine-related hair loss
    \item Autoimmune and connective tissue diseases, such as lupus
    \item Syphilis, which can cause patchy hair loss
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if hair loss is sudden, severe, patchy, or accompanied by other symptoms, or if you notice a change in hair in association with weight change, fatigue, or skin changes. Early assessment identifies treatable causes.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item This condition is not an emergency; however, sudden severe hair loss with scalp pain, swelling, or skin changes should be assessed promptly
    \item Any sign of serious infection of the scalp with fever
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is guided by history and examination, with targeted tests.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} onset, pattern, recent events, medicines, and family history
    \item \textbf{Examination:} scalp inspection, pull test, and assessment of the pattern of loss
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} iron studies, thyroid function, vitamin levels, and hormone tests
    \item \textbf{Scalp biopsy:} for uncertain diagnoses
    \item \textbf{Microscopy:} for fungal infection
    \item \textbf{Hair pull and hair pluck tests:} assess shedding
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a dermatologist for complex or persistent cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on the cause, and many hair problems resolve on their own.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Telogen effluvium:} reassurance, as hair regrows once the trigger resolves, usually within six months
    \item \textbf{Pattern hair loss:} minoxidil lotion for men and women, finasteride tablets for men, and low-level laser therapy
    \item \textbf{Alopecia areata:} corticosteroid injections, creams, and other immune-modulating treatments; spontaneous regrowth is common
    \item \textbf{Treating underlying causes:} iron replacement, thyroid treatment, and management of hormonal disorders
    \item \textbf{Scalp conditions:} medicated shampoos and treatments for seborrhoeic dermatitis and psoriasis
    \item \textbf{Fungal infection:} oral antifungal medicines
    \item \textbf{Stopping culprit medicines:} under medical advice
    \item \textbf{Cosmetic options:} hair transplantation, wigs, and camouflage products
    \item \textbf{Support:} counselling for the psychological effects of hair loss
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Psychological distress, reduced confidence, and effects on body image and social life
    \item Permanent hair loss when progressive causes are untreated
    \item Scarring alopecia in some inflammatory conditions
    \item Side effects of treatments, including scalp irritation and, with finasteride, rare sexual effects
    \item Complications of hair transplantation surgery
    \item Missed diagnosis of underlying conditions such as thyroid disease or iron deficiency
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the cause. Telogen effluvium resolves completely once the trigger is addressed. Alopecia areata improves in most people, though it can recur. Pattern hair loss is progressive but can be slowed with treatment. Iron and thyroid-related hair loss reverses with treatment. For many people, hair regrowth takes several months, and the most important factor is an accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment, supported by realistic expectations and, where helpful, cosmetic support.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Eat a balanced diet with adequate iron, protein, and vitamins
    \item Avoid tight hairstyles and harsh chemical or heat treatments
    \item Handle hair gently, and avoid vigorous brushing when wet
    \item Manage stress, which can trigger shedding
    \item Treat scalp conditions early
    \item See a doctor early for sudden or patchy hair loss
    \item Be patient with regrowth, which takes months
    \item Discuss medicines with your doctor if hair loss starts after a new medicine
    \item Seek support for the emotional effects of hair loss
\end{itemize}
