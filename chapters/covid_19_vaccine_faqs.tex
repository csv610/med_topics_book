\chapter{COVID-19 Vaccine FAQs}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
COVID-19 vaccines are vaccines that protect against severe illness, hospitalisation, and death from SARS-CoV-2, the virus that causes COVID-19. They work by training the immune system to recognise the virus so that if exposure occurs, the body can respond quickly. Several types of COVID-19 vaccine have been developed, including mRNA vaccines, viral vector vaccines, and protein-based vaccines, and they have been given to billions of people worldwide. In Australia, COVID-19 vaccines are recommended for everyone aged six months and over, with boosters for those at higher risk. This chapter answers common questions about how the vaccines work, their safety, and their side effects.

\section*{Epidemiology}
COVID-19 caused millions of deaths worldwide before vaccines became available in late 2020. Vaccination has since dramatically reduced severe illness and death, and in Australia and comparable countries, the vast majority of adults have received at least one dose. Effectiveness against infection wanes over months, but protection against severe disease remains substantial, particularly after a booster dose. People at higher risk -- older adults, those with chronic illness, and those with immune suppression -- are recommended additional doses, and updated vaccines targeting newer variants are offered periodically.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Different vaccine technologies achieve the same goal through different mechanisms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{mRNA vaccines:} carry instructions for the cell to make the viral spike protein, which the immune system learns to recognise (for example, Pfizer and Moderna)
    \item \textbf{Viral vector vaccines:} use a harmless modified virus to deliver the spike protein instructions (for example, AstraZeneca)
    \item \textbf{Protein vaccines:} contain pieces of the spike protein directly (for example, Novavax)
    \item \textbf{Immune memory:} the vaccine triggers antibody and T-cell responses that persist and are boosted by later doses
    \item \textbf{Variant protection:} updated vaccines are formulated against newer strains, such as Omicron subvariants
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Vaccination does not cause symptoms of disease, but some people experience short-lived reactions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Soreness, redness, or swelling at the injection site
    \item Tiredness, headache, muscle aches, or mild fever for a day or two
    \item Swollen lymph nodes in the armpit or neck
    \item Very rarely, allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis
    \item Uncommon side effects vary by vaccine, including heart muscle inflammation (myocarditis) in young males after mRNA vaccines
    \item A rare clotting disorder (TTS) was associated with the AstraZeneca vaccine
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Side effects and complications are carefully monitored and distinguished from other conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A cold or other respiratory infection coinciding with vaccination
    \item COVID-19 itself, which can occur around the time of vaccination if exposed
    \item Myocarditis from other causes, such as viral infection
    \item Allergic reactions to components of the vaccine
    \item Anxiety-related reactions, including fainting after vaccination
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most side effects are mild and settle within days. Seek medical advice if side effects are severe, persistent, or concerning, or if you have a history of serious allergic reactions and are considering vaccination.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, swelling of the face or throat, or a severe allergic reaction after vaccination
    \item Chest pain, palpitations, or shortness of breath within days or weeks of vaccination (possible myocarditis)
    \item A severe headache, bruising, or neurological symptoms after an AstraZeneca dose
    \item Fainting, collapse, or seizures
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Vaccination programmes include careful safety monitoring.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Screening:} questions about previous reactions, allergies, and recent illness before each dose
    \item \textbf{Observation:} a short waiting period after vaccination to observe for immediate reactions
    \item \textbf{Adverse event reporting:} side effects are reported to the Therapeutic Goods Administration
    \item \textbf{Investigation of complications:} blood tests, ECG, or imaging for suspected myocarditis or clotting problems
    \item \textbf{Immunisation records:} doses recorded in the Australian Immunisation Register
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Vaccination is a preventive measure, delivered through a structured programme.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Routine doses:} primary course for everyone aged six months and over
    \item \textbf{Boosters:} recommended for adults, with additional doses for high-risk groups
    \item \textbf{Seasonal updating:} vaccines updated to match circulating variants
    \item \textbf{Timing with other vaccines:} can be given with influenza and other vaccines
    \item \textbf{Management of reactions:} paracetamol for fever and discomfort; adrenaline for anaphylaxis
    \item \textbf{Special populations:} adjusted advice for pregnancy, immune suppression, and children
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Mild injection-site reactions and systemic symptoms, which resolve within days
    \item Anaphylaxis, which is very rare and treatable
    \item Myocarditis and pericarditis, rare after mRNA vaccines, mostly in young males, and usually mild
    \item Thrombosis with thrombocytopenia syndrome (TTS), a very rare clotting disorder after AstraZeneca
    \item Vaccine failure, meaning infection despite vaccination, though severe disease is far less likely
    \item Guillain-Barr\u00e9 syndrome, a rare neurological condition associated with some vaccines
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
COVID-19 vaccines have been shown in large studies to be safe and highly effective at preventing severe disease, hospitalisation, and death. Protection against infection wanes over time, but protection against serious illness is more durable, and booster doses restore it. Serious side effects are very rare and are far outweighed by the risks of COVID-19 itself, particularly in older adults and people with chronic conditions. Ongoing monitoring continues to refine recommendations as the virus evolves.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep up to date with recommended doses and boosters
    \item Discuss any concerns with a GP, who can advise on the most suitable vaccine
    \item Tell the vaccinator about allergies, pregnancy, or immune suppression
    \item Plan to rest after vaccination and treat mild fever or aches with paracetamol
    \item Continue other prevention measures during surges, including hygiene and ventilation
    \item Use the Australian Immunisation Register to keep track of doses
\end{itemize}
