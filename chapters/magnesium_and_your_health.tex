\chapter{Magnesium and your health}
\index{Magnesium and your health}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Magnesium is an essential divalent intracellular cation and a critical cofactor for over 300 enzymatic reactions in the human body. It plays a fundamental role in energy production (adenosine triphosphate synthesis), protein synthesis, nucleic acid synthesis, cell growth, and the maintenance of genomic stability. Furthermore, magnesium is vital for the active transport of calcium and potassium ions across cell membranes, a process essential for nerve impulse conduction, muscle contraction, vasomotor tone, and normal cardiac rhythm. The human body contains approximately 25 grams of magnesium, with 50--60\% stored in the skeleton and the remainder in soft tissues, while less than 1\% circulates in the blood. Given its extensive physiological roles, maintaining adequate magnesium levels through dietary intake or supplementation is crucial for long-term health and the prevention of chronic disease.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Dietary deficiency of magnesium is highly prevalent in modern Western populations, primarily due to the high consumption of processed foods, which are depleted of minerals, and the decline of magnesium content in soil and agricultural products. In many countries, national nutrition surveys indicate that a substantial proportion of both men and women (up to 30--40\% in certain age groups) do not meet the Estimated Average Requirement for magnesium. Clinically significant hypomagnesaemia (defined as serum magnesium <0.7 mmol/L) is common in hospitalised patients, particularly those in intensive care units, and in patients with chronic alcoholism, malabsorptive gastrointestinal disorders, poorly controlled diabetes, or those taking certain medications. Hypermagnesaemia is much rarer, occurring almost exclusively in patients with severe renal impairment who ingest magnesium-containing antacids, laxatives, or supplements.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Magnesium homeostasis is regulated by the balance between gastrointestinal absorption, bone exchange, and renal excretion. Disruptions in these pathways lead to clinical deficiency or excess.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inadequate dietary intake:} Diets low in green leafy vegetables, legumes, nuts, seeds, and whole grains, and high in refined sugars and processed foods.
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal malabsorption:} Conditions such as coeliac disease, Crohn's disease, chronic diarrhoea, or small bowel resection that impair active and passive magnesium absorption in the ileum and colon.
    \item \textbf{Renal wasting:} Increased urinary excretion of magnesium due to chronic alcohol consumption, poorly controlled diabetes mellitus (osmotic diuresis), or genetic tubulopathies (such as Gitelman or Bartter syndromes).
    \item \textbf{Medication-induced depletion:} Chronic use of proton pump inhibitors (which impair intestinal absorption), loop and thiazide diuretics (which increase renal excretion), and calcineurin inhibitors.
    \item \textbf{Renal insufficiency:} Impaired glomerular filtration in acute kidney injury or stage 4--5 chronic kidney disease, leading to magnesium accumulation when exogenous magnesium is ingested (hypermagnesaemia).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Mild magnesium deficiency is often asymptomatic, but progressive hypomagnesaemia can cause significant neuromuscular, cardiovascular, and metabolic disturbances.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Early symptoms including loss of appetite, nausea, vomiting, fatigue, lethargy, and generalised physical weakness.
    \item Neuromuscular hyperexcitability, presenting as muscle fasciculations (twitching), muscle cramps, spasms, tremors, and hyperreflexia.
    \item Paresthesias (numbness and tingling sensations), particularly in the extremities, and in severe cases, tetany.
    \item Cardiac arrhythmias, including premature ventricular contractions, ventricular tachycardia, torsades de pointes, and atrial fibrillation.
    \item Neuropsychiatric disturbances, such as apathy, depression, anxiety, confusion, delirium, and in severe cases, generalised seizures.
    \item Refractory hypokalaemia and hypocalcaemia, where low potassium and calcium levels cannot be corrected until the underlying magnesium deficiency is resolved.
    \item Hypermagnesaemia features (magnesium toxicity) including hypotension, bradycardia, diminished deep tendon reflexes, flushing, nausea, and in severe cases, respiratory depression and cardiac arrest.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of magnesium imbalance overlap with other electrolyte disturbances and neuromuscular conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hypocalcaemia:} Presents with similar neuromuscular excitability, muscle cramps, tetany, and QT prolongation, and often coexists with hypomagnesaemia.
    \item \textbf{Hypokalaemia:} Causes muscle weakness, cramps, fatigue, and cardiac arrhythmias, but is distinguished by serum potassium measurements.
    \item \textbf{Chronic fatigue syndrome:} Causes persistent fatigue, weakness, and diffuse muscle aches, but is not associated with objective electrolyte imbalances.
    \item \textbf{Primary neuromuscular disorders:} Conditions like amyotrophic lateral sclerosis or peripheral neuropathies that cause muscle twitching and cramping, but have distinct neurological findings and normal serum chemistry.
    \item \textbf{Anxiety and panic disorders:} Can cause hyperventilation, paresthesias, and muscle spasms due to respiratory alkalosis, which must be differentiated from metabolic hypomagnesaemia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Individuals who experience persistent muscle cramps, unexplained fatigue, or tingling sensations should consult their general practitioner to check their electrolyte levels.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden onset of palpitations, irregular heartbeat, or chest pain.
    \item A generalised seizure or sudden loss of consciousness.
    \item Severe muscle weakness accompanied by difficulty breathing or shortness of breath.
    \item Signs of severe magnesium toxicity, such as extreme lethargy, slow breathing, and inability to stand or speak, in a person taking magnesium supplements.
\end{itemize}

Other non-emergency reasons to see a doctor include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent, painful muscle cramps or twitching that does not resolve with hydration.
    \item Chronic numbness, tingling, or crawling sensations in the hands or feet.
    \item Unexplained fatigue, weakness, and loss of appetite lasting more than two weeks.
    \item Discussion of magnesium supplement safety, especially if you have kidney disease.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessing magnesium status is challenging because serum magnesium represents only a tiny fraction of total body magnesium.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Serum magnesium test:} The most common initial test; however, a normal level (typically 0.7 to 1.0 mmol/L) does not rule out intracellular magnesium depletion.
    \item \textbf{Ionised magnesium test:} Measures the biologically active, free form of magnesium in the blood, providing a more accurate assessment than total serum magnesium.
    \item \textbf{24-hour urinary magnesium excretion test:} Helps differentiate renal wasting (high urinary excretion despite low serum levels) from gastrointestinal loss or poor dietary intake.
    \item \textbf{Serum potassium and calcium tests:} Essential to screen for coexisting hypokalaemia and hypocalcaemia, which are common in hypomagnesaemia.
    \item \textbf{Electrocardiogram:} Indicated in moderate-to-severe hypomagnesaemia to look for conduction abnormalities, such as widening of the QRS complex, prolonged PR interval, T-wave flattening, or QT prolongation.
    \item \textbf{Renal function tests:} Crucial to assess glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and creatinine, particularly before starting or monitoring magnesium supplementation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of magnesium imbalance involves dietary modifications, oral or intravenous supplementation, and treating any underlying cause.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary adjustments:} Encouraging consumption of magnesium-rich foods, including green leafy vegetables (spinach, kale), pumpkin seeds, almonds, cashews, black beans, whole grains, and dark chocolate.
    \item \textbf{Oral magnesium supplementation:} Used for mild deficiency and general health maintenance. Various formulations are available, with organic salts (such as magnesium citrate, glycinate, or aspartate) having higher bioavailability than inorganic salts (such as magnesium oxide).
    \item \textbf{Intravenous magnesium replacement:} Administered as magnesium sulphate in hospital settings for severe hypomagnesaemia, cardiac arrhythmias (specifically torsades de pointes), or as neuroprotection in pre-eclampsia/eclampsia.
    \item \textbf{Avoidance of offending medications:} Reviewing and, if possible, adjusting medications that cause magnesium wasting, such as substituting diuretics or modifying proton pump inhibitor therapy.
    \item \textbf{Monitoring in renal impairment:} Restricting magnesium intake and avoiding magnesium-containing antacids or laxatives in patients with advanced chronic kidney disease to prevent hypermagnesaemia.
    \item \textbf{Treating malabsorption:} Managing underlying conditions like coeliac disease with a strict gluten-free diet to restore normal intestinal mineral absorption.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Life-threatening arrhythmias:} Severe hypomagnesaemia can trigger torsades de pointes or ventricular fibrillation, leading to sudden cardiac arrest.
    \item \textbf{Neurological sequelae:} Severe deficiency can cause delirium, coma, or status epilepticus.
    \item \textbf{Osteoporosis and osteomalacia:} Chronic magnesium deficiency impairs bone mineralisation and blunts the secretion and action of parathyroid hormone, contributing to bone thinning.
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular disease progression:} Low magnesium levels are linked to endothelial dysfunction, arterial calcification, and worsening hypertension.
    \item \textbf{Severe hypermagnesaemia complications:} Cardiac arrest, flaccid paralysis, and respiratory failure due to magnesium-induced blockade of neuromuscular transmission.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for magnesium deficiency is generally excellent when the condition is recognised and treated early. Most patients experience rapid resolution of symptoms (such as muscle cramps and fatigue) within days to weeks of initiating appropriate oral or intravenous replacement. Long-term outcomes depend on the successful management of the underlying cause, such as dietary improvement, stopping offending medications, or controlling malabsorptive disorders. In hospital settings, correcting hypomagnesaemia reduces the risk of dangerous arrhythmias and shortens intensive care stays.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Focus on eating a varied, whole-food diet rich in magnesium; aim for daily servings of nuts, seeds, whole grains, and dark green vegetables.
    \item Limit consumption of highly processed foods, refined sugars, and excessive alcohol, which increase magnesium excretion.
    \item Consult a doctor or pharmacist before starting magnesium supplements, especially if you have a history of kidney disease or are taking other prescription medications.
    \item Be aware of the laxative effect of certain magnesium formulations (such as magnesium oxide or citrate) and reduce the dose if you develop loose stools or diarrhoea.
    \item Stay well-hydrated, particularly during exercise or hot weather, to help maintain optimal electrolyte balance.
    \item Monitor for symptoms of deficiency if you are taking long-term medications known to deplete magnesium, such as proton pump inhibitors or diuretics.
\end{itemize}
