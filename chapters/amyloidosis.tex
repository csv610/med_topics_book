\chapter{Amyloidosis}
\index{Amyloidosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Amyloidosis is a group of rare disorders in which abnormal proteins called amyloid are produced in excess and deposited outside cells, where they form stiff, insoluble fibrils that crowd out normal tissue. Amyloid can accumulate in many organs, including the heart, kidneys, liver, spleen, nerves, and digestive system, gradually interfering with their function.

The condition may affect a single site (localised amyloidosis) or many organs at once (systemic amyloidosis), and the outlook depends heavily on which type is present and how early it is recognised. Although amyloidosis was historically regarded as uniformly fatal, modern treatments targeting the underlying protein and improved supportive care have substantially changed the outlook for many people.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Amyloidosis is uncommon, with estimates suggesting roughly 1 in 100,000 people are newly diagnosed each year, though milder forms may be underdiagnosed. Most cases occur after the age of 50, and several types are slightly more common in men. The most frequently diagnosed systemic type is AL (light-chain) amyloidosis, which is linked to a disorder of antibody-producing cells in the bone marrow.

AA amyloidosis, caused by chronic inflammation or infection, is now much rarer in developed countries because underlying inflammatory conditions are treated earlier and more effectively. A separate type caused by the protein transthyretin (ATTR amyloidosis) may be inherited or may arise spontaneously with advancing age; the age-related form is increasingly recognised as a cause of heart failure in older adults.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Amyloidosis develops when a normally soluble protein is produced in excess, or in a misfolded form, and deposits as amyloid fibrils in the extracellular space. The type of disease is defined by the source protein:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{AL (light-chain) amyloidosis:} excess antibody fragments (light chains) produced by abnormal bone-marrow cells called plasma cells, often in the setting of a clonal plasma-cell disorder
    \item \textbf{AA (secondary) amyloidosis:} deposition of serum amyloid A, an acute-phase protein produced at high levels during chronic inflammation, infection, or certain cancers
    \item \textbf{ATTR amyloidosis:} deposition of transthyretin, a transport protein made mainly in the liver, which can occur from an inherited gene mutation or from age-related misfolding of the normal protein
    \item \textbf{Localised amyloidosis:} amyloid deposited at a single site such as the skin, bladder, or eye, without evidence of systemic disease
    \item \textbf{Dialysis-related amyloidosis:} build-up of beta-2 microglobulin in people who have been on long-term kidney dialysis
\end{itemize}
Whichever protein is involved, the deposited fibrils physically displace and damage normal tissue, interfering with organ structure and function and producing the symptoms of the disease.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on which organs are affected and are often vague and non-specific, which frequently delays diagnosis:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{General:} fatigue, weakness, and unintentional weight loss
    \item \textbf{Kidneys:} swelling of the ankles and legs (oedema), frothy or bubbly urine from leaking protein, and rising creatinine
    \item \textbf{Heart:} shortness of breath, palpitations, and reduced exercise tolerance from a stiffened, thickened heart muscle
    \item \textbf{Nerves:} numbness, tingling, or burning pain in the hands and feet (peripheral neuropathy) and dizziness on standing (autonomic neuropathy)
    \item \textbf{Digestive system:} diarrhoea, nausea, early fullness, and altered bowel habit
    \item \textbf{Other features:} an enlarged tongue (macroglossia), bruising around the eyes, easy bruising and bleeding, carpal tunnel syndrome often in both wrists, and an enlarged liver or spleen
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of amyloidosis overlap with many far more common conditions, including:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic kidney disease from diabetes, hypertension, or glomerulonephritis
    \item Heart failure from coronary artery disease, valve disease, or hypertensive heart disease
    \item Peripheral neuropathy from diabetes, alcohol misuse, vitamin deficiency, or other causes
    \item Multiple myeloma and other monoclonal gammopathies, which can coexist with AL amyloidosis
    \item Chronic inflammatory bowel disease or malabsorption syndromes
    \item Age-related changes, which can produce milder deposition of transthyretin without overt disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if you have persistent swelling, unexplained fatigue or weight loss, or any combination of symptoms that is unusual for you. Seek medical attention promptly if swelling is worsening, you become increasingly breathless, you notice blood in the urine or faeces, you bruise very easily, or numbness and weakness in the limbs is spreading.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe chest pain, fainting, or collapse
    \item Sudden severe breathlessness or inability to catch your breath
    \item Symptoms suggestive of a serious heart rhythm disturbance, such as a racing, fluttering, or irregular heartbeat with dizziness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis requires a high index of suspicion, because no single symptom or sign is specific.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} symptoms across multiple organ systems, family history of amyloidosis or neuropathy, and any chronic inflammatory condition
    \item \textbf{Examination:} blood pressure (including on standing), evidence of heart failure, peripheral oedema, macroglossia, bruising, and peripheral neuropathy
    \item \textbf{Blood and urine tests:} kidney and liver function, and tests for abnormal proteins including serum free light chains and serum and urine protein electrophoresis
    \item \textbf{Tissue biopsy:} a small sample of tissue, often from abdominal fat, bone marrow, or an affected organ, stained with Congo red and examined under polarised light for apple-green birefringence
    \item \textbf{Bone marrow biopsy:} to identify the abnormal plasma-cell clone producing the light chains in AL amyloidosis
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} echocardiography and cardiac MRI to assess heart involvement, and ultrasound or CT for the liver, spleen, and kidneys
    \item \textbf{Nuclear medicine scans:} specialised scans that can detect cardiac transthyretin deposits without a biopsy in selected cases
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} to identify inherited mutations when a familial form is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
There is no single cure for all forms of amyloidosis, but treatment can slow or stop the build-up of amyloid and relieve symptoms. The approach depends on the type and on which organs are affected:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{AL amyloidosis:} chemotherapy, targeted immunotherapy, and, in selected cases, autologous stem-cell transplantation to eliminate the abnormal plasma cells
    \item \textbf{AA amyloidosis:} treating the underlying inflammatory disease or infection so that the amyloid precursor protein is no longer produced
    \item \textbf{ATTR amyloidosis:} medicines that stabilise or silence the abnormal protein; in the inherited form, liver transplantation may be considered in selected cases
    \item \textbf{Organ support:} treatment for heart failure, kidney protection with blood-pressure control, and dialysis or transplantation for advanced kidney disease
    \item \textbf{Symptom management:} medicines for diarrhoea, pain, and cardiac arrhythmias, together with physiotherapy, dietary advice, and management of postural dizziness
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular review of heart, kidney, and blood tests by a specialist team to assess response and detect toxicity early
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive heart failure and serious abnormal heart rhythms (arrhythmias), the leading cause of death
    \item Kidney failure requiring dialysis or kidney transplantation
    \item Peripheral and autonomic nerve damage, causing weakness, numbness, and problems with blood-pressure control
    \item Malnutrition and bleeding from gastrointestinal involvement
    \item An enlarged or bleeding tongue that interferes with eating and speaking
    \item Bleeding problems due to fragile blood vessels and reduced clotting factors
    \item Side effects of chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and stem-cell transplantation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for amyloidosis has improved considerably over recent decades and now varies widely with the type, the organs involved, and how early the diagnosis is made. Some localised forms are harmless and may never need treatment. In AL amyloidosis, people who respond well to modern treatment may survive for many years, but advanced heart involvement still carries a poorer outlook. Inherited transthyretin amyloidosis progresses gradually, although newer treatments can slow its course and improve quality of life. Because the disease can affect so many organs, outcomes are best when a specialist team manages the person early and treats both the amyloid process itself and the organ damage it has caused.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item There is no proven way to prevent most forms of amyloidosis, but treating chronic inflammatory conditions promptly reduces the risk of AA amyloidosis
    \item Attend recommended follow-up so that heart and kidney function are monitored regularly
    \item Take all treatments exactly as prescribed and report side effects early
    \item Eat a balanced diet and adjust salt and fluid intake if the heart or kidneys are affected
    \item Protect against infection with routine vaccinations and prompt treatment of infections, especially while receiving chemotherapy
    \item Seek genetic counselling if an inherited form of amyloidosis runs in the family
    \item Stay as physically active as symptoms allow, and ask about physiotherapy or cardiac rehabilitation
    \item Reach out for psychological support if living with amyloidosis is affecting mood or quality of life
\end{itemize}
