\chapter{Anaemia Medicines}
\index{Anaemia Medicines}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Anaemia medicines are the treatments used to correct low red blood cell counts and restore the blood's ability to carry oxygen. They work in different ways depending on the type of anaemia: replacing a missing nutrient such as iron, folate, or vitamin B12; stimulating the bone marrow to produce more red cells; or, in an emergency, delivering ready-made red cells directly by transfusion.

Because anaemia is a sign of an underlying problem, medicines alone are rarely sufficient. They are used alongside treatment of the cause and are prescribed on the basis of blood test results, so that the right medicine is matched to the right deficiency.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The use of anaemia medicines is very common. Iron supplements are among the most widely used medicines in the world, particularly in pregnancy, in women with heavy menstrual bleeding, and in people with gut disorders that impair absorption. Vitamin B12 injections are used long-term by people with pernicious anaemia and other vitamin B12-deficiency states.

Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents are used by many people with chronic kidney disease, including most of those on dialysis, and blood transfusion remains a frequent therapy in hospitals. Rates of anaemia are higher in low- and middle-income countries, where iron deficiency predominates, but iron supplementation and screening programmes have reduced its impact in many regions.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The choice of medicine follows directly from the cause of the anaemia, because different deficiencies require different replacements:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Iron deficiency:} iron is a component of haemoglobin; when stores are low, red cells become small and pale (microcytic)
    \item \textbf{Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency:} both are needed for DNA synthesis in dividing cells, so deficiency slows red-cell production and produces large, immature red cells
    \item \textbf{Chronic disease or kidney failure:} inflammatory signals and low production of erythropoietin reduce the marrow's output of red cells
    \item \textbf{Severe or rapid anaemia:} transfusion replaces whole red cells when the body cannot correct the shortfall quickly enough
\end{itemize}
Each treatment corrects a specific step in red-cell production, so giving the wrong one is ineffective and can be harmful, for example treating a B12 deficiency with folate alone, which masks the anaemia while nerve damage continues.

\section*{Clinical Features}
People may be treated for anaemia or develop symptoms while being treated:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Symptoms of the anaemia itself: fatigue, paleness, breathlessness, and dizziness
    \item Improvement in energy and symptoms after treatment, usually developing over several weeks
    \item Side effects of iron tablets: nausea, constipation, dark stools, and stomach discomfort
    \item Side effects of injections or infusions: pain at the site, flushing, allergic reactions, and, rarely, anaphylaxis
    \item Over-treatment: iron overload, or too rapid a rise in red cells causing high blood pressure or blood clots with some agents
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When someone on anaemia medicine fails to improve, the following should be considered:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The wrong diagnosis, such as treating iron deficiency when the true cause is vitamin B12 deficiency
    \item Continued blood loss or malabsorption, such as persistent gut bleeding
    \item A coexisting second cause of anaemia, such as chronic kidney disease or inflammation
    \item Poor adherence to tablets, or intolerance leading to stopped treatment
    \item An incorrect dose, or an iron product that is not well absorbed
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical advice if:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Your anaemia symptoms do not improve after several weeks of treatment
    \item You cannot tolerate the side effects of a medicine
    \item You think you may have taken too much; for suspected iron overdose, contact local emergency services or the poison information service
    \item You develop a rash, itching, or swelling shortly after an injection or infusion
    \item You are pregnant and have questions about which supplements are safe
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden difficulty breathing, swelling of the face or throat, or collapse shortly after a treatment
    \item Symptoms of anaphylaxis developing during or after any infusion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the type of anaemia being treated, how long treatment has continued, adherence, and any ongoing bleeding or malabsorption
    \item \textbf{Full blood count:} to confirm anaemia and check the response to treatment
    \item \textbf{Iron studies:} serum ferritin and related tests to confirm iron deficiency and monitor replacement
    \item \textbf{Vitamin B12 and folate levels:} measured before and sometimes after replacement
    \item \textbf{Reticulocyte count:} an early indicator that the bone marrow is responding to treatment
    \item \textbf{Kidney and inflammatory markers:} when anaemia of chronic disease is suspected
    \item \textbf{Monitoring during treatment:} repeat blood counts at intervals to guide dosing and avoid over-treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Iron supplements:} oral tablets are first-line; intravenous iron is used when oral iron fails, is not tolerated, or rapid correction is needed
    \item \textbf{Vitamin B12 and folate:} replacement by mouth or injection, with ongoing treatment for conditions such as pernicious anaemia
    \item \textbf{Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents:} injections that stimulate red-cell production, used mainly in chronic kidney disease and selected other settings
    \item \textbf{Blood transfusion:} red-cell transfusion for severe, symptomatic, or rapidly developing anaemia
    \item \textbf{Timing of medicines:} iron is often taken away from tea, coffee, calcium, and some antacids, which reduce its absorption
    \item \textbf{Addressing the cause:} treating heavy periods, gut bleeding, or malabsorption alongside the medicines
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} repeat blood tests to confirm the blood count has normalised and remains stable
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Gastrointestinal side effects of oral iron, including nausea, constipation, and dark stools
    \item Iron overload from repeated replacement without ongoing blood loss, affecting the liver and heart
    \item Allergic and infusion reactions to intravenous iron
    \item Progressive nerve damage (neuropathy) if vitamin B12 deficiency is treated with folate alone, which masks the anaemia
    \item Raised blood pressure, blood clots, or stroke with some erythropoiesis-stimulating agents if overused
    \item Transfusion reactions, infection, and iron overload from repeated transfusions
    \item Iron poisoning in children who accidentally swallow iron tablets, so these should be stored securely
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people with nutrient-deficiency anaemia respond well to replacement medicines, with symptom improvement within weeks and a normal blood count within one to three months. Iron-deficiency anaemia treated properly usually resolves, but it recurs if the underlying cause is not corrected. Vitamin B12 deficiency may require lifelong injections but is easily managed. The anaemia of chronic disease often improves when the underlying illness is treated. Severe anaemias that require transfusion or specialist agents improve in most cases but need ongoing monitoring and investigation of the cause.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take anaemia medicines exactly as prescribed, and continue even when you feel better unless told to stop
    \item Keep your doctor informed of side effects rather than stopping on your own
    \item Store iron tablets out of the reach of children, as overdose is dangerous
    \item Eat iron-rich foods and foods containing vitamin C alongside treatment where advised
    \item Do not take extra iron or other supplements without a blood test
    \item Attend all follow-up blood tests
    \item If you are having injections or infusions, report any reaction during or after treatment
    \item Bring your current medicine list to every appointment so that doses and interactions can be checked
\end{itemize}
