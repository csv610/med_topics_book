\chapter{Metformin}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Metformin is a biguanide oral antihyperglycaemic agent that serves as the gold-standard, first-line pharmacological treatment for type 2 diabetes mellitus. Unlike other oral antidiabetic agents (such as sulfonylureas), metformin does not stimulate insulin secretion and therefore does not cause weight gain or carry a significant risk of hypoglycaemia when used as monotherapy. Beyond its primary indication in diabetes, metformin is widely used off-label to manage insulin resistance in polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), treat gestational diabetes, and assist in weight management. It is excreted unchanged by the kidneys. While generally safe and well-tolerated, its use requires careful monitoring of renal function, and it is contraindicated in patients with severe renal impairment due to the rare but life-threatening risk of metformin-associated lactic acidosis (MALA).

\section*{Epidemiology}
Metformin is one of the most widely prescribed medications globally, with hundreds of millions of users. In Australia, it consistently ranks among the top ten most frequently dispensed medications under the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS). It is prescribed to patients of all ages who have type 2 diabetes, though its use is most concentrated in adults aged 50 years and older. In women of reproductive age (18 to 45 years), metformin is highly utilised to treat the metabolic manifestations of PCOS. The global prevalence of its use continues to rise, driven by the increasing incidence of obesity and type 2 diabetes.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The primary therapeutic effect of metformin is the reduction of blood glucose levels through multiple molecular mechanisms, primarily targetting the liver and skeletal muscle.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inhibition of mitochondrial complex I:} Metformin accumulates in hepatocytes and inhibits complex I of the mitochondrial respiratory chain. This reduces cellular ATP synthesis and increases the AMP-to-ATP ratio.
    \item \textbf{Activation of AMPK:} The elevated AMP-to-ATP ratio activates AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a key cellular energy sensor. AMPK activation suppresses hepatic gluconeogenesis (the production of glucose by the liver) and reduces glycogenolysis.
    \item \textbf{Enhancement of peripheral insulin sensitivity:} Metformin increases insulin receptor tyrosine kinase activity and stimulates glucose transporter-4 (GLUT-4) translocation in skeletal muscle, facilitating increased peripheral glucose uptake and utilisation.
    \item \textbf{Delayed gastrointestinal glucose absorption:} Metformin slows the absorption of glucose from the intestinal lumen and increases local glucose utilisation by the gut mucosa, which also increases local GLP-1 secretion.
    \item \textbf{Lack of pancreatic stimulation:} Because metformin does not act on beta-islet cells of the pancreas, it does not stimulate endogenous insulin release, thereby avoiding hyperinsulinaemia and hypoglycaemic episodes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features of metformin therapy include its therapeutic benefits and its characteristic profile of side effects and toxicity.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Therapeutic effects:} Lowering of fasting and postprandial blood glucose, reduction in HbA1c levels, mild weight loss or weight stabilisation, and improvement in menstrual regularity and ovulation in women with PCOS.
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal side effects:} The most common adverse effects, including watery diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, abdominal bloating, cramping, flatulence, and a metallic taste in the mouth, which typically occur during treatment initiation or dose escalation.
    \item \textbf{Vitamin B12 deficiency:} Long-term use (typically over several years) can impair intestinal absorption of vitamin B12, presenting with fatigue, pallor, or neurological symptoms such as peripheral neuropathy.
    \item \textbf{Lactic acidosis symptoms (Toxicity):} A rare but severe complication presenting with Kussmaul breathing (deep, rapid respiration), severe muscle cramps, profound weakness, abdominal pain, hypothermia, hypotension, and bradycardia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When evaluating a patient taking metformin who presents with illness, clinicians must differentiate drug side effects from other acute or chronic conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Metformin-associated lactic acidosis (MALA) vs.\ diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) vs.\ sepsis:} Distinguishing high-lactate metabolic acidosis caused by metformin accumulation from ketone-driven acidosis (DKA) or hypoperfusion-induced lactic acidosis in sepsis.
    \item \textbf{Metformin-induced diarrhoea vs.\ Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) vs.\ Coeliac disease:} Differentiating drug-related gastrointestinal side effects from primary bowel disorders or infectious gastroenteritis.
    \item \textbf{Metformin-induced B12 neuropathy vs.\ Diabetic peripheral neuropathy:} Distinguishing numbness and tingling in the feet caused by vitamin B12 deficiency (which is reversible) from progressive, irreversible diabetic microvascular nerve damage.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients starting metformin should consult their general practitioner or pharmacist if they experience persistent, severe diarrhoea or vomiting, or if they develop numbness, tingling, or burning sensations in their hands or feet.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Symptoms suggestive of lactic acidosis, including sudden deep and rapid breathing, severe muscle pain, extreme lethargy, and a slow, irregular heartbeat.
    \item \textbf{Acute overdose:} Accidental or intentional ingestion of a large quantity of metformin, which can trigger severe metabolic acidosis.
    \item Severe dehydration (inability to keep fluids down due to vomiting or diarrhoea) while taking metformin, which requires immediate medical attention and temporary cessation of the drug.
    \item Loss of consciousness, confusion, or seizures.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Monitoring safety and efficacy in patients taking metformin requires routine laboratory assessments.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Renal function testing (eGFR and Creatinine):} Mandatory before initiating therapy and at least annually thereafter. Metformin is contraindicated if the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is less than 30 mL/min/1.73m$^2$, and the dose should be reduced if the eGFR is between 30 and 45 mL/min/1.73m$^2$.
    \item \textbf{HbA1c and fasting blood glucose:} Performed every 3 to 6 months to assess long-term glycaemic control and therapeutic efficacy.
    \item \textbf{Serum Vitamin B12 levels:} Checked periodically (every 1 to 2 years), particularly in patients who develop macrocytic anaemia or peripheral neuropathy.
    \item \textbf{Arterial blood gas (ABG) and lactate levels:} In suspected lactic acidosis, confirming a pH of less than 7.35, a blood lactate concentration greater than 5 mmol/L, and an elevated anion gap.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management of metformin therapy focuses on reducing gastrointestinal side effects, managing contraindications, and handling emergencies.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gradual dose titration:} Starting with a low dose (e.g. 500 mg once daily with the evening meal) and increasing the dose by 500 mg increments every 1 to 2 weeks up to the maximum tolerated dose (typically 2000 mg to 2500 mg daily).
    \item \textbf{Extended-release (XR) formulations:} Switching to extended-release metformin, which is taken once daily and is associated with significantly fewer gastrointestinal side effects.
    \item \textbf{"Sick Day Rules" implementation:} Instructing patients to temporarily withhold metformin during acute illnesses that cause dehydration (e.g. severe vomiting, diarrhoea, high fever, or acute infections) to prevent renal impairment and subsequent lactic acidosis.
    \item \textbf{Contrast media protocol:} Withholding metformin at the time of, or prior to, procedures involving the administration of iodinated contrast media in patients with an eGFR less than 60 mL/min/1.73m$^2$, and restarting only after 48 hours once renal function has been re-evaluated and confirmed stable.
    \item \textbf{Treatment of MALA:} Management in an intensive care unit (ICU) involving intravenous sodium bicarbonate to correct acidosis, supportive cardiovascular therapy, and urgent haemodialysis to clear metformin and lactate from the blood.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The primary complications associated with metformin relate to accumulation of the drug or long-term nutritional deficiencies.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Metformin-associated lactic acidosis (MALA):} A rare but critical complication with a mortality rate of 30\% to 50\%, caused by the accumulation of metformin inhibiting mitochondrial lactate conversion, particularly in patients with acute kidney injury.
    \item \textbf{Severe Vitamin B12 deficiency:} Can lead to megaloblastic anaemia and subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord (neuropathy).
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal intolerance and non-adherence:} Severe, persistent diarrhoea and bloating leading to patient self-discontinuation of therapy, resulting in poor glycaemic control.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for patients using metformin is highly positive. It has a proven track record of reducing microvascular and macrovascular complications, as well as cardiovascular mortality, in patients with type 2 diabetes. When prescribing guidelines are followed and renal function is monitored, the risk of lactic acidosis is extremely low (approximately 3 to 9 cases per 100,000 patient-years). Metformin remains one of the most cost-effective and clinically successful medications in modern medicine.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Patients can optimize their metformin therapy and avoid complications through simple self-management guidelines.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Take with meals:} Always take metformin with or immediately after a meal to minimise stomach upset, nausea, and diarrhoea.
    \item \textbf{Understand "Sick Day" protocols:} Memorising to temporarily stop taking metformin if experiencing severe vomiting, diarrhoea, or inability to drink fluids, and contacting a doctor immediately.
    \item \textbf{Attend regular blood tests:} Ensuring kidney function is checked before starting, and at least once a year during treatment.
    \item \textbf{Report tingling symptoms:} Reporting any new numbness, tingling, or weakness in the extremities to a doctor, and requesting a vitamin B12 test.
    \item \textbf{Limit alcohol consumption:} Avoiding excessive alcohol intake while taking metformin, as alcohol increases the risk of lactic acidosis and hypoglycaemia.
\end{itemize}
