\chapter{Accidents: Falls}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A fall is an event in which a person unintentionally comes to rest on the ground, floor, or another lower level. Falls are the leading cause of injury in older adults and one of the most common causes of childhood injury. They range from harmless slips to serious events that cause hip fractures, head injuries, and loss of independence. Because a substantial proportion of falls can be prevented, they are a major public health priority, particularly for people aged over 65, in whom the physical, social, and psychological consequences are greatest.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Approximately one in three people aged over 65 falls at least once each year, and about half of those who fall will do so again. Falls are the most common cause of injury-related hospital admission and of injury-related death in older adults, and hip fractures account for a large share of the associated morbidity. Falls in children most often affect the under-fives, who fall from furniture, stairs, and play equipment as they explore. In younger adults, falls are frequently work-related or occur from height. Older women fall more often than men, partly because of higher rates of osteoporosis, and the consequences of falls increase with advancing age.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Falls usually result from a combination of risk factors rather than a single cause. In older people these are traditionally divided into intrinsic factors, which lie within the person, and extrinsic factors, which lie in the environment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intrinsic:} age-related loss of muscle strength, balance, and coordination
    \item \textbf{Intrinsic:} poor vision, including cataracts, and outdated spectacle prescriptions
    \item \textbf{Intrinsic:} medical conditions such as stroke, Parkinson's disease, arthritis, dementia, and diabetic foot problems
    \item \textbf{Intrinsic:} medicines that cause drowsiness, postural hypotension, or unsteadiness
    \item \textbf{Extrinsic:} trip hazards such as rugs, cords, and uneven floors; poor lighting; slippery surfaces; stairs; and unsuitable footwear
    \item \textbf{Situational:} climbing on chairs or ladders, carrying heavy loads, or hurrying
\end{itemize}

In children, falls reflect normal curiosity and climbing before judgement and coordination have developed, combined with hazards such as unguarded windows, steep stairs, and unsuitable play equipment. The injury itself results from the impact of the body on the ground: broken bones of the hip, wrist, spine, and skull, bruises, and head injury.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The presentation depends on the site and severity of the injury.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain, swelling, bruising, or tenderness at the site of impact
    \item Inability to stand or bear weight through a leg, suggesting a hip or leg fracture
    \item A deformed or swollen wrist or arm, typical of a wrist fracture
    \item Headache, confusion, drowsiness, or loss of consciousness after hitting the head
    \item Back pain or numbness after a fall, which may indicate spinal injury
    \item Fear of falling, reduced confidence, and avoidance of activity afterwards
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A fall is a symptom as much as an event, and its cause must be distinguished from the injury it produces.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A mechanical slip or trip versus a collapse from a medical cause such as a seizure, syncope, an arrhythmia, a stroke, or hypoglycaemia
    \item A fracture versus a sprain or bruise
    \item Head injury versus a stroke that presented as a fall
    \item In children: an accidental fall versus possible non-accidental injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:} a fall with unconsciousness, suspected head or spinal injury, severe bleeding, or an inability to move.

Otherwise, medical care should be sought after a fall in the following circumstances.

\begin{itemize}
    \item The person cannot bear weight, or a limb looks deformed or is severely painful
    \item A head injury with confusion, vomiting, or a worsening headache, especially in someone taking blood-thinning medicines
    \item The fall occurred during a collapse or blackout
    \item An older person has fallen, even with no obvious injury, so that the cause and fall risk can be assessed
    \item Numbness, tingling, or a pale, cold limb below the injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment has two aims: to diagnose the injury and to identify why the person fell.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} what happened before, during, and after the fall, including whether it was a slip or a collapse, and any symptoms such as palpitations, dizziness, or loss of consciousness
    \item \textbf{Examination:} assessment of the injured area together with a check for other injuries
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} for suspected fractures
    \item \textbf{CT scan of the head:} after head injury with confusion, or when the person takes blood-thinning medicines
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} to check for anaemia, diabetes, thyroid disease, or other contributing causes
    \item \textbf{ECG and heart monitoring:} when an arrhythmia or cardiac collapse is suspected
    \item \textbf{Postural blood pressure:} to detect a drop in blood pressure on standing
    \item \textbf{Falls risk assessment:} tests of balance, gait, muscle strength, and vision, together with a medication review
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management has two parts: treating the injury and preventing the next fall.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Treat the injury:} adequate pain relief, and surgery where needed, such as pinning or replacing a fractured hip
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy to restore strength, balance, and confidence after the injury
    \item \textbf{Medication review:} reducing or stopping medicines that cause dizziness or unsteadiness
    \item \textbf{Vision care:} updated glasses and cataract surgery where appropriate
    \item \textbf{Bone health:} calcium and vitamin D, and specific osteoporosis treatment when diagnosed
    \item \textbf{Home safety:} removing rugs and cords, improving lighting, and installing grab rails and non-slip surfaces
    \item \textbf{Walking aids and footwear:} appropriate aids and well-fitted, non-slip shoes
    \item \textbf{Treat the cause:} managing arrhythmias, postural hypotension, or the medical condition that caused the collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hip fracture, which usually requires surgery and carries significant risk in older people
    \item Head injury and intracranial bleeding, especially in people taking anticoagulant or antiplatelet medicines
    \item Other fractures, including of the wrist, arm, spine, and pelvis
    \item Loss of confidence and fear of falling, which lead to reduced activity and further decline
    \item Prolonged lying on the floor, causing dehydration, pressure injuries, and pneumonia
    \item Loss of independence, including the need for residential care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most falls in children and younger adults cause no serious injury and resolve fully. In older people, outcomes depend mainly on the injuries sustained: those without fracture usually recover with rehabilitation, whereas hip fracture is associated with a significant decline in mobility and independence. Even after a fall with no injury, the risk of falling again is high, but it can be substantially reduced with a targeted prevention programme addressing strength, balance, medicines, vision, and the home environment.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take part in strength and balance exercise programmes, such as tai chi or physiotherapy-led classes
    \item Have vision checked regularly and wear up-to-date glasses
    \item Review medicines with a doctor or pharmacist for anything that causes dizziness or drowsiness
    \item Remove trip hazards, secure rugs, improve lighting, and install grab rails in the bathroom
    \item Wear well-fitted, non-slip footwear around the house
    \item Use a walking stick or frame if advised, and keep it within reach
    \item Eat well, stay hydrated, and keep bones strong with calcium, vitamin D, and weight-bearing exercise
    \item Supervise young children near stairs, windows, and play equipment, and use safety gates and window guards
    \item If you feel unsteady, sit down before you fall and ask for help when needed
\end{itemize}
