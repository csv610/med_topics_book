\chapter{Morning after pill}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The morning after pill is a type of emergency contraception (EC) used to prevent pregnancy after unprotected sexual intercourse, contraceptive failure (such as a broken condom or missed birth control pills), or in cases of sexual assault. Available over-the-counter in many countries, including Australia, it is intended for occasional backup use rather than as a primary method of contraception. The two main oral formulations are levonorgestrel (LNG) and ulipristal acetate (UPA). Both medications work primarily by delaying or inhibiting ovulation, thereby preventing fertilisation, but they do not terminate an established pregnancy and are ineffective once implantation has occurred.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Emergency contraception is widely used globally. In Australia, survey data indicates that approximately 25 to 30\% of reproductive-aged women have used the morning after pill at least once in their lives. Its use is most common among women aged 18 to 25, who may experience higher rates of inconsistent contraceptive use or condom failure. Access to oral emergency contraception has increased substantially since it became available as a pharmacist-only medicine without a prescription, reducing barriers to timely administration, which is critical for efficacy.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The physiological action of the morning after pill depends on the formulation administered and the timing relative to the woman's menstrual cycle.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Levonorgestrel formulation:} A synthetic progestogen that prevents pregnancy by suppressing the luteinising hormone (LH) surge, thereby delaying follicular rupture and preventing ovulation. It must be administered before LH levels begin to rise.
    \item \textbf{Ulipristal acetate formulation:} A selective progesterone receptor modulator (SPRM) that binds to progesterone receptors to inhibit or delay ovulation. It is effective even after the LH surge has started but before LH reaches its peak.
    \item \textbf{Implantation inhibition:} Although historically suggested, current clinical evidence indicates that neither formulation significantly alters the endometrium to prevent the implantation of a fertilised egg, nor do they act as abortifacients.
    \item \textbf{Time-dependent efficacy:} The effectiveness of both agents decreases as the delay between intercourse and administration increases, with levonorgestrel recommended within 72 hours and ulipristal acetate within 120 hours.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation following morning after pill administration is characterised by transient hormonal side effects and subsequent changes in the timing of the next menstrual period.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal symptoms:} Nausea is common, occurring in about 10 to 20\% of users; vomiting occurs less frequently but requires medical review if it happens within 2 to 3 hours of administration.
    \item \textbf{Menstrual cycle alteration:} The next menstrual period may arrive a few days earlier or later than expected, and may be lighter or heavier than normal.
    \item \textbf{Breast and pelvic symptoms:} Transient breast tenderness, lower abdominal cramping, and pelvic discomfort are frequently reported.
    \item \textbf{Systemic signs:} Dizziness, fatigue, and headaches commonly occur within the first 24 hours after taking the medication.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When assessing patients presenting after taking the morning after pill, clinicians must rule out other potential causes of their symptoms or clinical concerns.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Established pregnancy:} Must be considered if the next menstrual period is delayed by more than 7 days, or if there is atypical, persistent spotting.
    \item \textbf{Ectopic pregnancy:} A critical differential diagnosis if the patient presents with severe lower abdominal pain or irregular bleeding, as emergency contraception does not prevent ectopic implantation if fertilisation has already occurred.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID):} Lower abdominal pain and abnormal discharge could indicate an infection rather than drug side effects, particularly if barrier protection was not used.
    \item \textbf{Ovarian cyst rupture or torsion:} Can present with acute pelvic pain shortly after taking the pill, mimicking severe side effects or ectopic pregnancy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should seek medical advice if they experience persistent abdominal pain, severe side effects, or if their next period is delayed by more than a week.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, sudden, or worsening pain in the lower abdomen or pelvic region.
    \item Heavy vaginal bleeding that saturates more than two sanitary pads per hour for consecutive hours.
    \item Dizziness, fainting, or signs of severe circulatory shock.
    \item Shortness of breath or sudden chest pain.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnostic evaluation is focused on determining the risk of pregnancy, excluding existing pregnancy, and assessing the need for further medical care.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detailed menstrual history:} Assessing the date of the last menstrual period, cycle length, and the exact timing and number of unprotected intercourse episodes.
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy testing:} A urine or serum beta-hCG test should be performed if the patient's next period is delayed by more than 7 days, or if they present with symptoms of pregnancy.
    \item \textbf{Sexually transmitted infection (STI) screen:} Highly recommended, as unprotected intercourse carries a risk of STI transmission; screening is typically performed 2 weeks post-exposure.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic ultrasound:} Indicated if pregnancy is suspected and the patient has lower abdominal pain, to exclude or locate a pregnancy (e.g., ectopic pregnancy).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The clinical management of a patient requesting or having taken emergency contraception involves selection of the appropriate agent, correct administration, and counselling.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Selection of emergency contraception:} Levonorgestrel is licensed for use up to 72 hours (3 days) after intercourse, whereas ulipristal acetate is licensed for use up to 120 hours (5 days) and has higher efficacy.
    \item \textbf{Vomiting protocol:} If vomiting occurs within 2 hours of taking levonorgestrel, or within 3 hours of taking ulipristal acetate, the dose must be repeated immediately or a copper intrauterine device (IUD) inserted.
    \item \textbf{Intrauterine device (IUD) alternative:} The insertion of a copper IUD within 120 hours of unprotected intercourse is the most effective form of emergency contraception (greater than 99\% efficacy) and should be offered to all patients.
    \item \textbf{Drug interactions:} Concomitant use of liver enzyme-inducing drugs (e.g., carbamazepine, phenytoin, rifampicin, or St John's wort) reduces the efficacy of oral emergency contraception; a double dose of levonorgestrel (3 mg) or a copper IUD is recommended in these patients.
    \item \textbf{Ongoing contraception counselling:} Oral emergency contraception does not provide ongoing protection; patients must resume their regular contraceptive method or start a new one immediately, utilising barrier methods for at least 7 days.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Contraceptive failure:} Resulting in an unplanned pregnancy, particularly if the pill was taken after ovulation had already occurred.
    \item \textbf{Delayed diagnosis of ectopic pregnancy:} If symptoms of ectopic pregnancy are mistaken for normal side effects of the medication.
    \item \textbf{Menstrual irregularity:} Persistent disruption of the menstrual cycle, leading to anxiety and unnecessary pregnancy testing.
    \item \textbf{STI transmission:} Unprotected intercourse without barrier methods increases the risk of acquiring infections such as chlamydia, gonorrhoea, or HIV.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for preventing pregnancy with the morning after pill is highly favourable, especially when taken as soon as possible after unprotected sex. Levonorgestrel reduces the risk of pregnancy by approximately 85\% when taken within 72 hours, while ulipristal acetate reduces the risk by approximately 95\% when taken within 120 hours. There are no long-term adverse health outcomes, and the medication has no effect on future fertility. Regular menstrual cycles typically resume within one month of administration.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rapid access:} Obtaining the morning after pill as soon as possible, as early administration significantly increases effectiveness.
    \item \textbf{Barrier protection usage:} Consistent use of condoms to prevent both unplanned pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections.
    \item \textbf{Adherence to regular birth control:} Understanding how to correctly use daily or long-acting reversible contraception (LARC) to avoid the need for emergency contraception.
    \item \textbf{Menstrual tracking:} Noting the date of the next period to monitor for delays and ensure the emergency contraception was successful.
    \item \textbf{Post-treatment observation:} Resting and monitoring for side effects, such as severe nausea or pelvic pain, in the days following administration.
\end{itemize}
