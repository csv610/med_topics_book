\section*{Definition and Overview}
Trachoma is a contagious eye infection caused by particular strains of \textit{Chlamydia trachomatis}. Repeated infection can scar the inside of the eyelid. The lid may then turn inward and eyelashes can rub against the cornea, causing pain, corneal damage, and permanent loss of vision. Trachoma remains a public-health problem in some communities where crowding, limited water, poor sanitation, and difficulty accessing care allow transmission to continue.

The World Health Organization's SAFE strategy combines Surgery for trichiasis, Antibiotics, Facial cleanliness, and Environmental improvement. Elimination requires sustained, community-led work rather than isolated treatment. This chapter gives general information and does not replace an eye examination.

\section*{How infection spreads}
Infection may spread through contact with eye or nose discharge, contaminated hands, towels, or bedding, and possibly by eye-seeking flies. Repeated infections during childhood are most likely to cause the scarring that leads to blindness years later. Clean faces, hand hygiene, adequate water, sanitation, and reduced household crowding help interrupt transmission.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Red, irritated, watery, or sticky eyes, especially in children
    \item Follicles or inflammation on the inside of the upper eyelid
    \item White scars on the inner eyelid after repeated infection
    \item In-turned eyelid or eyelashes rubbing on the eye (trichiasis)
    \item Light sensitivity, a gritty sensation, pain, tearing, or reduced vision
    \item Corneal clouding or permanent visual loss in advanced disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of red eyes include viral or bacterial conjunctivitis, allergy, blepharitis, corneal injury, sexually transmitted eye infection, and inflammatory eye disease. A clinician may need to examine the eyelid and cornea to distinguish these conditions.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical review for persistent or recurrent red or sticky eyes, eyelashes touching the eye, eye pain, light sensitivity, or any change in vision. In an area where trachoma is known to occur, attend community screening or treatment programmes when invited.

\textbf{Contact local emergency services urgently for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe eye pain or sudden loss of vision
    \item A chemical splash, penetrating injury, or significant blow to the eye
    \item Severe swelling around the eye with fever
    \item Severe eye discharge in a newborn
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is usually clinical. A trained health worker examines the eyelids, conjunctiva, cornea, and visual acuity. Community programmes may use standardised grading and prevalence surveys to decide whether antibiotics are needed for a population. Laboratory testing can be useful when the diagnosis is uncertain or another infection is suspected. People with trichiasis need assessment for eyelid surgery.

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} prescribed or distributed according to local and programme guidelines; do not self-treat with leftover antibiotics
    \item \textbf{Trichiasis surgery:} turns the eyelid away from the cornea and protects sight
    \item \textbf{Temporary lash removal:} may provide short-term relief if surgery is delayed, but lashes can regrow
    \item \textbf{Facial cleanliness:} gently clean discharge and avoid sharing towels or cloths
    \item \textbf{Environmental improvement:} support reliable water, sanitation, waste management, and housing measures
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} recurrent trichiasis and corneal damage require ongoing eye care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications and Outlook}
Active infection may resolve, but repeated infection can leave permanent scarring. Trichiasis causes chronic pain and corneal injury; untreated corneal damage can lead to irreversible blindness. Early treatment and surgery protect remaining vision. Elimination is possible when communities, health services, and water and sanitation programmes sustain the SAFE strategy.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep children's faces clean and wipe away eye and nose discharge safely
    \item Wash hands after touching an affected eye and before touching another person's face
    \item Avoid sharing towels, face cloths, or bedding during active infection
    \item Use screening, antibiotics, and surgery offered through local programmes
    \item Seek early care if an eyelash turns inward or touches the eye
\end{itemize}
