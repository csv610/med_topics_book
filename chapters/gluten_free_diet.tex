\chapter{Gluten-Free Diet}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A gluten-free diet is a way of eating that completely excludes gluten, a protein found in wheat, barley, rye, and products made from them. It is the essential medical treatment for coeliac disease, an autoimmune condition in which gluten damages the lining of the small intestine. A gluten-free diet is also needed for people with wheat allergy and some with gluten sensitivity. Following the diet strictly requires reading food labels carefully, because gluten is hidden in many processed foods, sauces, and seasonings. With education and support, a gluten-free diet is safe, nutritious, and allows people with coeliac disease to live healthy, symptom-free lives.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Coeliac disease affects around 1 in 70 Australians, though many remain undiagnosed. Alongside them, growing numbers of people without coeliac disease choose gluten-free products, making it one of the fastest-growing dietary movements in developed countries. Around 10--15\% of the Australian population reports some sensitivity to gluten or wheat, and the gluten-free food industry is worth billions of dollars. Coeliac disease can develop at any age and runs in families, with around 1 in 10 first-degree relatives affected.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The gluten-free diet is based on the mechanism by which gluten causes disease in susceptible people.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gluten:} a storage protein in wheat, barley, and rye that gives dough its elasticity; it is also found in oats contaminated with wheat during processing
    \item \textbf{Coeliac disease:} in genetically susceptible people, gluten triggers an autoimmune reaction that damages the villi (finger-like projections) of the small intestine, causing malabsorption
    \item \textbf{Wheat allergy:} an allergic reaction to wheat proteins, which can cause hives, asthma, or anaphylaxis
    \item \textbf{Non-coeliac gluten sensitivity:} symptoms such as bloating and fatigue that improve on a gluten-free diet without the intestinal damage or allergy of coeliac disease
    \item \textbf{Removal of gluten:} excluding gluten allows the intestinal lining to heal in coeliac disease and stops symptoms in sensitivity
    \item \textbf{Strictness matters:} even small amounts of gluten, including cross-contamination, can cause harm in coeliac disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The gluten-free diet is appropriate for people with conditions that require it, which present with:

\begin{itemize}
    \item In coeliac disease: diarrhoea, bloating, abdominal pain, weight loss, fatigue, anaemia, and mouth ulcers; in children, poor growth and failure to thrive
    \item Skin manifestations: dermatitis herpetiformis, an intensely itchy blistering rash
    \item In wheat allergy: immediate reactions such as hives, swelling, wheezing, or anaphylaxis after eating wheat
    \item In gluten sensitivity: bloating, wind, abdominal pain, brain fog, and fatigue that improve when gluten is removed
    \item Many people with coeliac disease have mild or atypical symptoms, or none at all
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before adopting a gluten-free diet, the underlying condition should be correctly identified.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Coeliac disease, confirmed by blood tests and intestinal biopsy
    \item Wheat allergy, confirmed by allergy testing
    \item Non-coeliac gluten sensitivity, diagnosed when the other two are excluded
    \item Irritable bowel syndrome, which can cause similar symptoms
    \item Inflammatory bowel disease
    \item Other causes of malabsorption, including bacterial overgrowth and pancreatic insufficiency
    \item Anaemia and fatigue from other causes
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone considering a gluten-free diet for symptoms should first see a doctor, because testing for coeliac disease must be done while still eating gluten. People diagnosed with coeliac disease should be referred to a dietitian for education and follow-up.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of anaphylaxis after eating wheat: difficulty breathing, swelling of the face or throat, or collapse, which requires urgent treatment with adrenaline
    \item Severe dehydration from persistent diarrhoea
    \item Severe abdominal pain or signs of intestinal obstruction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The gluten-free diet is prescribed after a formal diagnosis, not before.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coeliac blood tests:} tissue transglutaminase (tTG) and other antibody tests, which are accurate only when gluten is being eaten
    \item \textbf{Small intestinal biopsy:} endoscopy with biopsy confirms coeliac disease
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} HLA-DQ2/DQ8 testing can help exclude coeliac disease
    \item \textbf{Allergy testing:} skin prick or blood tests for wheat allergy
    \item \textbf{Exclusion trial:} for gluten sensitivity, a supervised trial of gluten removal and reintroduction
    \item \textbf{Food diary:} helps identify trigger foods
    \item \textbf{Dietitian assessment:} ensures the diet is nutritionally adequate and truly gluten-free
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
A gluten-free diet is the only treatment for coeliac disease and is managed with education and support.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Complete avoidance:} all foods and drinks containing wheat, barley, and rye must be excluded, along with contaminated oats
    \item \textbf{Label reading:} learning to identify gluten in ingredients lists and to look for the "gluten-free" symbol
    \item \textbf{Safe alternatives:} rice, corn, potato, quinoa, buckwheat, millet, and certified gluten-free products
    \item \textbf{Avoiding cross-contamination:} using separate toasters, utensils, and preparation areas; watching for shared fryers in restaurants
    \item \textbf{Dietitian support:} education on eating out, travelling, and reading labels
    \item \textbf{Nutritional monitoring:} checking for and correcting deficiencies of iron, B12, folate, calcium, and vitamin D
    \item \textbf{Follow-up testing:} repeat antibody tests and, where needed, repeat biopsy to confirm healing
    \item \textbf{Ongoing review:} regular monitoring for complications and for adherence to the diet
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item In untreated coeliac disease: malnutrition, anaemia, osteoporosis, infertility, and an increased risk of intestinal lymphoma
    \item Accidental gluten exposure causing symptoms and intestinal damage even in those on a strict diet
    \item Nutritional deficiencies if the gluten-free diet replaces cereals with low-nutrient foods
    \item Higher cost and limited availability of gluten-free products
    \item Social difficulties with eating out and travel
    \item The psychological burden of a lifelong strict diet
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With a strict gluten-free diet, the intestinal lining heals in most people within six to twelve months, and symptoms resolve. Many people feel significantly better within weeks of starting the diet. Long-term complications are largely prevented by adherence to the diet, and life expectancy for people with well-managed coeliac disease approaches that of the general population. Children who start the diet early usually catch up on growth and development. The main challenge is maintaining strictness, which is why ongoing dietitian support is important.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Get a formal diagnosis before starting a gluten-free diet
    \item Work with an accredited practising dietitian to plan a balanced diet
    \item Learn to read ingredient labels and recognise hidden sources of gluten
    \item Choose naturally gluten-free whole foods, including fruits, vegetables, meat, fish, eggs, and legumes
    \item Check that oats, if eaten, are certified gluten-free
    \item Prevent cross-contamination at home with separate equipment and storage
    \item Carry safe snacks when travelling or eating out
    \item Have regular follow-up with your doctor, including blood tests and bone density checks as advised
    \item Connect with support groups such as Coeliac Australia for resources and community
\end{itemize}
