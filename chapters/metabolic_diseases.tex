\chapter{Metabolic diseases}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Metabolic diseases, or metabolic disorders, comprise a diverse group of clinical conditions characterised by a disruption in the normal biochemical processes that convert nutrients from food into energy at the cellular level. These disorders interfere with the synthesis, breakdown, or transport of proteins, carbohydrates, lipids, or nucleic acids. Metabolic diseases are broadly divided into two categories: acquired metabolic disorders, which are highly prevalent systemic conditions linked to lifestyle, obesity, and ageing (such as metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidaemia), and inherited metabolic diseases (inborn errors of metabolism), which are rare genetic disorders caused by monogenic defects in specific enzymes or transport proteins. Together, metabolic diseases represent a major global health burden, acting as primary drivers of cardiovascular disease, kidney failure, and neurodevelopmental disabilities.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of metabolic diseases is marked by a stark contrast between acquired and inherited forms. Acquired metabolic disorders have reached epidemic proportions globally. In Australia, metabolic syndrome affects approximately 20\% to 30\% of the adult population, and type 2 diabetes mellitus affects more than 1.3 million Australians, with incidence rates rising in parallel with increasing rates of obesity. Conversely, individual inherited metabolic diseases, such as phenylketonuria (PKU), galactosemia, and urea cycle disorders, are rare. However, when considered collectively, inborn errors of metabolism affect approximately 1 in 800 to 1 in 2,500 live births. Acquired metabolic diseases are strongly associated with advancing age, lower socioeconomic status, and specific ethnic backgrounds (including Indigenous Australian, South Asian, and Middle Eastern populations).

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The underlying causes and physiological mechanisms of metabolic disease depend on whether the condition is genetic or acquired.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic mutations (Inherited forms):} Inborn errors of metabolism are caused by inherited genetic mutations, typically autosomal recessive, that lead to a partial or complete lack of a specific enzyme or cofactor. This enzymatic block leads to the toxic accumulation of upstream substrates, or a critical deficiency of downstream metabolic end products.
    \item \textbf{Insulin resistance (Acquired forms):} The primary pathophysiological driver of acquired metabolic diseases. Excess visceral adiposity leads to chronic, low-grade systemic inflammation and elevated circulating free fatty acids. These inflammatory mediators impair insulin receptor signalling pathways in skeletal muscle, adipose tissue, and the liver, resulting in decreased glucose uptake and increased hepatic glucose output.
    \item \textbf{Mitochondrial dysfunction:} Impaired mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation reduces the cell's ability to metabolise fatty acids, leading to lipotoxicity, cellular stress, and progressive metabolic dysregulation.
    \text{Epigenetic programming:} In-utero exposure to maternal obesity, gestational diabetes, or malnutrition can alter gene expression in the foetus, predisposing the offspring to metabolic diseases in adulthood.
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle determinants:} Chronically high caloric intake (particularly from refined carbohydrates and saturated fats) combined with physical inactivity leads to fat accumulation in visceral organs (such as the liver and pancreas), impairing their endocrine functions.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical manifestations of metabolic diseases differ significantly between adults with acquired disorders and infants with inherited defects.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acquired metabolic features (Metabolic Syndrome and Diabetes):}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Central obesity, characterised by an increased waist circumference (typically $\ge$94 cm for men and $\ge$80 cm for women of Europid descent).
            \item Acanthosis nigricans: Hyper-pigmented, velvety skin patches in body folds (such as the neck and axilla), indicating severe systemic insulin resistance.
            \text{Classic hyperglycaemic symptoms:} Polyuria (frequent urination), polydipsia (excessive thirst), fatigue, and unexplained weight loss.
            \item Asymptomatic hypertension and elevated fasting triglycerides, which are core diagnostic criteria of metabolic syndrome.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inherited metabolic features (Inborn Errors of Metabolism):}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Acute metabolic crisis in newborns: Presenting as severe lethargy, poor feeding, persistent vomiting, hypotonia (floppiness), hypothermia, or seizures shortly after birth.
            \item Failure to thrive and progressive developmental delay in infants.
            \text{Unusual body or urine odour:} A musty or mousy odour in phenylketonuria (PKU), or a sweet, maple syrup-like odour in maple syrup urine disease (MSUD).
            \item Organomegaly: Enlargement of the liver (hepatomegaly) or spleen (splenomegaly), common in lysosomal storage diseases.
        \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because metabolic diseases present with broad, systemic symptoms, they must be distinguished from other medical conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary endocrine disorders:} Hypothyroidism or Cushing's syndrome, both of which can cause weight gain, fatigue, lipid abnormalities, and hypertension, mimicking metabolic syndrome.
    \item \textbf{Chronic fatigue syndrome:} Presenting with profound, persistent exhaustion, but lacking the biochemical abnormalities (such as impaired fasting glucose or dyslipidaemia) characteristic of metabolic disease.
    \item \textbf{Genetic obesity syndromes:} Monogenic obesity disorders, such as Prader-Willi syndrome, which present with early-onset hyperphagia and severe obesity.
    \item \textbf{Toxic ingestions:} Accidental ingestion of drugs, toxins, or alcohol in infants, which can present with encephalopathy and metabolic acidosis mimicking an acute inborn error of metabolism.
    \item \textbf{Sepsis:} Neonatal sepsis presents with lethargy, poor feeding, and respiratory distress, overlapping clinical signs with an acute metabolic crisis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Adults should seek medical advice if they notice persistent fatigue, unexplained weight changes, slow-healing skin wounds, or excessive thirst. Parents must seek immediate paediatric assessment if an infant exhibits progressive developmental delay, failure to thrive, or an inability to feed.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) or hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS) symptoms, including deep rapid breathing, confusion, abdominal pain, a sweet or fruity breath odour, and loss of consciousness.
    \item An infant presenting with sudden onset of severe lethargy, persistent vomiting, floppy muscles, breathing difficulties, or seizures.
    \item Symptoms of acute cardiovascular events, such as crushing chest pain or sudden weakness on one side of the body, which are common complications of underlying metabolic disease.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnostic pathways involve routine biochemical screens for acquired conditions, and specialised genetic or enzymatic assays for inherited disorders.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Newborn screening programme (Heck prick test):} Performed on all infants in Australia between 48 and 72 hours of life to detect major inherited metabolic disorders, including PKU, galactosemia, and congenital adrenal hyperplasia.
    \item \textbf{Glycaemic biomarkers:} Fasting plasma glucose and Glycated Haemoglobin (HbA1c) are used to screen for and diagnose prediabetes and type 2 diabetes.
    \item \textbf{Lipid sub-fractions:} Measuring fasting total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, and triglycerides to diagnose dyslipidaemia.
    \item \textbf{Blood gas and electrolyte panel:} Crucial in acute presentations to identify metabolic acidosis with an elevated anion gap, common in organic acidaemias and diabetic ketoacidosis.
    \item \textbf{Ammonia and lactate levels:} Elevated plasma ammonia is diagnostic of urea cycle defects, while elevated lactate suggests mitochondrial or respiratory chain disorders.
    \item \textbf{Molecular genetic testing:} Gene sequencing is utilised to identify specific genetic mutations confirming inherited metabolic diseases.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management strategies combine strict lifestyle modifications with pharmacological interventions and, for genetic diseases, specialized metabolic therapies.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary interventions:}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{For acquired disorders:} Adoption of a nutrient-dense diet (such as the Mediterranean diet) emphasizing whole grains, lean proteins, and unsaturated fats, while restricting simple sugars and trans fats to achieve weight loss.
            \item \textbf{For inherited disorders:} Strict restriction of dietary substrates that the body cannot metabolise. For example, a lifelong low-phenylalanine diet supplemented with medical amino acid formulas is required for phenylketonuria.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pharmacotherapy for acquired metabolic diseases:}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Insulin sensitizers:} Metformin is the first-line medication for type 2 diabetes.
            \item \textbf{Advanced diabetic agents:} GLP-1 receptor agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors to improve glycaemic control, promote weight loss, and provide cardiovascular protection.
            \item \textbf{Lipid-lowering therapies:} Statins and fibrates to target elevated LDL cholesterol and triglycerides.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Enzyme Replacement Therapy (ERT):} Intravenous administration of recombinant enzymes to treat specific lysosomal storage disorders (such as Gaucher disease or Fabry disease).
    \item \textbf{Physical exercise:} Engaging in at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise combined with progressive resistance training weekly to improve skeletal muscle insulin sensitivity.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated or poorly managed metabolic diseases lead to progressive, multi-system damage.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Macrovascular disease: Accelerated atherosclerosis leading to coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, peripheral arterial disease, and ischaemic stroke.
    \item Microvascular disease (in diabetes): Diabetic retinopathy leading to blindness, diabetic nephropathy progressing to end-stage renal failure, and diabetic neuropathy.
    \item Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), which can progress to liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma.
    \text{Irreversible neurodevelopmental damage:} Severe intellectual disability, spasticity, and microcephaly in children with untreated inborn errors of metabolism.
    \item Sleep apnoea and osteoarthritis, secondary to severe obesity associated with metabolic syndrome.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for acquired metabolic diseases is highly dependent on patient compliance with lifestyle and pharmacological therapies. Early intervention can reverse prediabetes and metabolic syndrome, preventing vascular complications and preserving normal life expectancy. For inherited metabolic diseases, the prognosis has dramatically improved with the advent of universal newborn screening, allowing treatment to begin before irreversible organ or brain damage occurs. However, severe genetic phenotypes or cases diagnosed late carry a guarded prognosis, often resulting in permanent cognitive or physical disability.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventing acquired metabolic diseases relies on maintaining energy balance and metabolic fitness.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Consume a balanced diet rich in dietary fibre, vegetables, and complex carbohydrates, while avoiding highly processed foods and sugar-sweetened beverages.
    \item Maintain a consistent routine of physical activity, combining cardiovascular exercise with muscle-strengthening activities.
    \item Monitor health parameters such as blood pressure, waist circumference, and body weight regularly.
    \item Limit sedentary screen time and ensure adequate, restorative sleep (7 to 8 hours per night for adults).
    \text{Adhere to screening protocols:} Ensure children undergo newborn screening, and adults undergo regular cardiovascular and metabolic health checks from age 40 (or younger for high-risk groups).
\end{itemize}
