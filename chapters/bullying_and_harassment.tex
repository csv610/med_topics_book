\chapter{Bullying and Harassment}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bullying is repeated, intentional harmful behaviour directed at a person who typically has less power or ability to defend themselves. It may be physical, verbal, or social (such as exclusion or spreading rumours), and it may be carried out online or through phones as cyberbullying. Harassment is a closely related term, usually defined as unwanted behaviour that offends, humiliates, or intimidates a person, often based on personal characteristics such as sex, race, disability, or sexual orientation. Bullying and harassment occur among children at school, among adults in the workplace, and within families and other settings. Although they are social and behavioural problems rather than diseases, they have well-recognised physical and psychological health consequences, which is why they belong in a health reference. In Australia, workplace bullying and harassment are also governed by occupational health and safety and anti-discrimination laws, and employers have legal duties to prevent them.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bullying is common. Around one in four or five school students reports being bullied at some point, and rates are similar or higher for cyberbullying, which is more prevalent among older children and teenagers. In the workplace, roughly one in ten adults may experience bullying during their working life, with higher rates reported in certain occupations such as health care, teaching, and hospitality. Bullying affects people of any age or sex, but boys are more often involved in physical bullying, while girls report social and cyberbullying more frequently. Children who are perceived as different, who have disabilities, or who belong to minority groups are at increased risk of being targeted.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The causes of bullying are complex and involve individual, family, school, and community factors.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Individual factors:} both those who bully and those who are bullied may have particular temperaments, low self-esteem, or underdeveloped social skills
    \item \textbf{Power imbalance:} bullying relies on a real or perceived imbalance of power, whether from size, strength, popularity, or position of authority
    \item \textbf{Family and school environment:} harsh or inconsistent parenting, exposure to aggression at home, and poorly supervised or highly competitive settings all contribute
    \item \textbf{Cultural and social norms:} environments that tolerate or reward aggressive behaviour make bullying more likely
    \item \textbf{Online factors:} anonymity, a large audience, and the round-the-clock reach of digital devices intensify cyberbullying
\end{itemize}

The health effects are largely mediated through chronic stress. Sustained threat and humiliation repeatedly activate the body's stress response, disturbing sleep, appetite, mood, and immune function. Over time this is associated with anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress, and physical symptoms such as headaches, abdominal pain, and fatigue. Effects on the developing brain and on self-concept in childhood can persist into adult life.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Behavioural signs in children:} reluctance to attend school, falling grades, lost belongings or unexplained injuries, and changes in friendships
    \item \textbf{Emotional symptoms:} low mood, irritability, tearfulness, anxiety, fear of particular places or people, and loss of confidence
    \item \textbf{Physical symptoms:} headaches, stomach aches, poor sleep, nightmares, and reduced appetite
    \item \textbf{Workplace presentations:} absenteeism, falling performance, insomnia, and symptoms of anxiety or depression
    \item \textbf{Warning signs of severe distress:} social withdrawal, self-harm, and, in severe cases, thoughts of suicide
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal peer conflict:} a single unpleasant incident that is not repeated or power-imbalanced
    \item \textbf{General stress:} family, school, or workplace pressures unrelated to bullying
    \item \textbf{Mental health conditions:} depression, anxiety disorders, or adjustment disorder occurring independently
    \item \textbf{Neurodevelopmental differences:} attention deficit hyperactivity disorder or autism spectrum traits that affect social interactions
    \item \textbf{Medical causes of physical symptoms:} conditions such as migraine or functional abdominal pain that coincide with the bullying
    \item \textbf{Other stressors:} relationship difficulties, discrimination, or family violence as separate or coexisting problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if a child or adult shows persistent distress, sleep disturbance, or physical symptoms that are affecting daily life, and especially if there are signs of depression or anxiety. Schools and workplaces should be informed where bullying is occurring, and workplace issues can be raised with managers, human resources, or unions.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) or seek emergency help if there is:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Any suggestion of self-harm or suicide, or threats of violence
    \item Severe distress or agitation, or an inability to keep someone safe
    \item Physical injury requiring medical attention
\end{itemize}

In Australia, support is also available from Lifeline (13 11 14) and the Kids Helpline (1800 55 1800).

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
There is no medical test for bullying. The assessment rests on taking a careful history from the person affected and, where appropriate, from parents or teachers.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the nature, frequency, and duration of the behaviour; who is involved; where it occurs; and any online component
    \item \textbf{Assessment of impact:} mood, sleep, appetite, school or work functioning, and social withdrawal
    \item \textbf{Screening for mental health problems:} validated questionnaires for anxiety and depression may be used
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} usually normal, but helpful to exclude physical causes of the reported symptoms
    \item \textbf{Information from others:} with consent, input from teachers, school counsellors, or workplace contacts can clarify what is happening
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stopping the behaviour:} report the bullying to the school or workplace, or to the police where conduct is criminal, and document incidents with written records
    \item \textbf{School-based approaches:} anti-bullying policies, supervision of problem areas, and counselling for all involved
    \item \textbf{Workplace approaches:} raising the matter with management or human resources, using formal complaint processes, and accessing employee assistance programs
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} counselling and cognitive behaviour therapy can help with anxiety, low mood, and rebuilding confidence
    \item \textbf{Building resilience and social skills:} assertiveness training, supportive friendships, and structured activities
    \item \textbf{Medical care:} treatment of depression or anxiety if these develop, and review of any physical symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Depression, anxiety disorders, and post-traumatic stress
    \item Self-harm and, in severe cases, suicidal thoughts and behaviour
    \item Chronic physical symptoms such as headaches, abdominal pain, and sleep disturbance
    \item Falling academic performance in children and young people
    \item Job loss and financial difficulty in adults affected by workplace bullying
    \item Social withdrawal and lasting damage to self-esteem
    \item Long-term effects on physical health linked to chronic stress in adulthood
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Many people who are bullied recover well, especially when the bullying is stopped early and good support is provided. Children who experience chronic bullying have higher rates of anxiety and depression that can persist into adulthood, although many show considerable resilience. Recovery depends on the severity and duration of the bullying, the response of schools or workplaces, and the support available at home. People with persisting mental health effects often improve substantially with counselling and treatment.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Schools and workplaces should have clear, well-enforced anti-bullying policies and promote a respectful culture
    \item Teach children about respectful relationships, online safety, and how to report bullying
    \item Encourage open communication so children and colleagues feel able to speak up early
    \item Limit screen time and supervise children's online activity to reduce exposure to cyberbullying
    \item Seek help early from a teacher, school counsellor, manager, or employee assistance program
    \item Maintain friendships, physical activity, sleep, and stress-management routines to protect well-being
    \item Keep a record of incidents, which supports any formal complaint or health assessment
\end{itemize}
