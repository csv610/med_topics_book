\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cholera is an acute infection of the bowel caused by the bacterium \textit{Vibrio cholerae}. It is spread through contaminated water and food and causes sudden, profuse, watery diarrhoea that can lead to severe dehydration within hours. In its most severe form, cholera is rapidly fatal if untreated, but it is easily treated with prompt fluid replacement. The infection is rare in many countries and other countries with safe water supplies, but it remains a major cause of illness and death in places where sanitation is poor, and it is a risk for travellers to affected areas.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cholera is a disease of poverty and poor sanitation. It is endemic in parts of Africa, South and Southeast Asia, and the Middle East, and outbreaks occur wherever clean water and sanitation are disrupted, such as after natural disasters, conflict, or displacement. It is estimated that millions of cases occur worldwide each year, though many are mild or unreported. Death rates are highest where treatment is unavailable. In developed countries cholera is almost always caught while travelling, and household spread is uncommon because hygiene and sewage systems are good. Young children and people with reduced stomach acid are more vulnerable to severe disease.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Cholera is caused by infection with the bacterium \textit{Vibrio cholerae}, most often the strains that produce a specific toxin.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} faecal-oral spread through contaminated drinking water, food washed in contaminated water, and shellfish from polluted waters
    \item \textbf{Ingestion:} the bacteria are swallowed and must survive the acid of the stomach to reach the bowel
    \item \textbf{Cholera toxin:} the toxin binds to the lining of the small intestine and makes cells pour large amounts of water and salts into the bowel
    \item \textbf{Secretory diarrhoea:} the result is the classic rapid loss of large volumes of watery fluid
    \item \textbf{Low stomach acid:} people with reduced stomach acid (older age, antacid use, malnutrition) are more easily infected
    \item \textbf{Carriers:} some people excrete the bacteria without being ill and can unknowingly spread it
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Only about 1 in 10 people infected develop severe disease; many have mild diarrhoea or no symptoms at all. When symptoms appear, they can be dramatic.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sudden onset of watery diarrhoea} that looks pale and cloudy, often described as "rice-water" stools
    \item \textbf{Very large volumes of stool} -- litres per day in severe cases
    \item \textbf{Vomiting} without much nausea beforehand
    \item \textbf{Severe dehydration:} intense thirst, dry mouth, sunken eyes, and reduced skin elasticity
    \item \textbf{Muscle cramps:} caused by loss of salts such as potassium
    \item \textbf{Faintness, low blood pressure, and rapid heart rate}
    \item \textbf{Shock and reduced or absent urine output} in the most severe cases
    \item \textbf{Usually no fever} -- temperature is often normal or low
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Watery diarrhoea has many causes, and cholera is only one of them.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Other bacterial gut infections: travellers' diarrhoea (\textit{E.\ coli}), salmonella, shigella, and campylobacter
    \item Viral gastroenteritis (rotavirus, norovirus)
    \item Protozoal infections such as giardiasis and cryptosporidiosis
    \item Food poisoning from toxins in spoiled food
    \item Inflammatory bowel disease presenting with diarrhoea
    \item Medication-related diarrhoea (e.g.\ from antibiotics or laxatives)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with profuse watery diarrhoea, especially after travel to an affected area, needs prompt medical assessment because dehydration can develop within hours.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services or attend an emergency department for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of severe dehydration: extreme thirst, dry mouth, sunken eyes, or passing little or no urine
    \item Dizziness on standing, faintness, or confusion
    \item Weakness or reduced responsiveness
    \item Blood in the stool
    \item Inability to keep fluids down
    \item A child or older person who becomes lethargic or listless
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis is confirmed in the laboratory, but treatment for dehydration should begin immediately without waiting for results.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} recent travel history and the characteristic profuse watery diarrhoea
    \item \textbf{Stool culture:} growing \textit{Vibrio cholerae} from a fresh stool sample confirms the diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Rapid tests:} dipstick tests exist and are used during outbreaks for quick screening
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} to check salt levels and kidney function in severely dehydrated people
    \item \textbf{Reporting:} confirmed cases are reported to public health authorities so outbreaks can be contained
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The cornerstone of treatment is replacing the fluid and salts that are being lost -- more than any medicine, this is what saves lives.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Oral rehydration solution (ORS):} a balanced mix of water, sugar, and salts, taken in small frequent amounts; the mainstay of treatment
    \item \textbf{Intravenous fluids:} for people who cannot drink enough, are vomiting persistently, or are in shock
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} shorten the illness and reduce fluid loss in severe cases (e.g.\ doxycycline or azithromycin)
    \item \textbf{Zinc supplements:} reduce the severity and duration of diarrhoea in children
    \item \textbf{Continue feeding:} eating (and breastfeeding in infants) is encouraged as soon as vomiting settles
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} for severe dehydration, shock, or people who cannot manage ORS at home
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The complications of cholera stem chiefly from the loss of fluid and salts.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Severe dehydration} and hypovolaemic shock
    \item \textbf{Kidney failure:} from reduced blood flow to the kidneys
    \item \textbf{Low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia):} especially in children
    \item \textbf{Low potassium:} causing muscle weakness, cramps, and abnormal heart rhythms
    \item \textbf{Coma or seizures:} from very low salt levels or low blood sugar
    \item \textbf{Death:} rapid, sometimes within hours, in untreated severe cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt rehydration, the outlook for cholera is excellent: fewer than 1 in 100 treated people die, and most recover within a few days. Without treatment, severe cases can be fatal within hours because of dehydration and shock, which is why early fluid replacement is so important. Once the infection is over, the bowel lining repairs quickly, and recovery is usually complete without long-term problems. Outbreaks are controlled by improving water, sanitation, and hygiene and, where appropriate, by vaccination.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Drink only boiled, bottled, or treated water when travelling in affected areas
    \item Eat only thoroughly cooked, hot food; avoid raw or unpeeled produce and ice made from untreated water
    \item Wash hands thoroughly with soap and water before eating and after using the toilet
    \item Use safe sanitation and dispose of faeces hygienically, especially during outbreaks
    \item Consider the oral cholera vaccine before travelling to areas with active transmission (seek advice from a travel clinic)
    \item During an outbreak, report cases early and treat with oral rehydration immediately
    \item Breastfed infants should continue breastfeeding, which protects against infection
\end{itemize}
