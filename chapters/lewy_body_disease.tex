\chapter{Lewy body disease}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Lewy body disease (LBD) is a progressive, neurodegenerative disorder characterised by the abnormal accumulation of alpha-synuclein protein aggregates --- known as Lewy bodies --- within neurons. LBD is an umbrella term that encompasses two closely related clinical entities: Dementia with Lewy Bodies (DLB) and Parkinson's Disease Dementia (PDD). The disease is clinically distinguished by a triad of symptoms: fluctuating cognitive impairment, recurrent visual hallucinations, and spontaneous features of parkinsonism. Additionally, patients frequently experience severe sleep disturbances and autonomic dysfunction. LBD significantly compromises cognitive, motor, and behavioural functions, leading to progressive disability and presenting a profound challenge to patients, families, and healthcare systems.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Lewy body disease is the second most common form of degenerative dementia in older adults, surpassed only by Alzheimer's disease. It accounts for approximately 10--15\% of all pathologically confirmed dementia cases. The disease primarily affects individuals over the age of 65, with the incidence rising steeply with advancing age. LBD is slightly more common in men than in women. In Australia, it is estimated that tens of thousands of individuals are living with LBD, placing a substantial demand on aged care services and memory clinics.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The exact cause of Lewy body disease remains incompletely understood, but it is characterised by widespread neurochemical and neuroanatomical changes. The key pathological mechanisms include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Alpha-synuclein aggregation:} The core pathology is the misfolding and accumulation of alpha-synuclein protein, forming insoluble Lewy bodies in the neuronal cytoplasm and Lewy neurites in neuronal processes. These aggregates disrupt cellular transport and trigger cell death.
    \item \textbf{Neurotransmitter deficits:} Marked depletion of acetylcholine in the neocortex and basal forebrain (which drives cognitive fluctuations and visual hallucinations) and dopamine in the substantia nigra (which causes parkinsonism).
    \item \textbf{Genetic risk factors:} While most cases are sporadic, genetic associations have been identified, including mutations in the GBA (glucocerebrosidase) gene, the SNCA (alpha-synuclein) gene, and the presence of the APOE $\epsilon4$ allele.
    \item \textbf{Autonomic nervous system involvement:} Deposition of Lewy bodies in the brainstem autonomic nuclei, sympathetic ganglia, and enteric nervous system, leading to widespread dysautonomia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of LBD is diverse and can change dynamically, even from hour to hour. Key clinical features include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fluctuating cognition:} Spontaneous variations in attention, alertness, and cognitive function, which can manifest as episodes of confusion, staring spells, or lethargy interspersed with periods of lucidity.
    \item \textbf{Visual hallucinations:} Recurrent, detailed, and well-formed visual hallucinations, typically of people or animals, which are often non-threatening to the patient initially.
    \item \textbf{REM sleep behaviour disorder (RBD):} A parasomnia characterised by the loss of normal muscle atonia during rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, leading patients to physically act out vivid, often violent dreams by thrashing, punching, or shouting.
    \item \textbf{Parkinsonism:} Motor features such as bradykinesia (slowness of movement), limb rigidity, resting tremor, and a shuffling, unstable gait.
    \item \textbf{Autonomic dysfunction:} Symptoms such as orthostatic hypotension (blood pressure drops upon standing, causing dizziness), chronic constipation, urinary urgency or incontinence, and temperature dysregulation.
    \item \textbf{Neuroleptic sensitivity:} Extreme and potentially life-threatening sensitivity to antipsychotic medications, which can cause acute worsening of parkinsonism, sedation, or features resembling neuroleptic malignant syndrome.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Diagnosing LBD is challenging because its symptoms overlap significantly with other neurological disorders. Differential diagnoses include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Alzheimer's disease:} Characterised by early, prominent short-term memory deficits (amnestic syndrome) rather than early visual hallucinations, parkinsonism, or prominent cognitive fluctuations.
    \item \textbf{Idiopathic Parkinson's disease:} Differentiated by the timeline of symptom onset. In Parkinson's disease, motor symptoms precede cognitive impairment by at least one year (often many years), whereas in DLB, cognitive decline occurs before or concurrently with motor symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Vascular dementia:} Cognitive impairment featuring a stepwise decline and focal neurological deficits, associated with neuroimaging evidence of cerebrovascular disease.
    \item \textbf{Frontotemporal dementia:} Characterised by early and prominent alterations in personality, social conduct, or language, with relative preservation of visuospatial skills.
    \item \textbf{Delirium:} An acute confusional state triggered by infections (such as urinary tract infections), metabolic derangements, or medications, which must be excluded during fluctuating cognitive episodes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients showing signs of progressive memory loss, sleep disturbances, or motor stiffness should seek a comprehensive assessment from a GP, geriatrician, or neurologist.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unexplained falls, particularly those resulting in head trauma or suspected fractures.
    \item Severe, acute confusion, agitation, or aggression that poses an immediate risk of harm to the patient or their caregivers.
    \item Swallowing difficulties that result in choking, breathing difficulty, or suspected aspiration of food or fluid.
    \item A severe reaction to an antipsychotic medication, presenting as extreme muscle stiffness, high fever, rapid heart rate, and fluctuating blood pressure.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of Lewy body disease is primarily clinical, based on established consensus diagnostic criteria. Investigations are utilised to support the diagnosis and exclude other pathologies:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical cognitive testing:} Detailed neuropsychological assessments (such as the MMSE or MoCA) highlighting deficits in executive function, attention, and visuospatial skills rather than isolated memory loss.
    \item \textbf{Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):} Performed to rule out vascular lesions or tumours; MRI in LBD typically shows relative preservation of the temporal lobe and hippocampal structures compared to Alzheimer's disease.
    \item \textbf{Dopamine transporter SPECT imaging (DaTscan):} Demonstrates reduced dopamine transporter uptake in the striatum, which helps differentiate LBD from Alzheimer's disease.
    \item \textbf{Polysomnography (sleep study):} Confirms the diagnosis of REM sleep behaviour disorder by documenting muscle activity (lack of atonia) during REM sleep.
    \item \textbf{Myocardial MIBG scintigraphy:} Evaluates cardiac sympathetic innervation; reduced uptake is highly specific for Lewy body pathology.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management of LBD is symptomatic and multidisciplinary, requiring a balance between treating cognitive, motor, and psychiatric symptoms without worsening others. Strategies include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cholinesterase inhibitors:} First-line pharmacological agents (e.g.\ donepezil, rivastigmine) to improve cognitive function, reduce fluctuations, and alleviate visual hallucinations.
    \item \textbf{Levodopa:} Administered cautiously in low doses to manage disabling motor parkinsonism, though with close monitoring as it can exacerbate visual hallucinations.
    \item \textbf{REM sleep management:} Melatonin or low-dose clonazepam to treat REM sleep behaviour disorder and reduce the risk of sleep-related injury.
    \item \textbf{Autonomic symptom support:} Fludrocortisone or midodrine for severe orthostatic hypotension, along with high-fibre diets and laxatives for chronic constipation.
    \item \textbf{Non-pharmacological interventions:} Tailored exercise programmes, occupational therapy home assessments to reduce fall risks, and cognitive stimulation therapy.
    \item \textbf{Caregiver education and support:} Providing resources to help families manage cognitive fluctuations and behavioural changes, reducing carer distress.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The progression of Lewy body disease is associated with several debilitating complications:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Recurrent falls and injury:} Caused by a combination of parkinsonian gait, postural instability, and orthostatic hypotension.
    \item \textbf{Aspiration pneumonia:} A major cause of death, secondary to progressive swallowing dysfunction (dysphagia) in advanced stages.
    \item \textbf{Neuroleptic malignant syndrome:} A severe, life-threatening reaction triggered by the administration of traditional antipsychotic medications.
    \item \textbf{Severe depression and anxiety:} Common neuropsychiatric comorbidities that further impair the patient's quality of life.
    \item \textbf{Malnutrition and dehydration:} Resulting from poor appetite, feeding difficulties, and swallowing deficits.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Lewy body disease is a progressive, terminal condition for which there is currently no disease-modifying cure. The clinical course is characterised by a gradual decline in cognitive and physical function. The average survival from the time of clinical diagnosis ranges between 5 and 8 years, though individual trajectories vary. In the advanced stages, patients require comprehensive, round-the-clock nursing care due to severe immobility, profound dementia, and complete dependence for activities of daily living.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
There are no established methods to prevent the onset of Lewy body disease. However, self-care strategies can help manage symptoms and maintain independence for as long as possible:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Establish a safe sleeping environment:} Place the mattress close to the floor, remove sharp bedside furniture, and consider sleeping in separate beds to prevent injury to the patient or partner during dream enactment.
    \item \textbf{Prevent falls at home:} Secure loose rugs, install grab rails in the bathroom, ensure excellent lighting, and eliminate clutter.
    \item \textbf{Maintain a stable daily routine:} A consistent schedule for meals, waking, and sleeping helps minimise confusion and disorientation.
    \item \textbf{Manage orthostatic hypotension:} Encourage the patient to rise slowly from sitting or lying positions, wear compression stockings, and stay well hydrated.
    \item \textbf{Engage in gentle exercise:} Daily walking, stretching, or seated exercises under the guidance of a physiotherapist can help preserve mobility and balance.
\end{itemize}
