\chapter{Flexible Sigmoidoscopy}
\index{Flexible Sigmoidoscopy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A flexible sigmoidoscopy is an examination of the lower part of the large bowel, the rectum and the sigmoid colon, using a thin, flexible tube with a camera on its end. It is used to investigate symptoms such as bleeding from the back passage, change in bowel habit, diarrhoea, and lower abdominal pain, and it can also be used to screen for bowel cancer. Because it examines only the lower bowel, it is a quicker and less invasive procedure than a full colonoscopy, and it usually needs only a simple preparation rather than a full bowel washout. Polyps and small growths can be removed during the procedure, and tissue samples (biopsies) can be taken. This chapter explains what happens before, during, and after a flexible sigmoidoscopy.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Flexible sigmoidoscopy is a common procedure in many countries, used both for diagnosis and for screening. Lower bowel symptoms such as rectal bleeding affect a large proportion of the population, and flexible sigmoidoscopy provides a direct look at the most common site of bowel cancer and polyps. Around half of bowel cancers arise in the lower bowel that this test can see. As a screening tool, flexible sigmoidoscopy has been shown in large studies to reduce the risk of dying from bowel cancer, though the national screening programme uses immunochemical faecal tests (FIT) for the population and colonoscopy for those with positive results. The procedure is safe, with serious complications being uncommon.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visualisation:} the scope allows direct inspection of the lining of the rectum and sigmoid colon, and part of the descending colon
    \item \textbf{Anatomy:} the sigmoid colon is the S-shaped section of bowel just above the rectum; many polyps and cancers develop here
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} small samples of tissue are taken through the scope for pathology
    \item \textbf{Polypectomy:} polyps can be removed during the procedure to prevent them becoming cancerous
    \item \textbf{The procedure:} with you lying on your side, the lubricated scope is passed through the back passage and gently advanced, with air used to open the bowel
    \item \textbf{Comfort:} most people find it uncomfortable rather than painful, and sedation is usually not needed
    \item \textbf{Limitations:} the test does not see the upper bowel, so a full colonoscopy or other imaging may be needed if symptoms suggest disease higher up
    \item \textbf{Why symptoms occur:} inflammation, polyps, cancer, and diverticular disease in the lower bowel cause bleeding, changed bowel habit, and pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The procedure lasts 10--20 minutes, usually without sedation, in an outpatient setting
    \item Preparation is simpler than for colonoscopy: often one or two enemas rather than a full bowel preparation
    \item You lie on your side, and the scope is passed gently through the back passage
    \item A feeling of pressure, cramping, and the urge to open the bowels as air is introduced
    \item Results may be discussed straight away, with biopsy results later
    \item Afterwards: some wind, cramping, and occasional slight bleeding from a biopsy or polyp removal
    \item Normal activities can usually be resumed the same day
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The test is performed to distinguish the causes of lower bowel symptoms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bowel cancer and polyps
    \item Diverticular disease
    \item Inflammatory bowel disease, including ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease
    \item Haemorrhoids and anal fissures, which cause bleeding
    \item Irritable bowel syndrome, with pain and changed bowel habit
    \item Infectious colitis
    \item Ischaemic colitis in older adults
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with bleeding from the back passage, a persistent change in bowel habit, unexplained diarrhoea, or persistent lower abdominal pain should see a doctor, who will decide whether a flexible sigmoidoscopy is appropriate.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain, especially with vomiting and an inability to pass wind or stool
    \item Heavy rectal bleeding with faintness or collapse
    \item Fever with severe pain, which may indicate perforation or peritonitis
    \item Sudden severe abdominal pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} symptoms, risk factors, family history, and a digital rectal examination
    \item \textbf{The sigmoidoscopy:} the definitive view of the lower bowel, with biopsy and polyp removal as needed
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, iron studies, and inflammatory markers
    \item \textbf{Stool tests:} faecal immunochemical testing and calprotectin where indicated
    \item \textbf{Follow-up tests:} colonoscopy or imaging of the upper bowel when disease beyond the sigmoid is suspected
    \item \textbf{Histology:} pathology of biopsies to confirm inflammation, polyps, or cancer
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Before the procedure:} follow the preparation instructions, usually enemas and a light diet
    \item \textbf{During:} lying on your side, the scope is passed gently; you can ask for a pause
    \item \textbf{After:} passing wind, walking, and resuming normal food and activity
    \item \textbf{Polyps:} removed during the test are sent for pathology, and their size and type guide surveillance
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory bowel disease:} diagnosed on biopsy and treated with medicines under specialist care
    \item \textbf{Cancer:} if found, referral for staging, surgery, and specialist treatment
    \item \textbf{Normal results:} reassurance, with ongoing attention to bowel screening
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding after biopsy or polyp removal, usually minor
    \item Perforation of the bowel, a rare but serious complication needing urgent surgery
    \item Discomfort and cramping during and after the procedure
    \item Incomplete examination if the bowel is not adequately prepared
    \item Missed disease above the reach of the scope, which is why other tests may follow
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Flexible sigmoidoscopy is a safe, quick procedure that provides accurate diagnosis of lower bowel disease and allows polyps to be removed before they become cancer. For people with rectal bleeding and lower bowel symptoms, it is often the definitive investigation, and a normal result is reassuring. When polyps or cancer are found, removal or early treatment is highly effective: most bowel cancers are curable when detected early. The procedure is a key tool in the effort to reduce deaths from bowel cancer, and its benefits are well established.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discuss bowel symptoms with a doctor early, especially bleeding or a change in bowel habit
    \item Participate in the bowel-cancer screening programme available where you live, if invited
    \item Follow preparation instructions exactly for the best view
    \item Tell the doctor about medicines, including blood thinners
    \item Report severe pain, heavy bleeding, or fever after the procedure
    \item Attend follow-up and surveillance colonoscopy as recommended after polyp removal
    \item Maintain a diet high in fibre, exercise, and avoid smoking and excess alcohol to reduce bowel cancer risk
\end{itemize}
