\chapter{Dental Bridge}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A dental bridge is a fixed dental restoration used to replace one or more missing teeth by attaching an artificial tooth (or teeth) to the healthy teeth on either side of the gap. The supporting teeth, called abutment teeth, are prepared and crowned, and the bridge is cemented permanently in place. A bridge restores the ability to chew, maintains the shape of the face, prevents remaining teeth from drifting, and improves appearance. This chapter explains the types of bridges, the procedure involved, and how to care for a bridge.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Missing teeth are common; in Australia, a large proportion of adults have lost at least one permanent tooth, and many have multiple missing teeth. Tooth loss results mainly from decay and gum disease, and it increases with age. Dental bridges are one of several replacement options, along with implants and dentures. Bridges are particularly suitable when the teeth beside the gap are healthy and strong, and they are widely used in restorative dentistry.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tooth loss:} bridges replace teeth lost to decay, gum disease, trauma, or failed previous restorations
    \item \textbf{Consequences of gaps:} adjacent teeth tilt into the gap, opposing teeth over-erupt, chewing efficiency falls, and bone shrinks
    \item \textbf{Support:} the bridge relies on the abutment teeth for anchorage; their health determines the long-term success of the bridge
    \item \textbf{Types:} traditional bridges (crowns on abutments), cantilever bridges (supported on one side), and resin-bonded (Maryland) bridges, which are more conservative
    \item \textbf{Implant-supported bridges:} supported by dental implants rather than natural teeth
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A gap left by one or more missing teeth
    \item Difficulty chewing on the affected side
    \item Drifting of adjacent teeth and gaps appearing between remaining teeth
    \item Speech changes in some people
    \item With a bridge in place: restored chewing function and appearance
    \item Signs of bridge problems: looseness, food trapping, pain, or swelling
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dental implant, a titanium post that replaces the tooth root and supports a crown
    \item Removable partial denture, which can be taken in and out
    \item No replacement, which may be acceptable for some gaps
    \item Orthodontic space closure in selected cases
    \item Resin-bonded bridge versus traditional bridge, depending on the site and support teeth
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with missing teeth should discuss replacement options with a dentist, as gaps can affect oral and general health. A bridge needs dental review if it becomes loose, painful, or traps food, and for routine maintenance checks.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A bridge that comes loose with significant trauma to the mouth
    \item Severe pain, swelling, or signs of infection around a bridge
    \item Bleeding that does not settle after the fitting procedure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dental examination:} assessment of the gap, supporting teeth, and gum health
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} evaluate the roots and bone around the abutment teeth
    \item \textbf{Assessment of abutment health:} including existing fillings, decay, and periodontal condition
    \item \textbf{Discussion of options:} comparison of bridges, implants, and dentures based on cost, durability, and clinical suitability
    \item \textbf{Impressions or digital scans:} used to construct the bridge
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tooth preparation:} the abutment teeth are reshaped to receive crowns
    \item \textbf{Temporary bridge:} worn while the permanent bridge is being made
    \item \textbf{Bridge fitting:} the permanent bridge is cemented onto the prepared teeth
    \item \textbf{Materials:} porcelain fused to metal, all-ceramic, or zirconia, chosen for strength and appearance
    \item \textbf{Implant-supported bridges:} where implants are placed first and the bridge is fixed to them after healing
    \item \textbf{Maintenance:} regular dental check-ups, professional cleaning around the bridge, and good home care
    \item \textbf{Repairs:} recementation or replacement of a bridge that fails
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Decay or gum disease developing under or around the bridge
    \item Loosening or fracture of the bridge or the underlying teeth
    \item Food trapping and bad breath if cleaning is inadequate
    \item Sensitivity or damage to the prepared abutment teeth
    \item Need for root canal treatment on abutment teeth in some cases
    \item Bridge failure requiring replacement
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With good oral hygiene and regular dental care, a well-made dental bridge can last 10--15 years or longer. The main threats to its longevity are decay and gum disease around the supporting teeth, which are largely preventable. Bridges restore chewing function and appearance effectively, and most people are very satisfied with the result. Regular check-ups allow early detection and management of any problems, maximising the life of the bridge.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Brush twice daily and clean around and under the bridge with floss threaders or interdental brushes
    \item Attend regular dental check-ups and professional cleaning
    \item Avoid very hard foods that can damage the bridge
    \item Report any looseness, pain, or food trapping promptly
    \item Maintain good gum health to protect the supporting teeth
    \item Discuss replacement options early when a tooth is lost
\end{itemize}
