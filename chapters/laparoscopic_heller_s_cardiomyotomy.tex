\chapter{Laparoscopic Heller's cardiomyotomy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Laparoscopic Heller's cardiomyotomy is a minimally invasive surgical procedure performed to treat achalasia, a primary oesophageal motility disorder characterised by the failure of the lower oesophageal sphincter (LES) to relax and the loss of orderly peristalsis in the distal oesophagus. The procedure involves making a longitudinal incision through the muscular layers of the cardia (the junction between the oesophagus and stomach) and the distal oesophagus, thereby relieving the functional obstruction and allowing food and liquids to pass into the stomach. To prevent the development of severe post-operative gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD), the cardiomyotomy is routinely paired with a partial anti-reflux procedure, such as a Dor (anterior) or Toupet (posterior) fundoplication. Laparoscopic Heller's cardiomyotomy is considered a gold-standard treatment, offering durable symptomatic relief and a rapid recovery compared to open surgical approaches.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Achalasia is a rare condition, with an estimated annual incidence of approximately 1.0 to 1.6 cases per 100,000 individuals and a prevalence of roughly 10 cases per 100,000 individuals globally. It affects males and females in equal proportions and can occur at any age, though it is most frequently diagnosed in adults between the ages of 30 and 60. In Australia, the epidemiological profile matches international trends. While achalasia is the primary indication for Heller's cardiomyotomy, the procedure may also be selectively employed for other severe, non-responsive spastic oesophageal motility disorders, such as diffuse oesophageal spasm or jackhammer oesophagus, which have an even lower clinical prevalence.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The primary pathophysiology of achalasia involves the selective loss of inhibitory ganglion cells within the myenteric (Auerbach's) plexus of the distal oesophageal wall.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Loss of inhibitory neurones:} The destruction of postganglionic, inhibitory nitric oxide- and vasoactive intestinal peptide-producing neurones leads to an imbalance, leaving excitatory cholinergic neurones unopposed. This results in failure of the LES to relax in response to swallowing.
    \item \textbf{Degeneration of the myenteric plexus:} Progressive inflammation and autoimmune-mediated destruction of the myenteric plexus lead to the loss of coordinated peristalsis along the smooth muscle portion of the oesophageal body.
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune response:} Although the exact trigger is idiopathic, evidence suggests a T-cell-mediated autoimmune response directed against myenteric neurones, potentially triggered by a viral infection (such as herpes simplex virus-1) in genetically susceptible individuals.
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} Rare familial associations and specific human leucocyte antigen (HLA) class II associations (e.g. HLA-DQB1) suggest a genetic predisposition to developing the autoimmune inflammatory process.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of achalasia is typically insidious, with symptoms progressing gradually over several months or years before a diagnosis is established.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dysphagia:} Progressive difficulty swallowing both solids and liquids is the hallmark symptom, often described by patients as food sticking in the lower retrosternal region.
    \item \textbf{Regurgitation:} The effortless backflow of undigested food or saliva, particularly when recumbent, which can lead to nocturnal coughing, choking, or aspiration.
    \item \textbf{Retrosternal chest pain:} Squeezing, non-cardiac chest pain that may occur during or after meals, believed to be caused by oesophageal spasm or distension.
    \item \textbf{Weight loss:} Gradual, significant weight loss resulting from dietary restriction and poor oral intake due to severe dysphagia.
    \item \textbf{Heartburn:} Often misdiagnosed as GORD, but actually caused by the stasis and fermentation of retained food in the oesophagus, producing lactic acid.
    \item \textbf{Aspiration pneumonia:} Recurrent lower respiratory tract infections caused by the silent inhalation of retained oesophageal contents into the airway.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
It is critical to distinguish achalasia and the necessity for a Heller's cardiomyotomy from other conditions causing dysphagia and chest pain.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pseudoachalasia:} A condition presenting identically to achalasia but caused by an underlying malignancy (e.g. gastric cardia or distal oesophageal adenocarcinoma) infiltrating the myenteric plexus.
    \item \textbf{Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD):} Chronic acid reflux causing heartburn and occasionally dysphagia due to stricture formation, which must be differentiated from achalasia-related stasis.
    \item \textbf{Oesophageal stricture:} A mechanical narrowing of the oesophageal lumen, typically secondary to chronic peptic injury, caustic ingestion, or prolonged intubation.
    \item \textbf{Oesophageal spasm and hypercontractile oesophagus:} Spastic disorders (such as diffuse oesophageal spasm) characterized by uncoordinated or high-amplitude contractions but with preserved, albeit sometimes impaired, LES relaxation.
    \item \textbf{Oesophageal cancer:} Primary squamous cell carcinoma or adenocarcinoma of the oesophagus presenting with progressive dysphagia (primarily solids initially) and rapid weight loss.
    \item \textbf{Chagas disease:} An infectious disease caused by the parasite \textit{Trypanosoma cruzi}, which destroys the myenteric plexus, leading to megacolon and megaoesophagus identical to idiopathic achalasia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients experiencing persistent or worsening swallowing difficulties, frequent regurgitation of food, unexplained weight loss, or chronic retrosternal chest pain should consult a medical professional for evaluation.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, sudden-onset chest pain that may mimic a myocardial infarction.
    \item Acute airway obstruction, severe choking, or persistent difficulty breathing after regurgitating.
    \item Signs of oesophageal perforation (e.g. sudden, excruciating retrosternal or epigastric pain, fever, and rapid breathing following an intervention or vomiting).
    \item High fever accompanied by productive cough and shortness of breath, suggesting severe aspiration pneumonia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A definitive diagnosis of achalasia must be established using a combination of physiological and imaging studies before proceeding to surgery.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High-resolution oesophageal manometry:} The gold standard diagnostic test, which demonstrates incomplete or absent relaxation of the LES (integrated relaxation pressure $\ge 15$ mmHg) and a lack of peristalsis in the oesophageal body.
    \item \textbf{Barium swallow study:} An imaging test showing a dilated, aperistaltic oesophagus with a classic smooth tapering at the lower oesophageal sphincter, often referred to as a "bird's beak" or "funnel" appearance, and delayed emptying.
    \item \textbf{Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (gastroscopy):} Essential to visualise the oesophagus, evaluate the mucosa, and rule out mechanical obstruction or pseudoachalasia at the gastro-oesophageal junction.
    \item \textbf{Computerised tomography (CT) scan:} Performed if pseudoachalasia or malignancy is suspected, to evaluate the distal oesophagus and gastric cardia for mass lesions or wall thickening.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management focuses on reducing the outflow resistance at the level of the lower oesophageal sphincter to improve bolus clearance.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Laparoscopic Heller's cardiomyotomy:} The primary surgical intervention. The muscle fibres of the distal oesophagus (usually 5--6 cm) and proximal stomach (usually 1.5--2 cm) are split longitudinally to expose the intact mucosa, combined with a partial fundoplication (Dor or Toupet) to prevent post-operative reflux.
    \item \textbf{Peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM):} A minimally invasive endoscopic alternative where an endoscope is used to create a submucosal tunnel to perform the myotomy without external abdominal incisions.
    \item \textbf{Pneumatic dilatation:} An endoscopic procedure utilizing large-diameter balloons (30, 35, or 40 mm) to forcefully disrupt the muscular ring of the LES, which may require repeat sessions.
    \item \textbf{Botulinum toxin injection:} Endoscopic injection of botulinum toxin into the LES to inhibit acetylcholine release, used primarily as a temporary measure in elderly patients or those unfit for surgery.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacotherapy:} Oral nitrates or calcium channel blockers (e.g. nifedipine) taken before meals to temporarily relax the LES smooth muscle, reserved for patients who are not candidates for definitive procedures.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While laparoscopic Heller's cardiomyotomy is generally safe, it is associated with specific procedural and long-term complications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mucosal perforation:} Accidental tearing of the oesophageal or gastric mucosa during the muscle division, which is typically repaired intra-operatively.
    \item \textbf{Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD):} Chronic acid reflux resulting from the division of the LES, which can lead to oesophagitis or Barrett's oesophagus if the anti-reflux wrap fails.
    \item \textbf{Persistent or recurrent dysphagia:} Return of swallowing difficulties due to an incomplete myotomy, early scarring, or tight/malpositioned fundoplication.
    \item \textbf{Splenic or hepatic injury:} Inadvertent trauma to the spleen or liver during surgical retraction or dissection.
    \item \textbf{Post-operative infection:} Wound infection at the laparoscopic port sites, or peritonitis/mediastinitis in the event of an undetected mucosal leak.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The long-term prognosis after a laparoscopic Heller's cardiomyotomy is excellent, with approximately 85\% to 90\% of patients reporting significant and durable improvement in dysphagia and regurgitation. The recovery period is relatively short, with most patients discharged from the hospital within 24 to 48 hours and returning to normal light activities within two weeks. Although the surgery successfully relieves the mechanical obstruction, it does not restore normal oesophageal peristalsis. Some patients may experience minor, manageable reflux symptoms requiring long-term proton pump inhibitor therapy.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
There are no known preventive measures for achalasia, but specific self-care and dietary strategies are essential post-operatively to optimise healing and manage symptoms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Post-operative dietary progression:} Adhere strictly to a clear liquid diet initially, transitioning gradually to a pureed and soft diet over 3 to 4 weeks to allow mucosal healing.
    \item \textbf{Eating habits:} Consume small, frequent meals throughout the day, chew food thoroughly, and avoid eating in a hurried manner.
    \item \textbf{Fluid intake:} Drink adequate fluids, especially water, during and after meals to help flush food down the aperistaltic oesophagus.
    \item \textbf{Postprandial positioning:} Remain upright for at least 2 to 3 hours after eating, and avoid lying down or sleeping immediately after meals.
    \item \textbf{Head-of-bed elevation:} Raise the head of the bed by 15 to 20 cm using bed blocks to minimise nocturnal regurgitation and reflux.
\end{itemize}
