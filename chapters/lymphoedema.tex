\chapter{Lymphoedema}
\index{Lymphoedema}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Lymphoedema is a chronic, progressive medical condition characterised by the accumulation of protein-rich fluid in the interstitial spaces, leading to swelling, tissue fibrosis, and inflammatory changes, most commonly in the limbs. It arises due to a compromised lymphatic system that is unable to adequately drain fluid from the tissues. Lymphoedema is classified as primary (congenital or hereditary abnormalities in lymphatic development) or secondary (damage or obstruction to a previously normal lymphatic system, typically due to cancer treatments, filariasis, trauma, or infection). Although there is no cure, early detection and structured management are crucial to prevent progression, maintain limb function, and reduce the risk of debilitating complications.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Secondary lymphoedema is far more common than primary lymphoedema. Globally, lymphatic filariasis (a parasitic infection) is the leading cause, affecting over 120 million people. In developed nations like some countries, secondary lymphoedema is primarily a consequence of oncological interventions, particularly breast cancer surgery involving axillary lymph node dissection and radiotherapy, where it affects approximately 20\% to 30\% of survivors. Primary lymphoedema is rare, with an estimated prevalence of 1 in 6,000 live births, and is more frequently diagnosed in females. It can present at birth (lymphoedema congenita), during puberty (lymphoedema praecox), or after age 35 (lymphoedema tardum).

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Lymphoedema develops when the lymphatic transport capacity falls below the rate of microvascular filtration, leading to interstitial fluid accumulation and subsequent chronic inflammation and fibrosis.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary lymphoedema:} Inherited or developmental lymphatic hypoplasia, hyperplasia, or aplasia, sometimes associated with genetic syndromes such as Milroy's disease (FLT4 gene mutation) or Meige disease.
    \item \textbf{Oncological treatments:} Surgical removal of lymph nodes (e.g.\ axillary, inguinal, or pelvic dissection) and local radiation therapy, which cause scarring and disruption of lymphatic pathways.
    \item \textbf{Infections:} Parasitic infestation by Wuchereria bancrofti (lymphatic filariasis) or recurrent bacterial infections like cellulitis, which destroy lymphatic vessels.
    \item \textbf{Trauma and surgery:} Direct physical injury, major burns, or orthopaedic/vascular surgeries that sever or damage regional lymphatic channels.
    \item \textbf{Chronic venous insufficiency:} Long-standing venous hypertension that overloads the lymphatic drainage capacity, eventually leading to secondary phlebolymphoedema.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of lymphoedema varies depending on the severity and stage of the disease, usually progressing from soft, pitting oedema to firm, non-pitting swelling.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Limb swelling:} Gradual, asymmetrical swelling of an extremity, initially soft and fluctuating (improving with elevation) but later becoming hard, woody, and persistent.
    \item \textbf{Sensation of heaviness:} A subjective feeling of tightness, fullness, or aching in the affected limb or region.
    \item \textbf{Skin changes:} Hyperkeratosis, papillomatosis, thickening of the skin, and a positive Stemmer's sign (inability to pinch a fold of skin at the base of the second toe or finger).
    \item \textbf{Restricted range of motion:} Reduced flexibility and joint mobility in the affected limb due to size and weight increases.
    \item \textbf{Recurrent infections:} Increased susceptibility to cellulitis, erysipelas, or lymphangitis because of impaired local immune surveillance.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
It is essential to differentiate lymphoedema from other causes of limb swelling to ensure appropriate treatment.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Deep vein thrombosis (DVT):} Acute, painful, unilateral limb swelling, typically diagnosed using venous duplex ultrasound.
    \item \textbf{Congestive heart failure (CHF):} Bilateral, symmetric pitting oedema of the lower extremities, accompanied by dyspnoea and jugular venous distension.
    \item \textbf{Chronic venous insufficiency (CVI):} Swelling associated with venous stasis skin changes, hyperpigmentation (haemosiderin staining), and varicose veins.
    \item \textbf{Lipoedema:} Symmetrical, painful fat deposition in the lower extremities, sparing the feet (negative Stemmer's sign), occurring almost exclusively in females.
    \item \textbf{Renal or hepatic failure:} Systemic fluid retention causing generalised, bilateral pitting oedema due to hypoalbuminaemia or volume overload.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should consult a healthcare professional if they notice persistent, unexplained swelling in any part of their body, particularly after cancer treatment.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, rapidly spreading redness, warmth, and severe pain in the affected limb, which may indicate acute cellulitis.
    \item High fever, chills, and rigours accompanied by limb swelling.
    \item Difficulty breathing or chest pain, which may suggest a pulmonary embolism if DVT is co-present.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is primarily clinical, based on a detailed medical history and physical examination, but investigations help confirm the diagnosis and rule out other pathologies.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} Measuring limb circumference at standardised intervals, assessing for Stemmer's sign, and evaluating skin texture.
    \item \textbf{Lymphoscintigraphy:} The gold standard imaging test, which uses a radiotracer to visualise lymphatic flow and identify lymphatic vessel hypoplasia or obstruction.
    \item \textbf{Duplex ultrasound:} Used to rule out deep vein thrombosis or venous insufficiency as primary or contributing factors.
    \item \textbf{Bioimpedance spectroscopy:} A non-invasive method measuring extracellular fluid changes to detect subclinical lymphoedema before visible swelling occurs.
    \item \textbf{CT or MRI scans:} Useful for evaluating structural changes, identifying lymphatic obstruction caused by pelvic or abdominal tumours, and assessing subcutaneous tissue thickness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
While lymphoedema cannot be cured, a combination of conservative therapies can effectively manage symptoms, prevent progression, and reduce infectious complications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Decongestive lymphatic therapy (DLT):} The primary two-phase treatment comprising manual lymphatic drainage (MLD), compression bandaging, skin care, and specialised exercises.
    \item \textbf{Compression garments:} Elastic sleeves or stockings worn during the maintenance phase to maintain volume reduction and support lymphatic drainage.
    \item \textbf{Skincare and hygiene:} Diligent daily cleansing, moisturising, and protection of the skin to prevent cracks, minor cuts, and bacterial entry.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacotherapy:} Empiric oral antibiotics (e.g.\ cephalexin) prescribed promptly at the first sign of cellulitis; diuretics are generally avoided as they do not remove tissue proteins.
    \item \textbf{Surgical options:} In advanced cases, procedures like lymphaticovenular anastomosis (LVA), vascularised lymph node transfer (VLNT), or liposuction for chronic non-pitting tissue changes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Chronic lymphoedema can lead to significant physical and psychological sequelae if left untreated.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cellulitis and lymphangitis:} Recurrent, severe bacterial infections of the skin and subcutaneous tissues that can lead to septicaemia.
    \item \textbf{Lymphangiosarcoma (Stewart-Treves syndrome):} A rare, highly aggressive malignant vascular tumour that develops in chronic, long-standing lymphoedema.
    \item \textbf{Severe skin changes:} Deep skin folds, hyperkeratosis, papillomatosis, and ulceration with lymphangioma (leakage of lymph fluid).
    \item \textbf{Functional disability:} Significant limitation in mobility, dexterity, and the ability to perform activities of daily living due to limb weight.
    \item \textbf{Psychosocial distress:} Negative body image, anxiety, depression, and social withdrawal due to the chronic nature and appearance of the limb.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Lymphoedema is a lifelong, progressive condition that requires continuous self-management. Early intervention during the subclinical or reversible stages leads to excellent outcomes, often keeping the limb swelling to a minimum. Without proper management, the condition progresses to irreversible tissue fibrosis and fat deposition, increasing the risk of recurrent infections and permanent disability. Regular follow-ups with a specialised lymphoedema therapist significantly improve long-term outcomes and quality of life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Individuals at risk of secondary lymphoedema or those managing the condition should adopt protective strategies to support lymphatic health.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Avoid skin injury:} Protect the affected limb from cuts, scratches, insect bites, burns, and medical procedures (such as blood draws and blood pressure checks).
    \item \textbf{Practice daily skin hygiene:} Keep the skin clean and apply high-quality moisturisers daily to maintain a healthy skin barrier.
    \item \textbf{Maintain a healthy weight:} Obesity increases the risk of developing lymphoedema and exacerbates lymphatic dysfunction.
    \item \textbf{Exercise regularly:} Perform gentle, progressive muscle-pump exercises (e.g.\ walking, swimming) while wearing compression garments if prescribed.
    \item \textbf{Avoid extreme heat or cold:} Limit exposure to saunas, hot baths, or direct sun, which can increase fluid filtration into the tissues.
\end{itemize}
