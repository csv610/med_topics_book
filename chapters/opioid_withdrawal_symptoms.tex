\chapter{Opioid withdrawal symptoms}
\index{Opioid withdrawal symptoms}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Educational information; seek professional advice for individual care.}

\section*{What it is}
Opioid withdrawal occurs when a person who has developed physical dependence suddenly stops or substantially reduces an opioid. Dependence can occur with prescribed medicines as well as non-prescribed opioids. Withdrawal is usually very uncomfortable, but relapse after tolerance falls can create a serious overdose risk.

\section*{Symptoms and timing}
Early symptoms may include anxiety, restlessness, yawning, sweating, runny nose, watery eyes, muscle aches, and insomnia. Later symptoms may include abdominal cramps, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, dilated pupils, gooseflesh, and strong cravings. Timing and severity vary with the opioid, dose, duration, and other health conditions.

\section*{Assessment and treatment}
A clinician should assess opioid use, other medicines or substances, pregnancy, pain, mental health, dehydration, and infection risk. Treatment may include supervised buprenorphine or methadone, symptom medicines, fluids, counselling, and ongoing treatment for opioid use disorder. Detoxification alone does not reliably prevent relapse; continuing evidence-based treatment should be discussed.

\section*{When to seek urgent care}
Seek urgent help for severe vomiting or diarrhoea, dehydration, chest pain, pregnancy with withdrawal, severe mental distress, confusion, or suspected overdose. Get emergency help for slow or stopped breathing, blue or grey lips, collapse, seizure, or unresponsiveness. Naloxone can reverse opioid overdose when available, but emergency care is still needed.

\section*{Safety and follow-up}
Do not restart the previous opioid dose after a period without opioids unless a clinician has advised it. Avoid alcohol and sedatives, do not use another person’s medicine, and connect with local addiction or mental-health services. Keep naloxone available where appropriate and follow the treatment plan.
