\chapter{Lice (crabs)}
\index{Lice (crabs)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Pubic lice, commonly referred to as "crabs" due to their crab-like appearance, are small, blood-sucking ectoparasites of the species \textit{Pthirus pubis}. They primarily infest the coarse hair of the pubic and perianal regions, but can occasionally spread to other areas of coarse body hair, such as the armpits, chest, abdomen, eyelashes, eyebrows, or beard. The infestation, known as phthiriasis pubis, is characterised by intense pruritus (itching) and is classified as a sexually transmitted infection (STI), although transmission can occasionally occur through close non-sexual contact or sharing infested clothing, bedding, or towels. Pubic lice infestations are not a sign of poor hygiene and do not vector other pathogens, but they require prompt pharmacological treatment and contact tracing.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Pubic lice infestations occur worldwide, affecting individuals of all socioeconomic backgrounds and ages, though the highest prevalence is seen in sexually active adolescents and young adults aged 15 to 30 years. There is no major sex preference, although differences in grooming habits (such as pubic hair removal) have been associated with declining rates of infestation in some modern populations. Because they are primarily transmitted via close physical contact, pubic lice are often co-diagnosed with other STIs, such as chlamydia or gonorrhoea.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The infestation is caused by the parasite \textit{Pthirus pubis}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} primary transmission is via skin-to-skin contact, usually during sexual activity. Fomite transmission (clothing, towels, bedding) is less common but possible due to the louse's ability to survive off a host for up to 24--48 hours.
    \item \textbf{Lifecycle:} a female louse lives for about 3--4 weeks and lays approximately 30 eggs (nits), cementing them to the base of hair shafts. Nits hatch in 6--10 days into nymphs, which mature into adults in about 1--2 weeks.
    \item \textbf{Feeding and Pruritus:} the lice feed on host blood several times a day. Their saliva contains anticoagulants and immunogenic proteins which trigger a hypersensitivity reaction in the host's skin, leading to intense pruritus.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms typically develop 2 to 4 weeks after the initial infestation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Intense itching in the pubic or perianal area, which is typically worse at night when the lice are more active
    \item Visible nits (tiny, oval, white-to-grey eggs glued to hair shafts) or adult lice (1--2 mm, flat, grey-brown parasites resembling miniature crabs clinging to hairs)
    \item Small blue-grey spots on the skin (maculae caeruleae) caused by the anticoagulant saliva of the feeding lice
    \item Mild inflammatory skin reactions, scratch marks (excoriations), and signs of secondary bacterial skin infections (like impetigo) from scratching
    \item In eyelashes/eyebrows: blepharitis (eyelid irritation), crusting, and redness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scabies:} caused by the mite \textit{Sarcoptes scabiei}, presenting with generalized itching and linear burrows, particularly in webbed spaces of fingers and wrists, rather than parasites clinging to pubic hairs.
    \item \textbf{Contact dermatitis:} skin irritation from soaps, latex, or lubricants, without visible parasites or nits.
    \item \textbf{Tinea cruris (jock itch):} a fungal infection presenting with a red, scaly, itchy rash in the groin folds, with no louse activity.
    \item \textbf{Folliculitis:} bacterial infection of hair follicles causing small pustules, but not associated with crawling parasites.
    \item \textbf{Seborrheic dermatitis:} causing itching and scaling in hair-bearing areas, without nits.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical care if you suspect a pubic lice infestation, if over-the-counter treatments fail, or if signs of skin infection develop.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of a severe systemic allergic reaction to treatment (anaphylaxis): difficulty breathing, swelling of the face or throat, widespread hives, or collapse
    \item Severe, spreading skin infection with high fever, chills, or red streaks extending from the groin
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} the primary diagnostic method, involving direct visual inspection of hair-bearing areas for live lice, crawling nymphs, or nits using a magnifying glass.
    \item \textbf{Microscopy:} confirming the species by examining a plucked hair with attached louse or nit under a light microscope.
    \item \textbf{STI screening:} because of the high rate of co-transmission, individuals diagnosed with pubic lice should undergo a comprehensive STI screen (chlamydia, gonorrhoea, syphilis, HIV).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment involves topical insecticides and environmental hygiene:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First-line topical treatments:} Permethrin 1\% cream or Pyrethrins with piperonyl butoxide, applied to all affected hair-bearing areas (usually from the neck down, focusing on pubic, perianal, thigh, and axillary hair) and washed off after 10 minutes.
    \item \textbf{Second-line treatments:} Malathion 0.5\% lotion (applied for 8--12 hours) or oral Ivermectin, reserved for refractory cases.
    \item \textbf{Repeat application:} treatment must be repeated in 7--10 days to kill any newly hatched nymphs, as insecticides may not fully penetrate eggs.
    \item \textbf{Eyelash involvement:} treated with occlusive petrolatum ointment applied to eyelid margins twice daily for 10 days, or manual removal with tweezers. Insecticides must NOT be used near the eyes.
    \item \textbf{Partner treatment:} sexual partners within the last month must be treated concurrently to prevent reinfestation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Secondary bacterial skin infections (cellulitis, impetigo) from scratching
    \item Persistent post-treatment pruritus due to lingering hypersensitivity
    \item Psychological distress, anxiety, and social embarrassment
    \item Blepharitis or conjunctivitis in cases of eyelash infestation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for pubic lice is excellent with appropriate treatment. The infestation is completely cured once two courses of topical therapy (spaced 7--10 days apart) are correctly applied and environmental hygiene measures are followed. Itching may persist for a few days after successful eradication but gradually resolves. There are no long-term physical sequelae once the parasites are cleared.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wash all clothing, bedding, and towels used by the infested person in hot water (at least 60\textdegree{}C) and dry them on the hot cycle to destroy lice and eggs
    \item Dry-clean items that cannot be washed, or seal them in a plastic bag for at least 2 weeks
    \item Avoid sexual or close physical contact with others until treatment is complete and lice are fully eradicated
    \item Vacuum mattresses, carpets, and upholstered furniture thoroughly
    \item Inform sexual partners so they can seek testing and treatment simultaneously
\end{itemize}
