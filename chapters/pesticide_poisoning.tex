\chapter{Pesticide Poisoning}
\index{Pesticide Poisoning}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{This chapter is for general education. Suspected pesticide poisoning is an emergency; contact local emergency services or a poison information service immediately.}

\section*{What it is}
Pesticide poisoning occurs when a pesticide enters the body by swallowing, breathing it in, or contact with the skin or eyes. Risk depends on the chemical, concentration, route, and time since exposure. Children, older people, pregnant people, and workers may need specific advice.

\section*{Possible symptoms}
Symptoms may include headache, dizziness, nausea, vomiting, sweating, salivation, coughing, breathing difficulty, blurred vision, weakness, confusion, seizures, or collapse. Some effects are delayed, so feeling well immediately does not always mean exposure is harmless.

\section*{Immediate first aid}
Move away from the source only if it is safe and get fresh air. Remove contaminated clothing and rinse exposed skin or eyes with plenty of clean water. Do not induce vomiting, give food or drink to an unconscious person, or apply chemicals to neutralise the pesticide. Do not enter a contaminated area without appropriate protection.

\section*{Information for clinicians}
Keep the container, label, product name, active ingredient, or a photograph if this can be done safely. Tell emergency staff the route, amount, time, and symptoms. Do not delay emergency treatment while searching for the label.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek emergency help immediately for breathing difficulty, severe vomiting, confusion, weakness, seizures, loss of consciousness, widespread burns, or any suspected intentional exposure. Anyone exposed to an unknown or concentrated pesticide should obtain urgent expert advice even without symptoms.
