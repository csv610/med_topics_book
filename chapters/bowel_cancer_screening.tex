\chapter{Bowel Cancer Screening}
\index{Bowel Cancer Screening}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bowel cancer screening tests people who have no symptoms, aiming to detect early cancer or polyps before they cause problems. Programmes vary by country: they may use a home stool blood test at set intervals and offer colonoscopy when results are abnormal. Screening exploits the biology of bowel cancer: many cancers arise from polyps that grow slowly, and early cancers may bleed invisibly into stool. Screening differs from diagnostic testing, which is needed when symptoms are present.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bowel cancer is the second most common cause of cancer death in many countries, and screening is one of the most effective public health measures available against it. Randomised trials show that regular screening with faecal blood tests reduces the risk of dying from bowel cancer by about a third. Bowel cancer is rare before age 50, which is why population screening begins at that age, although incidence in younger adults has been rising. Participation in the national program has improved over time, but around half of eligible people still do not complete their test, meaning many early cancers and polyps are missed. Uptake is lower in men, in younger age groups, and in people from lower socio-economic backgrounds, who are the very groups at highest risk.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Screening works because of the natural history of bowel cancer:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Polyp-to-cancer pathway:} most bowel cancers arise from adenomatous polyps that take roughly 5--10 years to become invasive, creating a window for detection and removal.
    \item \textbf{Occult bleeding:} polyps and early cancers shed small amounts of blood into the stool, usually too small to be seen, but detectable by the FOBT.
    \item \textbf{The test:} the FOBT uses an immunochemical method to detect human haemoglobin in stool samples, so it is not confounded by dietary blood from red meat.
    \item \textbf{Interruption of the pathway:} polyps found at colonoscopy are removed (polypectomy), which prevents progression to cancer.
    \item \textbf{Earlier stage at diagnosis:} screen-detected cancers are typically earlier stage than those found through symptoms, and early-stage bowel cancer is highly curable.
    \item \textbf{Non-bleeding lesions:} a small proportion of polyps and cancers bleed intermittently or not at all, which is why repeated screening every two years is important.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Screening tests people who have no symptoms -- that is the entire point, because early bowel cancer usually causes none. The warning signs below should still be taken to a doctor promptly, whether or not screening has been done, because a negative test does not guarantee protection:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A change in bowel habit lasting more than a few weeks
    \item Blood in the stool or bleeding from the bottom
    \item Unexplained weight loss or tiredness
    \item Abdominal pain, cramping, or bloating
    \item A feeling that the bowel does not empty completely
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A positive screening result means only that blood was found in the stool; it does not mean a person has cancer, and most positive results are due to other causes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Haemorrhoids (piles):} the most common cause of trace bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Anal fissures:} small tears that bleed with defecation.
    \item \textbf{Diverticular disease:} outpouchings of the bowel wall that can bleed.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory bowel disease:} ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease cause bowel inflammation and bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Benign polyps:} adenomas that have not become malignant.
    \item \textbf{Menstrual bleeding or other sources:} can contaminate samples if collected incorrectly, which is why kit instructions must be followed.
    \item \textbf{Bowel cancer:} the minority of positive results are from cancer, which is why prompt follow-up colonoscopy is essential.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
A person who has been screened should still see a doctor without delay if any warning symptoms develop between screening rounds. Anyone with a positive FOBT result must attend the follow-up test recommended by their doctor, usually a colonoscopy, and this should not be postponed out of embarrassment or fear.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain with vomiting and an inability to open the bowels (possible bowel obstruction)
    \item Sudden heavy bleeding from the bottom with faintness, light-headedness, or collapse
    \item Severe abdominal pain with fever, which may indicate a perforated bowel
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Faecal occult blood test (FOBT):} a home test in which small stool samples are collected over two consecutive days and posted to a laboratory; results are returned by mail, and positive results trigger an invitation for follow-up.
    \item \textbf{Colonoscopy:} the standard follow-up investigation, in which the whole large bowel is examined with a camera under sedation, and polyps can be removed during the same procedure.
    \item \textbf{Sigmoidoscopy:} examination of the lower bowel and rectum, used in some screening pathways or for people who cannot have a full colonoscopy.
    \item \textbf{CT colonography:} a CT scan of the bowel, an alternative for people who cannot undergo colonoscopy.
    \item \textbf{Repeat testing:} people with a negative FOBT are invited again in two years; a single negative result provides only time-limited reassurance.
    \item \textbf{Risk-adapted assessment:} people with a family history of bowel cancer or long-standing inflammatory bowel disease may be offered colonoscopy directly rather than FOBT screening.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Screening is only useful if the results are acted upon:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Complete the test:} follow the kit instructions, return the samples promptly, and do not put the test off.
    \item \textbf{Act on a positive result:} attend the follow-up colonoscopy; most people will not have cancer, but this is the only way to be certain.
    \item \textbf{Polypectomy:} polyps found at colonoscopy are usually removed immediately, preventing future bowel cancer.
    \item \textbf{Treatment of screen-detected cancer:} follows the same principles as bowel cancer found through symptoms -- surgery with chemotherapy or radiotherapy as needed -- but at a much earlier and more curable stage.
    \item \textbf{Surveillance:} people found to have polyps are given a plan for repeat colonoscopy at appropriate intervals, which continues to protect them for years.
    \item \textbf{Risk-adapted screening:} earlier or more frequent screening for those at increased risk, arranged through a GP.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Screening is very safe overall, but there are limitations and small risks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{False-negative results:} the test can miss polyps or cancers that are not bleeding at the time.
    \item \textbf{False-positive results:} blood from another cause leads to a colonoscopy that shows no cancer, causing temporary anxiety.
    \item \textbf{Over-diagnosis:} detection of polyps or slow-growing cancers that might never have caused harm, with the risks of treatment attached.
    \item \textbf{Colonoscopy risks:} uncommon, but include bleeding after polyp removal and perforation (a tear) of the bowel.
    \item \textbf{Missed lesions:} colonoscopy can miss flat polyps or those hidden behind folds.
    \item \textbf{False reassurance:} some people screen once and stop, losing the cumulative benefit of regular testing.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Screening substantially improves the outlook for bowel cancer. Screen-detected cancers are much more likely to be at an early stage, and early-stage bowel cancer has a five-year survival of more than 90\%. Randomised trials and national programs show that regular screening reduces deaths from bowel cancer by roughly a third, and removal of polyps prevents many cancers from ever developing. The benefits accumulate over repeated rounds, so completing the test every two years for as long as you are eligible is far more valuable than a single one-off test. For people found to have polyps, surveillance colonoscopy provides ongoing protection against future cancer.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Complete your free FOBT kit when it arrives, every two years from age 50 (or as recommended for your risk group).
    \item Do not let embarrassment or fear of a positive result stop you; screening is routine, painless, and saves lives.
    \item Ask a GP or pharmacist for help understanding the kit if you need it.
    \item Discuss earlier or more frequent screening with your doctor if you have a family history of bowel cancer or inflammatory bowel disease.
    \item Report any change in bowel habit or bleeding to a doctor, even between screening rounds.
    \item Eat a high-fibre diet, limit red and processed meat, keep a healthy weight, and stay active.
    \item Limit alcohol and do not smoke.
    \item Attend follow-up colonoscopies at the intervals your doctor recommends.
\end{itemize}
