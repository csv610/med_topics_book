\chapter{Calcium Channel Blockers (CCB)}
\index{Calcium Channel Blockers (CCB)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Calcium channel blockers (CCBs) are a group of medicines used mainly to treat high blood pressure, angina (chest pain caused by reduced blood flow to the heart), and certain heart rhythm problems. They work by relaxing and widening the blood vessels, which lowers blood pressure, and by reducing the workload on the heart.

Common examples include amlodipine, nifedipine, diltiazem, and verapamil. CCBs are among the most widely prescribed medicines for cardiovascular disease and are frequently combined with other blood pressure medicines to achieve good control. They are generally well tolerated, and most side effects are mild and manageable.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Calcium channel blockers are among the most commonly prescribed medicines worldwide for high blood pressure, which affects roughly one in three adults. They are particularly useful in older people and in people of African or South Asian background, in whom they often work better than some other drug classes. Amlodipine is one of the most frequently prescribed medicines in many countries. CCBs are also widely used to prevent angina attacks and to control certain heart rhythm disorders such as supraventricular tachycardia.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Calcium channel blockers work by blocking the entry of calcium into the muscle cells of the heart and blood vessels. Calcium is required for these cells to contract, so blocking its entry makes the muscles relax:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vessel relaxation:} dilating the arteries reduces the resistance the heart must pump against, lowering blood pressure
    \item \textbf{Heart effects:} some CCBs also slow the heart rate and reduce the force of heart contractions, which helps in angina and rhythm problems
    \item \textbf{Two classes:} dihydropyridines (such as amlodipine and nifedipine) act mainly on blood vessels, while non-dihydropyridines (diltiazem and verapamil) act mainly on the heart
    \item \textbf{Distinct mechanism:} CCBs work differently from beta blockers, although both are used for similar conditions and may be combined
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The medicine itself produces few symptoms at the right dose, but the conditions it treats and its side effects have recognisable features:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Therapeutic effect:} lower blood pressure, fewer or less severe angina attacks, and better control of heart rhythm
    \item \textbf{Ankle swelling:} fluid pooling in the lower legs is a common side effect of amlodipine and nifedipine
    \item \textbf{Flushing and headache:} common soon after starting a CCB and often settling within the first week or two
    \item \textbf{Dizziness and fatigue:} may occur if blood pressure drops too low
    \item \textbf{Slow heart rate:} more likely with verapamil and diltiazem
    \item \textbf{Constipation:} a well-known side effect of verapamil
    \item \textbf{Gum swelling:} uncommon, but can occur with long-term use
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Side effects of CCBs can be confused with other problems:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ankle swelling from heart failure, kidney disease, or other medicines such as steroids
    \item Headache from migraine or tension-type headache
    \item Dizziness from low blood pressure of other causes, dehydration, or inner-ear problems
    \item A slow heart rate from an underlying heart problem or other medicines such as beta blockers
    \item Constipation from other medicines, poor diet, or bowel disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People should contact a doctor if they develop troublesome side effects while taking a CCB or if blood pressure control seems poor.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, pressure, or tightness that is new or worse than usual
    \item Fainting, severe dizziness, or an unusually slow or irregular heartbeat
    \item Sudden difficulty breathing or swelling of the face, lips, or tongue, which may indicate an allergic reaction
    \item Severe or unusual swelling in the legs
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis concerns the conditions being treated and any suspected side effects:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure measurement:} to check that treatment is working and to detect a drop that is too large
    \item \textbf{Heart assessment:} an electrocardiogram (ECG) to check heart rate and rhythm, especially with verapamil or diltiazem
    \item \textbf{Kidney and liver tests:} blood tests to monitor kidney function and to adjust the dose if needed
    \item \textbf{Medication review:} checking for interactions with other medicines
    \item \textbf{Review of side effects:} asking about swelling, dizziness, headache, and constipation at each visit
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management centres on choosing the right medicine and dose and on dealing with side effects:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Regular dosing:} take the medicine at the same time each day, as prescribed, and do not stop suddenly
    \item \textbf{Combination therapy:} CCBs are often combined with other blood pressure medicines to improve control
    \item \textbf{Managing ankle swelling:} reduce the dose, change to another drug class, or use a medicine that affects both vessel types; elevating the legs may help
    \item \textbf{Managing constipation:} increase fluid and fibre, and seek advice if verapamil is the cause
    \item \textbf{Regular monitoring:} review blood pressure, heart rate, and side effects at check-ups
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} weight, diet, exercise, salt, and alcohol all affect blood pressure and work alongside the medicine
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Low blood pressure with dizziness and fainting if the dose is too high
    \item A very slow heart rate, mainly with verapamil and diltiazem
    \item Worsening of heart failure symptoms in people with poor heart pump function, especially with verapamil
    \item Interactions with other medicines, such as beta blockers, which can slow the heart excessively
    \item Grapefruit juice, which can raise the blood level of some CCBs and cause side effects
    \item Sudden withdrawal, which can worsen angina or raise blood pressure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Calcium channel blockers are very effective and are taken long-term by many people with high blood pressure and angina. When taken as prescribed, they substantially reduce the risk of stroke, heart attack, and kidney damage. Most side effects are mild and settle with dose adjustment or a change of medicine. Because high blood pressure usually causes no symptoms, the medicine must be continued even when the person feels well, to protect against future cardiovascular events.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take your CCB at the same time daily and never stop it without medical advice
    \item Check and record your blood pressure at home as recommended by your doctor
    \item Limit salt, keep alcohol moderate, and stay physically active to support blood pressure control
    \item Avoid grapefruit and grapefruit juice if your medicine is affected, and ask your pharmacist
    \item Report ankle swelling, dizziness, or any new symptoms at your next review
    \item Tell every doctor and pharmacist you see that you take a calcium channel blocker
    \item Do not take cold-and-flu or pain medicines without checking for interactions first
\end{itemize}
