\chapter{Candidal Onychomycosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Candidal onychomycosis is a fungal infection of the nail caused by \textit{Candida} species, most commonly \textit{Candida albicans}. While most fungal nail disease is caused by dermatophyte moulds, \textit{Candida} characteristically invades the fingernails rather than the toenails, usually beginning at the base of the nail (the proximal nail fold) or along the side of the nail, and frequently following damage to the cuticle or prolonged exposure to moisture. Unlike the classic fungal nail infection that discolours and thickens the whole nail from the tip, candidal infection typically causes swelling of the nail folds, separation of the nail from its bed, and a smooth, glassy nail surface. Candidal onychomycosis is one of several clinical forms of cutaneous candidiasis and is closely related to chronic paronychia, with which it often coexists.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Candidal nail infection is much less common than dermatophyte nail fungus and is seen mainly in people whose hands are frequently wet, such as cleaners, food handlers, bartenders, and hairdressers. It is more common in women, in people with diabetes, and in anyone with a weakened immune system, including those with HIV infection, those receiving chemotherapy, and organ transplant recipients. Candidal nail disease is also a recognised feature of chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis, an uncommon condition in which the body is unable to clear \textit{Candida} from the skin, nails, and mucous membranes. In contrast to dermatophyte infection, which preferentially affects the feet, the feet are rarely affected by candidal onychomycosis. The condition may account for a small but clinically important proportion of all cases of nail fungus, and it tends to be chronic and relapsing where occupational hand wetness persists.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\textit{Candida} yeasts live harmlessly as commensals on the skin and in the gastrointestinal tract of most healthy people. Infection develops when the normal skin barrier is broken and the organism grows into the nail folds and nail plate. Factors that allow this transition from colonisation to infection include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prolonged wetness:} constant immersion of the hands in water softens the keratin and strips protective oils
    \item \textbf{Injury:} damage to the cuticle or nail base, including over-zealous manicuring and cuticle trimming
    \item \textbf{Chronic paronychia:} longstanding inflammation of the nail folds, which destroys the seal between nail and skin
    \item \textbf{Immunosuppression:} from diabetes, HIV infection, chemotherapy, or corticosteroid treatment
    \item \textbf{Poor circulation} in the fingers, which impairs local immune defence
    \item \textbf{Underlying nail disorders} such as psoriasis, which distort the nail plate
\end{itemize}
The yeast grows down into the potential space between the nail fold and the nail plate, provoking swelling and inflammation and eventually lifting the nail away from its bed (onycholysis). If untreated, the nail plate itself becomes invaded, thickened, and distorted, and secondary bacterial infection may supervene.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Candidal nail infection typically affects one or more fingernails and may show:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Redness, swelling, and tenderness of the nail fold (paronychia)
    \item Separation of the nail from the nail bed, beginning at the base or side
    \item A whitish, greenish, or brown discolouration of the nail
    \item The nail becoming thickened, ridged, or crumbly at the edges
    \item A smooth, glassy appearance of the nail surface with loss of the cuticle
    \item Absence of pain in mild cases, but discomfort when the nail fold is inflamed
    \item Involvement of several fingernails at once, often with fluctuant swelling that comes and goes over months or years
\end{itemize}
The symptoms can fluctuate with episodes of acute inflammation superimposed on chronic change, particularly when the hands are exposed to water or irritants.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A number of other conditions can look similar to candidal nail infection, and the distinction matters because treatments differ:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dermatophyte fungal infection of the nail, the common form of nail fungus, which usually starts at the free edge of the toenails
    \item Chronic bacterial paronychia, frequently due to \textit{Pseudomonas} species, which may produce greenish discolouration
    \item Nail psoriasis, with pitting, oil spots, and onycholysis
    \item Eczema of the hands with secondary nail changes
    \item Trauma-related nail separation (traumatic onycholysis) in the absence of infection
    \item Lichen planus affecting the nails, which can cause thinning, ridging, and scarring
    \item Nail changes in thyroid disease, anaemia, or other systemic conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor or podiatrist if you have persistent nail or nail-fold symptoms, especially if:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The nail fold is red, swollen, and painful
    \item The nail is separating, discoloured, or crumbling
    \item You have diabetes or a weakened immune system
    \item The problem is spreading to other fingers or does not improve with self-care
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rapidly spreading redness, heat, or swelling of the finger or hand with fever
    \item Signs of a serious bacterial infection, such as severe pain and feeling systemically unwell
    \item Spreading red streaks up the arm (lymphangitis), which suggest bloodstream spread
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} reviewing moisture exposure, occupation, immune status, and the appearance of the nails, cuticles, and skin
    \item \textbf{Microscopy of nail scrapings and subungual debris} treated with potassium hydroxide to look for budding yeast cells and pseudohyphae
    \item \textbf{Fungal culture:} to confirm \textit{Candida} and distinguish it from dermatophytes, which guides the choice of antifungal
    \item \textbf{Nail clipping for laboratory analysis:} polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing may be used where available for rapid species identification
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} for example, blood glucose or immune function tests if diabetes or another predisposing condition is suspected
    \item \textbf{Biopsy} is rarely needed but may be considered in atypical, resistant, or diagnostic uncertainty
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment works best when the contributing factors, such as repeated hand wetness or poorly controlled diabetes, are also addressed; topical agents alone rarely succeed while the hands remain constantly wet.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Keeping the nails dry:} the single most important step; use gloves for wet work and dry the hands thoroughly afterwards
    \item \textbf{Topical antifungals:} creams or solutions containing clotrimazole, miconazole, or ketoconazole applied to the nail folds and nail, often needed for several months
    \item \textbf{Antifungal nail lacquer} or medicated solutions where the plate itself is involved
    \item \textbf{Oral antifungal medicines:} such as fluconazole or itraconazole, particularly for infection of the nail plate or in people who do not respond to topical treatment
    \item \textbf{Gentle nail-fold care:} avoid aggressive pushing back or cutting of the cuticle, and treat inflammation of the fold early
    \item \textbf{Treatment of any underlying condition:} such as improved blood sugar control in diabetes
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} repeated clinical checks, because the nail grows slowly and relapse is common
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent or recurrent infection when occupational or habitual hand wetness continues
    \item Spread to other fingernails or to the skin of the hands
    \item Permanent nail deformity or complete loss of the nail in severe, longstanding cases
    \item Secondary bacterial infection of the nail folds, sometimes requiring antibiotics
    \item Oral antifungal side effects, including nausea, headache, drug interactions, and liver enzyme changes that require monitoring with blood tests
    \item In immunocompromised people, progression of \textit{Candida} beyond the nail, with the theoretical risk of systemic candidiasis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With treatment and attention to drying, most candidal nail infections improve over several months. The nail grows slowly, so full recovery can take six months to a year or longer, and the nail may remain ridged or slightly discoloured for a while after the infection has cleared. Relapse is common if the hands are frequently immersed in water or the underlying predisposition is not corrected. In people with a normal immune system and good self-care, the outlook is generally good, and permanent damage is uncommon with prompt treatment.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep the hands as dry as possible; wear gloves for dishwashing, cleaning, and wet work, and change wet gloves promptly
    \item Dry thoroughly between the fingers and around the nails after washing
    \item Avoid biting the nails or picking at the cuticles
    \item Be gentle when manicuring; do not push the cuticle back aggressively or cut it deeply
    \item Treat any early redness or swelling of the nail fold promptly with a topical antifungal
    \item Manage diabetes and other predisposing conditions with medical help
    \item Do not share nail care equipment, and keep personal tools clean and dry
\end{itemize}
