\chapter{Baby Immunisation}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Baby immunisation is the giving of vaccines to infants according to a scheduled timetable, so that they develop protection against serious infectious diseases before they are most at risk. Vaccines work by training the immune system to recognise and fight specific germs, using a harmless part of the germ or a weakened form of it.

In Australia, babies are offered vaccination under the National Immunisation Program at birth and again at two, four, six, twelve and eighteen months. These vaccines protect against diseases including hepatitis B, diphtheria, tetanus, whooping cough (pertussis), polio, Haemophilus influenzae type b, pneumococcal disease, rotavirus, meningococcal disease, and measles, mumps and rubella. Immunisation is one of the most effective public health measures ever developed.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Vaccine-preventable diseases once caused enormous illness and death in children. Immunisation has reduced cases of measles, whooping cough, meningitis and other diseases by more than 95\% in vaccinated populations, and smallpox has been eradicated worldwide. In Australia, coverage among one-year-olds for the core vaccines is high, around nine in ten children.

When coverage falls, diseases return: outbreaks of measles and whooping cough occur mainly in communities with low vaccination rates. Unvaccinated babies are also protected indirectly by herd immunity, because most people around them cannot catch or spread the infection. Diseases that are now rare, such as polio and diphtheria, could return quickly if vaccination were stopped.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Vaccines work with the body's own defences. When a vaccine is given, the immune system recognises the vaccine components as foreign and produces antibodies and memory cells. If the real germ is encountered later, the immune system responds quickly and usually prevents illness or makes it much milder.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Live attenuated vaccines:} weakened forms of the germ (e.g.\ measles, mumps, rubella, rotavirus) that provoke a strong, lasting response
    \item \textbf{Inactivated vaccines:} killed germs or parts of them (e.g.\ polio, hepatitis B, whooping cough)
    \item \textbf{Toxoid vaccines:} inactivated toxins that cause disease (e.g.\ tetanus, diphtheria)
    \item \textbf{Conjugate vaccines:} germ sugars joined to a protein to improve the response in young infants (e.g.\ pneumococcal, meningococcal, Hib)
    \item \textbf{Combination vaccines:} several vaccines in one injection, reducing the number of needles
\end{itemize}

Babies are vaccinated on schedule because their immune systems respond well to vaccines but their natural immunity to these diseases is still developing, and infections can be most dangerous in the first year of life.

\section*{Clinical Features}
At each vaccination visit, the baby receives the vaccines due for their age, usually as injections into the thigh or arm and, for rotavirus, as drops into the mouth. Most babies cope well, especially when comforted during the needle.

After vaccination, it is normal to see mild effects for a day or two.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Soreness, redness or slight swelling at the injection site
    \item Mild fever
    \item Irritability, fussiness or drowsiness
    \item Reduced appetite for a short time
    \item For rotavirus vaccine, mild loose stools or tummy upset
    \item Serious reactions are very rare
\end{itemize}

These effects show that the immune system is responding, and they are not the same as the disease the vaccine prevents.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Symptoms after vaccination are sometimes confused with other things, and parents may wonder whether a reaction is normal.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A mild fever after vaccination versus an early infection, which is common and coincidental in babies
    \item Redness at the injection site versus a wound infection, which is extremely unlikely but should be considered if it worsens or becomes tender
    \item Normal post-vaccine irritability versus a baby who is genuinely unwell
    \item A rash after MMR vaccine versus measles or another viral rash
    \item An allergic reaction versus a normal injection-site response
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if a baby seems more unwell than the mild, expected reaction, or if fever, irritability or local swelling is getting worse rather than settling.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for signs of a serious reaction:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, wheezing or a hoarse voice
    \item Swelling of the face, lips or throat
    \item Collapse, going limp or a fit
    \item A widespread rash, especially one that does not fade when pressed
    \item High-pitched, persistent crying that does not settle
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Immunisation itself does not require tests, but the vaccination record is checked at each visit to ensure the schedule is on track.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vaccination record check:} review of the schedule and any doses already given, including through the Australian Immunisation Register
    \item \textbf{Catch-up planning:} if a dose was missed or delayed, a schedule is drawn up to bring the child up to date
    \item \textbf{Screening before vaccination:} questions about previous reactions, allergies and current illness
    \item \textbf{Assessment of reactions:} if an adverse event is suspected, a doctor takes a history and examines the baby, and the event is reported to the vaccine safety system
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The main management task is giving the vaccines safely and on time.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Following the schedule:} vaccines are given at birth, two, four, six, twelve and eighteen months
    \item \textbf{Combination vaccines:} reduce the number of injections at each visit
    \item \textbf{Pain relief during vaccination:} comfort, breastfeeding and gentle holding help babies cope
    \item \textbf{Paracetamol:} can be given after vaccination if needed for fever or discomfort, as advised by the nurse or doctor
    \item \textbf{Catch-up immunisation:} available for children who have missed doses; it is never too late to start
    \item \textbf{Managing adverse events:} mild reactions settle on their own; any serious reaction is reported and future doses planned accordingly
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Mild local reactions and fever, which are common and harmless
    \item Febrile seizures, which can rarely follow some vaccines, are brief and do not cause lasting harm
    \item Allergic reactions, which are very rare and usually occur within minutes, which is why babies are observed briefly after vaccination
    \item The complications of the diseases themselves, which are far more common and serious when children are not vaccinated: measles can cause pneumonia and brain inflammation, whooping cough can be fatal in young babies, and meningitis can cause deafness or brain damage
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Vaccinated children have a very low risk of the diseases the vaccines prevent, and protection from most infant vaccines lasts for many years. Some need booster doses later in childhood and adult life to maintain protection, which is why the schedule continues beyond infancy. The safety record of infant vaccines is strong, and serious reactions are extremely rare. When a baby is unvaccinated and catches a preventable disease, the outcome is far less certain, so staying on schedule is the best protection.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Start immunisation on time, at birth, and keep every appointment on the schedule
    \item Keep a record of vaccinations, including through the Australian Immunisation Register
    \item Ask the nurse or doctor about any concerns before the vaccine is given
    \item Comfort and breastfeed or cuddle the baby during and after the needle
    \item Offer paracetamol only if advised, and only for genuine fever or discomfort
    \item Report any unusual reaction to the vaccination provider
    \item Keep siblings and other household members up to date, so they do not bring disease home to the baby
\end{itemize}
