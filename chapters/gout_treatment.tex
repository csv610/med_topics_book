\chapter{Gout Treatment}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Gout treatment has two goals: relieving the pain of acute attacks and lowering uric acid levels to prevent future attacks and joint damage. Acute attacks are treated with anti-inflammatory medicines started as early as possible, together with rest and ice. Long-term treatment uses urate-lowering medicines, principally allopurinol, taken daily to keep uric acid below the target level. Lifestyle measures, including reducing alcohol and purine-rich foods, maintaining a healthy weight, and staying well hydrated, support the effects of medication. With a combination of prompt attack treatment, effective urate-lowering therapy, and sensible lifestyle changes, gout can be controlled completely in most people.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Gout is the most common inflammatory arthritis in Australia, affecting around 4--6\% of adults, and its prevalence has risen in recent decades. Most people with gout have recurrent attacks, and around a third have tophi or chronic gouty arthritis if undertreated. Gout treatment is highly effective, yet studies consistently show it is underused: many people never start urate-lowering therapy, and of those who do, a large proportion stop within a year. Improving the use of effective treatment is a key priority, because untreated gout causes significant pain, disability, and reduced quality of life.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Treatment is based on the mechanisms of gout: urate crystal formation and the inflammatory response to crystals.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hyperuricaemia:} high blood uric acid, from overproduction or under-excretion, is the underlying driver
    \item \textbf{Crystal formation:} urate crystals deposit in joints, where they trigger attacks of inflammation
    \item \textbf{Acute inflammation:} the immune system attacks the crystals, causing pain, redness, heat, and swelling
    \item \textbf{Chronic damage:} persistent crystals cause tophi, joint erosion, and kidney stones
    \item \textbf{Lowering uric acid:} treatment reduces the supply of crystals, so existing deposits dissolve and new ones do not form
    \item \textbf{Attack prevention:} keeping uric acid low prevents the immune flare that produces symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Treatment is tailored to the phases and severity of the disease.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A first or recurrent acute attack: sudden severe joint pain, swelling, and redness
    \item Frequent attacks, typically two or more per year
    \item Tophi, chronic gouty arthritis, or joint damage
    \item Kidney stones related to uric acid
    \item High uric acid levels between attacks in a person with gout
    \item Risk factors requiring attention, including obesity, high blood pressure, kidney disease, and diuretic use
    \item Gout in people with coexisting conditions that affect medicine choice
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before treatment, the diagnosis of gout should be confirmed and other conditions excluded.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Septic arthritis, which requires urgent antibiotics and joint drainage
    \item Pseudogout, caused by calcium pyrophosphate crystals
    \item Cellulitis and other skin or soft tissue infections
    \item Trauma and fracture
    \item Other inflammatory arthritides, including rheumatoid and psoriatic arthritis
    \item Osteoarthritis
    \item Reactive arthritis and other crystal or autoimmune conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for any suspected gout attack, and for recurrent attacks to establish a long-term treatment plan. Seek urgent review for a hot, swollen joint with fever, because this can be a joint infection.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A hot, swollen joint with fever or severe illness, which may be septic arthritis
    \item Sudden severe chest pain or breathlessness
    \item Signs of a severe medicine reaction, including extensive rash, fever, or swelling after starting a new gout medicine
    \item Severe abdominal or flank pain with inability to pass urine, which may indicate a kidney stone
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Treatment is guided by an accurate diagnosis and monitoring.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} the classic pattern of attacks and examination of affected joints
    \item \textbf{Blood uric acid:} measured to plan and monitor treatment; the target is usually below 0.36 mmol/L
    \item \textbf{Joint aspiration:} confirms the diagnosis and excludes infection when there is doubt
    \item \textbf{Kidney and liver function:} baseline tests guide medicine choice and doses
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} ultrasound or X-rays to detect crystal deposits and joint damage
    \item \textbf{Regular monitoring:} uric acid levels and side-effect checks during treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Gout treatment is planned in stages and individualised.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute attack treatment:} NSAIDs, colchicine, or corticosteroids started within hours and continued until the attack settles, usually a few days
    \item \textbf{Supportive measures:} rest the joint, elevate it, and apply ice packs
    \item \textbf{Urate-lowering therapy:} allopurinol is first line, started two to four weeks after the attack settles, at a low dose and increased gradually
    \item \textbf{Flare prophylaxis:} low-dose colchicine or an NSAID during the first months of urate-lowering therapy
    \item \textbf{Treat to target:} dose adjustment until uric acid reaches the target level
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} limit alcohol (especially beer), reduce red meat and seafood, drink plenty of water, and lose weight gradually
    \item \textbf{Lifelong therapy:} urate-lowering treatment is usually continued indefinitely
    \item \textbf{Managing coexisting disease:} treatment of hypertension, kidney disease, and other conditions associated with gout
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Untreated: recurrent attacks, chronic arthritis, tophi, joint deformity, and kidney stones
    \item Poorly controlled gout: reduced mobility, work absence, and impaired quality of life
    \item Side effects of treatment medicines
    \item Flares when urate-lowering therapy is started without prophylaxis
    \item Interactions between gout medicines and other medications
    \item Increased cardiovascular risk, which is associated with gout and its risk factors
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Gout is one of the most treatable forms of arthritis, and complete control is achievable in most people. Acute attacks resolve within days with early treatment, and urate-lowering therapy eliminates attacks within months in the large majority of adherent patients. Tophi dissolve over months to years, joint damage stops, and quality of life is restored. The keys to success are early treatment of attacks, consistent long-term urate-lowering therapy, reaching the target uric acid level, and combining medication with lifestyle change.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep acute-attack medicines available and start treatment at the first symptom
    \item Take urate-lowering therapy every day as prescribed
    \item Attend blood tests to keep uric acid in the target range
    \item Limit alcohol, especially beer, and avoid binge drinking
    \item Reduce intake of red meat, organ meats, and high-purine seafood
    \item Drink at least two litres of water daily
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and avoid crash dieting
    \item Exercise regularly, but avoid dehydration
    \item Discuss all medicines, including diuretics, with your doctor
    \item Seek medical review before stopping any gout medicine
\end{itemize}
