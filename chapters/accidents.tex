\chapter{Accidents}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An accident, in medical terms, is an unintended event that causes injury, such as a road traffic crash, a fall, a burn, a poisoning, or a near-drowning. The field of injury prevention now prefers the term unintentional injury, reflecting that these events are rarely random and are largely preventable. Unintentional injuries are a leading cause of death and disability worldwide, particularly among the young, yet a large proportion can be prevented through simple, well-established measures. This chapter provides an overview of how accidents happen, how injuries are assessed and treated, and how the risk of them occurring at home, on the road, at work, and during sport can be reduced.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Unintentional injuries are among the leading causes of death and hospitalisation in most countries and are the leading cause of death in children and young adults. Road traffic crashes, falls, drowning, burns, and poisoning account for the great majority of this burden. Males are injured more often than females at every age. Very young children and older adults are at particular risk of injuries in the home, while workplace and sporting injuries predominantly affect working-age people. Beyond deaths, accidents cause millions of non-fatal injuries each year, many leading to long-term disability.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Accidents result from a combination of hazards, behaviour, and circumstances, classically described as the interaction between the person, the vehicle or equipment, and the environment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blunt force:} road crashes and falls crush and tear tissue, causing bruising, fractures, and internal organ injury
    \item \textbf{Penetrating injury:} cuts and objects that pierce the body
    \item \textbf{Thermal injury:} burns and scalds from heat, and frostbite from cold
    \item \textbf{Chemical and electrical injury:} corrosive substances and electric current damaging tissue directly
    \item \textbf{Poisoning:} medicines, household chemicals, or other substances swallowed or inhaled
    \item \textbf{Asphyxia and drowning:} oxygen deprivation from water, smoke inhalation, or choking
\end{itemize}

Severe injury triggers bleeding, tissue swelling, and a widespread inflammatory response that can damage organs remote from the site of injury. Head injury and major haemorrhage are the main causes of death in the early hours after serious accidents.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features depend on the type, site, and severity of the injury.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain, tenderness, swelling, bruising, or deformity at the injured site
    \item A wound, graze, or break in the skin
    \item Inability to move, use, or weight-bear through a limb
    \item Numbness, tingling, or a cold, pale limb beyond the injury, suggesting damage to nerves or blood vessels
    \item Confusion, headache, vomiting, or loss of consciousness after a head injury
    \item Difficulty breathing, cough, or chest pain after a chest injury
    \item Abdominal pain, tenderness, or signs of internal bleeding after abdominal injury
    \item Burns, blistering, or signs of smoke in the airway after a fire
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Assessment of an injured person must exclude important alternative explanations.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Fracture versus soft-tissue injury such as a sprain, strain, or bruise
    \item Closed versus open (compound) fracture
    \item Head injury versus a medical collapse that caused the accident, such as a seizure, fainting, or an arrhythmia
    \item Accidental versus intentional injury or self-harm, an important distinction particularly in children
    \item Internal organ injury versus simple bruising
    \item Spinal injury versus simple muscular pain after a fall
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:} any major accident with suspected head or spine injury, severe bleeding, difficulty breathing, unconsciousness, or a serious burn.

Otherwise, medical care should be sought for the following.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A suspected broken bone, or a limb that looks deformed or cannot be used
    \item A deep, dirty, or gaping wound, or one containing a foreign body
    \item Persistent numbness, tingling, or colour change below an injury
    \item A head injury with confusion, repeated vomiting, or a worsening headache
    \item Burns larger than the person's hand, or burns to the face, hands, feet, or genitals
    \item Suspected poisoning, however mild it appears
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Serious injury is assessed systematically, beginning with life-threatening problems.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary survey:} checking and managing the airway, breathing, circulation, and conscious state in the field and in the emergency department
    \item \textbf{History:} how the accident happened, whether consciousness was lost, and the person's pre-existing conditions and medications
    \item \textbf{Examination:} systematic assessment of the injured region together with a full check for other injuries
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} X-rays for suspected fractures; CT scans for head, spine, and serious internal injuries; and ultrasound to detect intra-abdominal bleeding
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} to assess blood loss, with blood gas and lactate measurement in severe injury
    \item \textbf{Toxicology:} tests for suspected poisoning
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management spans first aid through to rehabilitation.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First aid:} protect the airway, control bleeding with direct pressure, and cool burns with running water
    \item \textbf{Emergency transport:} ambulance transfer for serious injury
    \item \textbf{Resuscitation:} intravenous fluids, blood transfusion, and support of breathing and circulation as needed
    \item \textbf{Wound care:} cleaning, closure, and tetanus vaccination where indicated
    \item \textbf{Immobilisation:} splints, casts, and slings for fractures and dislocations
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} to fix fractures, repair injured organs, blood vessels, or nerves, or to relieve pressure in the head or a limb
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple analgesics for minor injuries and stronger options in hospital
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy and a graded return to activity and work
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wound infection, including infection of broken bones and of the chest
    \item Compartment syndrome, in which swelling within a limb compromises its blood supply
    \item Deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism after prolonged immobility
    \item Slow or failed healing of fractures
    \item Head injury complications, including bleeding on the brain
    \item Long-term disability, chronic pain, and post-traumatic arthritis
    \item Post-traumatic stress and anxiety following the accident
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Minor accidents heal within days to weeks with simple care. Simple fractures usually unite within 6--8 weeks, with longer times in older adults. Serious accidents can cause long-term disability, and recovery depends on the severity of the injury, the quality of care, and the effectiveness of rehabilitation. Most injuries, however, heal well, and many serious outcomes are substantially reduced by prompt first aid and hospital treatment. The emotional effects of an accident can persist even after physical recovery and should not be overlooked.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wear seatbelts and helmets, follow speed limits, and never drive under the influence of alcohol or drugs
    \item Install and maintain smoke alarms and have a home escape plan
    \item Store medicines, cleaning products, and poisons out of children's reach
    \item Fence and supervise pools, and supervise young children near water
    \item Use appropriate protective equipment for sport and work
    \item Keep homes well lit and fix or remove trip hazards
    \item Learn basic first aid and cardiopulmonary resuscitation
    \item Manage medical conditions such as epilepsy or diabetes that can cause collapse and lead to accidents
\end{itemize}
