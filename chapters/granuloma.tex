\chapter{Granuloma}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A granuloma is a small area of chronic inflammation in which the body's immune system walls off an irritant that it cannot eliminate. Granulomas form when immune cells, mainly macrophages, cluster together in a nodule to contain foreign material, bacteria, fungi, or material it cannot break down. They can occur in any organ but are most often found in the lungs, skin, and lymph nodes. Granulomas can be caused by infections such as tuberculosis, by inflammatory diseases such as sarcoidosis and Crohn disease, or by exposure to substances such as beryllium. Many granulomas cause no symptoms and are found by chance on imaging, while others cause symptoms depending on their location and cause.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Granulomas are a common finding, though their frequency varies with the cause and setting. Pulmonary granulomas are found on a significant proportion of CT scans, often as an incidental finding, and are more common in areas where fungal infections such as histoplasmosis are prevalent. Sarcoidosis, a granulomatous disease, affects around 2--5 people per 100,000 in Australia and is more common in people of African and Scandinavian descent. Tuberculosis, the classic cause of granulomas, is uncommon in Australia but remains a major global cause, with over 10 million cases worldwide each year.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Granulomas form when the immune system cannot clear a persistent irritant.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immune response:} macrophages engulf the offending material but cannot destroy it, so they fuse into multinucleated giant cells and recruit other immune cells, forming a nodule
    \item \textbf{Infections:} tuberculosis and other mycobacteria, fungi such as histoplasmosis, and some parasites
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory diseases:} sarcoidosis, Crohn disease, rheumatoid arthritis, and granulomatosis with polyangiitis
    \item \textbf{Exposure:} beryllium (chronic beryllium disease), talc, and other inhaled particles
    \item \textbf{Foreign bodies:} retained surgical material, splinters, and injected substances
    \item \textbf{Unknown causes:} many granulomas, particularly in the lung, have no identifiable cause
    \item \textbf{Consequences:} granulomas can calcify, remain stable, resolve, or cause organ damage through fibrosis or mass effect
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend entirely on the location and cause of the granuloma.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Lung granulomas: usually no symptoms; found incidentally on imaging, or causing cough, shortness of breath, or chest pain when extensive
    \item Skin granulomas: raised, firm nodules, sometimes with a specific appearance (for example, granuloma annulare)
    \item Sarcoidosis: fatigue, cough, breathlessness, skin lesions, eye problems, and swollen lymph nodes
    \item Tuberculosis: fever, night sweats, weight loss, cough, and sometimes coughing blood
    \item Crohn disease: abdominal pain, diarrhoea, and weight loss
    \item Liver granulomas: usually no symptoms, occasionally abnormal liver tests
    \item Many granulomas cause no symptoms at all
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The cause of a granuloma must be determined, because treatments differ.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection: tuberculosis, fungal infection, and other mycobacterial disease
    \item Sarcoidosis, a multisystem inflammatory disease
    \item Inflammatory bowel disease, particularly Crohn disease
    \item Vasculitis, including granulomatosis with polyangiitis
    \item Autoimmune disease, including rheumatoid arthritis
    \item Foreign body reaction
    \item Occupational exposure, such as beryllium
    \item Cancer, which can sometimes be mistaken for or coexist with granulomas
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if you have persistent cough, fever, weight loss, night sweats, breathlessness, or unexplained lumps or skin lesions, particularly if symptoms persist or worsen.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Coughing up blood
    \item Sudden severe breathlessness or chest pain
    \item High fever with confusion, which may indicate serious infection
    \item Severe abdominal pain or signs of intestinal obstruction in Crohn disease
    \item Sudden loss of vision or severe eye pain if the eyes are affected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Finding a granuloma is often the start of an investigation to determine its cause.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT of the chest or other affected organs shows the size, number, and location of granulomas
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} a tissue sample examined under a microscope confirms the granuloma and may reveal its cause
    \item \textbf{Stains and cultures:} tests for tuberculosis, fungi, and other infections
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} inflammatory markers, calcium, and autoimmune antibodies
    \item \textbf{Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) level:} can be elevated in sarcoidosis
    \item \textbf{Lung function tests:} if the lungs are affected
    \item \textbf{Follow-up imaging:} repeat scans determine whether granulomas are stable, growing, or resolving
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on the cause, location, and symptoms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observation:} stable, asymptomatic granulomas often need no treatment beyond monitoring
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} antituberculous therapy for tuberculosis and related infections
    \item \textbf{Antifungals:} for fungal causes
    \item \textbf{Immunosuppression:} corticosteroids and other immune-modulating medicines for sarcoidosis, vasculitis, and inflammatory disease
    \item \textbf{Management of Crohn disease:} medicines that reduce intestinal inflammation
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} occasionally needed for large, symptomatic, or inaccessible lesions
    \item \textbf{Avoidance:} removing exposure to beryllium or other causative substances
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular review of symptoms, imaging, and organ function
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Organ damage from extensive granulomatous disease, including pulmonary fibrosis and heart, eye, or kidney involvement
    \item Complications of the underlying cause, including untreated tuberculosis
    \item Mass effects from large granulomas, such as airway or bowel obstruction
    \item Side effects of immunosuppressive treatment
    \item Misdiagnosis when a granuloma is assumed benign but the underlying condition is progressive
    \item Psychological impact of finding a "nodule" that may cause anxiety about cancer
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis depends on the cause. Benign, stable granulomas, particularly incidental lung granulomas, have an excellent outlook and need no treatment. Sarcoidosis resolves or stabilises in most people, though some have progressive disease. Tuberculosis is curable with a full course of antibiotics. Granulomatous diseases managed with appropriate immunosuppression usually respond well, though some require long-term treatment. The key to good outcomes is an accurate diagnosis of the cause and appropriate follow-up.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Follow infection-control measures, including vaccination and prompt treatment of tuberculosis contacts
    \item Use protective equipment in workplaces with exposure to beryllium, dust, and particles
    \item Attend follow-up imaging as advised to confirm granulomas are stable
    \item Take prescribed treatment exactly as directed, including the full course of antibiotics
    \item Report new symptoms, such as cough, breathlessness, or weight loss, promptly
    \item Maintain good general health, including not smoking, which reduces lung complications
    \item Seek specialist care for sarcoidosis and other systemic granulomatous diseases
    \item Ask questions if an incidental finding causes anxiety, so you understand what it means
\end{itemize}
