\chapter{Medicines and breastfeeding}
\index{Medicines and breastfeeding}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Medicines and breastfeeding refers to the clinical evaluation, safety assessment, and management of maternal pharmacological therapy during lactation. While the benefits of breastfeeding for both infant nutrition and maternal recovery are well established, many lactating individuals require short-term or chronic medication for acute postpartum complications, pre-existing chronic conditions, or acute illnesses. The primary clinical challenge is to balance the maternal need for effective treatment with the potential risk of infant exposure to the drug via breast milk. The vast majority of medications are compatible with breastfeeding, as only a small fraction of the maternal dose enters breast milk. However, certain drugs are contraindicated, require close monitoring, or can negatively affect milk supply, demanding careful clinical decision-making.

\section*{Epidemiology}
A significant majority of breastfeeding mothers require or use at least one medication during the postpartum period. Epidemiological data indicate that approximately 60\% to 90\% of women take at least one medication (ranging from over-the-counter analgesics to prescription therapies) in the first week postpartum, with usage declining but remaining prevalent throughout the duration of lactation. In many countries and other developed countries, the most common reasons for medication use during lactation include postpartum pain, mastitis, postpartum depression, anxiety, chronic hypertension, and asthma. Despite the safety of most drugs, fear of infant exposure leads to high rates of unnecessary breastfeeding cessation or maternal non-adherence to essential treatments, emphasizing the need for accurate clinical guidance.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The excretion of medicines into breast milk is governed by maternal pharmacokinetics, the physiology of lactation, and infant pharmacology. The transfer of drugs from maternal plasma to breast milk and their subsequent effect on the infant involve:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Passive diffusion:} The primary mechanism by which drugs cross the lipid membrane of the mammary alveolar epithelium, depending on the concentration gradient of the unbound drug between maternal plasma and milk.
    \item \textbf{Molecular weight:} Low molecular weight drugs (under 200 Daltons) pass easily into milk, while large molecules (such as monoclonal antibodies, insulin, or heparin) are largely excluded.
    \item \textbf{Lipid solubility and ionization:} Lipophilic drugs concentrate in the lipid-rich fractions of breast milk. Weakly basic drugs (such as certain beta-blockers) become ionized in the slightly acidic environment of breast milk (pH approximately 7.2 compared to plasma pH 7.4) and become "trapped" (ion trapping), leading to higher milk-to-plasma ratios.
    \item \textbf{Protein binding:} Drugs with high maternal plasma protein binding (such as ibuprofen or warfarin) remain in the maternal circulation, leaving very little free drug available to enter breast milk.
    \item \textbf{Infant pharmacokinetics:} Neonates, particularly premature infants, have immature hepatic conjugation (glucuronidation) and renal excretion pathways, making them highly susceptible to drug accumulation and toxicity.
    \item \textbf{Relative Infant Dose (RID):} The percentage of the maternal weight-adjusted dose received by the infant via milk. An RID of less than 10\% is generally considered clinically safe, with most compatible medicines having an RID of less than 1\%.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Maternal medication use during lactation usually does not cause clinical features in the infant, but adverse events must be monitored. The features to watch for include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sedation and poor feeding:} Somnolence, hypotonia, and a weak suck reflex in the infant, commonly associated with maternal use of central nervous system depressants (such as opioids, benzodiazepines, or sedating antihistamines).
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal distress:} Diarrhoea, loose stools, or colic in the infant, which can occur secondary to maternal antibiotic use altering the infant's gut microbiota.
    \item \textbf{Irritability and sleep disturbance:} Excess crying, hyperactivity, or poor sleep patterns, which may be triggered by high maternal intake of stimulants, caffeine, or certain decongestants.
    \item \textbf{Hypoglycaemia or bradycardia:} Jitteriness, lethargy, or low heart rate, which can occur if the mother is taking certain lipophilic beta-blockers (like atenolol).
    \item \textbf{Reduced milk supply:} Inadequate milk production, which can be caused by medications that inhibit prolactin secretion (such as pseudoephedrine, combined oral contraceptive pills, or dopamine agonists).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When a breastfed infant exhibits symptoms, clinicians must differentiate maternal drug effects from other neonatal conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Drug-induced sedation vs. Neonatal sepsis or jaundice:} Somnolence and poor feeding must be distinguished from systemic infections, hyperbilirubinaemia, or metabolic disorders.
    \item \textbf{Drug-induced diarrhoea vs. Gastroenteritis or cow's milk protein allergy:} Changes in infant stool consistency must be differentiated from primary viral/bacterial infections or dietary intolerances.
    \item \textbf{Infant irritability vs. Colic or gastro-oesophageal reflux:} Crying and sleeplessness may be due to normal developmental colic or reflux rather than maternal caffeine or medication exposure.
    \item \textbf{Maternal low milk supply vs. Poor attachment or infrequent feeding:} Insufficient milk must be evaluated for mechanical breastfeeding issues before blaming maternal pharmacological therapy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Breastfeeding mothers should consult a healthcare professional before starting any new medication.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infant respiratory depression:} Slow, shallow, or irregular breathing, or periods of apnea, which can occur with maternal opioid use (especially codeine).
    \item \textbf{Severe infant lethargy:} An infant who is extremely difficult to wake, will not feed, or is unresponsive.
    \item \textbf{Anaphylaxis in the infant:} Swelling of the lips, face, or tongue, hives, or wheezing after feeding.
    \item \textbf{Acute maternal poisoning:} Accidental or intentional overdose of any medication by the lactating mother.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Evaluating the safety of a medicine during breastfeeding involves clinical assessment and referencing specialized databases rather than invasive tests:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Referencing lactation databases:} Consult reputable resources such as LactMed, the local Medicines Handbook (AMH), or local obstetric drug information services to obtain up-to-date, evidence-based safety data.
    \item \textbf{Determining gestational age and infant clinical status:} Assess the infant's age (preterm vs. full term, as risk drops significantly after 2 months of age) and renal/hepatic function.
    \item \textbf{Calculating Relative Infant Dose (RID):} Estimate the potential infant dose to determine if it falls within the safe threshold (under 10\%).
    \item \textbf{Monitoring infant clinical signs:} Serial weight checks, monitoring wet and dirty nappies, and assessing alertness to confirm normal development during maternal therapy.
    \item \textbf{Infant drug serum levels:} Rarely indicated, but may be measured in specific clinical trials or if toxicity is strongly suspected.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of medicines in breastfeeding focuses on optimizing maternal health while minimizing infant drug exposure. Management strategies include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Selecting compatible alternatives:} Choose drugs with established safety profiles in lactation, low lipid solubility, high protein binding, and short half-lives (e.g. choosing paracetamol or ibuprofen over aspirin; sertraline over fluoxetine).
    \item \textbf{Timing medication administration:} Advise the mother to take the medication immediately after a feed (or before the infant's longest sleep cycle) to allow maternal serum levels to peak and decline before the next feeding.
    \item \textbf{Minimising topical exposure:} Ensure topical creams applied to the breast or nipple (such as steroid creams) are washed off thoroughly before feeding, and avoid applying toxic substances (like salicylic acid or podophyllin) near the breast.
    \item \textbf{"Pump and dump" strategy:} Temporarily express and discard breast milk during short courses of high-risk drugs (e.g. radioactive isotopes or certain chemotherapies) to maintain lactation without exposing the infant.
    \item \textbf{Infant monitoring protocols:} Educate parents on specific symptoms to monitor based on the maternal medication (e.g. monitoring for drowsiness if the mother is taking a sedating medication).
    \item \textbf{Avoiding milk-suppressing drugs:} Avoid prescribing medications that reduce prolactin (such as combined oral contraceptive pills or pseudoephedrine) and choose progesterone-only options or alternative decongestants instead.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infant toxicity:} Adverse drug events in the infant, particularly sedation or respiratory depression from opioid exposure.
    \item \textbf{Premature weaning:} Unnecessary cessation of breastfeeding due to incorrect advice or maternal anxiety regarding medication safety, leading to loss of nutritional and immunological benefits.
    \item \textbf{Undertreated maternal disease:} Worsening of maternal physical or mental health (e.g. postpartum depression) because the mother avoids taking necessary medication out of fear of harming the infant.
    \item \textbf{Altered infant gut flora:} Alterations in infant microbiota or oral thrush secondary to maternal antibiotic therapy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for both mother and infant is excellent when maternal medication is managed using evidence-based guidelines. Most women can continue breastfeeding safely while receiving effective treatment for their medical conditions. By utilizing compatible drug choices, timing doses, and monitoring the infant, the risk of adverse infant effects is virtually eliminated. Educating mothers and healthcare providers about the safety of most modern medications reduces the rate of unnecessary weaning and promotes better postpartum mental and physical health outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Consulting before taking drugs:} Discuss all prescription, over-the-counter, and herbal medicines with a general practitioner, pharmacist, or lactation consultant before taking them.
    \item \textbf{Utilising safe analgesics:} Stick to paracetamol or ibuprofen for pain relief, avoiding codeine or high-dose aspirin unless specifically prescribed.
    \item \textbf{Avoiding herbal supplements:} Limit the use of herbal preparations, as their ingredients, quality control, and safety profiles in lactation are often poorly studied.
    \item \textbf{Minimising alcohol and caffeine:} Limit alcohol intake to standard limits (waiting 2 hours per standard drink before breastfeeding) and restrict caffeine consumption to moderate levels (under 200 mg per day) to avoid infant irritability.
    \item \textbf{Observing the infant:} Watch for changes in the infant's usual sleep, feeding, or bowel habits when starting a new maternal medication.
\end{itemize}
