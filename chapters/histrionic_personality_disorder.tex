\chapter{Histrionic Personality Disorder}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Histrionic personality disorder is a mental health condition characterised by a persistent pattern of attention-seeking behaviour, excessive emotionality, and dramatic self-expression. People with the disorder feel a strong need to be the centre of attention, and they may dress, speak, and behave in ways designed to draw attention, often with rapidly shifting and exaggerated emotions. The disorder begins in early adulthood and affects functioning in relationships, work, and social life. With psychotherapy, many people learn to manage the pattern and improve their relationships.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Histrionic personality disorder is diagnosed in around 1--3\% of the general population and is one of the more common personality disorders. It has traditionally been diagnosed more often in women, though this may reflect diagnostic bias rather than true differences. The disorder often coexists with other personality disorders and with depression, anxiety, and somatic symptom disorders. People with the disorder frequently seek help for relationship problems or depression rather than for the personality pattern itself.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} personality traits have a substantial heritable component
    \item \textbf{Childhood experiences:} inconsistent or conditional attention from caregivers is thought to contribute
    \item \textbf{Temperament:} high emotional reactivity and extraversion may predispose
    \item \textbf{Learning:} attention-seeking behaviour that was rewarded in childhood becomes entrenched
    \item \textbf{Cognitive patterns:} beliefs that one is unlovable unless noticed, and that attention equals worth
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} the pattern reflects deeply ingrained ways of thinking, feeling, and relating rather than a distinct brain disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A compelling need to be the centre of attention
    \item Inappropriately seductive or provocative behaviour
    \item Rapidly shifting, shallow emotional expressions
    \item Use of physical appearance to draw attention
    \item Speech that is impressionistic and lacking in detail
    \item Self-dramatisation, theatricality, and exaggerated emotions
    \item Suggestibility and easy influence by others
    \item Relationships perceived as more intimate than they are
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Other personality disorders, particularly borderline and narcissistic types, with which it overlaps
    \item Bipolar disorder, where emotional instability is episodic rather than persistent
    \item Depression and anxiety disorders, which often coexist
    \item Substance use disorders affecting behaviour
    \item Somatic symptom disorders, where physical symptoms dominate
    \item A specialist mental health assessment distinguishes these
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with histrionic personality disorder often seek help for relationship distress, depression, or anxiety. A GP can arrange referral to a psychologist or psychiatrist for assessment and therapy.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Thoughts of self-harm or suicide
    \item Sudden severe distress or agitation with risk of harm
    \item Symptoms of mania or psychosis
    \item Dangerous substance use
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical interview:} assessment of the longstanding pattern of emotionality and attention-seeking
    \item \textbf{History:} functioning in relationships, work, and social settings across adult life
    \item \textbf{Mental health assessment:} exclusion of mood, anxiety, and substance disorders
    \item \textbf{Structured interviews and questionnaires:} assist diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Collateral information:} from family with consent
    \item \textbf{Diagnosis requires:} the pattern to be persistent, pervasive, and impairing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychotherapy:} the main treatment, including psychodynamic therapy and cognitive behavioural therapy
    \item \textbf{Therapy goals:} increasing emotional awareness, reducing attention-seeking, and improving relationships
    \item \textbf{Treatment of coexisting conditions:} antidepressants or other medicines for depression and anxiety
    \item \textbf{Skills training:} building distress tolerance and interpersonal skills
    \item \textbf{Family and couple therapy:} where relationship patterns contribute
    \item \textbf{Support:} structured, consistent therapy with clear boundaries
    \item \textbf{No specific medication:} medicines are used for coexisting symptoms, not the disorder itself
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulties maintaining stable relationships and friendships
    \item Occupational problems from interpersonal friction
    \item Depression, anxiety, and substance misuse
    \item Risk-taking behaviour and its consequences
    \item Being perceived as insincere, leading to social rejection
    \item Frequent changes of therapist or treatment
    \item Somatic symptoms and excessive health care use
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Personality patterns are enduring, but histrionic personality disorder responds to psychotherapy, and many people make substantial gains in emotional stability and relationship quality over time. Symptoms often soften with age, particularly the more dramatic features. Outcomes are better when coexisting depression and anxiety are treated and when therapy is consistent and long term. With insight and support, people with the disorder can lead satisfying and productive lives.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek help early when relationship or emotional problems emerge
    \item Engage consistently in psychotherapy and complete treatment
    \item Build self-esteem independent of external attention
    \item Develop healthy ways of expressing emotion
    \item Practise listening and paying attention to others
    \item Treat coexisting depression and anxiety
    \item Maintain supportive friendships and routines
    \item Avoid alcohol and substances, which worsen emotional instability
\end{itemize}
