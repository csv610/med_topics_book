\chapter{Gastro-Oesophageal Reflux Disease (GORD)}
\index{Gastro-Oesophageal Reflux Disease (GORD)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) is a common condition in which stomach acid flows back up into the oesophagus (the food pipe), causing heartburn, regurgitation, and other symptoms. Some reflux is normal, but GORD is diagnosed when it is frequent or severe enough to damage the oesophagus or affect quality of life. It is caused by a weak valve at the lower end of the oesophagus, and it is made worse by obesity, pregnancy, certain foods, and lying down after meals. GORD is very treatable with lifestyle changes and acid-suppressing medicines, though it can return when treatment stops. Untreated, it can damage the oesophagus. This chapter explains the causes, symptoms, and treatment of GORD.

\section*{Epidemiology}
GORD is one of the most common conditions in many countries, affecting around 1 in 5 adults at least weekly, and it is the most common reason for prescribing acid-suppressing medicines. The prevalence rises with age and obesity, and it affects men and women equally. Around 1 in 10 people with GORD develops oesophagitis (inflammation of the oesophagus), and a small minority develop Barrett's oesophagus, a pre-cancerous change. Most people with GORD are managed successfully in general practice, and the condition has a major impact on sleep and quality of life when uncontrolled.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The lower oesophageal sphincter:} a ring of muscle at the junction of oesophagus and stomach normally prevents reflux; in GORD it is weak or relaxes inappropriately
    \item \textbf{Hiatus hernia:} part of the stomach slides up into the chest, weakening the valve mechanism
    \item \textbf{Obesity:} increased abdominal pressure pushes stomach contents up
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} hormones relax the sphincter, and the uterus presses on the stomach
    \item \textbf{Foods and drinks:} fatty foods, chocolate, alcohol, coffee, and carbonated drinks trigger reflux
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} some medicines relax the sphincter
    \item \textbf{Delayed stomach emptying:} a full stomach promotes reflux
    \item \textbf{Damage:} acid burns the oesophageal lining, causing oesophagitis, ulcers, and strictures; chronic reflux can cause Barrett's oesophagus, which increases the risk of oesophageal cancer
    \item \textbf{Smoking:} damages the sphincter and reduces saliva, which normally neutralises acid
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heartburn: a burning sensation behind the breastbone, often after meals and at night
    \item Regurgitation: sour or bitter fluid rising into the mouth or throat
    \item Chest pain, which can mimic heart pain
    \item Difficulty or pain on swallowing
    \item A chronic cough, hoarse voice, or sore throat, especially in the morning
    \item The sensation of a lump in the throat (globus)
    \item Excess salivation, belching, and nausea
    \item Asthma-like symptoms and dental erosion from acid in some people
    \item Symptoms worse with large meals, bending, lying down, and at night
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Chest and upper gut symptoms have several causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heart disease, including angina and heart attack, which must always be considered with chest pain
    \item Peptic ulcer disease and gastritis
    \item Gallbladder disease
    \item Eosinophilic oesophagitis and swallowing disorders
    \item Functional dyspepsia
    \item Asthma and other causes of chronic cough
    \item Oesophageal cancer, which must be considered with difficulty swallowing, weight loss, and bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Frequent heartburn, reflux affecting sleep or quality of life, or reflux with swallowing difficulty, weight loss, or bleeding should be assessed by a doctor. Chest pain should always be assessed to exclude heart disease.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain or pressure, especially with sweating, breathlessness, or nausea
    \item Difficulty swallowing food or fluids
    \item Vomiting blood or passing black stools
    \item Sudden severe pain or collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} typical symptoms are usually sufficient for diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Trial of treatment:} a short course of acid suppression, with improvement supporting the diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Gastroscopy:} for warning features, long-standing symptoms, or failure of treatment, to assess the oesophagus and detect Barrett's oesophagus
    \item \textbf{pH monitoring:} measurement of acid in the oesophagus in unclear cases
    \item \textbf{Oesophageal manometry:} assessment of the muscle function before surgery
    \item \textbf{Blood tests and other investigations:} as indicated by the symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} weight loss, smaller meals, avoiding trigger foods, not lying down for 2--3 hours after meals, raising the head of the bed, and stopping smoking
    \item \textbf{Antacids and alginates:} rapid symptom relief for mild, occasional reflux
    \item \textbf{H2 blockers:} for milder disease, taken before meals
    \item \textbf{Proton pump inhibitors:} the mainstay, healing oesophagitis and controlling symptoms; taken once daily before breakfast
    \item \textbf{Long-term treatment:} the lowest dose that controls symptoms, with regular review; many people need long-term PPI treatment
    \item \textbf{Treat complications:} dilation of strictures, and surveillance for Barrett's oesophagus
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} fundoplication, in which the stomach is wrapped around the oesophagus, for people with severe reflux who do not respond to or wish to avoid long-term medicines
    \item \textbf{Endoscopic treatments:} newer procedures for selected people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Oesophagitis, with inflammation and erosions
    \item Oesophageal strictures causing difficulty swallowing
    \item Bleeding from oesophageal ulcers
    \item Barrett's oesophagus, which carries a small risk of oesophageal cancer
    \item Chronic cough, asthma worsening, and dental damage
    \item Impaired sleep and quality of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
GORD is a chronic condition, but it is well controlled in the vast majority of people with lifestyle measures and acid suppression. Oesophagitis heals within weeks of treatment, symptoms are suppressed, and serious complications are uncommon. The small minority with Barrett's oesophagus is monitored with surveillance endoscopy to detect early cancer, which is curable. For most people, ongoing low-dose treatment keeps GORD under control, and surgery offers a permanent alternative for those who need it.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Lose weight if overweight, the most effective lifestyle measure
    \item Eat smaller meals and avoid late-night eating
    \item Identify and avoid personal trigger foods
    \item Do not lie down for 2--3 hours after meals
    \item Raise the head of the bed by 10--15 cm
    \item Stop smoking and limit alcohol
    \item Take medicines as prescribed and attend review
    \item Seek urgent assessment for chest pain or difficulty swallowing
\end{itemize}
