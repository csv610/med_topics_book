\chapter{Beta Blockers}
\index{Beta Blockers}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Beta blockers are a class of medicines that reduce the effects of the body's stress hormones, adrenaline and noradrenaline, on the heart and blood vessels. By blocking beta-adrenergic receptors, they slow the heart rate, reduce the force of contraction, and lower blood pressure, thereby decreasing the workload on the heart.

They are used to treat high blood pressure, angina, abnormal heart rhythms, and heart failure, and to protect the heart in the period after a heart attack. Some beta blockers are also prescribed for the prevention of migraine, for the control of anxiety symptoms and essential tremor, and, in eye-drop form, for glaucoma. Together with their long record of safety, this breadth of use makes beta blockers among the most widely prescribed medicines in the world.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Beta blockers are taken by millions of people worldwide, because the conditions they treat are extremely common.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High blood pressure:} affects roughly 1 in 3 adults and is a leading contributor to heart attack, stroke, and kidney disease
    \item \textbf{Heart attack and heart failure:} major causes of illness and death, for which beta blockers are recommended long-term in appropriate patients
    \item \textbf{Age distribution:} used across all age groups but prescribed most often in older adults with cardiovascular disease
    \item \textbf{Other uses:} significant numbers of prescriptions are written for migraine, anxiety, tremor, and glaucoma
\end{itemize}

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Beta blockers work by occupying beta-adrenergic receptors, the protein sites on cell surfaces where adrenaline and noradrenaline normally attach. Blocking these receptors in the heart slows the heart rate, reduces the force of each beat, and lowers blood pressure, which together reduce the oxygen demands of the heart muscle and stabilise electrical activity.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cardioselective beta blockers:} such as metoprolol, bisoprolol, and atenolol act mainly on the heart (beta-1 receptors) and are less likely to cause breathing problems
    \item \textbf{Non-selective beta blockers:} such as propranolol and carvedilol also block receptors in the airways, blood vessels, and other tissues, widening their range of effects
    \item \textbf{Glaucoma:} in the eye, beta blockers reduce the production of aqueous humour, lowering intraocular pressure
    \item \textbf{Pharmacokinetic differences:} individual agents differ in how they are broken down and excreted by the body, which influences dosing and suitability for patients with liver or kidney disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
When treatment is working well, beta blockers improve the symptoms of the underlying condition, such as fewer angina attacks, better exercise tolerance in heart failure, fewer migraines, or lower blood pressure. People may also notice the medicine's expected physiological effects and its side effects.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A slower resting heart rate and lower blood pressure
    \item Dizziness or light-headedness, especially on standing, particularly when starting treatment or increasing the dose
    \item Fatigue and reduced exercise capacity
    \item Cold hands and feet, from reduced blood flow to the extremities
    \item Sleep disturbance, vivid dreams, or nightmares
    \item Shortness of breath or wheeze in people with asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)
    \item A reduced awareness of the rapid heart beat that normally signals low blood sugar in people with diabetes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When a person taking a beta blocker develops new symptoms, the possible explanations include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A \textbf{side effect of the medicine}, such as fatigue, a low pulse, or breathlessness
    \item \textbf{Worsening of the underlying heart condition} being treated
    \item \textbf{Drug interactions} with other medicines taken at the same time
    \item A \textbf{new independent condition}, such as depression, anaemia, or thyroid disease, which can produce similar symptoms
    \item \textbf{Worsening asthma or COPD} triggered by the medicine in susceptible people
    \item \textbf{Hypoglycaemia} in people with diabetes, the warning signs of which can be partly masked
    \item \textbf{Withdrawal effects} if the medicine was stopped or reduced suddenly
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor before starting a beta blocker, whenever a dose change is needed, or if concerning symptoms develop.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A resting pulse that is very slow (for example below 50 beats per minute) with dizziness or fainting
    \item New or worsening shortness of breath, wheezing, or swelling of the ankles
    \item Persistent dizziness, fainting, or blackouts
    \item Worsening depression, vivid nightmares, or unusual mood changes
    \item Erectile dysfunction or other sexual side effects
    \item Pregnancy, or planning a pregnancy, while taking a beta blocker
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, severe shortness of breath, or collapse
    \item Fainting or a seizure
    \item Signs of stroke, remembered with the FAST mnemonic (Face, Arms, Speech, Time)
    \item Swelling of the face or throat with difficulty breathing, suggesting a severe allergic reaction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-treatment assessment:} measurement of blood pressure and pulse, an electrocardiogram (ECG), and a review of any history of asthma, COPD, diabetes, or heart rhythm problems
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} kidney function, and sometimes liver function or blood sugar, depending on the medicine and the condition being treated
    \item \textbf{ECG and heart monitoring:} to assess heart rate and rhythm before and during treatment, particularly in heart failure or arrhythmias
    \item \textbf{Regular review:} repeated blood pressure and pulse checks, assessment of symptoms, and monitoring for side effects
    \item \textbf{Exercise testing:} occasionally used to confirm the adequacy of treatment for angina
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Taking the medicine as prescribed:} at the same time each day, and never stopping it suddenly without medical advice
    \item \textbf{Starting slowly:} doses are built up gradually, especially in heart failure, to limit side effects and allow the body to adjust
    \item \textbf{Regular monitoring:} blood pressure, pulse, and symptom review to confirm the medicine is effective
    \item \textbf{Dose adjustment:} for older people and for those with kidney or liver disease
    \item \textbf{Switching medicines:} if side effects are troublesome, a doctor may change the dose or select a different beta blocker
    \item \textbf{Tapering on withdrawal:} beta blockers must usually be reduced gradually under medical supervision, because sudden withdrawal can cause a rebound rise in heart rate and blood pressure, angina, or even a heart attack
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bradycardia:} a very slow heart rate, with dizziness, fatigue, or fainting
    \item \textbf{Hypotension:} low blood pressure, particularly on standing
    \item \textbf{Worsening of asthma or COPD:} bronchospasm and wheeze, especially with non-selective agents
    \item \textbf{Worsening heart failure:} if treatment is started or increased too quickly
    \item \textbf{Masked hypoglycaemia:} reduced awareness of the warning signs of low blood sugar in people with diabetes taking insulin or certain tablets
    \item \textbf{Raynaud phenomenon:} cold, numb, or discoloured fingers and toes from reduced peripheral circulation
    \item \textbf{Mood and sleep effects:} fatigue, sleep disturbance, depression, and nightmares
    \item \textbf{Sexual dysfunction:} including erectile dysfunction in men
    \item \textbf{Withdrawal syndrome:} rebound hypertension, angina, or arrhythmia if the medicine is stopped abruptly
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Beta blockers are safe and highly effective when prescribed for the right reasons and monitored properly.

\begin{itemize}
    \item After a heart attack, long-term treatment reduces the risk of death and of further cardiac events
    \item In heart failure, beta blockers improve symptoms, reduce hospital admissions, and improve survival
    \item Side effects are usually mild and reversible with dose adjustment or a change of agent
    \item Long-term benefit depends on continuing the treatment as prescribed and managing the underlying condition and other risk factors
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Never stop a beta blocker suddenly; ask your doctor how to reduce the dose safely if it needs to stop
    \item Take the medicine at the same time each day and keep an up-to-date list of all your medicines
    \item Learn to check your pulse, and report a very slow or irregular pulse accompanied by dizziness
    \item Rise slowly from sitting or lying to avoid light-headedness
    \item Tell your doctor, dentist, or pharmacist about all medicines you take, including over-the-counter products
    \item Combine the medicine with a heart-healthy lifestyle: a balanced diet, regular physical activity, stopping smoking, and limiting alcohol
    \item Attend scheduled reviews so the dose can be adjusted as your condition changes over time
\end{itemize}
