\chapter{Glaucoma Surgery}
\index{Glaucoma Surgery}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Glaucoma surgery refers to a range of procedures used to lower the pressure inside the eye (intraocular pressure) when eye drops and laser treatment have not controlled it adequately. Glaucoma damages the optic nerve through raised eye pressure, and surgery aims to create a new drainage pathway so that fluid can leave the eye, protecting the remaining vision. The main surgical options include laser procedures, minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS), trabeculectomy, and drainage implants. Surgery does not restore vision that has already been lost, but it can slow or stop further damage. For many people, successful surgery reduces or eliminates the need for daily eye drops.

\section*{Epidemiology}
A substantial minority of people with glaucoma eventually require surgery. Around 10--20\% of people with open-angle glaucoma need surgery within 15--20 years of diagnosis because drops and laser alone are insufficient, intolerable, or not adhered to. Trabeculectomy has been the standard surgical treatment for decades, while minimally invasive techniques have grown rapidly in popularity since the 2010s and are now common, particularly when cataract surgery is performed at the same time. The need for surgery increases with age, advanced disease, and high eye pressure.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Glaucoma surgery works by addressing the underlying problem of impaired fluid drainage.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fluid dynamics:} the eye constantly produces aqueous humour, which normally drains through the trabecular meshwork and other channels
    \item \textbf{Impaired drainage:} in open-angle glaucoma, drainage is inefficient, raising pressure; in angle-closure glaucoma, the angle is physically blocked
    \item \textbf{Laser trabeculoplasty:} laser energy stimulates the drainage meshwork to drain more efficiently
    \item \textbf{Laser iridotomy:} a small hole in the iris allows fluid to bypass a blocked angle in angle-closure glaucoma
    \item \textbf{Trabeculectomy:} a surgically created channel allows fluid to drain into a reservoir (bleb) beneath the conjunctiva
    \item \textbf{Drainage implants:} a small tube shunts fluid to a plate on the surface of the eye
    \item \textbf{MIGS:} tiny stents or devices placed through micro-incisions enhance natural drainage with less trauma and faster recovery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Candidates for glaucoma surgery often have features that indicate drops are not enough.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Eye pressure that remains high despite maximally tolerated medical treatment
    \item Evidence of progressive optic nerve damage or visual field loss on testing
    \item Intolerance of, or inability to use, eye drops
    \item Poor adherence to medical therapy
    \item Advanced glaucoma at diagnosis requiring aggressive pressure control
    \item Acute angle-closure glaucoma that has been relieved but needs preventive laser treatment
    \item Glaucoma in the setting of cataract surgery, where combined procedures can be performed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The decision to operate is made after confirming the diagnosis and considering alternatives.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Open-angle glaucoma versus other types of glaucoma that may require different approaches
    \item Secondary glaucomas, including uveitic, traumatic, and steroid-induced glaucoma, which may need tailored treatment
    \item Normal-tension glaucoma, where pressure control is still the aim but targets are lower
    \item Cataract, which can coexist and may be addressed at the same time
    \item Corneal and other anterior segment diseases that affect surgical choice
    \item Age and general health, which influence surgical risk and the choice of procedure
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with glaucoma who are offered surgery should discuss the risks, benefits, and alternatives fully with their ophthalmologist. Surgery is elective in most cases and is scheduled to prevent further vision loss.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute angle-closure glaucoma: sudden severe eye pain, blurred vision, haloes, red eye, and nausea, which needs emergency treatment
    \item Sudden loss of vision after any eye surgery, which requires immediate review
    \item Severe pain, swelling, or discharge from an operated eye, which may indicate infection or a serious complication
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Before surgery, a full assessment ensures the right procedure and reduces risk.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Eye examination:} measurement of eye pressure, inspection of the drainage angle, and assessment of the optic nerve
    \item \textbf{Visual field testing:} documents the extent of existing vision loss
    \item \textbf{OCT imaging:} measures optic nerve fibre thickness
    \item \textbf{Corneal thickness:} influences pressure measurement and risk
    \item \textbf{General health assessment:} medicines such as blood thinners are reviewed, and fitness for anaesthesia is assessed
    \item \textbf{Discussion of options:} the surgeon explains the expected benefits, success rates, risks, and alternatives
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The choice of procedure depends on the type and severity of glaucoma, coexisting eye disease, and the patient's circumstances.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Laser trabeculoplasty:} a first-line option for open-angle glaucoma; quick, safe, and repeatable, but pressure may rise again over time
    \item \textbf{Laser iridotomy:} preventive treatment for angle-closure glaucoma, creating a hole in the iris to open the drainage angle
    \item \textbf{MIGS:} minimally invasive devices combined with cataract surgery in suitable patients, with rapid recovery and few complications
    \item \textbf{Trabeculectomy:} the traditional "filtration" surgery, highly effective and appropriate for advanced or aggressive glaucoma
    \item \textbf{Drainage implants:} used when trabeculectomy has failed or is unsuitable
    \item \textbf{Post-operative care:} antibiotic and anti-inflammatory drops, avoidance of rubbing the eye, and restrictions on heavy lifting and swimming
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} frequent early reviews, then lifelong monitoring of pressure and visual fields
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Early: bleeding, infection, inflammation, and low or high eye pressure after surgery
    \item Late: scarring of the drainage channel (bleb failure), cataract formation, and persistent inflammation
    \item Infection of the bleb (blebitis or endophthalmitis), which is rare but serious
    \item Vision loss, including from the surgery itself, though this is uncommon
    \item The need to continue eye drops in some patients after surgery
    \item Repeated surgery if the first procedure fails
    \item Drooping eyelid or double vision in rare cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Glaucoma surgery is successful in controlling pressure in the majority of patients. Trabeculectomy succeeds in around 70--90\% of eyes in the medium term, though many patients need to continue some drops, and MIGS offers good pressure control with fewer risks in suitable patients. Successful surgery stabilises vision and prevents further loss in most people. Lifelong monitoring remains necessary because glaucoma can progress and drainage channels can scar over time. The overall aim is achieved in most cases: preserving useful vision and reducing dependence on daily medication.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Follow all pre-operative instructions, including medicine adjustments advised by the surgeon
    \item Use post-operative eye drops exactly as directed
    \item Do not rub the operated eye, and protect it from injury
    \item Avoid heavy lifting, straining, and swimming for the period advised
    \item Attend every follow-up appointment, even if the eye feels well
    \item Report pain, redness, discharge, or any change in vision immediately
    \item Continue long-term monitoring of pressure and visual fields
    \item Maintain a healthy lifestyle and manage other health conditions that affect the eyes
\end{itemize}
