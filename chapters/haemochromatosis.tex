\chapter{Haemochromatosis}
\index{Haemochromatosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Haemochromatosis is a genetic condition in which the body absorbs and stores too much iron from the diet. The excess iron accumulates in organs over many years, particularly the liver, pancreas, heart, joints, and skin, and can cause organ damage if untreated. It is the most common inherited disorder in people of northern European descent. The condition is caused in most cases by mutations in the HFE gene. Many people with the genetic changes never develop symptoms or organ damage, but in those who do, the condition is easily treated by regular removal of blood (venesection), which lowers iron levels and prevents or reverses damage. Early detection through blood tests is important.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hereditary haemochromatosis is one of the most common genetic disorders in many countries. Around 1 in 200 people of northern European descent carry the gene changes that can cause it, and around 1 in 7--10 carry a single copy of the common mutation. However, only a minority of those with the gene changes develop significant iron overload, and men are affected more often and more severely than women, because women lose iron through menstruation and pregnancy. Symptoms typically appear between ages 40 and 60 in men and after menopause in women. Haemochromatosis is significantly underdiagnosed.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Haemochromatosis results from increased intestinal absorption of iron due to genetic changes.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HFE gene:} most cases are caused by mutations in the HFE gene, most commonly the C282Y mutation; the gene regulates the hormone hepcidin, which controls iron absorption
    \item \textbf{Reduced hepcidin:} the mutations reduce hepcidin production, causing the gut to absorb more iron than the body needs
    \item \textbf{Iron accumulation:} excess iron is stored as ferritin and haemosiderin in the liver, pancreas, heart, joints, and other tissues
    \item \textbf{Oxidative damage:} excess iron generates free radicals that damage cells and organs
    \item \textbf{Progressive damage:} over years, iron overload causes liver damage and fibrosis, diabetes, heart disease, arthritis, and skin pigmentation
    \item \textbf{Inheritance:} autosomal recessive, so both parents must contribute a gene change for full risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms develop slowly and can be non-specific, so the condition is often found on routine blood tests.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Fatigue and weakness, which are the most common symptoms
    \item Joint pain, particularly of the hands and knees
    \item Abdominal pain and discomfort
    \item Darkening or bronze colouring of the skin
    \item Diabetes, sometimes appearing as new-onset diabetes
    \item Irregular heart rhythm and heart failure
    \item Loss of libido and erectile dysfunction
    \item Liver problems, from abnormal liver tests to cirrhosis
    \item Many people have no symptoms at all
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of iron overload and similar symptoms should be considered.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Secondary iron overload from repeated blood transfusions or high iron intake
    \item Chronic liver disease, including alcohol-related liver disease, hepatitis, and fatty liver
    \item Other causes of diabetes, arthritis, and fatigue
    \item Other causes of abnormal liver function tests
    \item Iron-loading anaemias, such as thalassaemia
    \item Non-hereditary causes of high ferritin, including inflammation and metabolic syndrome
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for blood tests if you have unexplained fatigue, joint pain, diabetes, abnormal liver tests, or a family history of haemochromatosis. Early diagnosis allows treatment before organ damage occurs.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain, which may indicate liver complications
    \item Signs of heart failure, including severe breathlessness or swelling of the legs
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or signs of liver failure
    \item Sudden severe chest pain or an irregular heartbeat
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Haemochromatosis is diagnosed with blood tests and confirmed by genetic testing.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Iron studies:} high transferrin saturation and high ferritin levels suggest iron overload
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} identifies HFE gene mutations (C282Y and H63D)
    \item \textbf{Liver function tests:} assess liver damage
    \item \textbf{Liver imaging:} ultrasound, MRI, or FibroScan assess liver fat, iron, and fibrosis
    \item \textbf{Liver biopsy:} rarely needed now, but can quantify iron and fibrosis
    \item \textbf{Family screening:} close relatives are offered testing
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular iron studies during treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is simple and highly effective: removing iron from the body.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Venesection (therapeutic phlebotomy):} regular removal of blood, initially weekly, until iron levels are normal, then at longer intervals to maintain them
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} iron studies guide the frequency of venesection
    \item \textbf{Dietary measures:} avoiding iron supplements, limiting alcohol (which worsens liver damage), and not eating raw shellfish
    \item \textbf{Treatment of complications:} diabetes management, joint treatment, and liver and heart care
    \item \textbf{Liver surveillance:} regular scans for people with fibrosis or cirrhosis
    \item \textbf{Family screening:} testing of siblings and children
    \item \textbf{Lifelong maintenance:} most people need ongoing maintenance venesections
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Liver disease, including cirrhosis and an increased risk of liver cancer
    \item Diabetes, which can be difficult to control with iron overload
    \item Heart disease, including cardiomyopathy and arrhythmias
    \item Joint damage and chronic arthritis
    \item Erectile dysfunction and infertility in men
    \item Skin pigmentation
    \item Increased risk of certain infections
    \item Complications of undertreatment, which are largely preventable with early treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With early diagnosis and treatment, people with haemochromatosis have a normal life expectancy. Venesection normalises iron levels, and symptoms such as fatigue and joint pain often improve; organ damage that has already occurred cannot always be reversed, but further damage is prevented. People diagnosed before organ damage develops do very well. Untreated haemochromatosis causes progressive organ damage and premature death, which is why genetic screening of families and early diagnosis are so important.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Have iron studies checked if you have risk factors, symptoms, or a family history
    \item If you have the gene changes, attend regular iron tests and venesection appointments
    \item Do not take iron supplements unless prescribed
    \item Limit alcohol, especially if iron levels are elevated
    \item Do not eat raw or undercooked shellfish
    \item Maintain a healthy diet and regular exercise
    \item Encourage family members to be tested
    \item Attend monitoring for liver health if recommended
    \item Know that treatment is simple and effective, and that early detection prevents harm
\end{itemize}
