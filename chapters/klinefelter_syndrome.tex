\chapter{Klinefelter Syndrome}
\index{Klinefelter Syndrome}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Klinefelter syndrome is a genetic condition that affects males who are born with an extra X chromosome. Most males have one X and one Y chromosome, but those with Klinefelter syndrome have at least two X chromosomes and one Y chromosome, most commonly 47,XXY. The extra X chromosome affects testicular development and testosterone production, leading to small, firm testes, reduced fertility, and low levels of the male sex hormone. Features vary greatly between individuals, and many men with the condition are unaware of it until difficulties with fertility or puberty bring them to medical attention. This chapter covers the causes, features, diagnosis, and management of Klinefelter syndrome across the lifespan.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Klinefelter syndrome is one of the most common sex chromosome abnormalities, affecting approximately 1 in 600 to 1 in 1,000 live-born males. It is estimated that only a minority of affected males are ever diagnosed, because the features are often mild or subtle. Diagnosis typically occurs in three peaks: during prenatal testing, in adolescence when puberty appears delayed or testicular growth is poor, and in adulthood when a man is investigated for infertility or low testosterone. The condition occurs across all ethnic groups and is not inherited in most cases.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cause:} an extra copy of the X chromosome (47,XXY), which arises from a random error in cell division during the formation of the father's sperm or the mother's egg; the risk rises slightly with older maternal age
    \item \textbf{Testicular failure:} the extra X chromosome impairs the function of the testicular cells that produce sperm and the Leydig cells that produce testosterone
    \item \textbf{Small testes:} the seminiferous tubules become progressively scarred and fibrotic, leading to firm, small testes and azoospermia (no sperm in the ejaculate) in most men
    \item \textbf{Hormonal effects:} low testosterone leads to reduced muscle mass, body hair, and bone density; raised follicle-stimulating hormone and luteinising hormone reflect the failing testes
    \item \textbf{Variants:} some males have mosaicism, where only some cells carry the extra X chromosome, which is associated with milder features
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Small, firm testes, usually the most consistent physical feature
    \item Delayed, incomplete, or otherwise normal puberty with reduced testicular growth
    \item Tall stature with long legs relative to the trunk
    \item Reduced facial and body hair and reduced muscle bulk
    \item Gynaecomastia (breast development) in a proportion of men
    \item Reduced libido and erectile dysfunction related to low testosterone
    \item Infertility due to absent or severely reduced sperm production
    \item Learning difficulties, particularly with language, reading, and executive skills, in some boys
    \item Social anxiety, shyness, and low self-esteem are more common than in the general population
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Other causes of delayed puberty:} constitutional delay, hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism, or chronic illness
    \item \textbf{Other sex chromosome variants:} XXYY syndrome or XXXY syndrome have additional features
    \item \textbf{Azoospermia from other causes:} obstructive causes, prior chemotherapy, or Y chromosome microdeletions
    \item \textbf{Hypogonadism from other causes:} pituitary disease, testicular damage from injury or infection, or haemochromatosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents should seek review if a boy's pubertal development seems delayed, if the testes appear small, or if there are concerns about learning or behaviour. Men should see a doctor if they have difficulty fathering a child, reduced libido, or features suggestive of low testosterone. A diagnosis of Klinefelter syndrome should prompt assessment by an endocrinologist and, where fertility is desired, early referral to a fertility specialist.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, sudden breathlessness, or swelling or pain in one leg, which may indicate a blood clot (men with Klinefelter syndrome have a slightly increased risk of thromboembolism)
    \item Severe, sudden testicular pain, which may indicate testicular torsion
    \item Any sudden, severe symptom that worries you
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Karyotype (chromosome analysis):} a blood test that counts and arranges the chromosomes, confirming the diagnosis by showing the extra X chromosome
    \item \textbf{Hormone tests:} testosterone, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and luteinising hormone (LH); testosterone is often low while FSH and LH are elevated
    \item \textbf{Semen analysis:} confirms azoospermia or severe oligospermia in most affected men
    \item \textbf{Bone density scanning:} recommended because of the increased risk of osteoporosis from low testosterone
    \item \textbf{Prenatal diagnosis:} Klinefelter syndrome may be detected incidentally on amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling or through non-invasive prenatal screening
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Testosterone replacement therapy:} beginning at puberty, or in adulthood where levels are low, improves muscle mass, bone density, energy, mood, and libido; given by injection, gel, or patches
    \item \textbf{Fertility treatment:} assisted reproduction, including surgical sperm retrieval from the testes combined with intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), offers many men with Klinefelter syndrome the chance of biological fatherhood; success is highest when sperm retrieval is performed in early adulthood
    \item \textbf{Educational and psychological support:} speech therapy, learning support, and counselling for language difficulties, anxiety, and self-esteem
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular reviews of hormone levels, bone density, and general health; men with Klinefelter syndrome have a slightly increased risk of breast cancer, osteoporosis, and blood clots
    \item \textbf{Gynaecomastia:} surgical correction may be offered where breast development causes distress
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infertility, present in the majority of affected men
    \item Osteoporosis and increased fracture risk from long-standing low testosterone
    \item Increased risk of breast cancer, metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, and venous thromboembolism
    \item Learning difficulties and psychological distress, including anxiety and depression
    \item Low self-esteem related to body image, gynaecomastia, or fertility concerns
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Klinefelter syndrome does not reduce life expectancy dramatically, but it is associated with modestly increased risks of certain health conditions, which regular monitoring can address. With appropriate treatment, most men live healthy, active, and fulfilling lives. Testosterone therapy substantially improves quality of life, and advances in fertility treatment mean that fathering a child is now a realistic option for many affected men, particularly when specialist care is sought early.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ensure regular follow-up with an endocrinologist and a GP who understands the condition
    \item Maintain a healthy weight, exercise regularly, and eat a calcium-rich diet to protect bone health
    \item Have bone density scans as recommended and take vitamin D if levels are low
    \item Attend screening and be alert to breast changes, since risk is slightly increased
    \item Seek fertility advice early if parenthood is desired, since options are best earlier in life
    \item Access counselling or support groups to manage anxiety, self-esteem, and social challenges
    \item Inform clinicians of the diagnosis, since it affects risks for blood clots and other conditions
\end{itemize}
