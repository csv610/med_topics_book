\chapter{Bronchiolitis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bronchiolitis is a common viral infection of the small airways (bronchioles) in the lungs that affects almost exclusively babies and young children under two years of age. It typically begins with cold-like symptoms, including a runny or blocked nose and a mild cough, and then progresses over a few days to coughing, wheezing, fast breathing, and sometimes difficulty feeding. Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) causes most cases, but several other viruses can produce the same illness. The majority of cases are mild and resolve at home within one to two weeks, but a minority of babies become significantly unwell and require hospital care, chiefly because of breathing difficulty or reduced feeding. Bronchiolitis is the leading cause of hospital admission in infants during winter.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bronchiolitis is extremely common: by the age of two, almost every child has been infected with RSV at least once. It occurs mainly in winter and early spring and peaks in babies aged three to six months. Most affected children recover at home, yet roughly 1 in 50 infants with bronchiolitis requires hospital admission, making it the most common reason for hospitalisation in the first year of life. Premature babies, infants with congenital heart or chronic lung disease, and those with weakened immune systems are at highest risk of severe disease. Breastfeeding appears to confer some protection against severe bronchiolitis.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Respiratory syncytial virus:} the cause of the majority of cases, transmitted by droplets from coughs and sneezes and by contact with contaminated hands and surfaces
    \item \textbf{Other viruses:} rhinovirus, human metapneumovirus, influenza, parainfluenza, and adenovirus can all cause bronchiolitis
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} the virus infects the epithelial lining of the small airways, provoking inflammation, swelling, and excessive mucus production; in an infant the narrow bronchioles become partially obstructed, trapping air distally and producing noisy, laboured breathing
    \item \textbf{Age-related susceptibility:} babies have small-calibre airways and immature immune defences, so a virus that causes only a cold in an older child or adult can cause bronchiolitis in an infant
    \item \textbf{Spread:} RSV survives for hours on hands and surfaces, so infection spreads rapidly in households, childcare centres, and waiting rooms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item An initial phase resembling a cold: runny or blocked nose, mild cough, and sometimes a low-grade fever
    \item After a few days: worsening cough, wheezing, and faster or more laboured breathing
    \item Visible breathing effort, including nostril flaring, intercostal or subcostal recession, and grunting
    \item Difficulty feeding or swallowing, so the baby feeds less or for shorter periods
    \item Reduced urine output from decreased fluid intake
    \item Apnoea (pauses in breathing), particularly in young and premature infants
    \item Irritability, or a baby who appears tired and less responsive
    \item Symptoms generally peak around days three to five and then gradually improve
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Virus-triggered wheeze and asthma:} wheezing in an older child, in whom bronchiolitis is less likely
    \item \textbf{Pneumonia:} bacterial infection of the lung, suspected when the child is very unwell or has a high, sustained fever
    \item \textbf{Pertussis (whooping cough):} paroxysms of coughing often ending in a whoop or vomiting, especially in a partially vaccinated infant
    \item \textbf{Foreign body aspiration:} sudden choking or wheeze after placing an object in the mouth
    \item \textbf{Congenital heart disease with heart failure:} can cause poor feeding, sweating, and tachypnoea
    \item \textbf{Gastro-oesophageal reflux:} cough and feeding difficulty that may be mistaken for a chest infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if a baby under three months of age has cold symptoms, if breathing seems fast or effortful, if feeding is reduced, or if you are at all worried. Babies with bronchiolitis can deteriorate quickly, so early review is sensible.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, including fast breathing, grunting, or the chest sucking in with each breath
    \item Pauses in breathing (apnoea)
    \item Blue or grey colour of the lips, face, or tongue
    \item A baby who is floppy, unusually sleepy, or difficult to wake
    \item Not feeding at all, or no wet nappy for around six hours
    \item A baby under three months of age with a fever
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the baby's age, the pattern and timing of symptoms, feeding and nappy counts, and whether household contacts have colds
    \item \textbf{Examination:} chest auscultation, measurement of the respiratory rate, observation of work of breathing, and pulse oximetry
    \item \textbf{Nasal swab:} a respiratory viral swab can confirm RSV or another virus; this is not always required but may guide management in hospital
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} not usually necessary, but may be performed when the diagnosis is unclear or complications are suspected
    \item \textbf{Hospital assessment:} monitoring of oxygen saturation, respiratory effort, feeding, and hydration; blood tests are only occasionally indicated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Home care:} most babies are managed at home with rest, small frequent feeds, and clearing of the nose with saline drops or a bulb syringe before feeding
    \item \textbf{Supporting feeding:} offering extra breastfeeds or small, frequent bottle feeds, as babies with bronchiolitis tire easily
    \item \textbf{Fluids and oxygen:} admitted babies may require intravenous fluids if unable to drink enough, and supplementary oxygen when saturation is low
    \item \textbf{Feeding support:} some infants need short-term nasogastric feeding
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular assessment of breathing and oxygen levels in hospital
    \item \textbf{Not recommended:} antibiotics are ineffective because the cause is viral, and routine use of bronchodilators or corticosteroids is not advised; cough medicines must not be given to infants
    \item \textbf{Prevention for high-risk infants:} prophylactic antibody treatment (palivizumab) may be offered in winter to selected premature or medically fragile infants
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dehydration or feeding difficulty, the most common reason for hospital admission
    \item Hypoxaemia requiring oxygen therapy
    \item Apnoea, especially in young or premature infants
    \item Secondary bacterial infections, such as otitis media or, less commonly, pneumonia
    \item Severe respiratory distress requiring intensive care and, rarely, mechanical ventilation
    \item An increased likelihood of subsequent viral-induced wheeze and childhood asthma, although the causal relationship is not fully understood
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Almost all babies with bronchiolitis recover fully. The cough generally settles within one to two weeks, although a mild residual cough may persist for three to four weeks. Infants at greatest risk are those under three months, premature babies, and those with cardiac, pulmonary, or immune compromise; even in these groups, recovery with hospital care is the rule. Bronchiolitis does not appear to cause lasting lung damage in most children, but the association between infant bronchiolitis and later wheezing or asthma continues to be an area of active research.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wash hands regularly, and wash babies' hands and faces frequently
    \item Keep babies away from people with colds, and never smoke around the baby
    \item Clean and disinfect toys, surfaces, and objects that babies put in their mouths
    \item Breastfeed if you are able, as breast milk provides some protection
    \item Keep all household members up to date with vaccination, including whooping cough and influenza vaccines
    \item Keep the baby's nose clear with saline drops, especially before feeds
    \item Offer small, frequent feeds and seek help early if the baby stops feeding or becomes difficult to wake
    \item Discuss RSV prevention with your doctor before winter if your baby is premature or medically fragile
\end{itemize}
