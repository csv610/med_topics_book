\chapter{Giving Birth: Contractions}
\index{Giving Birth: Contractions}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Contractions are the rhythmic tightening and relaxing of the uterine muscle that occur throughout labour, working to open (dilate) the cervix and push the baby down the birth canal. Early in labour, contractions are often mild, irregular, and similar to period pain; as labour progresses they become stronger, longer, more frequent, and more regular. Recognising the difference between early "practice" contractions (Braxton Hicks) and true labour contractions helps women know when labour has begun and when to contact their maternity team. Contractions are a normal and expected part of giving birth, and there are many ways to manage the pain they cause, from breathing techniques and positions to pharmacological options such as nitrous oxide and epidurals.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Almost all women who give birth vaginally experience contractions, and they are a universal part of childbirth. Around one in three women who have a baby will have a caesarean section, and some women have planned caesareans without going through labour. Of women who labour, the vast majority manage with a combination of non-pharmacological and pharmacological pain relief. Epidural analgesia is used by around 40--50\% of women in labour in many countries. The duration of labour and the experience of contractions vary widely between women and between pregnancies.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Contractions are driven by complex hormonal and mechanical processes that begin labour and progress it.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hormonal triggers:} the fetal brain and placenta signal labour onset through hormones, including a rise in oestrogen, prostaglandins, and oxytocin
    \item \textbf{Uterine muscle:} the smooth muscle of the uterus (myometrium) contracts rhythmically; each contraction squeezes the upper part of the uterus and helps pull the cervix open
    \item \textbf{Cervical change:} contractions, together with pressure from the baby, cause the cervix to soften, shorten (efface), and open (dilate)
    \item \textbf{Ferguson reflex:} as the baby descends, pressure on the cervix and vagina stimulates more oxytocin, strengthening contractions
    \item \textbf{Stages of labour:} first stage (dilation), second stage (pushing and birth of the baby), and third stage (delivery of the placenta)
    \item \textbf{Oxytocin augmentation:} in some labours, synthetic oxytocin is used to strengthen or regulate contractions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
True labour contractions have features that distinguish them from Braxton Hicks contractions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Regular, rhythmic contractions that come at predictable intervals and get progressively closer together
    \item Contractions that last longer (usually 45--60 seconds) and feel stronger over time
    \item Pain that is felt in the lower back, abdomen, or both, and often builds to a peak and then fades
    \item Contractions that continue regardless of rest, position changes, or activity
    \item Accompanying signs of labour: a "show" (blood-stained mucus), waters breaking, and cervical dilation
    \item Pain intensity that increases as labour progresses
    \item A rest period between contractions, during which the pain settles
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Not every pain in late pregnancy is labour, and distinguishing true labour from other causes of pain matters.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Braxton Hicks contractions: irregular, painless or mild "practice" tightenings that do not dilate the cervix
    \item Back pain from the weight of the baby and pelvic ligament strain
    \item Urinary tract infection or kidney pain
    \item Round ligament pain and pelvic girdle pain
    \item Gastrointestinal pain, including constipation and gastroenteritis
    \item Placental abruption, which causes constant pain rather than rhythmic contractions and is an emergency
    \item Preterm labour, which requires urgent obstetric assessment when contractions begin before 37 weeks
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact your maternity hospital or midwife when contractions are regular, coming every five minutes for an hour, lasting about a minute each, or earlier if your waters break, there is a "show" with fresh bleeding, you have concerns about reduced fetal movements, or you are before 37 weeks.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Contractions before 37 weeks (preterm labour)
    \item Heavy vaginal bleeding
    \item Sudden severe or constant abdominal pain
    \item Reduced or absent fetal movements
    \item Water breaking with green or brown fluid, which may indicate the baby has passed meconium
    \item Signs of pre-eclampsia, such as severe headache, visual disturbance, or upper abdominal pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Maternity teams assess whether labour has truly begun and how it is progressing.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the pattern, frequency, and strength of contractions, and any other symptoms
    \item \textbf{Abdominal and vaginal examination:} checking the baby's position and the dilation and effacement of the cervix
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} fetal heart rate monitoring, either intermittent or continuous, to check the baby's wellbeing
    \item \textbf{Observation over time:} contractions that are regular and dilating the cervix confirm established labour
    \item \textbf{Assessment of the waters:} checking whether the membranes have ruptured
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management of contractions focuses on maternal comfort, progress of labour, and the wellbeing of mother and baby.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Movement and positions:} walking, upright positions, kneeling, and using a birth ball can ease pain and aid progress
    \item \textbf{Breathing and relaxation:} slow breathing, focusing, and relaxation techniques help women work with contractions
    \item \textbf{Comfort measures:} warm showers or baths, massage, heat packs on the lower back, and a supportive birth partner
    \item \textbf{Nitrous oxide (gas and air):} inhaled during contractions for short-term pain relief
    \item \textbf{Opioid analgesia:} injections of medicines such as pethidine or morphine provide some relief
    \item \textbf{Epidural:} regional anaesthesia provides excellent pain relief and allows many women to rest
    \item \textbf{Augmentation:} if contractions are not strong enough, synthetic oxytocin may be used to regulate them
    \item \textbf{Second stage:} when fully dilated, contractions are used with pushing efforts to birth the baby
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Prolonged or stalled labour when contractions are ineffective, sometimes requiring augmentation or caesarean
    \item Preterm labour and its consequences for the baby
    \item Fetal distress related to strong or prolonged contractions
    \item Chorioamnionitis, infection of the membranes, with prolonged labour after waters break
    \item Exhaustion and dehydration in long labours
    \item Postpartum haemorrhage, which is more likely after long or augmented labour
    \item Side effects of pain relief, including nausea, drowsiness, and effects on the baby
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The vast majority of labours proceed safely, and healthy women having healthy babies can expect good outcomes whether they birth vaginally or by caesarean. First labours typically last 12--18 hours, with contractions building gradually; subsequent labours are often shorter. Effective pain relief, good support, and freedom to move during labour improve the experience. Even when interventions are needed, modern maternity care is very safe, and most complications are detected and managed promptly.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend antenatal classes to learn about labour, contractions, and pain relief options
    \item Write a birth plan that states your preferences, while remaining flexible
    \item Practise breathing and relaxation techniques during pregnancy
    \item Stay active and upright in early labour, and rest when possible
    \item Drink fluids and eat light snacks early in labour for energy
    \item Use a supportive birth partner who knows your preferences
    \item Contact your maternity team early if you have any concerns or signs of preterm labour
    \item Know the signs of complications, such as reduced fetal movements or heavy bleeding, and act on them
\end{itemize}
