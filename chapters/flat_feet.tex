\chapter{Flat Feet}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Flat feet, also called pes planus or fallen arches, is a condition in which the arch of the foot is low or absent, so that most or all of the sole rests on the ground when standing. Flat feet are very common and are normal in babies and young children, whose arches develop over the first years of life. In many adults, flat feet cause no symptoms and need no treatment, but in others they cause foot, ankle, knee, or back pain, particularly after activity. Most flexible flat feet, in which the arch appears when the foot is lifted off the ground, are harmless. Less commonly, flat feet are rigid or develop suddenly in adulthood, which can indicate an underlying problem such as posterior tibial tendon dysfunction and needs medical assessment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Flat feet are among the most common foot conditions, affecting around 20--30\% of adults to some degree. Most are flexible and symptom-free, and the prevalence of painful flat feet is much lower. Flat feet are present in nearly all infants and most toddlers, with arches forming by around age 6--10 in most children. Adult-acquired flat foot, usually from posterior tibial tendon dysfunction, is more common in middle-aged and older adults, in women, and in people who are overweight. Most people with flat feet never need treatment, and the condition rarely causes disability when appropriately managed.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal development:} babies are born with flat feet; the arch develops as the foot's muscles, tendons, and ligaments strengthen with walking
    \item \textbf{Flexible flat feet:} the arch flattens on weight bearing but is present when not standing; this is usually inherited and benign
    \item \textbf{Ligamentous laxity:} loose connective tissue, common in some families and in conditions such as hypermobility, allows the arch to flatten
    \item \textbf{Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction:} the tendon that supports the arch weakens or tears, causing adult-acquired flat foot with pain, swelling, and a change in foot shape
    \item \textbf{Rigid flat feet:} the foot is flat even without weight bearing, which may reflect tarsal coalition (fused foot bones) or arthritis
    \item \textbf{Effects on the body:} a flattened arch alters the alignment of the leg, which can strain the ankle, knee, hip, and lower back
    \item \textbf{Mechanism of pain:} overloading of the soft tissues and joints of the midfoot and the Achilles tendon
    \item \textbf{Contributing factors:} obesity, pregnancy, prolonged standing, and occupations involving heavy loads
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A low or absent arch on standing, with the foot appearing to roll inwards (overpronation)
    \item In children: an out-toeing gait and rapid wear on the inner side of shoes
    \item No symptoms in most people with flexible flat feet
    \item Pain in the arch, heel, or inner ankle, worse after activity or long standing
    \item Swelling along the inner ankle and difficulty rising onto tiptoes in posterior tibial tendon dysfunction
    \item A rigid foot that is flat in all positions, often with limited movement
    \item Secondary symptoms: shin, knee, hip, or lower back pain from altered gait
    \item Sudden onset of one-sided flat foot in an adult is a warning sign
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of foot pain and arch changes must be considered.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Plantar fasciitis, heel pain from inflammation of the plantar fascia, which often coexists
    \item Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction, the common cause of adult-acquired flat foot
    \item Tarsal coalition, a congenital fusion of foot bones causing rigid flat foot in adolescents
    \item Arthritis, including rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis
    \item Achilles tendinopathy and other tendon problems
    \item Peripheral neuropathy and diabetes-related foot problems
    \item Stress fractures and other injuries
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Flat feet that are painless need no medical care. See a doctor or podiatrist if there is foot, ankle, or leg pain, swelling, stiffness, or a sudden change in foot shape, especially on one side, or if flat feet interfere with daily activities.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe foot or ankle pain with swelling, which may indicate a fracture or tendon rupture
    \item An inability to bear weight after an injury
    \item Signs of a blood clot: sudden calf swelling, pain, or redness
    \item A foot that is pale, cold, or numb, which needs urgent assessment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} assessment of the arch when standing and sitting, foot and leg alignment, and walking gait
    \item \textbf{Tiptoe test:} the ability to rise onto the toes tests the posterior tibial tendon and distinguishes flexible from rigid flat feet
    \item \textbf{Footwear and activity review:} shoe wear patterns and the level of activity
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} standing foot X-rays assess alignment and exclude arthritis or tarsal coalition
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound or MRI:} for suspected tendon tears or ligament injuries
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} for inflammatory arthritis when suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observation:} painless, flexible flat feet, especially in children, need no treatment beyond comfortable shoes
    \item \textbf{Footwear:} supportive shoes with a firm heel counter and good arch support are the first-line measure
    \item \textbf{Orthotics:} custom or over-the-counter arch supports and insoles relieve pain in symptomatic adults
    \item \textbf{Activity modification:} reduce high-impact loading during flares and build up gradually
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} exercises to strengthen the posterior tibial tendon and calf muscles, and stretches for the Achilles and plantar fascia
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} anti-inflammatory medicines and ice for flare-ups
    \item \textbf{Weight management:} reducing excess weight lessens the load on the feet
    \item \textbf{Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction:} early treatment with rest, orthotics, physiotherapy, and sometimes a boot or brace; surgery is considered when the tendon is torn or the foot has stiffened
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} rarely needed, for rigid symptomatic flat feet or failed non-surgical treatment of tendon dysfunction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic foot, ankle, knee, hip, and back pain from altered alignment
    \item Plantar fasciitis and Achilles problems, which commonly accompany flat feet
    \item Progression of posterior tibial tendon dysfunction to a stiff, deformed foot if untreated
    \item Reduced mobility and quality of life when pain is unmanaged
    \item Falls in older adults with painful, unstable feet
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people with flat feet live with no symptoms and need no treatment, and childhood flexible flat feet usually resolve or remain harmless. Symptomatic flat feet respond well to supportive footwear, orthotics, and physiotherapy, and the majority of people get good relief without surgery. Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction responds best to early treatment, so prompt attention to a newly flattened, painful foot is important. With appropriate management, flat feet rarely limit activity or cause lasting disability.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wear supportive, well-fitting shoes, including for work and exercise
    \item Use arch supports or orthotics if they relieve symptoms
    \item Do regular calf and foot strengthening exercises
    \item Maintain a healthy weight
    \item Stretch the calves and Achilles tendon regularly
    \item Ice and rest the foot during flare-ups
    \item See a podiatrist or doctor early for pain, swelling, or a change in foot shape
    \item Replace worn shoes, which worsen arch problems
\end{itemize}
