\chapter{Bladder-Neck Incision}
\index{Bladder-Neck Incision}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bladder-neck incision is a minimally invasive surgical procedure that widens the outlet of the bladder by making one or more small cuts in the ring of tissue where the bladder joins the urethra. The bladder neck is a muscular sphincter that normally remains closed until a person chooses to pass urine; when it is abnormally tight, it obstructs the flow of urine, a condition known as bladder outlet obstruction. The procedure is usually performed transurethrally, using a telescope passed along the urethra, and carries the advantages of a short operation time and a quick recovery. When the obstruction arises from a small prostate gland, the same operation is called transurethral incision of the prostate (TUIP).

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bladder outlet obstruction is common in older men, usually as a consequence of benign prostate enlargement. Bladder-neck incision is performed much less often than transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP), and it is typically chosen for younger men with a small prostate gland, men who wish to avoid the greater side-effect profile of TURP, or men with a congenitally tight bladder neck. Primary bladder-neck obstruction can affect men from early adulthood. The procedure is only rarely used in women, in whom bladder-neck obstruction is uncommon.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The bladder neck can become obstructed through several mechanisms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary bladder-neck obstruction:} narrowing of the bladder neck in the absence of prostate enlargement, thought to relate to muscular hypertrophy or altered innervation of the bladder-neck sphincter
    \item \textbf{Benign prostate enlargement:} when a relatively small gland projects into the bladder outlet and impedes flow
    \item \textbf{Fibrosis or scarring} of the bladder neck after previous surgery, radiation, or instrumentation
    \item \textbf{Neurological disorders} that impair relaxation of the bladder neck during voiding, producing a functional obstruction
\end{itemize}

Whatever the cause, obstruction forces the detrusor (bladder muscle) to contract against increased resistance. Over time the muscle thickens (hypertrophies) and may become overactive, poorly compliant, or weak, resulting in incomplete emptying, recurrent infections, and, in severe cases, back-pressure that damages the upper urinary tract and kidneys.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The presentation is that of lower urinary tract voiding symptoms, which typically develop gradually:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty starting the urine stream (hesitancy)
    \item A weak, slowed, or interrupted stream
    \item Straining to pass urine
    \item A sensation of incomplete bladder emptying
    \item Dribbling after finishing urination
    \item Urgency and increased urinary frequency, including waking at night (nocturia)
    \item Recurrent urinary tract infections
    \item In severe cases, complete inability to void (acute urinary retention)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of obstructive voiding symptoms must be considered before surgery is planned.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Benign prostate enlargement
    \item Prostate cancer
    \item Urethral stricture, a narrowing lower in the urethra
    \item Overactive bladder syndrome, where urgency and frequency occur without obstruction
    \item Neurogenic bladder due to spinal or neurological disease
    \item Bladder stones, tumours, or foreign bodies
    \item Medications with anticholinergic effects that impair bladder emptying
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek assessment for persistent difficulty passing urine, a weak or intermittent stream, the feeling of incomplete emptying, recurrent infections, or blood in the urine. Men of any age with these symptoms should be evaluated, as early treatment prevents progressive bladder damage.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Complete inability to pass urine with a painfully full lower abdomen (acute urinary retention)
    \item Severe lower abdominal or suprapubic pain
    \item High fever with shaking chills, which may indicate infection behind a blocked urinary tract
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment aims to confirm obstruction, exclude other causes, and gauge the impact on the kidneys and bladder.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} duration and nature of voiding symptoms, prior surgery, radiotherapy, and relevant neurological history
    \item \textbf{Examination:} abdominal palpation for a distended bladder and, in men, a digital rectal examination to assess the prostate
    \item \textbf{Urine tests:} dipstick and culture to exclude infection, and urinary flow measurement
    \item \textbf{Post-void residual measurement:} bladder ultrasound to quantify urine left after voiding
    \item \textbf{Urodynamics:} pressure-flow studies that characterise the obstruction and bladder function in selected cases
    \item \textbf{PSA blood test:} in men, where prostate assessment or exclusion of cancer is needed
    \item \textbf{Cystoscopy:} visual inspection of the bladder neck, prostate, and urethra to plan the operation
    \item \textbf{Renal tract ultrasound:} to exclude hydronephrosis from chronic obstruction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is directed at relieving obstruction and protecting the bladder and kidneys.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bladder-neck incision:} one or more small incisions widen the outlet; performed as a day procedure under general or spinal anaesthesia, with a catheter usually removed within a day
    \item \textbf{Transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP):} the preferred alternative when the prostate is large
    \item \textbf{Medical therapy:} alpha-blocker drugs (such as tamsulosin) that relax the bladder-neck and prostate smooth muscle, used either before surgery or as a long-term alternative in mild cases
    \item \textbf{Temporary catheterisation:} to drain the bladder if acute or chronic retention has occurred
    \item \textbf{Post-operative follow-up:} a urine flow test and symptom review several weeks after surgery to confirm improvement
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Bladder-neck incision is generally well tolerated, but all surgery carries some risk.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding in the urine, usually settling within a few days
    \item Urinary tract infection or epididymitis
    \item Temporary difficulty passing urine after surgery, sometimes requiring re-insertion of a catheter
    \item Scarring with recurrence of the obstruction, requiring further treatment
    \item Retrograde ejaculation (semen passes into the bladder instead of out of the penis) in men
    \item Rarely, damage to surrounding structures, prolonged bleeding, or a persistent need to re-treat
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Bladder-neck incision relieves obstruction in the large majority of men, with a quicker recovery and a lower rate of retrograde ejaculation than TURP. Symptom scores typically fall substantially and remain improved for many years. A small proportion of men require repeat treatment if symptoms recur, and those with pre-existing bladder or renal damage may not regain full function. Treating obstruction early, before the bladder muscle and kidneys are damaged, gives the best long-term results.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Primary bladder-neck obstruction cannot be reliably prevented, but its consequences can be limited by early detection and good self-care.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek early medical review for difficulty passing urine or a weak stream
    \item Take prescribed medication, such as alpha-blockers, exactly as directed and report any side effects
    \item Follow post-operative advice, including drinking plenty of fluids and reporting heavy bleeding, fever, or an inability to void
    \item Attend follow-up appointments for flow testing and bladder-emptying checks
    \item For men with prostate enlargement, maintain a healthy weight, stay active, and avoid excessive evening fluid intake to limit nocturia
\end{itemize}
