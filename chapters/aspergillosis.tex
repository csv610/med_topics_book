\chapter{Aspergillosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Aspergillosis refers to a spectrum of diseases caused by fungi of the genus \textit{Aspergillus}, ubiquitous moulds found in soil, decaying vegetation, dust, and indoor air. Most people inhale \textit{Aspergillus} spores regularly without harm, because their immune systems clear them effectively. In susceptible people, however, the fungus can cause disease in several distinct forms: allergic aspergillosis, in which the immune system overreacts to the fungus; an aspergilloma, a ball of fungus that grows within a pre-existing lung cavity; chronic pulmonary aspergillosis, a slowly progressive infection and scarring of lung tissue; and invasive aspergillosis, in which the fungus invades the lungs and spreads to other organs, usually in people with profoundly weakened immunity.

\section*{Epidemiology}
\textit{Aspergillus} is found throughout the environment worldwide, so exposure to spores is essentially universal and disease in healthy people is rare. Invasive aspergillosis occurs mainly in people with very low white-cell counts or suppressed immunity, such as those receiving chemotherapy, undergoing bone marrow or solid organ transplantation, or taking long-term high-dose corticosteroids. Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) affects mostly people with asthma or cystic fibrosis, while aspergillomas develop in people with pre-existing lung cavities, most commonly from treated tuberculosis, sarcoidosis, or other fibrosing lung diseases. The global burden of chronic pulmonary aspergillosis is substantial, particularly following pulmonary tuberculosis.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Source:} airborne spores inhaled from the environment; the fungus is not transmitted from person to person
    \item \textbf{Allergic disease:} in susceptible people the immune system overreacts to the fungus, causing airway inflammation, mucus plugging, and bronchiectasis
    \item \textbf{Saprophytic disease:} within an existing lung cavity the fungus grows as a tangled ball of hyphae, forming an aspergilloma
    \item \textbf{Invasive disease:} in profoundly immunocompromised people the fungus invades blood vessels and lung tissue, causing infarction, haemorrhage, and haematogenous spread to the brain, heart, and other organs
    \item \textbf{Chronic disease:} slowly progressive cavitation and fibrosis occur in people with milder degrees of immune impairment or underlying lung disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms depend on the form of the disease:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ABPA:} worsening asthma, wheeze, a cough productive of thick brownish plugs, low-grade fever, and sometimes weight loss
    \item \textbf{Aspergilloma:} often asymptomatic, but may cause chronic cough and haemoptysis, which can occasionally be massive and life-threatening
    \item \textbf{Chronic pulmonary aspergillosis:} persistent cough, weight loss, night sweats, fatigue, and breathlessness developing over months to years
    \item \textbf{Invasive aspergillosis:} fever, cough, pleuritic chest pain, haemoptysis, and breathlessness, with rapid progression and possible spread to the brain, heart, or bones
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Other fungal infections, such as candidiasis, histoplasmosis, or mucormycosis
    \item Tuberculosis and other chronic pulmonary infections
    \item Lung cancer and other intrathoracic tumours
    \item Bacterial pneumonia
    \item Sarcoidosis and other causes of lung cavities or scarring
    \item Severe asthma or poorly controlled cystic fibrosis without fungal allergy
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor promptly if you have asthma or cystic fibrosis and your symptoms worsen, or if you develop a chronic cough, weight loss, or unexplained fatigue. People with weakened immunity should seek medical attention early for any fever, cough, or chest symptoms.

\textbf{Call 000 (or attend an emergency department) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Coughing up blood
    \item Sudden severe breathlessness or chest pain
    \item High fever with rapid breathing in a person with a weakened immune system
    \item Confusion, severe headache, or new neurological symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} underlying lung disease, immune status, medications, and any relevant environmental exposure
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} chest X-ray and CT scan to identify cavities, fungus balls, infiltrates, and scarring
    \item \textbf{Sputum culture:} growth of \textit{Aspergillus} from sputum supports the diagnosis, although the fungus can colonise the airway without causing disease
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} serum galactomannan antigen and antibody assays are particularly useful in invasive and chronic forms
    \item \textbf{Allergy testing:} total and specific IgE levels and skin-prick testing for ABPA
    \item \textbf{Bronchoscopy:} sampling of bronchial fluid or tissue when the diagnosis is uncertain
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} occasionally required to confirm invasive tissue infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ABPA:} oral corticosteroids to suppress inflammation together with antifungal therapy, such as itraconazole, to reduce the fungal burden
    \item \textbf{Aspergilloma:} monitoring if asymptomatic; haemoptysis is treated with bronchial artery embolisation or surgical resection, and antifungals are used in some cases
    \item \textbf{Chronic pulmonary aspergillosis:} long-term oral antifungal treatment and management of the underlying lung disease
    \item \textbf{Invasive aspergillosis:} urgent hospital treatment with intravenous antifungal agents such as voriconazole, together with efforts to reduce immunosuppression and restore immune function
    \item \textbf{Prophylaxis:} preventive antifungal medication for certain high-risk patients, such as those with prolonged severe neutropenia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe or recurrent haemoptysis
    \item Haematogenous spread of invasive disease to the brain, heart, bones, and other organs
    \item Respiratory failure
    \item Destruction of lung tissue with chronic respiratory disability
    \item Side effects of antifungal drugs, including hepatic injury and interactions with other medicines
    \item Relapse of chronic or allergic disease after treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis and aspergilloma are usually controllable with treatment, although symptoms may flare repeatedly and require long-term care. Chronic pulmonary aspergillosis typically progresses slowly and requires sustained antifungal therapy. Invasive aspergillosis is a serious illness with significant mortality, particularly in people whose immune function cannot recover, although outcomes have improved considerably with modern antifungal drugs and earlier diagnosis. Early recognition and appropriate treatment remain the most important determinants of outcome.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item People with weakened immunity should avoid dusty environments, construction work, and decaying vegetation
    \item Use protective masks in high-risk settings such as building and demolition sites
    \item High-risk hospital patients should receive antifungal prophylaxis as prescribed
    \item Keep homes and workplaces well ventilated and free of damp and visible mould
    \item People with asthma or cystic fibrosis should follow their asthma action plans and airway clearance routines
    \item Seek early medical review of any new cough, fever, or breathing problem if immune function is reduced
\end{itemize}
