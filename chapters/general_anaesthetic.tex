\chapter{General Anaesthesia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A general anaesthetic is a state of controlled, reversible unconsciousness produced by medicines, so that surgery can be performed without pain, awareness, or memory of the procedure. It is given by an anaesthetist, a specialist doctor, who puts the patient to sleep, maintains their breathing, monitors their vital signs, and wakes them up safely at the end. General anaesthesia is used for major and minor surgery, caesarean sections, and some procedures in imaging and emergency care, and it is remarkably safe: serious complications are rare, even in older and sicker patients, because of careful assessment and monitoring. This chapter explains how general anaesthesia works, what happens before, during, and after it, and the risks involved.

\section*{Epidemiology}
General anaesthesia is administered in millions of operations in Australia each year, and advances in monitoring and medicines have made it very safe. The risk of death directly attributable to anaesthesia is around 1 in 100,000 to 1 in 200,000 for healthy patients. The risk of complications is higher in older people, those with heart and lung disease, and in emergency surgery, but even in these groups modern anaesthesia is well tolerated. Around 1 in 20 people has a minor side effect such as nausea, sore throat, or drowsiness. Pre-operative assessment identifies most risk factors and reduces complications.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{How it works:} anaesthetic drugs act on the brain to produce unconsciousness, amnesia, and pain relief; the exact mechanisms involve effects on nerve signalling throughout the brain
    \item \textbf{Components:} induction agents (put you to sleep), inhaled anaesthetic gases (maintain sleep), muscle relaxants (paralyse for surgery), and opioids (pain relief)
    \item \textbf{Airway and breathing:} during anaesthesia, you cannot breathe adequately on your own, so the anaesthetist supports breathing, often with a breathing tube
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen, and breathing are monitored continuously, and the depth of anaesthesia is adjusted
    \item \textbf{The stages:} induction (going to sleep), maintenance (staying asleep during surgery), and emergence (waking up)
    \item \textbf{Awareness:} extremely rare with modern monitoring, occurring in about 1 in 15,000 anaesthetics
    \item \textbf{Recovery:} the drugs are eliminated and you wake in the recovery room under observation
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} the anaesthetist matches the anaesthetic to the patient's health, the surgery, and the urgency
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Before: a pre-operative consultation, including health history, medicines, allergies, and fasting instructions
    \item Induction: a cannula in the arm, and medicines that cause sleep within seconds
    \item During: you are unaware and feel nothing, with the anaesthetist continuously present
    \item Emergence: waking in the recovery room, monitored until safe
    \item After: drowsiness, nausea, a sore throat, and shivering in some people, usually settling within hours
    \item Side effects: confusion in older people, and effects on memory that resolve
    \item Discharge: day-surgery patients go home the same day, with restrictions on driving for 24 hours
    \item The return of normal sensation, movement, and alertness within hours
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The choice of anaesthetic is individualised.
\begin{itemize}
    \item General anaesthesia versus regional anaesthesia (such as spinal or epidural) or local anaesthesia, chosen for the surgery and patient
    \item The need for a breathing tube versus a laryngeal mask, based on the surgery and risk
    \item The balance of risks in people with heart, lung, and other diseases
    \item The timing: emergency surgery has higher risk than planned surgery
    \item Alternatives are discussed with the anaesthetist
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
The anaesthetist's consultation is part of planned care. Medical review before surgery is needed for new symptoms, poor control of chronic conditions, or concerns about fitness for anaesthesia.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, chest pain, or severe dizziness after anaesthesia
    \item Heavy bleeding or severe pain after surgery
    \item Confusion that does not settle
    \item Vomiting that prevents fluids
    \item Any emergency symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative assessment:} health history, medicines, allergies, previous anaesthetics, and family history of anaesthetic problems
    \item \textbf{Examination:} heart, lungs, airway, and body mass index
    \item \textbf{Tests:} blood tests, ECG, and chest X-ray when indicated by health conditions and the surgery
    \item \textbf{Medicines management:} instructions about continuing or stopping medicines, including blood thinners and diabetes drugs
    \item \textbf{Fasting:} no food for 6 hours and clear fluids for 2 hours before anaesthesia
    \item \textbf{Assessment on the day:} final checks of fitness, fasting, and consent
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Before:} follow fasting and medicine instructions, and bring a list of medicines
    \item \textbf{During:} the anaesthetist induces sleep, maintains and monitors anaesthesia, and manages pain and fluids
    \item \textbf{After:} care in the recovery room, then on the ward or at home
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} a plan for post-operative pain, including local anaesthetics, analgesia, and instructions for home
    \item \textbf{Nausea prevention:} anti-nausea medicines are given to many patients
    \item \textbf{Post-operative review:} the anaesthetist may visit after surgery, and complications are managed promptly
    \item \textbf{Discharge:} day surgery patients need a responsible adult and no driving for 24 hours
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Minor: nausea and vomiting, sore throat, drowsiness, headache, and shivering
    \item More serious: breathing complications, allergic reactions, and effects on the heart and circulation
    \item Awareness under anaesthesia, which is extremely rare
    \item Confusion and delirium, mainly in older people
    \item Nerve injury from positioning, which usually recovers
    \item The risks of the surgery itself, which are usually greater than those of the anaesthetic
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
General anaesthesia is very safe, and serious harm is rare. The large majority of people wake smoothly, recover in hours, and return home with no lasting effects. Modern monitoring, safe medicines, and thorough assessment have transformed safety, and even older and sicker people undergo major surgery with acceptable risk. The keys to a good outcome are honest disclosure of health and medicines, following fasting instructions, and the anaesthetist tailoring the plan to the individual.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Tell the anaesthetist about all medicines, supplements, allergies, and health conditions
    \item Follow fasting instructions exactly
    \item Stop smoking before surgery, as it reduces lung complications
    \item Arrange for someone to drive you home after day surgery
    \item Do not drive or make important decisions for 24 hours after general anaesthesia
    \item Take post-operative pain relief as directed
    \item Report unusual symptoms after discharge
    \item Ask questions until you understand the plan
\end{itemize}
