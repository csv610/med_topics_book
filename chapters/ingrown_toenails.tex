\chapter{Ingrown Toenails}
\index{Ingrown Toenails}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An ingrown toenail develops when the edge of a toenail grows into the surrounding skin, most commonly at the side of the big toe. The result is pain, redness, swelling, and often infection. It is a very common problem, especially in adolescents and young adults, and is usually caused by incorrect nail cutting, tight footwear, or naturally curved nails. Most ingrown toenails can be treated at home or by a podiatrist with simple measures, but painful, recurrent, or infected cases may need minor surgery. With proper nail care and footwear, most episodes can be prevented.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Ingrown toenails affect around one in fifty people each year and are one of the most frequent reasons for podiatry and GP visits. They are most common in teenagers and young adults and affect the big toe in the vast majority of cases, often on one side. Men are affected slightly more often than women. Risk is higher in people who cut nails too short or into the corners, wear tight or pointed shoes, sweat heavily, or have naturally thick or curved nails. People with diabetes, poor circulation, or weakened immunity are more prone to complications.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The toenail normally grows forward over the nail bed. An ingrown nail occurs when a corner or edge penetrates the skin of the nail fold, usually because of a spike left by cutting the nail too short or rounding its corner, pressure from footwear, or the nail plate being too wide for the nail bed. The embedded nail triggers inflammation, pain, and often bacterial infection, with the formation of granulation tissue, a fleshy overgrowth that bleeds easily. Without treatment, the infection can spread into the deeper tissues of the toe and foot. Recurrence is common when the underlying nail shape or cutting habit persists.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain and tenderness at the corner or edge of the toenail
    \item Redness and swelling of the skin beside the nail
    \item Discharge of pus or clear fluid, sometimes with a bad smell
    \item A visible edge or spike of nail embedded in the skin
    \item Overgrowth of soft tissue beside the nail in chronic cases
    \item Pain when wearing shoes or walking
    \item The toe may feel warm, and in severe cases the infection spreads up the toe or foot
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Several other conditions affect the toes and nails and may resemble an ingrown toenail.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Paronychia:} infection of the skin fold around the nail
    \item \textbf{Cellulitis:} deeper infection of the toe or foot
    \item \textbf{Nail fungus:} thickening, discolouration, and crumbling of the nail plate
    \item \textbf{Subungual haematoma:} bleeding under the nail after injury
    \item \textbf{Subungual tumours:} rare growths under the nail
    \item \textbf{Psoriasis:} nail pitting and thickening
    \item \textbf{Diabetic foot problems:} ulcers and infections in people with diabetes
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek care from a GP or podiatrist if the ingrown toenail is painful, recurrent, or showing signs of infection such as pus, spreading redness, or increasing pain. People with diabetes, poor circulation, or immune suppression should seek care early, and anyone with severe pain or an infected toe should be assessed promptly. Early treatment is simpler and less invasive than treating a long-standing infection.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rapidly spreading redness, pain, or swelling up the foot or leg
    \item Fever, chills, or feeling unwell
    \item Red streaks extending from the toe, which may indicate spreading infection
    \item Severe pain, coldness, or numbness of the toe in a person with diabetes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} nail-cutting habits, footwear, symptoms, and appearance of the toe
    \item \textbf{Assessment of infection:} redness, warmth, discharge, and spread
    \item \textbf{Microbiological swab:} where infection is present, to identify the organism
    \item \textbf{Blood glucose test:} to exclude diabetes in severe or recurrent cases
    \item \textbf{Vascular assessment:} checking foot pulses in older people and those with risk factors
    \item \textbf{X-ray:} rarely, to exclude bone involvement
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Soaking:} warm salty soaks several times a day to soften the skin and relieve inflammation
    \item \textbf{Gentle lifting:} a podiatrist may gently lift the nail edge and place cotton or a splint beneath it
    \item \textbf{Correct nail cutting:} cutting straight across, not down the corners
    \item \textbf{Footwear:} roomy shoes that do not compress the toes
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} topical or oral treatment for infection
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple analgesics
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} partial nail avulsion, often with phenol to destroy the nail root, for recurrent or severe cases
    \item \textbf{Ongoing podiatry care:} for people with diabetes, poor circulation, or recurring problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection progressing to cellulitis and, rarely, bone infection
    \item Abscess formation
    \item Chronic pain and difficulty walking
    \item Recurrence after treatment, especially without root destruction
    \item Gangrene in severe cases in people with diabetes or vascular disease
    \item Scarring and altered nail shape after surgery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With correct home care and footwear, most mild ingrown toenails settle within days to weeks. Painful, recurrent, or infected cases respond well to podiatry treatment or minor surgery, with recurrence rates under 10 per cent when the nail root is destroyed with phenol. The mainstays of long-term success are correct nail cutting and appropriate shoes. People with diabetes need regular foot checks because their complications can be serious.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cut toenails straight across and level with the end of the toe
    \item Never cut down the corners or into the skin
    \item Avoid cutting nails too short
    \item Wear well-fitting shoes with room for the toes
    \item Keep feet clean and dry
    \item Treat foot sweating and nail fungus promptly
    \item See a podiatrist for recurring problems
    \item Check feet daily if you have diabetes, and seek care early for any problem
\end{itemize}
