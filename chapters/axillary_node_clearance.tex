\chapter{Axillary Node Clearance}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Axillary node clearance is a surgical procedure in which the lymph nodes in the armpit (axilla) are removed. It is performed mainly as part of the treatment of breast cancer, to find out whether cancer has spread from the breast to the lymph nodes and to reduce the risk of it returning in the armpit. The procedure is also used occasionally for other cancers, such as melanoma.

The lymph nodes under the arm are the first place breast cancer commonly spreads. Removing them provides important information about the stage of the cancer, which helps guide further treatment such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy or hormone therapy. Because the nodes play a role in draining fluid from the arm, their removal carries a risk of arm swelling (lymphoedema), so surgeons now perform the procedure only when it is likely to be of benefit.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Axillary node clearance is one of the most common operations related to breast cancer, which is the most frequently diagnosed cancer in women. In the past, most women with breast cancer had the full set of armpit nodes removed. Today, many women undergo a smaller procedure called a sentinel node biopsy instead, in which only one or two key nodes are removed for testing.

Full clearance is now reserved mainly for women in whom cancer is already known to have spread to the armpit nodes, either from a sentinel node biopsy or from needle sampling. Because of this shift, fewer women undergo full clearance than in earlier decades, and the risk of lymphoedema is correspondingly lower than it once was.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The procedure is performed because lymph nodes are the main route by which breast cancer cells travel beyond the breast. Cancer cells can break away from a breast tumour, enter the lymph vessels and lodge in the lymph nodes of the armpit, where they may form secondary deposits.

Removing the nodes serves two purposes. First, examining them under a microscope tells the surgical team whether cancer has spread and how many nodes are affected, which is a key part of cancer staging. Second, removing involved nodes reduces the chance of cancer recurring in the armpit. The cost is disruption of the lymphatic drainage of the arm: with fewer nodes to carry fluid back into the bloodstream, some women develop lymphoedema.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Axillary node clearance is done under a general anaesthetic, usually at the same time as surgery on the breast. The nodes are removed through a cut in the armpit, and a temporary drain is sometimes left in place to collect fluid.

After the operation, common experiences include the following.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Soreness, bruising and swelling in the armpit and upper arm
    \item Numbness, tingling or a feeling of tightness in the upper arm or armpit
    \item A temporary drainage tube, usually removed within a few days
    \item Reduced movement and stiffness of the shoulder, which improves with exercises
    \item Accumulation of fluid under the skin (seroma), which sometimes needs draining
    \item Restricted activity for a few weeks, particularly heavy lifting on the affected side
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Axillary node clearance is one of several options for the armpit in breast cancer care, and the right choice depends on the individual case.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sentinel node biopsy alone:} sufficient when the sentinel nodes are clear of cancer
    \item \textbf{Observation with no armpit surgery:} an option for some older women with early cancer
    \item \textbf{Axillary radiotherapy:} can treat involved nodes in selected cases without surgery
    \item \textbf{Targeted treatment:} chemotherapy or hormone therapy may be preferred when surgery is unlikely to add benefit
    \item \textbf{Benign causes of armpit lumps:} such as infection-related lymph node swelling, which do not need clearance
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most recovery problems occur in the first weeks after surgery. See a doctor promptly if you notice any of the following.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Increasing redness, heat, discharge or pain at the wound site
    \item Fever or chills
    \item Sudden swelling of the arm or hand
    \item Heavy bleeding or a wound that opens up
    \item Severe pain not controlled by the prescribed medicines
    \item New numbness, weakness or difficulty moving the arm
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, difficulty breathing or sudden shortness of breath
    \item Coughing up blood or sudden calf swelling (possible blood clot)
    \item Collapse or loss of consciousness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The decision to perform axillary node clearance is based on staging information gathered before and during surgery.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} feeling for enlarged nodes in the armpit
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound of the armpit:} to look for abnormal nodes before surgery
    \item \textbf{Needle sampling:} fine needle aspiration or core biopsy of suspicious nodes
    \item \textbf{Sentinel node biopsy:} during the first operation, the key nodes are identified with a dye and radioactive tracer and tested
    \item \textbf{Pathology of removed nodes:} the number of nodes containing cancer is counted after clearance
    \item \textbf{Imaging and blood tests:} to stage the cancer overall, including the bones, liver and lungs if indicated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Axillary node clearance is never used alone; it is one part of a comprehensive breast cancer treatment plan.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} removal of the nodes with or without surgery on the breast
    \item \textbf{Adjuvant treatment:} chemotherapy, radiotherapy, hormone therapy or targeted therapy based on the node findings and tumour features
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple pain medicines after surgery, then as needed
    \item \textbf{Shoulder exercises:} physiotherapy and graded movement to restore arm function
    \item \textbf{Lymphoedema care:} compression garments, exercise and specialised therapy if the arm swells
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular reviews to check for recurrence, wound problems and arm swelling
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Lymphoedema: persistent swelling of the arm, hand or chest, which can develop months or years later
    \item Seroma: a fluid collection under the armpit that may need repeated drainage
    \item Wound infection and delayed healing
    \item Numbness, tingling or pain due to injury of the nerve that runs through the armpit
    \item Stiffness or reduced shoulder movement
    \item Persistent pain or altered sensation in the armpit
    \item The small risks of any operation, including bleeding, blood clots and anaesthetic complications
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The procedure is effective at controlling cancer in the armpit and provides crucial staging information. Survival is determined mainly by the type and stage of the breast cancer and the response to treatment, not by the operation itself. Most women recover well from the surgery, with arm movement returning within weeks to months with exercise. Lymphoedema affects a significant minority and can persist, but it is manageable in most cases. Overall, outcomes for women with node-positive breast cancer have improved considerably with modern combined treatment.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Cancer screening and careful armpit and arm care help prevent problems before and after surgery.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend routine breast screening so cancers are found earlier, when armpit surgery may be less extensive
    \item Follow the surgeon's advice on activity limits and arm use after surgery
    \item Do shoulder exercises as directed to regain movement
    \item Protect the affected arm from cuts, insect bites, burns and heavy lifting
    \item Avoid having blood pressure checks, injections or blood tests in the affected arm where possible
    \item Keep the skin of the arm and hand well moisturised and treat any breaks promptly
    \item Watch for swelling of the arm, hand or chest and report it early
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and stay active, which reduces lymphoedema risk
\end{itemize}
