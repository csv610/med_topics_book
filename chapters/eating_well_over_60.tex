\chapter{Eating Well Over 60}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Eating well over 60 means choosing foods that support health, independence, and quality of life in later years. Nutritional needs change with age: the body requires fewer kilojoules but more of some nutrients, including protein, calcium, vitamin D, and B vitamins, and appetite, taste, and digestive function all change. Good nutrition in later life helps maintain muscle and bone, supports the immune system, manages chronic conditions, and prevents falls, frailty, and hospital admission. This chapter explains the principles of healthy eating for older Australians and the practical challenges and solutions.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Malnutrition and poor nutrition are common in older people and are major contributors to ill health. A substantial proportion of older Australians living in the community, and a higher proportion in residential care, are undernourished or at risk. At the same time, many older people carry excess weight, reflecting the double burden of undernutrition and overnutrition. Poor nutrition in later life is linked to frailty, falls, infections, longer hospital stays, and higher mortality.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Changes in the body and circumstances affect what older people can and do eat.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Metabolic changes:} basal metabolic rate falls with age, so fewer kilojoules are needed, but nutrient needs rise
    \item \textbf{Muscle loss:} sarcopenia, the age-related loss of muscle, increases the need for protein and resistance exercise
    \item \textbf{Bone health:} falling oestrogen and testosterone, reduced calcium absorption, and lower vitamin D increase the need for calcium and vitamin D
    \item \textbf{Reduced appetite:} changes in taste and smell, slower digestion, and medicines can reduce appetite
    \item \textbf{Dental problems:} missing teeth, dentures, and dry mouth make eating difficult
    \item \textbf{Social factors:} living alone, bereavement, reduced income, and difficulty shopping or cooking
    \item \textbf{Health conditions:} diabetes, heart disease, kidney disease, and digestive disorders shape dietary needs
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} many medicines affect appetite, taste, and nutrient absorption
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The signs that an older person is not eating well may be gradual.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unintentional weight loss or gain
    \item Loss of appetite or interest in food
    \item Fatigue, weakness, and reduced mobility
    \item Poor wound healing and frequent infections
    \item Falls and frailty
    \item Dental problems and difficulty chewing
    \item Constipation and digestive discomfort
    \item Changes in mood and energy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Poor nutrition may be confused with, or caused by, other conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Depression and bereavement, which reduce appetite and interest in food
    \item Dementia and cognitive decline, affecting shopping, cooking, and eating
    \item Medical conditions causing weight loss, including cancer, thyroid disease, and malabsorption
    \item Swallowing difficulties, requiring texture-modified food
    \item Medication side effects, including nausea and taste changes
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Older people should see a GP for regular health checks, and promptly for unintended weight loss, persistent poor appetite, or new swallowing difficulties. A GP can arrange blood tests, a dietitian review, and dental care.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden confusion, weakness, or collapse
    \item Difficulty breathing or swallowing
    \item Chest pain or signs of stroke
    \item Severe dehydration, with no urine output or extreme lethargy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment of nutrition in older people involves several elements.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Weight monitoring:} regular weighing to detect unintended changes
    \item \textbf{Nutrition screening:} validated tools identify people at risk of malnutrition
    \item \textbf{Dietary review:} a dietitian assesses intake against needs
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} checking for anaemia, vitamin D, B12, and other deficiencies, and thyroid and kidney function
    \item \textbf{Dental review:} assessment of teeth, gums, and dentures
    \item \textbf{Functional assessment:} how well the person manages shopping, cooking, and eating
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Eating well over 60 is about both quality and practical support.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Protein at every meal:} aim for quality protein sources such as lean meat, fish, eggs, dairy, legumes, and nuts to preserve muscle
    \item \textbf{Calcium and vitamin D:} dairy foods, fortified products, and supplements where needed, along with safe sun exposure
    \item \textbf{Fibre and fluids:} wholegrains, fruits, vegetables, and plenty of fluids to prevent constipation
    \item \textbf{Small, frequent meals:} useful when appetite is poor; nutrient-dense choices such as eggs, yoghurt, and nourishing soups
    \item \textbf{Fortified foods:} adding milk powder, cheese, and sauces to boost energy and protein
    \item \textbf{Practical supports:} meals on wheels, shopping assistance, and community meals
    \item \textbf{Dental care:} maintaining teeth and well-fitted dentures
    \item \textbf{Dietitian input:} personalised advice for chronic conditions and specific needs
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Malnutrition, frailty, and loss of independence
    \item Sarcopenia and increased falls and fractures
    \item Osteoporosis and bone fractures
    \item Impaired immunity and delayed wound healing
    \item Longer hospital stays and slower recovery
    \item Depression and social isolation around meals
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Good nutrition in later life has a powerful effect on health and independence. Weight can be stabilised, muscle and bone maintained, and chronic conditions better controlled with appropriate eating. Even in older people who have lost weight, targeted nutritional support improves strength, function, and quality of life. It is never too late to improve eating habits.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Eat a varied diet based on the Australian Guide to Healthy Eating
    \item Include protein-rich foods at every meal
    \item Choose wholegrains, fruit, and vegetables daily
    \item Drink fluids regularly, aiming for water, milk, and herbal tea
    \item Share meals with others where possible
    \item Keep food simple and convenient, with healthy snacks to hand
    \item Have regular health and dental checks
\end{itemize}
