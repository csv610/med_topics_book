\chapter{Turner syndrome}
\index{Turner syndrome}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Turner syndrome (TS), also known as Ullrich-Turner syndrome, is a genetic disorder affecting females that is characterized by the complete or partial absence of one of the two X chromosomes. First clinically described by the American endocrinologist Dr. Henry Turner in 1938, and cytogenetically identified by Dr. Charles Ford and colleagues in 1959, this condition stands as one of the most common and clinically significant sex chromosome abnormalities in humans. The phenotypic expression of Turner syndrome is remarkably heterogeneous, ranging from severe congenital anomalies that are detectable prenatally to subtle developmental and endocrine discrepancies that may go unrecognized until adolescence or young adulthood.

The biological scope of Turner syndrome spans multiple organ systems, requiring lifelong multidisciplinary medical care. The missing genetic material, particularly on the short arm (p-arm) of the X chromosome, disrupts the normal developmental cascade. The two hallmark clinical features of Turner syndrome are short stature, which is almost universally present, and premature ovarian insufficiency (gonadal dysgenesis). The latter leads to an absence of spontaneous pubertal development, primary amenorrhoea, and subsequent infertility in the vast majority of cases. Beyond these primary endocrine manifestations, individuals with Turner syndrome are at a significantly elevated risk for cardiovascular malformations (most notably bicuspid aortic valve and coarctation of the aorta), renal abnormalities, autoimmune disorders (such as Hashimoto's thyroiditis and celiac disease), progressive hearing loss, and a specific neurocognitive profile characterized by visual-spatial and executive functioning challenges.

Understanding Turner syndrome is of paramount importance for clinicians, patients, and families alike. While the condition poses substantial physical and psychosocial challenges, early diagnosis, timely initiation of recombinant growth hormone therapy, appropriate estrogen and progesterone replacement, and vigilant screening for systemic complications have transformed the clinical outlook. Today, with comprehensive medical support, females with Turner syndrome can achieve height outcomes near their target ranges, navigate key developmental milestones, and lead highly successful, independent, and fulfilling lives.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of Turner syndrome is characterized by a stable global prevalence, showing no significant variation across geographical locations, ethnicities, or socio-economic backgrounds.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prevalence and Birth Incidence:} Turner syndrome occurs in approximately 1 in 2,000 to 1 in 2,500 live female births. This live-birth prevalence, however, represents only a tiny fraction of the total conceptuses affected by X-chromosome monosomy. It is estimated that approximately 99\% of all conceptuses with a 45,X karyotype terminate spontaneously in the first or second trimester of pregnancy. X-chromosome monosomy is responsible for approximately 10\% of all spontaneous abortions (miscarriages) worldwide.
    \item \textbf{Demographics and Inheritance:} Unlike many other chromosomal trisomies (such as Down syndrome), the occurrence of Turner syndrome is entirely sporadic and is not associated with advanced maternal or paternal age. The risk of recurrence in a subsequent pregnancy for parents who have had one child with Turner syndrome is extremely low, estimated at less than 1\%.
    \item \textbf{Mortality and Life Expectancy:} Although the majority of individuals with Turner syndrome lead long lives, the overall mortality rate is approximately three times higher than that of the general female population. This excess mortality is primarily driven by congenital and acquired cardiovascular diseases, including acute aortic dissection, coronary artery disease, and stroke. With modern proactive care, the life expectancy gap is closing.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The underlying cause of Turner syndrome is the loss of genetic material from the sex chromosomes in a female. In normal human development, females possess two X chromosomes (46,XX) and males possess one X and one Y chromosome (46,XY). In Turner syndrome, the absence of all or part of the second X chromosome disrupts the genomic dosage required for normal cellular function and somatic development.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Classical Monosomy (45,X):} Approximately 45\% to 50\% of individuals with Turner syndrome have classical monosomy, where a single X chromosome is present in all nucleated cells (45,X). This occurs due to non-disjunction during gametogenesis (the formation of egg or sperm cells), where a sex chromosome fails to segregate. In roughly 70\% to 80\% of these classical cases, the single intact X chromosome is maternal in origin, indicating that the paternal sex chromosome was lost during spermatogenesis or early mitotic division.
    \item \textbf{Chromosomal Mosaicism:} Around 30\% to 40\% of cases present with mosaicism (e.g., 45,X/46,XX or 45,X/46,XY). In these individuals, some cells contain a single X chromosome, while others contain a normal chromosomal complement. Mosaicism arises from mitotic errors occurring after fertilization during early embryonic cleavage. The clinical phenotype of mosaic individuals is typically milder and depends heavily on the tissue-specific distribution of the normal versus abnormal cell lines. Notably, individuals with Y-chromosome mosaicism (e.g., 45,X/46,XY) have a highly elevated risk of developing gonadoblastoma in their streak gonads, necessitating prophylactic surgical removal of the gonads.
    \item \textbf{Structural Abnormalities of the X Chromosome:} In 10\% to 15\% of cases, two X chromosomes are present, but one is structurally abnormal. These anomalies include isochromosomes (e.g., 46,X,i(Xq), where the chromosome has two long arms and no short arm), ring chromosomes (where the ends of the chromosome have deleted and fused), or partial deletions of the short (p) or long (q) arm.
    \item \textbf{SHOX Gene Haploinsufficiency:} The short stature and specific skeletal abnormalities in Turner syndrome are directly linked to the haploinsufficiency of the \textit{SHOX} (Short Stature Homeobox) gene. Located in the pseudoautosomal region 1 (PAR1) at the terminal ends of the short arms of the X and Y chromosomes (Xp22.3 and Yp11.3), this gene escapes normal X-inactivation. In healthy females, both copies of the \textit{SHOX} gene remain transcriptionally active. The loss of one copy in Turner syndrome disrupts chondrocyte proliferation and differentiation within the epiphyseal growth plates of long bones, leading to stunted longitudinal bone growth.
    \item \textbf{Oocyte Atresia and Gonadal Dysgenesis:} Normal ovarian development requires the presence of genes on both the short and long arms of the X chromosome. While germ cells (oogonia) migrate normally to the genital ridges in 45,X fetuses, the absence of the second X chromosome leads to accelerated oocyte atresia (programmed cell death) during late fetal development and infancy. By the time of birth, the ovaries are depleted of primordial follicles and are replaced by non-functional, fibrous connective tissue, clinically described as ``streak gonads.'' This lack of follicular development results in a profound deficiency of estrogen and progesterone.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features of Turner syndrome are highly variable and manifest across different life stages, from the neonatal period through adulthood.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Growth and Skeletal Features:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Short Stature:} The most consistent feature, present in over 95\% of untreated females. Without growth hormone therapy, the average adult height is approximately 143 cm, which is about 20 cm shorter than the mid-parental target height.
        \item \textbf{Webbed Neck (Pterygium colli):} A fold of skin extending from the mastoid process of the skull to the acromion, resulting from fetal lymphatic obstruction.
        \item \textbf{Cubitus Valgus:} An increased carrying angle of the elbow, causing the forearms to angle away from the body when the arms are held straight at the sides.
        \item \textbf{Short Fourth Metacarpal:} A shortened fourth knuckle on the hand, which becomes obvious when making a fist.
        \item \textbf{Skeletal Anomalies:} High-arched palate, micrognathia (small lower jaw), scoliosis (spinal curvature in 10\% to 20\% of cases), and a broad, shield-like chest with widely spaced nipples.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular System:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Bicuspid Aortic Valve:} The most common congenital heart defect, occurring in approximately 30\% of individuals, where the aortic valve has two cusps instead of three.
        \item \textbf{Coarctation of the Aorta:} Narrowing of the aortic arch, present in 10\% to 12\% of patients, which can lead to upper-body hypertension and left ventricular hypertrophy.
        \item \textbf{Aortic Dilation and Dissection:} A progressive widening of the ascending aorta that carries a high risk of life-threatening rupture.
        \item \textbf{Hypertension:} Systemic high blood pressure is common in children and adults, even in the absence of obvious cardiac or renal malformations.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reproductive and Endocrine Systems:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Gonadal Dysgenesis:} Over 90\% of females experience complete ovarian failure. Spontaneous breast development and menarche occur in only 10\% to 20\% of girls (mostly those with mosaicism), and almost all experience premature menopause. Infertility is present in over 99\% of cases.
        \item \textbf{Autoimmune Hypothyroidism:} Up to 30\% of individuals develop Hashimoto's thyroiditis, presenting with elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and anti-thyroid antibodies.
        \item \textbf{Metabolic Dysfunction:} Elevated risk of insulin resistance, impaired glucose tolerance, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and atherogenic dyslipidaemia.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Renal and Lymphatic Systems:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Renal Anomalies:} Horseshoe kidney (where the lower poles of both kidneys are fused), double collecting systems, or renal agenesis occur in 30\% to 40\% of cases.
        \item \textbf{Congenital Lymphedema:} Persistent swelling of the hands and feet in newborns due to impaired lymphatic drainage, which typically resolves in early childhood.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sensory and Cognitive Profiles:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Otologic Disease:} Recurrent otitis media in childhood is extremely common. Conductive and progressive sensorineural hearing loss develops in up to 50\% to 90\% of adults.
        \item \textbf{Ocular Anomalies:} Strabismus (crossed eyes), amblyopia, ptosis (drooping eyelids), and hyperopia.
        \item \textbf{Neurocognitive Profile:} General intelligence is usually within the normal range, but there is a characteristic cognitive profile including difficulties with visual-spatial processing, non-verbal memory, executive function, and mathematics.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A thorough evaluation is essential to differentiate Turner syndrome from other conditions presenting with short stature, delayed puberty, or similar physical anomalies.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Noonan Syndrome:} Frequently confused with Turner syndrome due to shared features like short stature, webbed neck, cubitus valgus, and lymphedema. However, Noonan syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by mutations in the RAS-MAPK pathway (most commonly the \textit{PTPN11} gene) and affects both males and females. Key differences include a normal karyotype, pulmonic stenosis (rather than left-sided heart defects), distinct facial features (hypertelorism, down-slanting eyes), and a higher prevalence of intellectual disability.
    \item \textbf{Swyer Syndrome (46,XY Complete Gonadal Dysgenesis):} Individuals with Swyer syndrome present as phenotypic females with primary amenorrhoea and streak gonads due to mutations in sex-determining genes (such as \textit{SRY}). They differ from Turner syndrome patients as they do not exhibit short stature, webbed neck, or cardiovascular abnormalities. They typically have a normal or slightly tall female height and a 46,XY karyotype.
    \item \textbf{Constitutional Delay of Growth and Puberty (CDGP):} A temporary delay in skeletal growth and pubertal onset in an otherwise healthy child who eventually reaches normal adult height and sexual maturity. It is distinguished from Turner syndrome by a normal 46,XX karyotype, normal gonadotropin levels, and the absence of dysmorphic somatic features.
    \item \textbf{Russell-Silver Syndrome:} A genetic disorder characterized by intrauterine growth restriction, postnatal short stature, relative macrocephaly, triangular face, limb asymmetry, and feeding difficulties. While short stature is shared, the typical dysmorphic features, normal karyotype, and asymmetric growth distinguish it from Turner syndrome.
    \item \textbf{Isolated Growth Hormone Deficiency (GHD):} Presents with severe short stature and delayed bone age. It lacks the systemic anomalies (cardiac, renal), dysmorphic physical signs (webbed neck, shield chest), and primary ovarian insufficiency seen in Turner syndrome. Endocrine stimulation tests reveal subnormal growth hormone levels, and karyotype is normal.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Given the complex, multi-systemic nature of Turner syndrome, timely medical consultation is essential for both diagnostic evaluation and the management of acute complications.

\textbf{Routine Medical Consultation:}
A medical evaluation by a general practitioner or pediatrician should be sought if a girl exhibits:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent growth failure (e.g., dropping across height percentiles or growing below the 3rd percentile on standard growth charts).
    \item Unexplained swelling of the hands or feet (lymphedema) in infancy.
    \item Lack of breast development by age 13 or lack of menstrual periods (amenorrhoea) by age 15.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Emergency Situations:}
Certain acute symptoms in individuals with Turner syndrome indicate potentially life-threatening complications that require immediate emergency services. Emergency medical care (calling local emergency services in many countries, 911 in the United States, or 999 in the United Kingdom) must be sought immediately for:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe, tearing or ripping pain in the chest, back, or abdomen (highly suggestive of acute aortic dissection).
    \item Sudden shortness of breath, severe chest tightness, or respiratory distress.
    \item Unexplained fainting, loss of consciousness, or severe dizziness.
    \item Signs of a stroke, such as sudden weakness or numbness of the face, arm, or leg (particularly on one side of the body), difficulty speaking, confusion, or visual loss.
    \item High fever with severe lethargy, neck stiffness, and confusion, indicating a systemic infection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Confirming a diagnosis of Turner syndrome requires a comprehensive clinical evaluation combined with genetic testing and screening for systemic anomalies.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Standard Karyotyping (Gold Standard):} A chromosomal analysis of peripheral blood is the definitive diagnostic test. A 30-cell karyotype must be performed to identify mosaicism reliably (detecting down to approximately 10\% of a minor cell line). If standard blood karyotyping is normal but clinical suspicion remains high, a high-resolution karyotype, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), or chromosomal microarray (CMA) should be performed on other tissues (e.g., skin fibroblasts or buccal swabs) to check for low-level mosaicism or microdeletions.
    \item \textbf{Y-Chromosome Material Screening:} In all individuals diagnosed with Turner syndrome, screening for Y-chromosome material (such as \textit{SRY} gene sequences) using PCR or FISH is recommended. The presence of Y-chromosome sequences carries a high risk of gonadoblastoma, requiring prophylactic surgical removal of the streak gonads.
    \item \textbf{Prenatal Diagnosis:} Turner syndrome is often suspected during prenatal screening due to findings on obstetric ultrasound, such as increased nuchal translucency, cystic hygroma, fetal hydrops, or cardiac defects. Non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) using cell-free fetal DNA is highly sensitive, but positive prenatal results must always be confirmed postnatally or via amniocentesis/chorionic villus sampling (CVS).
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular Evaluation:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Echocardiography:} All patients must undergo a comprehensive transthoracic echocardiogram at the time of diagnosis to evaluate the aortic valve anatomy, aortic dimensions, and check for coarctation.
        \item \textbf{Cardiac Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):} Recommended in adolescents and adults, or when echocardiography is insufficient. It provides accurate measurements of the aortic size index (ASI) to monitor for progressive aortic dilation.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Endocrine and Biochemical Screening:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Gonadotropins:} Measurement of serum follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinizing hormone (LH). These are elevated (hypergonadotropic hypogonadism) in early childhood and adolescence, confirming ovarian failure.
        \item \textbf{Thyroid Function:} Annual testing of serum TSH and free T4, alongside anti-thyroid peroxidase (TPO) antibodies, starting in early childhood to screen for Hashimoto's thyroiditis.
        \item \textbf{Celiac Serology:} Annual screening for celiac disease using tissue transglutaminase IgA (tTG-IgA) antibodies, starting at age 2.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Renal and Audiological Assessment:} A renal ultrasound is required at diagnosis to rule out structural anomalies. Comprehensive audiological evaluations are recommended every 1 to 2 years in children and every 3 to 5 years in adults to detect progressive hearing loss early.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of Turner syndrome is multidisciplinary and extends across the patient's lifetime. Treatment aims to optimize physical growth, induce pubertal development, manage systemic comorbidities, and support psychosocial health.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Recombinant Human Growth Hormone (rhGH) Therapy:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Indication and Timing:} Growth hormone therapy should be initiated as soon as growth failure is documented (usually when height falls below the 5th percentile), which can be as early as 9 to 12 months of age, but typically begins between 4 and 6 years.
        \item \textbf{Dosing and Goals:} Administered via daily subcutaneous injections at a dose of 0.35 to 0.375 mg/kg/week. The goal is to maximize childhood growth velocity and achieve a normal adult height. Therapy is continued until adult height is reached, bone age is greater than 14 years, or growth velocity falls below 2 cm/year.
        \item \textbf{Adjuvant Therapy:} In girls older than 12 years with severe short stature, low-dose oxandrolone (an anabolic steroid) may be added to growth hormone therapy to improve final height.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Pubertal Induction:} Estrogen replacement therapy is initiated at age 11 to 12 years to mimic the natural onset of puberty. Low-dose transdermal estrogen (using patches or gels) is preferred to avoid the hepatic first-pass effect and reduce thromboembolic risks. The dose is slowly titrated upward over 2 to 3 years.
        \item \textbf{Progesterone Integration:} Once breakthrough bleeding occurs, or after 2 years of estrogen therapy, sequential oral or transdermal progesterone (e.g., micronized progesterone) is added to establish regular menstrual cycles and protect the endometrium from hyperplasia.
        \item \textbf{Adulthood Maintenance:} Estrogen and progesterone therapy must be continued until the typical age of natural menopause (around age 50) to maintain bone mineral density, protect cardiovascular health, and prevent osteoporosis.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgical and Interventional Procedures:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Prophylactic Gonadectomy:} Recommended for patients with documented Y-chromosome material to eliminate the risk of developing gonadoblastoma.
        \item \textbf{Cardiovascular Interventions:} Surgical repair or balloon angioplasty with stenting for coarctation of the aorta. Surgical replacement of the aortic root or ascending aorta is indicated if the aortic size index exceeds critical thresholds (typically $\ge 2.5 cm/m^2$).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fertility and Family Planning:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Oocyte Donation:} In vitro fertilization (IVF) using donor oocytes is the most successful pathway to pregnancy for women with Turner syndrome.
        \item \textbf{Pregnancy Risk Management:} Because the risk of aortic dissection and rupture increases dramatically during pregnancy, a rigorous cardiovascular workup (including cardiac MRI) is mandatory prior to attempting pregnancy. Single-embryo transfer is strongly recommended to prevent the cardiovascular stress of multiple gestations.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychosocial and Educational Support:} Early evaluation for learning differences, especially in visual-spatial tasks and mathematics, is helpful. Psycho-educational support and participation in Turner syndrome support groups can assist with self-esteem and the transition to adult care.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The genetic changes in Turner syndrome lead to a range of potential systemic complications that require lifelong monitoring.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Aortic Dissection and Rupture:} This is the most serious and life-threatening complication of Turner syndrome. The risk of aortic dissection is approximately 100-fold higher than in the general female population. It is strongly linked to bicuspid aortic valve, hypertension, and progressive aortic root dilation.
    \item \textbf{Osteopenia and Osteoporosis:} Severe estrogen deficiency due to gonadal dysgenesis increases bone resorption. Without timely and consistent estrogen replacement therapy, females are at high risk of early-onset osteopenia, osteoporosis, and fragility fractures.
    \item \textbf{Hearing Loss and Otologic Disease:} Recurrent otitis media in childhood can lead to chronic middle ear disease, tympanic membrane scarring, and conductive hearing loss. In addition, progressive sensorineural hearing loss occurs in early adulthood in up to 90\% of patients.
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune Endocrinopathies:} Hashimoto's thyroiditis is highly prevalent and leads to hypothyroidism, which can exacerbate growth failure if untreated. There is also an increased risk of celiac disease, type 1 diabetes mellitus, and inflammatory bowel disease.
    \item \textbf{Metabolic Syndrome and Cardiovascular Risk:} Insulin resistance, abdominal obesity, dyslipidaemia, and physical inactivity are common, leading to a high prevalence of metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes, which further elevates the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease.
    \item \textbf{Renal Complications:} Structural renal anomalies (such as horseshoe kidney) increase the susceptibility to recurrent urinary tract infections, vesicoureteral reflux, and nephrolithiasis (kidney stones), which can contribute to chronic hypertension.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals with Turner syndrome has improved significantly due to early diagnosis, growth hormone therapy, and proactive cardiovascular care.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Height and Growth Outcomes:} With early initiation of recombinant growth hormone, the final adult height can be increased by an average of 5 to 8 cm. This allows many women to achieve an adult height within the normal female range, reducing the psychosocial impact of short stature.
    \item \textbf{Reproductive Outcomes:} Although infertility remains a major clinical challenge, assisted reproductive technologies, particularly oocyte donation, allow many women to experience pregnancy. Adoption and gestational surrogacy are also excellent options.
    \item \textbf{Social and Educational Outcomes:} The vast majority of females with Turner syndrome lead highly successful, independent lives. Most complete higher education, secure employment, and report a good quality of life. The specific visual-spatial learning difficulties do not typically limit overall academic or career success when managed early.
    \item \textbf{Survival and Lifespan:} The average life expectancy is reduced by approximately 10 to 13 years, primarily due to cardiovascular complications. However, early detection of cardiac defects, tight control of blood pressure, and regular follow-ups can help many patients achieve a normal lifespan.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Because Turner syndrome is a genetic condition arising from sporadic chromosomal errors, there are no primary prevention methods to prevent the disorder. However, secondary prevention focuses on avoiding complications, maximizing wellness, and promoting a healthy lifestyle.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adherence to Growth and Hormone Therapies:} Consistent daily administration of growth hormone during childhood and adherence to estrogen/progesterone replacement in adulthood are crucial. This prevents severe short stature, supports bone mineral density, and helps protect cardiovascular health.
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular Health and Blood Pressure Control:} Regular blood pressure monitoring at home and during clinic visits is essential. Maintaining strict control of blood pressure (target $< 120/80 mmHg$) is the most effective way to reduce the risk of aortic dilation and dissection.
    \item \textbf{Bone Health and Nutrition:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Dietary Factors:} Consuming a diet rich in calcium and vitamin D is essential for bone strength. Calcium supplementation should be considered if dietary intake is insufficient.
        \item \textbf{Physical Activity:} Participating in regular, moderate-intensity weight-bearing exercises (such as walking, running, or tennis) helps maintain bone density and cardiovascular fitness. High-intensity isometric exercises (like heavy weightlifting) should be avoided if aortic dilation is present, as they cause acute spikes in blood pressure.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Otic and Audiological Care:} Protecting hearing by avoiding exposure to loud noises and seeking prompt medical treatment for ear pain or signs of middle ear infection is important. Standard ear hygiene and regular audiological testing are key to managing progressive hearing loss.
    \item \textbf{Healthy Lifestyle to Prevent Metabolic Syndrome:} Adopting a balanced diet low in saturated fats and refined sugars, maintaining a healthy weight, and staying physically active help reduce the risk of developing type 2 diabetes and dyslipidaemia.
    \item \textbf{Immunizations:} Ensuring complete vaccination status, including pneumococcal vaccines, is recommended to lower the risk of respiratory infections that can lead to otitis media.
\end{itemize}
