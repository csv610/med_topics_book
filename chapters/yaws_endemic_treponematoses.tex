\chapter{Yaws endemic treponematoses}
\index{Yaws endemic treponematoses}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Yaws is a chronic, infectious, and debilitating childhood disease caused by the spirochete bacterium \textit{Treponema pallidum} subspecies \textit{pertenue}. It belongs to a group of infections known as the endemic treponematoses, which also includes endemic syphilis (bejel), caused by \textit{Treponema pallidum} subspecies \textit{endemicum}, and pinta, caused by \textit{Treponema carateum}. These conditions are non-venereal infections that are epidemiologically and clinically distinct from venereal syphilis, which is caused by \textit{Treponema pallidum} subspecies \textit{pallidum}. Unlike venereal syphilis, yaws is not sexually transmitted, does not cross the placental barrier to cause congenital infection, and primarily manifests in the skin, bones, and joints without involving the cardiovascular or central nervous systems.

The disease primarily affects children living in warm, humid, tropical forest regions of Africa, Southeast Asia, and the Pacific. Yaws is characterized by an early stage marked by highly infectious skin lesions (papillomas) and bone pain, followed by a latent period, and, in approximately 10\% of untreated individuals, a late stage characterized by severe, destructive lesions of the skin, bone, and cartilage. This destructive process can lead to permanent disfigurement, joint contractures, and physical disability, which in turn leads to severe social stigmatization and economic hardship for affected individuals and their families.

As a Neglected Tropical Disease (NTD), yaws has been the target of global eradication efforts for decades. It is considered prime for eradication because humans are the only significant reservoir of the infection, and it can be easily cured with a single, inexpensive oral dose of the macrolide antibiotic azithromycin. The World Health Organization (WHO) launched a renewed eradication campaign in 2012, known as the Morges Strategy, with the goal of completely eliminating transmission worldwide. If successful, yaws will become the second human disease in history to be eradicated, and the first bacterial disease to achieve this status.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of yaws is characterized by its focal distribution among rural populations. It occurs mainly in warm, humid settings in parts of West and Central Africa, Southeast Asia, and the Pacific. The number and location of affected countries change as surveillance improves, so current WHO country and outbreak information should be consulted.

The true global burden of yaws is difficult to estimate because surveillance and laboratory access are limited. Disease is highly focal and concentrated in specific villages and communities. Children under 15 are commonly affected, but people of any age can be infected. Humans are the important reservoir; children are a principal affected group, not a separate reservoir.

Transmission of yaws occurs through direct, skin-to-skin contact with the infectious lesions of an infected person. The spirochete bacterium enters the body of a susceptible individual through minor cuts, abrasions, scratches, or insect bites. Consequently, the transmission of yaws is strongly facilitated by socioeconomic factors associated with poverty, including overcrowding, poor personal hygiene, limited access to clean water, lack of protective footwear, and inadequate sanitation. Because these communities are often geographically isolated and lack basic primary healthcare services, early cases frequently go untreated, allowing the infection to spread unchecked among children.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The causative organism of yaws is \textit{Treponema pallidum} subspecies \textit{pertenue}, a spiral-shaped, motile bacterium (spirochete) that is microaerophilic and extremely sensitive to heat, drying, and oxygen. This spirochete is morphologically and serologically indistinguishable from the agents of venereal syphilis and bejel. Genomically, the subspecies of \textit{Treponema pallidum} share over 99.8\% sequence identity, with the few genetic variations present determining their distinct clinical presentations, tissue tropisms, and modes of transmission.

The pathophysiology and risk factors of yaws involve the following key processes and determinants:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inoculation and Local Multiplication}: The spirochetes are deposited into the dermis or subcutaneous tissue of a susceptible host through micro-trauma in the skin barrier. Following an incubation period ranging from 9 to 90 days (average of 21 days), the bacteria multiply locally, inciting a cellular immune response characterized by lymphocytic and histiocytic infiltration, leading to the formation of the primary lesion.
    \item \textbf{Dissemination}: Early in the course of the infection, often before the primary lesion has fully healed, the spirochetes invade the local lymphatic channels and enter the bloodstream. This systemic dissemination allows the bacteria to seed other areas of the skin, the periosteum of the bones, and joint capsules.
    \item \textbf{Humoral and Cellular Immune Responses}: The host mounts both humoral (antibody production) and cellular immune responses against the spirochete. These responses are sufficient to cause the spontaneous regression of early skin lesions but fail to completely clear the organism from the body, leading to a state of equilibrium known as latency.
    \item \textbf{Latency}: In the latent stage, the spirochetes remain viable but dormant within tissues, particularly in the bones, spleen, and lymph nodes. The patient remains asymptomatic, but serological tests remain positive. Latency can last for years or decades, with periodic reactivations causing recurrent early skin lesions.
    \item \textbf{Late Destructive Lesions (Gummata)}: In a subset of untreated individuals, the prolonged cellular immune response to persistent latent spirochetes leads to a delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction. This reaction causes the formation of chronic, destructive granulomatous lesions called gummata in the skin, subcutaneous tissues, cartilage, and bone, resulting in tissue necrosis and osteolysis.
    \item \textbf{Environmental and Socioeconomic Risk Factors}: Risk factors include living in a tropical climate with high humidity, which prevents the rapid drying of skin abrasions and enhances spirochete survival; lack of access to clean water and soap, which impairs personal hygiene; and overcrowding in household and school settings, which increases the likelihood of close physical contact.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical course of yaws is divided into early and late stages, separated by a variable period of latency. Early yaws consists of the primary and secondary stages and is characterized by infectious skin lesions and early bone pain. Late yaws, or the tertiary stage, involves destructive, non-infectious lesions of the skin, cartilage, and bone.

The clinical presentations of yaws progress through the following phases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary Stage}: The initial manifestation is a single, painless papule at the site of inoculation, most frequently on the lower extremities, buttocks, hands, or face. Over several weeks, this papule enlarges and becomes a raised, fleshy, raspberry-like growth (papilloma or frambesioma) that is typically 1 to 5 centimeters in diameter. The lesion is covered by a yellow-amber crust, which, when removed, reveals a red, weeping base that is highly infectious and rich in spirochetes. Although painless, it may be pruritic. The primary papilloma (often called the ``mother yaw'') usually heals spontaneously within 3 to 6 months, leaving an atrophic, hypopigmented scar with a hyperpigmented border.
    \item \textbf{Secondary Stage}: Within weeks to months after the appearance of the primary lesion, disseminated secondary lesions appear. These can take the form of widespread smaller papillomas, macular or papular rashes, or scaly, dry plaques. A characteristic feature of this stage is palmoplantar papillomas and hyperkeratosis, where painful lesions on the soles of the feet split and ulcerate. The intense pain causes patients to walk on the outer borders of their feet, resulting in a characteristic gait known as ``crab yaws.'' Bone and joint involvement is also common, presenting as acute periostitis and osteitis, particularly of the tibia, fibula, and phalanges. This causes severe nocturnal bone pain, swelling of the limbs, and dactylitis (swelling of the fingers), often accompanied by low-grade fever and joint stiffness.
    \item \textbf{Latent Stage}: Following the resolution of secondary lesions, the patient enters a clinically silent phase. During latency, there are no visible signs or symptoms of the disease, and diagnosis is possible only through serological testing. Latency can last for years, but reactivations of early infectious skin lesions may occur, particularly during the first 5 years.
    \item \textbf{Tertiary Stage}: Developing in approximately 10\% of untreated cases, this late stage is characterized by irreversible, destructive lesions. Gummata develop in the skin and subcutaneous tissues, leading to deep, chronic ulcers that heal with contractures and severe scarring. Bone destruction is prominent, with chronic periostitis causing the anterior bowing of the tibia, a condition known as ``sabre tibia.'' The most severe complication is gangosa (rhinopharyngitis mutilans), a rapid, destructive necrosis of the nasal septum, hard palate, and upper lip, which can obliterate the midface and create a single large facial cavity. Another late manifestation is goundou, which presents as large, bilateral bony outgrowths of the maxilla adjacent to the nose, though this is now exceedingly rare.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A broad differential diagnosis must be considered when evaluating suspected cases of yaws, as many of its skin and bone manifestations mimic other tropical, infectious, and dermatological conditions. Accurately distinguishing yaws from these conditions is essential for starting the correct treatment and preventing unnecessary epidemiological reporting.

The primary differential diagnoses include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Venereal Syphilis}: Caused by \textit{Treponema pallidum} subsp. \textit{pallidum}, venereal syphilis is serologically identical to yaws. It is differentiated by its mode of transmission (sexual rather than casual skin-to-skin contact), the location and nature of the primary lesion (a genital chancre rather than an extragenital papilloma), its potential for congenital transmission, and its late-stage complications, which involve the cardiovascular and central nervous systems.
    \item \textbf{Endemic Syphilis (Bejel)}: Caused by \textit{Treponema pallidum} subsp. \textit{endemicum}, this non-venereal treponematosis occurs in dry, arid regions of the Middle East and the Sahel, rather than the humid tropics. The primary lesion is rarely observed, and the disease typically presents with mucous patches in the mouth, followed by skin and bone lesions.
    \item \textbf{Pinta}: Caused by \textit{Treponema carateum}, this endemic treponematosis is restricted to Central and South America. It affects only the skin, presenting as scaly, hyperpigmented or hypopigmented plaques (pintids) that do not ulcerate, and it never involves bones, joints, or cartilage.
    \item \textbf{Tropical Ulcer}: Also known as tropical phagedenic ulcer, this is a painful, rapidly destructive skin infection caused by a mixture of bacteria, including \textit{Fusobacterium nucleatum} and spirochetes. It typically presents as a single, deep, foul-smelling ulcer on the lower leg with a flat, sloughy base, in contrast to the raised, painless, raspberry-like papilloma of primary yaws.
    \item \textbf{Cutaneous Leishmaniasis}: A parasitic infection transmitted by sandflies, cutaneous leishmaniasis causes chronic, painless skin ulcers with raised, indurated borders. It is distinguished from yaws by the absence of bone involvement and the identification of \textit{Leishmania} amastigotes in skin smears or biopsies.
    \item \textbf{Scabies}: An infestation by the mite \textit{Sarcoptes scabiei} characterized by intense nocturnal pruritus and papules, vesicles, or burrows in the web spaces of the fingers, wrists, and waistline. While secondary bacterial infection of scabies lesions can mimic yaws papules, scabies lacks the bone involvement and characteristic papillomas of yaws.
    \item \textbf{Impetigo}: A superficial bacterial skin infection caused by \textit{Staphylococcus aureus} or \textit{Streptococcus pyogenes}, presenting as honey-colored crusted lesions. It is highly contagious among children but is distinguished from yaws by its rapid onset, lack of underlying induration, and absence of skeletal pain and treponemal antibodies.
    \item \textbf{Leprosy (Hansen's Disease)}: Caused by \textit{Mycobacterium leprae}, leprosy can present with hypopigmented skin macules or plaques. However, leprosy is characterized by sensory loss in the skin lesions, peripheral nerve enlargement, and the absence of the joint pain and raspberry-like papillomas seen in yaws.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Although yaws is a chronic, slowly progressive disease that rarely presents as an acute, life-threatening emergency, early medical evaluation is vital to prevent permanent tissue destruction, skeletal deformities, and to halt transmission within the community. Parents and caregivers in endemic areas should seek medical care for children who exhibit any of the following symptoms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The appearance of any unexplained, persistent skin bump, nodule, or ulcer, especially if it has a raised, fleshy, raspberry-like appearance or is covered by a yellowish crust.
    \item Unexplained bone pain, joint swelling, or tenderness, particularly if the pain worsens at night or causes the child to limp or walk with difficulty.
    \item Cracks, sores, or painful thickenings on the soles of the feet or palms of the hands.
    \item Chronic nasal congestion, discharge, or visible ulceration and destruction of the tissue inside or around the nose and mouth.
\end{itemize}

Immediate emergency medical attention is not typically required for uncomplicated yaws, but urgent medical evaluation is necessary if the patient develops signs of secondary bacterial superinfection or systemic complications. These warning signs include a high fever, severe spreading redness, warmth, swelling, or extreme pain around a skin lesion (suggesting cellulitis), or signs of systemic sepsis such as rapid breathing, confusion, lethargy, or a rapid heart rate. In the event of a medical emergency, such as suspected sepsis or severe acute infection, individuals should contact their local emergency services immediately (for example, call \textbf{local emergency services} in many countries, \textbf{911} in the United States and Canada, \textbf{999} in the United Kingdom, or \textbf{112} in Europe) or transport the patient to the nearest hospital emergency department.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of yaws is established through a combination of clinical assessment, epidemiological context, and laboratory investigations. In remote, resource-limited endemic settings, clinical diagnosis based on the presence of characteristic raspberry-like papillomas or painful palmoplantar lesions is the primary tool. However, laboratory confirmation is critical to distinguish yaws from other dermatological conditions and to monitor the success of eradication programs.

The following investigations are utilized in the diagnosis of yaws:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical Examination}: A thorough physical examination to identify characteristic lesions (papillomas, plantar hyperkeratosis, nasal destruction) and assess for bone pain, joint swelling, or skeletal deformities. A travel or residential history in a known yaws-endemic region is essential.
    \item \textbf{Darkfield Microscopy}: Direct examination of exudate or serous fluid from early, moist skin lesions using darkfield microscopy. The presence of motile, corkscrew-shaped spirochetes confirms a treponemal infection. However, this method requires specialized equipment and trained personnel, making it less practical for remote field settings.
    \item \textbf{Serological Testing}: Like venereal syphilis, yaws stimulates both non-treponemal and treponemal antibodies. Because these tests cannot distinguish between the subspecies of \textit{Treponema pallidum}, clinical and epidemiological context is required for interpretation.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Non-Treponemal Tests}: These include the Rapid Plasma Reagin (RPR) and Venereal Disease Research Laboratory (VDRL) tests. They measure antibodies against cardiolipin-lecithin-cholesterol antigen. Titers rise during active, early infection and fall following successful treatment, making them excellent tools for monitoring therapeutic response and detecting reinfection.
        \item \textit{Treponemal Tests}: These include the \textit{T. pallidum} Hemagglutination Assay (TPHA), Treponema pallidum Particle Agglutination (TPPA), and Fluorescent Treponemal Antibody Absorption (FTA-ABS) tests. They detect antibodies specific to treponemal antigens. These tests remain positive for life, even after clinical cure, and therefore cannot distinguish between active and past infections.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Point-of-Care (POC) Tests}: Dual-path platform (DPP) tests can detect treponemal and non-treponemal antibodies from a finger-prick sample and support field ``screen-and-treat'' strategies. A treponemal-positive result may reflect current or past infection, while non-treponemal results are affected by timing and test performance; results require clinical and epidemiological interpretation.
    \item \textbf{Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)}: PCR on a lesion swab can confirm treponemal infection and, in reference laboratories, help distinguish \textit{T. pallidum} subsp. \textit{pertenue} from subsp. \textit{pallidum}. It is not available everywhere and is mainly used for confirmation and surveillance, including detection of 23S rRNA mutations associated with azithromycin resistance.
    \item \textbf{Radiological Imaging}: Plain radiographs of the long bones (tibia, fibula, ulna, radius) in patients presenting with skeletal pain can reveal characteristic features of early yaws (such as periosteal reaction, cortical thickening, and focal osteolytic lesions) or late yaws (such as the osteitis and anterior bowing of the tibia seen in sabre tibia).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of yaws has been simplified by the discovery that the causative spirochete is highly sensitive to antibiotics. The primary goal of treatment is to cure the individual infection, prevent the development of late-stage destructive complications, and interrupt transmission to eliminate the reservoir of infection in the community.

The standard therapeutic options and public health management strategies include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First-Line Antibiotic Therapy (Azithromycin)}: WHO recommends a single oral dose of azithromycin at 30 mg/kg (maximum 2 g). Lesions should be followed clinically because healing takes time and persistent or recurrent lesions require reassessment.
    \item \textbf{Alternative Antibiotic Therapy (Benzathine Penicillin G)}: A single intramuscular dose may be used when azithromycin is unsuitable, unavailable, or resistance is suspected: 600,000 units for children and 1.2 million units for adults, according to local and WHO guidance. Doxycycline is another specialist alternative for people who cannot receive either standard option.
    \item \textbf{Management of Close Contacts}: Close contacts should be assessed and treated according to the local yaws programme; in endemic settings, contacts may receive treatment even without visible lesions because incubating infection can be clinically silent.
    \item \textbf{Surgical and Reconstructive Interventions}: In patients with late-stage (tertiary) yaws, antibiotic therapy is necessary to arrest the infection but cannot reverse existing structural damage. Surgical intervention, including rhinoplasty, skin grafting, and maxillofacial reconstruction, may be required to repair facial deformities caused by gangosa or goundou. Physical therapy may also be needed to address joint contractures and muscle atrophy resulting from long-standing skeletal deformities.
    \item \textbf{WHO Morges Strategy for Eradication}: On a community level, yaws is managed through mass drug administration (MDA). The strategy involves two main phases:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Total Community Treatment (TCT)}: Treatment of the entire eligible population of an endemic community with oral azithromycin, regardless of clinical status, if the active prevalence of the disease is high (typically greater than 5\% or if cases are widespread).
        \item \textit{Total Targeted Treatment (TTT)}: Periodic surveillance and targeted treatment of any new active cases and their contacts during subsequent follow-up rounds (usually every 3 to 6 months) until zero cases are detected.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Monitoring and Follow-up}: Follow-up should assess clinical healing and, where available, serial non-treponemal titres. Failure to improve, a persistently positive or rising titre, or a new lesion may reflect reinfection, inadequate treatment, or resistance and requires specialist or public-health reassessment rather than automatic retreatment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
When diagnosed and treated early, yaws resolves without long-term consequences. However, if left untreated, the chronic, progressive nature of the infection can lead to devastating physical, psychological, and social complications.

Potential complications associated with yaws and its treatment include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Irreversible Skeletal Deformities}: Chronic periostitis and osteitis lead to permanent changes in bone structure. The most classic deformity is ``sabre tibia,'' resulting from the deposition of new bone on the anterior surface of the shin bone, causing it to bow forward. Chronic dactylitis can cause permanent shortening or deformity of the fingers and toes.
    \item \textbf{Severe Facial Disfigurement (Gangosa)}: The most mutilating complication of late yaws, gangosa (rhinopharyngitis mutilans), involves the destructive necrosis of the soft tissues, cartilage, and bone of the nose, nasal septum, palate, and upper lip. This results in the collapse of the nasal bridge and the formation of a single large cavity merging the nose and mouth, causing severe cosmetic deformity, difficulty speaking, eating, and drinking, and significant social isolation.
    \item \textbf{Joint Contractures and Functional Disability}: Long-standing joint inflammation and pain lead to disuse, muscle wasting, and joint contractures. This can cause permanent stiffness, restriction of movement, and the inability to walk or perform daily tasks, rendering the individual dependent on others.
    \item \textbf{Secondary Bacterial Infections}: Open, ulcerated skin lesions in tropical, rural environments are prone to secondary contamination by pyogenic bacteria (such as \textit{Staphylococcus aureus} or \textit{Pseudomonas aeruginosa}). This can lead to cellulitis, chronic osteomyelitis, and in severe cases, systemic sepsis, which can be life-threatening.
    \item \textbf{Jarisch-Herxheimer Reaction}: A complication of treatment, this is an acute inflammatory response triggered by the rapid clearance and lysis of spirochetes following the administration of antibiotics (penicillin or azithromycin). It presents within 2 to 24 hours of treatment with fever, chills, headache, muscle pain, and transient worsening of skin lesions. The reaction is typically self-limiting and managed symptomatically with rest, hydration, and antipyretics (such as paracetamol).
    \item \textbf{Antimicrobial Resistance}: The widespread use of mass azithromycin distribution carries the risk of selecting for resistant strains of \textit{Treponema pallidum} subsp. \textit{pertenue}. Resistance is mediated by point mutations in the 23S rRNA gene (specifically A2058G and A2059G), which prevent the antibiotic from binding to the bacterial ribosome. If resistance spreads, it threatens the success of global eradication efforts, necessitating a shift back to injectable penicillin.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for early yaws is generally very good when treatment is given promptly. Lesions and bone symptoms should improve after treatment, but healing time varies; persistent, worsening, or recurrent disease requires reassessment. Early bone changes may remodel, whereas late destructive changes can be permanent.

For individuals who have progressed to late-stage (tertiary) yaws, the prognosis for curing the active infectious process remains excellent with antibiotic treatment. However, the prognosis for restoring physical appearance and function is guarded, as the tissue destruction, cartilage loss, and bone bowing that occur in tertiary yaws are permanent and irreversible. While surgical reconstruction can improve the cosmetic appearance and functional capacity of patients with gangosa or goundou, these procedures are complex and often inaccessible in the remote, impoverished regions where yaws is endemic. Yaws is rarely directly fatal; mortality is low and is almost exclusively due to secondary complications, such as severe bacterial superinfections of open ulcers leading to sepsis, or complications arising from late-stage reconstructive surgeries.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
In the absence of an effective vaccine, the prevention of yaws relies on public health interventions, community-wide hygiene education, and individual self-care measures. Because humans are the only known significant reservoir of \textit{Treponema pallidum} subsp. \textit{pertenue}, interrupting transmission through these combined strategies has the potential to eliminate the disease entirely.

Practical prevention and self-care steps include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Personal and Environmental Hygiene}: Regular bathing with soap and clean water is the cornerstone of individual prevention. It helps maintain skin integrity and reduces the likelihood of bacterial colonization on the skin.
    \item \textbf{Wound Care and Protection}: Promptly cleaning all minor cuts, scrapes, scratches, and insect bites with soap and clean water, and keeping them covered with sterile bandages or clean cloth. This prevents the entry of the spirochete and reduces the risk of secondary bacterial infections.
    \item \textbf{Avoiding Contact with Active Lesions}: Educating children and community members to avoid direct skin-to-skin contact with the open, weeping, or crusted skin sores of infected individuals.
    \item \textbf{Socioeconomic and Sanitation Improvements}: Supporting community initiatives to improve access to clean water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) infrastructure. Providing adequate footwear and clothing to children reduces the frequency of minor skin injuries on limbs, preventing portals of entry.
    \item \textbf{Community Participation in Mass Drug Administration}: Participating in local community-wide or targeted treatment campaigns is important. Humans are the only important known reservoir, so treating cases and contacts interrupts transmission; the bacterium is not maintained in an environmental reservoir.
    \item \textbf{Active Surveillance and Reporting}: Promoting awareness among parents, teachers, and community leaders to recognize the early signs of yaws (such as skin bumps and bone pain) and report suspected cases immediately to local healthcare authorities. Early detection allows for prompt treatment of the index case and tracing of contacts, preventing local outbreaks.
\end{itemize}
