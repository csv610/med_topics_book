\chapter{Yellow fever}
\index{Yellow fever}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Yellow fever is an acute viral infection caused by yellow fever virus, a flavivirus transmitted mainly by infected day-biting mosquitoes. It occurs in tropical parts of sub-Saharan Africa and Central and South America. The illness ranges from a short, nonspecific fever to severe disease with jaundice, bleeding, liver and kidney failure, shock, and death. It is not spread by ordinary person-to-person contact.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Transmission occurs in three settings: sylvatic transmission between mosquitoes and non-human primates, intermediate (savannah) transmission involving mosquitoes, primates, and people, and urban transmission between mosquitoes and people. Risk varies with location, season, mosquito exposure, and vaccination coverage. Outbreaks can occur when infected people introduce the virus into areas with susceptible populations and competent mosquitoes.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
After a mosquito bite, the virus replicates and spreads through the bloodstream. The incubation period is usually 3--6 days. Most infections are asymptomatic or produce a short acute illness. A minority progress, after a brief remission, to a toxic phase with severe hepatic injury, jaundice, coagulopathy, renal injury, encephalopathy, and shock. Infection does not provide a reason to delay urgent assessment in a person with fever after travel to a risk area.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute phase:} fever, headache, muscle aches (often prominent backache), chills, loss of appetite, nausea, vomiting, and weakness.
    \item \textbf{Remission:} many people improve after 3--4 days.
    \item \textbf{Toxic phase:} recurrent high fever, jaundice, abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding from the mouth, nose, eyes, gastrointestinal tract or injection sites, reduced urine, confusion, and shock.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The differential diagnosis depends on travel and exposure history and includes malaria, dengue, chikungunya, Zika, viral hepatitis, leptospirosis, typhoid fever, other viral haemorrhagic fevers, sepsis, and poisoning. Malaria must be excluded urgently in a febrile returned traveller from a malaria-risk area.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who develops fever during or after travel to a yellow-fever-risk area should seek prompt medical assessment and mention the travel dates, locations, mosquito exposure, and vaccination history. Emergency care is required for jaundice, bleeding, persistent vomiting, confusion, severe weakness, reduced urine, difficulty breathing, collapse, or severe abdominal pain.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis requires clinical and epidemiological assessment plus laboratory testing. Reverse-transcription PCR can detect viral RNA early in illness; serology becomes more useful later, but results may cross-react with other flaviviruses and recent vaccination. Tests may include full blood count, kidney and liver function, coagulation studies, glucose, urinalysis, and investigations for malaria and other alternative diagnoses. Suspected cases should be reported according to local public-health requirements.

\section*{Management}
There is no routinely available specific antiviral treatment. Management is supportive and should be guided by severity: careful fluids, fever control, monitoring of glucose, kidney and liver function, treatment of shock and organ failure, and blood products when clinically indicated. Avoid medicines that increase bleeding risk, such as aspirin and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, unless specifically advised. Hospital or intensive-care management is appropriate for jaundice, bleeding, altered mental status, organ dysfunction, or shock. Antibiotics are used only when a bacterial infection is suspected or confirmed.

\section*{Complications}
Severe yellow fever may cause acute liver failure, kidney failure, coagulopathy, gastrointestinal bleeding, encephalopathy, shock, secondary infection, and death. Survivors of severe illness may require follow-up for organ injury and physical or psychological recovery.

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most symptomatic infections improve within several days. About 15\% of infected people develop severe disease; among those entering the toxic phase, approximately half may die without effective supportive care. Prognosis is better with early recognition and appropriate hospital management.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vaccination:} a single WHO-approved dose provides long-lasting, generally lifelong protection for most people. Routine recommendations and travel requirements vary by country.
    \item \textbf{Vaccine assessment:} people who are pregnant, immunocompromised, have severe egg allergy, thymus disease, or are aged 60 years or older should discuss benefits and risks with a qualified clinician before vaccination. Infants under 9 months are generally not vaccinated except under outbreak-specific guidance.
    \item \textbf{Mosquito protection:} use an effective repellent, wear long sleeves and trousers, use screens or air conditioning, and remove standing water. Yellow-fever mosquitoes bite mainly during the day, so bed nets alone are not sufficient.
    \item \textbf{Travel advice:} check country-specific vaccination and certificate requirements with an official travel-health service before departure.
\end{itemize}
