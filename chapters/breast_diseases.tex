\chapter{Breast Diseases}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
\emph{Breast diseases} is an umbrella term covering the many conditions that can affect the breast. The breast is composed of glandular tissue -- the milk-producing lobules and their ducts -- together with fatty tissue and supporting connective tissue, and it responds to hormonal changes throughout a woman's reproductive life and beyond. Breast disorders span the full spectrum from common benign conditions, such as breast cysts, fibroadenomas, and fibrocystic change, through infections including mastitis and breast abscess, to breast cancer. The vast majority of breast symptoms prove to be benign, but every change needs to be assessed properly, both to exclude cancer and to guide appropriate care. This chapter provides an overview of breast disease as a whole, its assessment, and the principles of management; individual conditions are covered in their dedicated chapters.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Breast problems are extremely common. Most women experience breast pain, lumps, or nipple changes at some point in their lives, and the majority of breast lumps that are investigated turn out to be benign. Benign conditions such as fibrocystic change and cysts peak during the reproductive years, while mastitis and breast abscess are most common in breastfeeding women. Breast cancer is the most common cancer in women worldwide, and its risk rises with age, particularly after the menopause. Because benign and malignant disease can look and feel identical, all breast symptoms warrant investigation, and specialist breast clinics are designed so that most women can be assessed, imaged, and, where necessary, biopsied in a single visit.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The causes of breast disease are varied:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hormonal:} fluctuations in oestrogen and progesterone across the menstrual cycle drive cysts, fibrocystic change, and breast pain, and can promote the growth of hormone-sensitive breast cancers
    \item \textbf{Infective:} bacteria, most commonly \textit{Staphylococcus aureus}, entering through cracked nipples or inflamed ducts cause mastitis and breast abscess
    \item \textbf{Genetic:} inherited gene faults such as \textit{BRCA1} and \textit{BRCA2} substantially raise the risk of breast cancer
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} obesity, alcohol consumption, and low physical activity increase breast cancer risk
    \item \textbf{Trauma:} injury to the breast can cause fat necrosis, a benign lump that can mimic cancer
    \item \textbf{Neoplastic:} benign tumours such as fibroadenomas, or malignant tumours such as invasive ductal and lobular carcinoma
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on the underlying condition but commonly include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A new lump or thickening in the breast or armpit
    \item Breast pain, tenderness, or discomfort, which may be cyclical
    \item Nipple discharge, which may be milky, coloured, or bloodstained
    \item Nipple inversion, crusting, or a sore that does not heal
    \item Skin changes such as dimpling, redness, thickening, or ulceration
    \item Swelling, heat, and redness, typical of infection
    \item A change in breast size or shape
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The central task in breast assessment is distinguishing benign from malignant disease. Conditions that can mimic one another include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Fibrocystic change versus breast cysts versus fibroadenomas
    \item Mastitis or breast abscess versus inflammatory breast cancer
    \item Duct ectasia versus nipple discharge from other causes
    \item Fat necrosis versus a breast cancer lump
    \item Breast cancer versus benign conditions of every type
    \item Gynaecomastia (benign male breast enlargement) versus male breast cancer
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor promptly about any new breast lump or any change in the look or feel of a breast, even if it is not painful. Most changes are benign, but early assessment is important because breast cancer is most treatable when found early, and a lump that does not behave like an infection should never be dismissed.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item High fever, chills, and a rapidly reddening, painful breast -- possible severe infection or sepsis
    \item A rapidly enlarging, hard, fixed lump with dimpling or ulceration
    \item Heavy bleeding from the nipple
    \item Difficulty breathing or confusion while unwell with a breast infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Breast conditions are assessed using the \textbf{triple test} -- clinical examination, imaging, and tissue sampling:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} a careful history and examination of both breasts, the armpits, and the neck
    \item \textbf{Mammography:} X-ray imaging, the main tool for women over 40
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} particularly useful in younger women and to distinguish cysts from solid lumps
    \item \textbf{Fine needle aspiration:} drawing fluid from a cyst or cells from a solid lump
    \item \textbf{Core needle biopsy:} a sample of tissue for laboratory analysis when a lump is solid or suspicious
    \item \textbf{MRI:} used in selected cases, such as high-risk screening or complex findings
    \item \textbf{Microbiology:} culture of milk or discharge where infection is suspected
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} where infection, advanced disease, or other illness is possible
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management depends entirely on the diagnosis:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Benign conditions:} reassurance, observation, aspiration of painful cysts, and simple pain relief
    \item \textbf{Mastitis and breast abscess:} antibiotics, drainage of any pus collection, and continued feeding from the affected breast
    \item \textbf{Fibroadenomas:} usually left alone if stable, with imaging follow-up; removal is offered if they grow or cause concern
    \item \textbf{Breast cancer:} surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, hormone therapy, and targeted therapy, planned by a multidisciplinary team
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} referral to a breast clinic, where one-stop assessment with imaging and biopsy is arranged promptly
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} surveillance imaging and reviews as recommended, and continued participation in screening programmes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Delay in the diagnosis of breast cancer when a lump is wrongly assumed to be benign
    \item Recurrence of cysts, infections, or benign lumps
    \item Chronic infection or abscess formation with untreated mastitis
    \item Scarring after biopsy or surgery
    \item Lymphoedema after treatment for breast cancer
    \item Anxiety and distress while awaiting results, even for ultimately benign conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the specific condition. Benign breast conditions such as cysts, fibrocystic change, and fibroadenomas carry an excellent prognosis and, for most women, do not increase the risk of breast cancer, although follow-up may be advised for some. Mastitis and abscesses usually resolve fully with treatment. Breast cancer outcomes have improved greatly, and most women whose cancer is detected early survive the disease. Whatever the diagnosis, prompt assessment and adherence to recommended follow-up produce the best results.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Be breast aware: know the normal look and feel of your breasts and report any new lump or change promptly
    \item Attend breast screening (mammography) as recommended for your age group
    \item Breastfeed where possible, which is associated with a modest reduction in breast cancer risk
    \item Maintain a healthy weight, be physically active, and limit alcohol
    \item Treat cracked nipples and early mastitis promptly to prevent worsening infection
    \item Stop smoking, which is linked to some benign and inflammatory breast conditions
    \item Follow up with your doctor or breast clinic as advised after any investigation
\end{itemize}
