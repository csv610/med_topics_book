\chapter{Vaccine preventable diseases}
\index{Vaccine preventable diseases}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs) constitute a diverse group of infectious conditions caused by viruses or bacteria for which effective, safe, and immunogenic vaccines exist. Widespread vaccination programs represent one of the most successful and cost-effective public health achievements in modern history, second only to the provision of clean water in reducing infectious disease morbidity and mortality. Vaccines work by introducing an antigen—either a weakened pathogen, a killed pathogen, a purified component, or a genetic sequence—to stimulate the host's immune system. This exposure generates immunological memory, including memory B and T cells and antigen-specific antibodies, without causing the disease itself. When a vaccinated individual encounters the wild-type pathogen in the future, their immune system mounts a rapid, secondary response that neutralizes the agent before it can cause severe disease.

The significance of vaccination extends beyond the protection of vaccinated individuals. When a high proportion of a population is immune to an infectious agent, the chain of transmission is disrupted, offering indirect protection to susceptible, unvaccinated individuals. This phenomenon is known as herd immunity, or community immunity. The herd immunity threshold—the proportion of the population that must be immune to prevent sustained transmission—varies by pathogen and is directly related to its basic reproduction number (R-nought). For highly contagious pathogens such as measles (R-nought typically 12 to 18), the herd immunity threshold is exceptionally high, requiring approximately 95\% coverage to prevent outbreaks. For other pathogens with lower transmissibility, such as rubella or mumps, the threshold ranges from 83\% to 90\%. Widespread immunization has led to the global eradication of smallpox, the near-elimination of wild poliovirus types 2 and 3, and a dramatic reduction in diseases like diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib), rotavirus, hepatitis B, and invasive pneumococcal disease. However, vaccine hesitancy, socioeconomic disparities, structural barriers to access, and the disruption of routine healthcare delivery systems present persistent challenges that threaten to reverse these public health achievements.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of vaccine-preventable diseases highlights both the historic achievements of global immunization and the persistent vulnerabilities of unvaccinated populations. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) and the United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF), global vaccination campaigns prevent an estimated 3.5 to 5 million deaths annually from diseases such as diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, influenza, and measles. Despite this success, millions of children, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, remain under-vaccinated or unvaccinated (often referred to as "zero-dose" children). In 2022, approximately 20.5 million children missed out on one or more basic vaccines through routine immunization services, leaving them highly vulnerable to outbreaks.

The incidence and prevalence of specific VPDs are highly dependent on vaccination coverage. In areas with high vaccine coverage, diseases like measles, diphtheria, and poliomyelitis are rare or imported. However, when coverage drops below the herd immunity threshold, outbreaks rapidly emerge. Measles, because of its extreme transmissibility, serves as an epidemiological indicator of gaps in immunization coverage; small declines in vaccination rates often lead to rapid resurgences. Demographically, infants and young children bear the heaviest burden of VPDs due to their immature immune systems. For instance, pertussis is most severe in infants under six months of age, who are too young to have completed their primary vaccination series. Conversely, older adults are disproportionately affected by diseases like influenza, Streptococcus pneumoniae infections, and herpes zoster (shingles) due to immunosenescence—the natural, age-related decline in immune function. Geographically, regions with fragmented healthcare infrastructure, ongoing conflict, or high rates of poverty experience the highest incidence and mortality of VPDs, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, South-East Asia, and parts of the Western Pacific. In high-income countries, outbreaks are increasingly associated with localized pockets of vaccine refusal, driven by misinformation and vaccine hesitancy.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Vaccine-preventable diseases are caused by a wide array of viral and bacterial pathogens, each possessing unique virulence factors, routes of transmission, and pathophysiological pathways. These pathogens typically colonize the mucosal surfaces of the respiratory or gastrointestinal tracts, or enter the host via cutaneous microabrasions or parenteral exposure, subsequently disseminating to target organs.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Measles Virus (Morbillivirus)}: This highly contagious, enveloped, single-stranded RNA virus is transmitted via respiratory droplets and aerosols. The virus infects host cells by binding to the CD150 (signaling lymphocytic activation molecule, or SLAM) receptor on immune cells and the nectin-4 receptor on epithelial cells. Initial replication occurs in the respiratory tract and local lymph nodes, followed by a primary viremia that disseminates the virus to the reticuloendothelial system. A secondary, high-titer viremia spreads the virus to the skin, respiratory tract, and other organs, causing a transient but profound suppression of cell-mediated immunity. This immunopathy increases the host's susceptibility to opportunistic secondary bacterial and viral infections.
    \item \textbf{Bordetella pertussis}: A small, gram-negative, aerobic coccobacillus that is the causative agent of pertussis (whooping cough). Transmission occurs via aerosolized respiratory droplets. The bacteria attach specifically to the ciliated epithelial cells of the respiratory tract using adhesins like filamentous hemagglutinin and pertactin. Once attached, \textit{B. pertussis} releases several toxins. Pertussis toxin (PT) ADP-ribosylates Gi proteins, leading to an accumulation of intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP), which impairs phagocytosis and inhibits immune cell recruitment. Tracheal cytotoxin (TCT) causes ciliostasis and the extrusion of ciliated cells, impairing the mucociliary escalator. This leads to the accumulation of mucus and cellular debris, triggering paroxysmal coughing fits.
    \item \textbf{Clostridium tetani}: An anaerobic, spore-forming, gram-positive bacillus found ubiquitously in soil and animal feces. Tetanus occurs when spores enter the body through contaminated wounds, burns, or umbilical cord stumps (neonatal tetanus). In the anaerobic microenvironment of devitalized tissue, the spores germinate into vegetative bacilli and release two toxins: tetanolysin and tetanospasmin. Tetanospasmin is a potent neurotoxin that binds to peripheral motor nerve terminals and travels retrogradely along axons to the cell bodies in the spinal cord and brainstem. There, it cleaves synaptobrevin, a vesicle-associated membrane protein (VAMP) required for the exocytosis of inhibitory neurotransmitters (gamma-aminobutyric acid [GABA] and glycine). The loss of inhibitory control results in unopposed, continuous motor neuron firing, leading to severe muscle spasms and autonomic dysfunction.
    \item \textbf{Corynebacterium diphtheriae}: A gram-positive, non-spore-forming, club-shaped bacillus transmitted through respiratory droplets or close physical contact. The bacteria colonize the mucous membranes of the upper respiratory tract. Pathogenicity is primarily mediated by diphtheria toxin, an A-B polypeptide toxin encoded by a lysogenic bacteriophage (beta-corynephage). The B subunit binds to heparin-binding epidermal growth factor receptors on host cells, facilitating receptor-mediated endocytosis. Inside the cell, the active A subunit catalyzes the ADP-ribosylation of elongation factor 2 (EF-2), halting protein synthesis and causing cell death. This necrotic process creates a thick, adherent, grey-green pseudomembrane composed of fibrin, dead epithelial cells, leukocytes, and bacteria. The systemic absorption of the toxin leads to myocarditis and demyelinating peripheral neuritis.
    \item \textbf{Poliovirus (Enterovirus C)}: A small, non-enveloped, single-stranded RNA virus belonging to the Picornaviridae family. It is transmitted primarily via the fecal-oral route. The virus infects the mucosal cells of the oropharynx and gastrointestinal tract, replicating in the tonsils and Peyer's patches. It then enters the cervical and mesenteric lymph nodes, leading to a transient viremia. In approximately 1\% of infections, the virus crosses the blood-brain barrier or travels retrogradely along peripheral nerves to the central nervous system. Poliovirus has a tropism for motor neurons, particularly those in the anterior horn of the spinal cord and the motor nuclei of the brainstem. Viral replication within these cells causes cell lysis, leading to motor neuron destruction and lower motor neuron paralysis.
    \item \textbf{Streptococcus pneumoniae (Pneumococcus)}: A gram-positive, lancet-shaped diplococcus that commonly colonizes the human nasopharynx. Transmission occurs through respiratory droplets. The primary virulence factor is its polysaccharide capsule, which inhibits opsonophagocytosis. The bacteria release pneumolysin, a pore-forming toxin that lyses host cells and impairs ciliary movement, and autolysin, which lyses the bacteria to release cell wall components that trigger a massive inflammatory response. If the host's local defenses are breached, the bacteria can invade the lower respiratory tract (causing pneumonia), the paranasal sinuses (causing sinusitis), the middle ear (causing otitis media), or cross mucosal barriers to enter the bloodstream, resulting in bacteremic sepsis and meningitis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of vaccine-preventable diseases varies widely depending on the offending pathogen, the patient's age, and their immunological status.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Measles}: The clinical course of measles begins after an incubation period of 10 to 14 days. The prodromal phase lasts 2 to 4 days and is characterized by high fever (often exceeding 40 degrees Celsius), cough, coryza, and conjunctivitis (the "three Cs"). Photophobia is also common. Approximately 1 to 2 days before the onset of the rash, pathognomonic Koplik spots appear on the buccal mucosa opposite the lower molars; these are small, irregular, red spots with bluish-white centers. The maculopapular rash then appears, beginning on the face and behind the ears, and spreading craniocaudally over 3 days to involve the trunk and extremities. The rash is confluent on the face and upper trunk, and typically lasts 5 to 6 days before fading in the order of its appearance, leaving a brownish discoloration and desquamation.
    \item \textbf{Pertussis}: The clinical course of pertussis classically spans three phases over several weeks or months. The catarrhal stage (lasting 1 to 2 weeks) is indistinguishable from a common upper respiratory infection, presenting with coryza, sneezing, low-grade fever, and a mild cough. The disease is highly infectious during this phase. The paroxysmal stage (lasting 2 to 6 weeks) is characterized by bursts of rapid, consecutive coughs on a single expiration due to difficulty clearing mucus. This is followed by a forced, deep inspiration that produces a high-pitched "whoop" in infants and children (often absent in adults). Post-tussive emesis, facial petechiae, subconjunctival hemorrhages, and exhaustion are common. The convalescent stage (lasting weeks to months) is characterized by a gradual reduction in the frequency and severity of coughing spells.
    \item \textbf{Tetanus}: Tetanus presents in four clinical forms: generalized, localized, cephalic, and neonatal. Generalized tetanus is the most common and severe form. The incubation period ranges from 3 to 21 days, with shorter periods associated with more severe disease. The initial symptom is typically trismus ("lockjaw"), caused by spasms of the masseter muscles. This is followed by stiffness of the neck, difficulty swallowing, and rigidity of the pectoral and abdominal muscles. Spasms of the facial muscles produce a characteristic, sinister expression known as risus sardonicus. As the disease progresses, generalized, painful spasms occur, often triggered by minor sensory stimuli (noise, light, or touch). These spasms can cause severe opisthotonus (arching of the back and neck) and can compromise the airway. Autonomic instability, including labile blood pressure, tachycardia, and hyperthermia, is common.
    \item \textbf{Diphtheria}: Respiratory diphtheria has an incubation period of 2 to 5 days. It presents insidiously with a sore throat, malaise, cervical lymphadenopathy, and a low-grade fever (typically under 38.5 degrees Celsius). The hallmark sign is a thick, adherent, greyish-green pseudomembrane that can cover the tonsils, pharynx, larynx, and nasal passages. The membrane is highly vascular; attempting to scrape it off causes bleeding. In severe cases, extensive swelling of the soft tissues of the neck, combined with prominent lymphadenopathy, produces a "bull neck" appearance. This swelling, along with the pseudomembrane, can cause stridor and acute airway obstruction. Systemic absorption of diphtheria toxin can lead to cranial nerve palsies, peripheral neuropathy, and myocarditis, which presents with arrhythmias, conduction blocks, or cardiogenic shock.
    \item \textbf{Poliomyelitis}: Over 90\% of poliovirus infections are asymptomatic or cause a mild, self-limiting illness (abortive poliomyelitis) featuring fever, malaise, sore throat, and headache. Aseptic meningitis (non-paralytic poliomyelitis) occurs in 1\% to 2\% of cases, presenting with neck and back stiffness. Paralytic poliomyelitis occurs in less than 1\% of infections. It begins with myalgia and fasciculations, followed by the rapid development of asymmetric, flaccid paralysis. Sensory function remains entirely intact. The lower extremities are affected more frequently than the upper extremities, and deep tendon reflexes are diminished or absent. Bulbar poliomyelitis involves the cranial nerves and medullary centers, leading to dysphagia, dysarthria, and respiratory or vasomotor failure.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the clinical features of many vaccine-preventable diseases overlap with those of other infectious and non-infectious conditions, a careful differential diagnostic workup is essential.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{For Measles}: The differential diagnosis includes other rash-associated febrile illnesses. \textbf{Rubella} presents with a milder, non-confluent rash, lower fever, and prominent post-auricular and suboccipital lymphadenopathy. \textbf{Roseola infantum} (caused by human herpesvirus 6) features a high fever that suddenly subsides, followed immediately by the appearance of a maculopapular rash. \textbf{Scarlet fever} (caused by Streptococcus pyogenes) presents with a diffuse, sandpaper-like rash, a "strawberry tongue," and circumoral pallor. \textbf{Kawasaki disease} must be considered in children; it presents with prolonged fever, bilateral non-exudative conjunctivitis, strawberry tongue, erythema of the palms and soles, and cervical lymphadenopathy. \textbf{Drug hypersensitivity reactions} and other viral exanthems (e.g., enterovirus, adenovirus) must also be ruled out.
    \item \textbf{For Pertussis}: In infants and children, \textbf{viral bronchiolitis} (commonly caused by respiratory syncytial virus) and \textbf{croup} (laryngotracheobronchitis) present with cough and respiratory distress but lack the characteristic paroxysmal whoop and post-tussive emesis. In older children and adults, atypical pneumonias caused by \textbf{Mycoplasma pneumoniae} or \textbf{Chlamydia pneumoniae} can cause a prolonged, paroxysmal cough. Non-infectious causes of chronic cough, such as \textbf{asthma}, \textbf{gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD)}, \textbf{post-nasal drip (upper airway cough syndrome)}, and \textbf{foreign body aspiration} must also be investigated.
    \item \textbf{For Tetanus}: The rigid spasms of tetanus can resemble \textbf{strychnine poisoning}, which also blocks glycine-mediated postsynaptic inhibition; however, strychnine poisoning typically causes rapid-onset spasms without the early prodrome of trismus and has a very rapid clinical progression. \textbf{Hypocalcemic tetany} presents with bilateral, symmetrical carpopedal spasms, hyperreflexia, and positive Chvostek and Trousseau signs, and is confirmed by low serum calcium levels. \textbf{Acute dystonic reactions} to neuroleptic or antiemetic medications (e.g., metoclopramide, haloperidol) can cause oculogyric crisis, torticollis, and facial grimacing, but these symptoms are rapidly reversible with anticholinergic agents like benztropine. Severe \textbf{temporomandibular joint (TMJ) dysfunction} or dental abscesses can cause localized trismus but lack generalized muscle rigidity.
    \item \textbf{For Diphtheria}: The pharyngeal pseudomembrane of diphtheria must be differentiated from other causes of exudative pharyngitis. \textbf{Infectious mononucleosis} (Epstein-Barr virus) presents with a thick tonsillar exudate, but the membrane is usually white or yellow, non-adherent, and accompanied by diffuse lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, and atypical lymphocytosis on a complete blood count. \textbf{Severe streptococcal pharyngitis} causes tonsillar exudates that are easily wiped away without bleeding, accompanied by high fever and sudden-onset sore throat. \textbf{Vincent's angina} (acute necrotizing ulcerative gingivostomatitis) causes painful, ulcerative lesions of the tonsils and gums, often with a putrid odor, but lacks the systemic toxic manifestations of diphtheria.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Early recognition and prompt medical evaluation of vaccine-preventable diseases are critical to prevent severe morbidity and mortality. Individuals should seek immediate emergency medical care if they experience any of the following red-flag symptoms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, stridor (a high-pitched inspiratory sound), or chest wall retractions.
    \item Inability to swallow fluids or manage secretions (drooling), which may indicate airway compromise.
    \item Severe, painful muscle spasms, particularly in the jaw, neck, or back.
    \item Sudden weakness, loss of motor function, or asymmetric paralysis in any limb.
    \item Altered mental status, confusion, extreme lethargy, or seizures.
    \item A high fever accompanied by a stiff neck, photophobia, and a non-blanching petechial or purpuric rash.
\end{itemize}
For these life-threatening emergencies, do not wait for a doctor's appointment. Call emergency services immediately: \textbf{local emergency services} in many countries, \textbf{911} in the United States and Canada, \textbf{999} in the United Kingdom, or the respective local emergency number.

In non-emergency scenarios, a healthcare provider should be consulted promptly if a person has been exposed to a confirmed case of a vaccine-preventable disease (e.g., measles or pertussis) and is unvaccinated, under-vaccinated, or immunocompromised, as post-exposure prophylaxis may be indicated. Additionally, a consultation is warranted for a persistent, worsening cough lasting more than two weeks, especially if accompanied by vomiting or a whooping sound, or if a child develops a high fever and a spreading, confluent rash.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of vaccine-preventable diseases relies on a combination of clinical evaluation, microbiological cultures, molecular diagnostics, and serological assays.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical Examination}: A thorough physical examination is essential to identify key signs, such as Koplik spots and a maculopapular rash (measles), trismus and muscle rigidity (tetanus), a pharyngeal pseudomembrane (diphtheria), or asymmetric flaccid paralysis (poliomyelitis).
    \item \textbf{Molecular Diagnostics (PCR)}: Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays have become the diagnostic standard for many VPDs due to their high sensitivity, specificity, and rapid turnaround time. PCR of nasopharyngeal swabs or aspirates is highly effective for detecting \textit{Bordetella pertussis}, measles virus, rubella virus, and influenza. Stool samples or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) can be analyzed via PCR for poliovirus or enteroviruses.
    \item \textbf{Serological Testing}: The detection of pathogen-specific immunoglobulin M (IgM) antibodies in a single serum specimen collected during the acute phase of illness strongly supports a diagnosis of acute infection (e.g., measles, rubella, or hepatitis B). Alternatively, a diagnostic four-fold rise in pathogen-specific immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibody titers between acute-phase and convalescent-phase serum samples (typically collected 2 to 4 weeks apart) confirms infection.
    \item \textbf{Microbiological Culture and Isolation}: Culture remains the gold standard for several bacterial VPDs, although it can be technically challenging. \textit{Bordetella pertussis} can be isolated from nasopharyngeal swabs on specialized media (Regan-Lowe or Bordet-Gengou agar), though sensitivity decreases significantly after the first two weeks of symptoms. \textit{Corynebacterium diphtheriae} is cultured on Löffler or Tellurite media; once isolated, its toxigenicity must be confirmed using the Elek test or PCR for the \textit{tox} gene. \textit{Clostridium tetani} is rarely isolated from wound cultures (positive in only about 30\% of cases), so tetanus remains a primarily clinical diagnosis.
    \item \textbf{CSF Analysis and Imaging}: In cases of suspected meningitis (pneumococcal or poliovirus-associated), a lumbar puncture is performed. CSF analysis in bacterial meningitis typically reveals elevated opening pressure, high polymorphonuclear leukocyte count, elevated protein, and low glucose. In poliomyelitis, CSF shows a lymphocytic pleocytosis with normal glucose and slightly elevated protein. Imaging studies, such as chest radiographs, can confirm lobar consolidation in pneumococcal pneumonia or detect complications like pneumothorax in pertussis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of vaccine-preventable diseases encompasses targeted antimicrobial therapy, immunotherapies, supportive care, and intensive care interventions to manage acute complications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antimicrobial Therapy}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Pertussis}: Macrolide antibiotics (azithromycin, clarithromycin, or erythromycin) are the treatment of choice. Administered early in the catarrhal stage, they can ameliorate the clinical course; if started later, they do not alter the course of the cough but are critical to eradicate the bacteria and limit transmission. Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole is an alternative for patients with macrolide hypersensitivity.
        \item \textbf{Diphtheria}: Antibiotic therapy with erythromycin or intramuscular/intravenous penicillin G is indicated to halt toxin production, eradicate the bacilli, and prevent transmission.
        \item \textbf{Tetanus}: Metronidazole is the preferred antibiotic because it is associated with lower mortality and less central nervous system excitation than penicillin G (which is a GABA antagonist).
        \item \textbf{Pneumococcal Infections}: Empiric therapy for invasive pneumococcal disease (meningitis, sepsis) typically involves a third-generation cephalosporin (ceftriaxone or cefotaxime) combined with vancomycin, due to the prevalence of penicillin-resistant strains. Therapy is tailored based on susceptibility testing.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immunotherapy and Antitoxins}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Diphtheria Antitoxin (DAT)}: Equine-derived DAT must be administered immediately upon clinical suspicion of respiratory diphtheria, without waiting for laboratory confirmation, to neutralize circulating, unbound toxin. Sensitivity testing to equine protein must be performed prior to administration.
        \item \textbf{Human Tetanus Immunoglobulin (HTIG)}: HTIG should be administered intramuscularly to neutralize unbound tetanospasmin. A portion of the dose is infiltrated around the wound site.
        \item \textbf{Measles Immunotherapy}: High-dose oral or parenteral Vitamin A is recommended by the WHO for all children with acute measles, as it reduces the risk of blindness, croup, and pneumonia, and significantly lowers mortality rates.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Supportive and Intensive Care}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Airway Management}: Patients with severe croup (measles), laryngospasm or severe diaphragmatic spasms (tetanus), or pseudomembrane obstruction (diphtheria) require urgent endotracheal intubation or tracheostomy. Mechanical ventilation is required for respiratory failure.
        \item \textbf{Spasm Control in Tetanus}: Intravenous benzodiazepines (such as diazepam or midazolam) are used to manage muscle rigidity and spasms. Continuous neuromuscular blockade (e.g., vecuronium) may be necessary. Autonomic instability is managed with magnesium sulfate, beta-blockers (e.g., labetalol), or alpha-2 agonists (e.g., clonidine).
        \item \textbf{General Supportive Care}: Maintaining hydration, nutrition (often via nasogastric or parenteral routes due to dysphagia), pain management, and minimizing sensory stimuli (dark, quiet ICU rooms for tetanus patients) are vital.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The complications of vaccine-preventable diseases can be severe, life-threatening, and result in long-term disability, particularly in vulnerable or untreated populations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Measles}: Common complications include otitis media (which can lead to permanent hearing loss) and laryngotracheobronchitis (croup). Bronchopneumonia, caused by secondary bacterial infection or the measles virus itself (Hecht's giant cell pneumonia), is the leading cause of measles-related mortality. Neurological complications include acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (ADEM), an autoimmune demyelinating disease occurring in approximately 1 in 1000 cases, and subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE). SSPE is a rare, progressive, and invariably fatal neurodegenerative disorder caused by persistent measles virus infection in the brain, manifesting 7 to 10 years after the primary infection with cognitive decline, myoclonus, and autonomic failure.
    \item \textbf{Pertussis}: The physical strain of paroxysmal coughing can cause subconjunctival hemorrhages, epistaxis, inguinal or umbilical hernias, petechiae, and rib fractures. In infants, severe pertussis can cause pertussis encephalopathy, characterized by seizures and altered consciousness due to cerebral hypoxia. The most common cause of death in pertussis is secondary bacterial pneumonia. "Malignant pertussis" is a severe syndrome in young infants characterized by hyperleukocytosis (white blood cell count often exceeding 100,000 cells/microliter), pulmonary hypertension, and cardiogenic shock, which carries a mortality rate exceeding 70\%.
    \item \textbf{Tetanus}: Complications are primarily related to severe muscle spasms and prolonged immobility. These include laryngospasm, diaphragmatic spasm, and chest wall rigidity leading to asphyxia. Long-term sedation and mechanical ventilation increase the risk of ventilator-associated pneumonia, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism. Fractures of the vertebrae and long bones can occur during violent spasms. Autonomic instability can cause refractory cardiac arrhythmias, sudden cardiac arrest, and severe renal failure due to rhabdomyolysis.
    \item \textbf{Diphtheria}: The primary complication of respiratory diphtheria is acute airway obstruction due to the sloughing of the pseudomembrane, which can cause sudden suffocation. Systemic complications arise from toxin-induced cellular damage. Diphtheria myocarditis occurs in up to 20\% of patients, typically 1 to 2 weeks after onset, presenting with severe conduction disturbances, heart block, and congestive heart failure. Toxin-mediated neuritis occurs 2 to 8 weeks after onset, causing cranial nerve palsies (palatal paralysis, ciliary paralysis) and ascending peripheral neuropathy that can involve the phrenic nerve, causing diaphragmatic paralysis and respiratory failure.
    \item \textbf{Poliomyelitis}: The principal complication of paralytic polio is permanent, flaccid muscle paralysis and atrophy, leading to joint contractures, scoliosis, and skeletal deformities. Bulbar paralysis can cause aspiration pneumonia and respiratory arrest. Decades after recovery from paralytic poliomyelitis, up to 40\% of survivors experience post-polio syndrome (PPS). PPS is characterized by a gradual onset of new, progressive muscle weakness, fatigue, muscle pain, and joint pain, caused by the age-related degeneration of remaining, overworked motor neurons.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of vaccine-preventable diseases has improved dramatically in the era of modern intensive care and antibiotics, yet it remains highly dependent on the patient's age, immunization status, underlying health conditions, and the promptness of therapy.
In high-income nations with access to advanced pediatric and adult intensive care, the case fatality rate for measles is relatively low (approximately 0.1\% to 0.2\%), but it remains high in developing countries with high rates of malnutrition and vitamin A deficiency, where mortality can reach 10\% to 30\% during outbreaks. The prognosis for patients who develop measles-associated SSPE is dismal, as the disease is uniformly fatal within 1 to 3 years of onset.

For pertussis, the prognosis is excellent for older children and adults, but guarded for infants under six months of age. In this young demographic, the case fatality rate is approximately 1\%, with the majority of deaths occurring in infants under two months who have not received their first dose of the DTaP vaccine. The prognosis of tetanus is directly related to the incubation period and the period of onset (the time between the first symptom and the first generalized spasm); shorter periods indicate a worse prognosis. In modern intensive care units, the mortality rate for tetanus is approximately 10\% to 20\%, but without access to mechanical ventilation, it exceeds 50\% to 60\%, and neonatal tetanus carries a mortality rate of over 80\% in rural, resource-limited settings.

Diphtheria continues to carry a significant risk of mortality, even with the administration of diphtheria antitoxin and antibiotics. The overall case fatality rate is 5\% to 10\%, but it increases to over 20\% in children younger than five years and adults older than 40 years. Most deaths from diphtheria are due to myocarditis or acute airway obstruction. The prognosis of paralytic poliomyelitis is variable; approximately 50\% of patients recover motor function completely, 25\% are left with mild to moderate disability, and 25\% experience severe, permanent paralysis. The mortality rate for paralytic polio is 2\% to 5\% in children and up to 15\% to 30\% in adults, rising to 40\% or more in cases with bulbar involvement due to respiratory muscle paralysis.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Prevention of vaccine-preventable diseases is achieved primarily through systematic immunization, public health surveillance, post-exposure prophylaxis, and personal hygiene measures.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immunization Schedules}: Timely adherence to national immunization schedules is the most effective way to prevent VPDs.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{DTaP/Tdap}: The DTaP vaccine (Diphtheria, Tetanus, acellular Pertussis) is administered to infants and young children (typically at 2, 4, 6, and 18 months, with a booster at 4 years). The Tdap vaccine, which contains a reduced dose of diphtheria and pertussis antigens, is given as a booster to adolescents (aged 11-12) and adults. Pregnant women should receive a dose of Tdap during each pregnancy (ideally between weeks 20 and 32) to facilitate the transplacental transfer of maternal antibodies, protecting the newborn during their first months of life. Tetanus boosters (as Td or Tdap) should be administered every 10 years throughout adulthood.
        \item \textbf{MMR/MMRV}: The MMR (Measles, Mumps, Rubella) or MMRV (with Varicella) vaccine is administered in a two-dose schedule: the first dose at 12 months and the second at 18 months (or 4 years, depending on jurisdiction). A two-dose series achieves 97\% effectiveness against measles.
        \item \textbf{Polio Vaccines}: The inactivated polio vaccine (IPV) is used in most developed countries to eliminate the risk of vaccine-associated paralytic poliomyelitis (VAPP) associated with the oral polio vaccine (OPV). OPV, which contains live attenuated virus, is still utilized in global eradication campaigns due to its ability to induce mucosal immunity and ease of administration.
        \item \textbf{Pneumococcal Vaccines}: The pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV13 or PCV15) is administered to infants, and the pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine (PPSV23) or PCV20 is recommended for adults aged 65 and older or younger individuals with chronic medical conditions (e.g., chronic heart, lung, or kidney disease, diabetes, or immunosuppression).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP)}: In unvaccinated or immunocompromised individuals exposed to measles, administration of the MMR vaccine within 72 hours of exposure, or intravenous/intramuscular immunoglobulin (IG) within six days of exposure, can prevent or modify the clinical course. For pertussis, post-exposure administration of macrolides is recommended for all close contacts, regardless of their vaccination status. Clean wound management and immediate administration of a tetanus toxoid-containing vaccine (with or without HTIG, depending on wound severity and vaccination history) are critical to prevent tetanus after injury.
    \item \textbf{Self-Care and Infection Control}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Isolation}: Individuals suspected of having measles, pertussis, or influenza must be isolated to prevent transmission. Measles requires airborne isolation, while pertussis requires droplet precautions for at least 5 days of appropriate antibiotic therapy.
        \item \textbf{Hygiene}: Frequent handwashing with soap and water, or using alcohol-based hand sanitizers, and practicing respiratory etiquette (covering coughs and sneezes) reduces transmission.
        \item \textbf{Symptom Management}: During recovery from mild viral infections, self-care measures include adequate bed rest, maintaining hydration, and using a cool-mist humidifier to ease coughs. Acetaminophen or ibuprofen may be used to control fever and pain. Aspirin must be avoided in children and adolescents due to the risk of Reye's syndrome.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
