\chapter{Thrush in males}
\index{Thrush in males}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Educational reference; always seek professional advice for individual care.}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Thrush in males is genital candidiasis, usually an inflammation of the glans penis (candida balanitis) and sometimes the foreskin (balanoposthitis) caused by overgrowth of Candida yeasts, most often Candida albicans. Men can also develop candidal infections in the groin folds, and less often oral thrush, but this chapter focuses on genital disease. Symptoms include redness, itch, burning, white discharge under the foreskin, unpleasant odour, and discomfort during sex or urination. Unlike some sexually transmitted infections, thrush is not formally classified as an STI, yet sexual contact can transfer yeast and friction can trigger flares; partners with vaginal thrush are a common context. Most cases respond to topical antifungals and simple hygiene measures, but recurrent balanitis needs assessment for diabetes, phimosis, and alternative diagnoses. This chapter provides practical, the affected region-relevant guidance for recognition and care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Candida balanitis is common in uncircumcised men because the warm, moist subpreputial space favours yeast. Incidence rises with diabetes, obesity, antibiotic use, immunosuppressive therapy, and poor genital ventilation from tight synthetic clothing. Sexually active men whose partners have vulvovaginal candidiasis frequently present around the same time. Circumcised men are affected less often but are not immune, especially if diabetes is poorly controlled. Recurrent or treatment-resistant cases cluster in undiagnosed hyperglycaemia. Stigma and embarrassment delay care; some men repeatedly buy partner treatments without examining their own risk factors.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Yeast overgrowth under the foreskin:} Candida multiplies where moisture, warmth, and organic debris accumulate
    \item \textbf{Host predisposition:} glucose-rich urine and tissue fluids in diabetes feed yeast; antibiotics reduce competing bacteria
    \item \textbf{Mechanical and chemical irritation:} soaps, shower gels, residual detergent, and sexual friction disrupt the barrier and invite secondary yeast
    \item \textbf{Partner transfer:} exposure to vaginal Candida can seed the glans; both may need concurrent treatment if both are symptomatic
    \item \textbf{Immunosuppression:} topical or systemic steroids, HIV, and chemotherapy lower local defences
    \item \textbf{Phimosis and scarring:} prevent cleaning and drying, perpetuating a cycle of inflammation and infection
    \item \textbf{Non-albicans species:} occasional role in refractory disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Red, inflamed glans:} shiny erythema, sometimes with a glazed appearance
    \item \textbf{Itch and burning:} especially after sex or urination
    \item \textbf{White papules or cottage-cheese material:} under the foreskin
    \item \textbf{Foreskin tightness and fissures:} pain on retracting; risk of worsening phimosis
    \item \textbf{Satellite pustules on nearby skin:} when infection spreads to the shaft or groin
    \item \textbf{Dysuria without frequency:} external urethral meatus irritation rather than typical UTI pattern
    \item \textbf{Post-coital discomfort:} for either partner
    \item \textbf{Odour:} may be present but strong fishy odour suggests other diagnoses such as anaerobic balanitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bacterial balanitis and anaerobic infection:} more purulent; different odour
    \item \textbf{Sexually transmitted infections:} herpes (ulcers, vesicles), gonorrhoea and chlamydia (urethral discharge), syphilis, and others---test when risk exists
    \item \textbf{Circinate balanitis of reactive arthritis:} geographic erosions; joint and eye clues
    \item \textbf{Psoriasis and eczema/lichen planus:} chronic plaques; extragenital skin findings help
    \item \textbf{Lichen sclerosus (balanitis xerotica obliterans):} white scarring, itchy, progressive phimosis---needs medical care, not endless antifungals
    \item \textbf{Contact dermatitis:} new lubricants, latex, or topical products
    \item \textbf{Erythroplasia and penile intraepithelial neoplasia:} persistent red patches in older men need specialist review
    \item \textbf{Zoon's balanitis:} shiny red plaque in older uncircumcised men
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a pharmacist or GP for first episodes if confident of the classic picture; see a GP promptly if you have diabetes risk, tight foreskin, ulcers, urethral discharge, severe swelling, urinary retention symptoms, or thrush that returns quickly after treatment. Sexual health clinic review is appropriate when STI risk is present.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rapidly spreading genital or groin swelling with fever suggesting invasive soft-tissue infection
    \item Inability to pass urine with severe foreskin swelling
    \item Severe allergic reaction to topical or oral antifungal medicine
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} of glans, foreskin retractability, groin, and skin elsewhere
    \item \textbf{Swab for microscopy, culture, and STI panel:} when atypical, severe, or recurrent
    \item \textbf{Blood glucose or HbA1c:} strongly recommended in recurrent candida balanitis
    \item \textbf{HIV and other tests:} based on history
    \item \textbf{Dermatology or urology referral:} for suspected lichen sclerosus, suspected neoplasia, or refractory phimosis
    \item \textbf{Avoid assuming every red glans is thrush:} mislabeling delays correct care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Topical azole cream:} clotrimazole or miconazole applied to the glans and under the foreskin as directed, usually for one to two weeks
    \item \textbf{Hygiene:} gentle daily cleansing with warm water; dry carefully; avoid soap on the glans during flares
    \item \textbf{Oral fluconazole:} considered when topical therapy fails or disease is extensive, after clinician advice; check medicine interactions
    \item \textbf{Treat symptomatic partners:} coordinated therapy reduces ping-pong reinfection
    \item \textbf{Temporarily pause sex:} or use condoms until symptoms settle, as preferred for comfort
    \item \textbf{Diabetes optimisation:} diet, medicines, and follow-up if hyperglycaemia found
    \item \textbf{Steroid--antifungal combinations:} short courses sometimes used when marked dermatitis coexists---only with clear instructions
    \item \textbf{Circumcision:} considered for recurrent balanitis with phimosis after medical options, via urology
    \item \textbf{Lichen sclerosus pathway:} potent topical steroid under specialist or experienced GP care, not antifungals alone
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chronic balanoposthitis and scarring phimosis:} difficulty retracting, sexual pain, hygiene problems
    \item \textbf{Fissures and secondary bacterial infection:} including cellulitis
    \item \textbf{Paraphimosis risk:} if a swollen foreskin is left retracted
    \item \textbf{Relationship stress and sexual avoidance}
    \item \textbf{Missed diabetes diagnosis:} if thrush is repeatedly treated in isolation
    \item \textbf{Missed STI or pre-malignant disease:} from self-diagnosis error
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most men improve within days of topical antifungal treatment and gentler washing. Recurrence falls substantially when glucose is controlled, irritants are removed, and partners with thrush are treated. Long-standing lichen sclerosus or severe phimosis may need longer dermatology or surgical pathways but still have good functional outcomes with timely care.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Retract and clean gently under the foreskin daily if retractable; dry before replacing
    \item Avoid fragranced soaps, talc, and leftover soap film
    \item Wear loose cotton underwear; change out of sweaty clothes after exercise
    \item Seek assessment for diabetes if thrush is recurrent or you have thirst, polyuria, or weight change
    \item Encourage partners with vaginal symptoms to seek proper treatment
    \item Do not share topical creams in ways that cross-contaminate without hygiene
    \item Return for review if the foreskin is tightening over months rather than only during acute thrush
\end{itemize}
