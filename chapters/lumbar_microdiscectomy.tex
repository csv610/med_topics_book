\chapter{Lumbar microdiscectomy}
\index{Lumbar microdiscectomy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Lumbar microdiscectomy (sometimes referred to as microdecompression) is a minimally invasive spinal surgical procedure performed to relieve pressure on a spinal nerve root caused by a herniated lumbar intervertebral disc. Utilising high-powered surgical microscopes or magnifying loupes, the surgeon accesses the spine through a small incision to excise only the specific fragment of the herniated disc that is compressing the nerve. This procedure is designed primarily to alleviate radiating lower limb pain (sciatica) and resolve associated neurological deficits. It is widely considered the gold standard surgical intervention for symptomatic lumbar disc herniation when conservative management has failed.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Lumbar disc herniation is a common clinical condition, with an estimated prevalence of 1\% to 3\% in Western societies, and an annual incidence of 5 to 20 cases per 1,000 adults. The vast majority of individuals with a herniated disc recover with non-surgical treatment. For the small percentage of patients who require surgical intervention, lumbar microdiscectomy is the most frequently performed spinal surgery. The operation is most commonly performed in young to middle-aged adults, typically between 30 and 50 years of age. It affects males approximately twice as frequently as females, which is often attributed to biomechanical stress from occupational or recreational lifting and twisting.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The need for lumbar microdiscectomy arises from structural failure of the intervertebral disc and subsequent mechanical and chemical irritation of the adjacent nerve root:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Disc herniation:} The outer fibrous ring of the disc (annulus fibrosus) tears, allowing the soft gel-like centre (nucleus pulposus) to extrude into the spinal canal.
    \item \textbf{Nerve root compression:} The displaced nucleus pulposus physically compresses the adjacent spinal nerve root (most commonly L4, L5, or S1) within the lateral recess or neural foramen.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory response:} The extruded nucleus pulposus releases pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as tumour necrosis factor-alpha), causing severe chemical radiculitis of the nerve root.
    \item \textbf{Repetitive biomechanical strain:} Chronic wear and tear, coupled with acute loading events (such as heavy lifting or sudden twisting), compromises the disc's structural integrity.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Patients presenting for lumbar microdiscectomy typically experience symptoms corresponding to the specific dermatomal and myotomal distribution of the compressed nerve:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Radicular pain (sciatica):} Sharp, shooting, electric, or burning pain that starts in the buttock and radiates down the leg, calf, and into the foot, typically worse with sitting, coughing, or sneezing.
    \item \textbf{Sensory deficits:} Paresthesia (tingling) or numbness in the skin area supplied by the affected nerve root (e.g., the lateral calf and dorsum of the foot for L5; the sole and lateral border of the foot for S1).
    \item \textbf{Motor weakness:} Weakness in specific muscle groups, such as difficulty dorsiflexing the foot (foot drop, indicating L5 compression) or weakness in plantar flexion (difficulty standing on tip-toes, indicating S1 compression).
    \item \textbf{Dermatomal reflex changes:} Depressed or absent deep tendon reflexes, such as a reduced patellar reflex (L4) or Achilles reflex (S1).
    \item \textbf{Positive tension signs:} Reproduction of radiating leg pain during a straight leg raise test (between 30 and 70 degrees of elevation) or a femoral nerve stretch test.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Prior to recommending surgery, other potential sources of lower back and lower limb pain must be excluded:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lumbar spinal stenosis:} Narrowing of the central spinal canal, typically presenting in older adults with bilateral leg pain exacerbated by walking (neurogenic claudication) and relieved by sitting.
    \item \textbf{Piriformis syndrome:} Sciatic nerve entrapment by the piriformis muscle in the buttock, causing pain without radiographic evidence of lumbar disc herniation.
    \item \textbf{Spondylolisthesis:} The displacement of one vertebra over another, which can cause both back pain and nerve root compression.
    \item \textbf{Hip joint pathology:} Osteoarthritis or labral tears of the hip, which can present with groin or thigh pain mimicking lumbar radiculopathy.
    \item \textbf{Peripheral neuropathy:} Systemic conditions (such as diabetic neuropathy) causing bilateral, symmetrical numbness and pain in a stocking-like distribution.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients with sciatica should seek medical evaluation if their symptoms do not improve after several weeks of physical therapy, or if they notice progressive leg weakness.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cauda equina syndrome:} A surgical emergency characterised by bilateral leg pain/numbness, saddle anaesthesia (numbness in the groin, buttocks, or perineum), and sudden bowel or bladder dysfunction (such as urinary retention or faecal incontinence).
    \item \textbf{Progressive motor paralysis:} Rapidly worsening weakness in the leg or foot (such as a sudden inability to lift the foot).
    \item \textbf{Intractable leg pain:} Excrutiating pain that is completely unresponsive to strong analgesics, preventing the patient from resting or lying down.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A thorough clinical evaluation is paired with advanced imaging to confirm the diagnosis and plan the surgical approach:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the lumbar spine:} The diagnostic gold standard, providing detailed cross-sectional views of the herniated disc, the compressed nerve root, and surrounding soft tissues.
    \item \textbf{Computed tomography (CT) scan:} An alternative imaging modality used when MRI is contraindicated (e.g., in patients with non-compatible pacemakers or metallic implants).
    \item \textbf{Electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction studies:} Utilised in complex cases to differentiate spinal radiculopathy from peripheral nerve entrapment or neuropathy.
    \item \textbf{Plain lumbar radiographs:} Obtained to assess overall spinal alignment, rule out spondylolisthesis or fractures, and identify transitional vertebrae.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management involves a trial of conservative therapies, followed by microscopic surgical decompression if indicated:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conservative trial:} A course of 6 to 12 weeks of non-operative management, including targeted physiotherapy, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), neuropathic agents (e.g., pregabalin or gabapentin), and lumbar epidural steroid injections.
    \item \textbf{Surgical microdiscectomy:} Performed under general anaesthetic. Through a 2- to 3-centimetre midline incision, a partial laminotomy (removal of a small amount of bone) is performed to access the spinal canal. The nerve root is gently retracted, and the herniated disc fragment is removed.
    \item \textbf{Post-operative mobilisation:} Patients are encouraged to mobilise on the day of surgery to reduce the risk of venous thromboembolism and prevent epidural scarring.
    \item \textbf{Activity modification (post-operative):} Adhering to a strict "no bending, heavy lifting (over 5 kilograms), or twisting" rule for 4 to 6 weeks to allow the annulus fibrosus to heal and prevent re-herniation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While microdiscectomy is highly successful and safe, potential complications include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Recurrent disc herniation:} The most common complication (occurring in 5\% to 15\% of patients), where additional disc material herniates through the pre-existing annular tear, often requiring revision surgery.
    \item \textbf{Dural tear:} Accidental puncture of the dural sac, leading to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, which can cause severe positional headaches and may require intraoperative repair.
    \item \textbf{Nerve root injury:} Transient or permanent damage to the nerve root, resulting in worsened numbness, pain, or weakness in the leg.
    \item \textbf{Infection:} Superficial wound infection or deep discitis (infection of the disc space), which occurs in less than 2\% of cases and is managed with antibiotics or debridement.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis following lumbar microdiscectomy is highly favourable, with 80\% to 90\% of patients reporting significant relief from radiating leg pain and high rates of overall satisfaction. Leg pain typically improves immediately after surgery, while numbness and motor weakness may take several weeks or months to resolve as the nerve recovers. Most patients can return to sedentary desk work within 2 to 4 weeks and resume strenuous manual labour or competitive sports by 8 to 12 weeks, depending on their physical rehabilitation progress. However, microdiscectomy is less predictable for resolving axial mechanical lower back pain.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Long-term self-care is essential to protect the remaining lumbar discs and prevent future herniations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spinal ergonomics:} Maintaining proper posture while sitting, standing, and driving, and using ergonomically designed chairs with lumbar support.
    \item \textbf{Core strengthening:} Regularly performing low-impact exercises, such as swimming or walking, and engaging in core-stabilisation exercises (e.g., Pilates or physiotherapy-guided routines).
    \item \textbf{Correct lifting mechanics:} Bending at the knees and hips, keeping objects close to the torso, and avoiding twisting the spine while carrying a load.
    \item \textbf{Weight management:} Maintaining a healthy body weight to reduce the constant mechanical load placed on the lumbar spine.
\end{itemize}
