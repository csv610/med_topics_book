\chapter{Healthcare Rights}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Healthcare rights are the rights that every person has when receiving health care in Australia. The Australian Charter of Healthcare Rights sets out the rights of patients to access safe, high-quality care, to be treated with dignity and respect, to be informed and to participate in decisions, to have their privacy protected, and to give feedback or make complaints. These rights apply to all health care, including public and private hospitals, general practice, and community services. Understanding your rights helps you receive safe, respectful care and know what to do if something goes wrong. The Charter is available in many languages and applies to everyone, regardless of their background.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Most Australians receive safe, high-quality care, and the vast majority report positive experiences. However, complaints about health care do occur: Australian health services handle tens of thousands of complaints each year, and health complaints organisations support resolution. Around 1 in 20 patients experiences some form of harm during health care, and open disclosure and complaints processes exist to address this. Knowing your rights is associated with better communication, safer care, and greater satisfaction.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
This chapter concerns patient rights rather than a disease. The Australian Charter of Healthcare Rights includes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Access:} the right to health care when needed
    \item \textbf{Safety:} the right to receive safe and high-quality care
    \item \textbf{Respect:} the right to be treated with dignity and respect, and to have values and culture considered
    \item \textbf{Communication:} the right to be informed about services, treatment, and costs in a way you understand
    \item \textbf{Participation:} the right to be involved in decisions about your care and to give or refuse consent
    \item \textbf{Privacy:} the right to have personal information kept private and confidential
    \item \textbf{Comment:} the right to provide feedback, make a complaint, and have it resolved
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Rights are exercised in everyday healthcare situations.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Being informed about your diagnosis, treatment options, and costs
    \item Giving or withholding consent before treatment
    \item Asking questions and having them answered clearly
    \item Having an interpreter provided when needed
    \item Accessing your medical records
    \item Being accompanied by a family member or advocate
    \item Receiving a second opinion
    \item Having your privacy respected during examinations and in records
    \item Making a complaint without fear of retribution
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Rights can be limited or complicated in specific situations.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Capacity: when a person cannot make decisions, substitute decision-makers act in their best interests
    \item Emergency treatment: care may be given without consent in emergencies when the person cannot consent
    \item Compulsory treatment: mental health legislation allows treatment in specific circumstances
    \item Public health powers: notification and quarantine laws apply to some conditions
    \item Financial rights: the right to information about costs, and the right to refuse care that is not affordable
    \item Research: participation in research is voluntary and requires informed consent
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek care for any health need, and exercise your rights as part of that care. If you feel your rights have not been respected, or you have concerns about safety or quality, raise them with the service or use the complaints process.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Any medical emergency; emergency care is provided regardless of your ability to pay
    \item Serious deterioration in your condition while receiving care
    \item Any situation where you feel unsafe in a healthcare setting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Knowing and asserting your rights involves practical steps.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ask for information:} about your condition, treatment, and costs
    \item \textbf{Ask for an interpreter:} free interpreters are available in hospitals and many services
    \item \textbf{Bring an advocate:} a family member or friend can support you
    \item \textbf{Request a copy of the Charter:} it is displayed in most health services
    \item \textbf{Access your records:} you can request copies of your medical records
    \item \textbf{Understand consent:} ask questions before signing consent forms
    \item \textbf{Complain effectively:} know how to make a complaint to the service or the health complaints commissioner
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Exercising your rights improves the safety and quality of your care.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Be informed:} ask questions and seek information until you understand
    \item \textbf{Participate in decisions:} express your values, preferences, and concerns
    \item \textbf{Give informed consent:} understand what is proposed before agreeing
    \item \textbf{Protect your privacy:} ask who will see your information and why
    \item \textbf{Give feedback:} positive and negative feedback improves services
    \item \textbf{Make complaints:} to the service first, then to the health complaints commissioner
    \item \textbf{Seek open disclosure:} services should explain any harm that occurs during care
    \item \textbf{Use advocacy services:} patient advocates and community legal services can help
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dignity and respect being overlooked in busy services
    \item Communication barriers, especially for people from non-English-speaking backgrounds
    \item Decisions made without adequate information or consent
    \item Privacy breaches
    \item Harm during care, which should be disclosed and addressed
    \item Complaints that are not well handled
    \item Fear of complaining discouraging feedback
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Patients who understand and exercise their rights receive safer, more respectful care and have better experiences and outcomes. Open communication, informed consent, and participation in decisions are associated with better adherence to treatment and fewer errors. The complaints process exists to improve services, and most complaints are resolved to the complainant's satisfaction. In Australia, the legal and regulatory framework strongly supports patient rights.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Learn about the Australian Charter of Healthcare Rights
    \item Ask questions until you understand your condition and treatment
    \item Take time to read and understand consent forms
    \item Bring an advocate to important appointments
    \item Ask for an interpreter when needed
    \item Keep records of your care, including medicines and test results
    \item Request copies of your medical records when useful
    \item Give feedback, and complain through the right channels if needed
    \item Know that emergency care is provided regardless of ability to pay
\end{itemize}
