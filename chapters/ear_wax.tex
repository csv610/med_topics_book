\chapter{Ear Wax}
\index{Ear Wax}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Ear wax, also called cerumen, is a natural substance produced by glands in the outer ear canal. It protects the ear by trapping dust and dirt, repelling water, and keeping the skin of the canal healthy. Normally, ear wax works its way out of the ear naturally and is shed without any need for cleaning. However, some people produce excess wax, or wax becomes impacted, causing blockage, hearing loss, discomfort, and tinnitus. Removing impacted wax is simple and effective, and the key message is that cotton buds and other objects should never be put into the ear.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Ear wax build-up is common, affecting a substantial proportion of adults, and it is one of the most frequent reasons for visiting a GP or audiologist. Impacted wax is more common in older people, in people who use hearing aids or earplugs, and in people who regularly insert cotton buds, which pushes wax deeper. The problem is easily treated, but it can cause significant temporary hearing loss and frustration when left.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The ear canal produces wax continuously, and the canal has a natural self-cleaning mechanism.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal function:} wax traps debris, moisturises the canal skin, and migrates outward with skin cells, falling out or being washed away
    \item \textbf{Impaction:} wax accumulates and hardens when production exceeds removal, the canal is narrow, or objects push wax deep against the eardrum
    \item \textbf{Cotton buds:} the most common cause of impaction, as they pack wax further into the canal
    \item \textbf{Hearing aids and earplugs:} prevent the natural outward migration of wax
    \item \textbf{Age:} the wax becomes drier and more prone to impaction with age
    \item \textbf{Anatomy:} a narrow or hairy ear canal predisposes to blockage
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on whether the wax is merely present or actually blocking the canal.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A feeling of fullness or blockage in the ear
    \item Muffled or reduced hearing
    \item Tinnitus, or ringing in the ear
    \item Itching or discomfort
    \item Dizziness or a sensation of imbalance in some people
    \item Earache
    \item Discharge or an unpleasant smell if the wax is infected
    \item Symptoms may come on after swimming, showering, or using earphones
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of wax impaction overlap with other ear conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Middle ear infection and fluid, which also cause fullness and hearing loss
    \item Outer ear infection, with pain and discharge
    \item Sensorineural hearing loss from noise, age, or other causes
    \item Foreign body in the ear
    \item Tinnitus and dizziness from inner ear conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a GP, audiologist, or ear care professional if the ear feels blocked, hearing is reduced, or symptoms are troublesome. Wax removal by a professional is quick and safe.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden hearing loss or severe ear pain
    \item Discharge of blood from the ear
    \item Signs of infection with fever and swelling
    \item A suspected perforated eardrum, such as after an injury or if the ear is painful with discharge
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is made by examining the ear.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Otoscopy:} inspection of the ear canal and eardrum to confirm wax and assess the eardrum
    \item \textbf{Hearing testing:} if hearing loss is present, to measure its severity
    \item \textbf{Microsuction or syringing:} professional removal methods performed under vision
    \item \textbf{Assessment of the eardrum:} important before any irrigation, to avoid damaging a perforated eardrum
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is simple and highly effective.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Wax softeners:} drops such as olive oil or commercial preparations used for a few days may soften and help wax out
    \item \textbf{Microsuction:} gentle suction under magnification, performed by a trained professional; the preferred method in many clinics
    \item \textbf{Ear irrigation:} gentle syringing with warm water, avoided if the eardrum may be perforated
    \item \textbf{Manual removal:} using instruments under vision in the clinic
    \item \textbf{Aftercare:} keeping the ears dry and avoiding objects in the ears afterwards
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Temporary hearing loss and its impact on communication
    \item Discomfort, earache, and tinnitus
    \item Infection of the skin of the canal, often caused by attempts to remove wax with objects
    \item Damage to the eardrum or ear canal from cotton buds or homemade methods
    \item Complications of removal, including perforation or infection, which are rare with professional care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for ear wax problems is excellent. Removal restores hearing and relieves symptoms, usually immediately, and complications are uncommon with professional treatment. For people prone to repeated build-up, regular use of wax softeners and professional review prevents recurrence.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Never put cotton buds, fingers, or other objects in your ears
    \item Clean the outer ear with a cloth over the finger
    \item Use wax-softening drops if you are prone to build-up
    \item See a professional for removal rather than attempting it yourself
    \item Dry the ears gently after swimming and showering
    \item Have regular ear checks if you use hearing aids
\end{itemize}
