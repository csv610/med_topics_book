\chapter{Glaucoma Treatment}
\index{Glaucoma Treatment}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Glaucoma treatment aims to lower the pressure inside the eye (intraocular pressure), which is the only proven way to slow or stop the optic nerve damage that causes vision loss. Treatment cannot restore vision already lost, but it can preserve remaining sight, and it is usually needed for life. The main treatments are medicated eye drops, laser procedures, and surgery, used alone or in combination. The choice of treatment depends on the type and severity of glaucoma, the level of eye pressure, other eye conditions, and the person's preferences and ability to use drops. With regular use and monitoring, treatment is highly effective at preventing blindness in most people.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Glaucoma affects around 300,000 people, many undiagnosed, and treatment is lifelong for those diagnosed. Eye drops are the most common first treatment, and most people with open-angle glaucoma start on one drop, with many eventually needing two or more. Laser treatment is increasingly offered as a first-line option because it is effective, safe, and removes the burden of daily drops. Around 10--20\% of people ultimately require surgery. Adherence to treatment is a major challenge: studies suggest around half of people do not use their drops as prescribed, which is the most common cause of progressive vision loss.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Treatment targets the mechanisms that damage the optic nerve in glaucoma.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Eye pressure:} elevated intraocular pressure damages optic nerve fibres; lowering it protects them
    \item \textbf{Aqueous humour:} the eye produces and drains this fluid continuously; most treatments reduce its production or improve its drainage
    \item \textbf{Open-angle glaucoma:} drainage is chronically inefficient; drops, laser, and surgery aim to improve outflow or reduce inflow
    \item \textbf{Angle-closure glaucoma:} the drainage angle is blocked; laser iridotomy creates an opening, and the fellow eye is often treated preventively
    \item \textbf{Blood flow and neuroprotection:} other factors may contribute to optic nerve damage, and research continues into treatments that protect the nerve directly
    \item \textbf{Target pressure:} each person has an individual "target pressure" below which damage is expected to slow or stop
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Treatment is tailored to the features of the individual's disease.

\begin{itemize}
    \item The type of glaucoma (open-angle, angle-closure, normal-tension, or secondary)
    \item The level of intraocular pressure at presentation
    \item The stage and rate of progression of visual field loss and optic nerve damage
    \item Age and life expectancy
    \item Coexisting eye conditions such as cataracts
    \item The person's ability and willingness to use daily drops
    \item Response to previous treatments
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before treatment, the diagnosis and type of glaucoma must be confirmed.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Ocular hypertension: high eye pressure without optic nerve damage, which may need monitoring rather than treatment
    \item Suspect glaucoma: optic nerve changes not yet meeting the threshold for diagnosis
    \item Normal-tension glaucoma versus true raised-pressure glaucoma
    \item Secondary glaucomas from inflammation, trauma, steroids, or other causes
    \item Conditions that mimic glaucoma, such as other causes of optic neuropathy
    \item Cataract, which can coexist and affect vision and pressure measurements
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone diagnosed with glaucoma needs regular specialist review, typically every six to twelve months, to check pressure and vision. Report any new symptoms or problems with drops promptly.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe eye pain, blurred vision, haloes around lights, red eye, and nausea, which indicate acute angle-closure glaucoma
    \item Sudden loss of vision
    \item Severe eye pain or vision change at any time, which needs immediate assessment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Treatment is guided by the same tests used to diagnose and monitor glaucoma.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tonometry:} measurement of eye pressure at each visit
    \item \textbf{Visual field testing:} regular mapping of peripheral vision to detect progression
    \item \textbf{OCT:} imaging that measures optic nerve fibre thickness over time
    \item \textbf{Gonioscopy:} examination of the drainage angle to confirm the type of glaucoma
    \item \textbf{Corneal thickness:} measured to interpret pressure readings correctly
    \item \textbf{Adherence review:} asking about drop use and technique, as missed doses are the most common reason for worsening
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is stepped, individualised, and lifelong.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Eye drops:} first-line therapy; prostaglandin analogues are usually used first because they are effective and taken once daily. Other classes include beta-blockers, alpha-agonists, and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors
    \item \textbf{Laser trabeculoplasty:} an outpatient laser treatment that improves drainage; it is often offered early and can replace or delay drops
    \item \textbf{Laser iridotomy:} for angle-closure glaucoma, creating a hole in the iris to open the drainage angle
    \item \textbf{Combination drops:} fixed combinations of two medicines in one bottle simplify regimens
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} trabeculectomy, drainage implants, or minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS) when drops and laser are insufficient
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular pressure checks, visual field tests, and imaging track success
    \item \textbf{Adherence support:} education, reminders, and simplifying regimens improve long-term outcomes
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} regular exercise, avoiding smoking, and managing blood pressure and diabetes support eye health
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive vision loss when treatment is ineffective or drops are missed
    \item Side effects of drops, including stinging, red eyes, blurred vision, eyelash changes, and darkening of the iris or eyelids
    \item Systemic side effects, such as slow heart rate or wheezing with beta-blocker drops
    \item Complications of laser and surgery, including infection, bleeding, inflammation, and cataract formation
    \item The need for escalating treatment as the disease progresses
    \item The psychological burden of a lifelong condition and fear of blindness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With treatment, the outlook for glaucoma is good. Most people who use their drops consistently and attend regular review maintain useful vision for life, and laser treatment offers a safe alternative that many find easier to sustain. The risk of blindness from treated glaucoma is low, particularly when the disease is detected early. Glaucoma remains the leading cause of irreversible blindness in some populations largely because of undiagnosed and untreated disease, so the single most effective measure is early detection through regular eye examinations.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use eye drops at the same time each day and never run out of medicine
    \item Learn the correct drop technique: wash hands, tilt the head back, and avoid touching the dropper to the eye
    \item Press the inner corner of the eye for a minute after each drop to reduce systemic absorption
    \item Bring your drops to every appointment
    \item Attend all scheduled eye checks and visual field tests
    \item Ask your doctor before starting any new medicine, as some can raise eye pressure
    \item Exercise regularly and maintain a healthy blood pressure
    \item Seek review promptly for any new eye symptoms
\end{itemize}
