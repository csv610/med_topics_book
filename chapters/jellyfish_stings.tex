\chapter{Jellyfish Stings}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Jellyfish stings occur when the tentacles of a jellyfish release tiny stinging cells, called nematocysts, into the skin, injecting venom that causes pain, redness, and swelling. Australia is home to some of the world's most dangerous jellyfish, including the box jellyfish and the Irukandji, whose stings can be life-threatening, and many others that cause painful but non-dangerous stings. Most stings are treated with vinegar, hot water, and pain relief, but stings from major box jellyfish require urgent medical care and antivenom. Knowing the correct first aid for Australian jellyfish can be lifesaving, and awareness of seasonal and regional risks reduces exposure.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Jellyfish stings are common in Australian waters, particularly in the tropics and subtropics. The box jellyfish (Chironex fleckeri) is found in northern Australian waters from October to May and has caused around 70 deaths in Australia. The Irukandji, a small box jellyfish, causes a distinctive syndrome of delayed severe pain and can be found from northern Queensland down the coast. The bluebottle (Portuguese man-of-war) is common along the east coast in summer and causes painful but non-dangerous stings. Many other jellyfish species cause milder stings, and most stings occur in summer when people swim.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Jellyfish tentacles are covered with nematocysts, microscopic capsules containing coiled, barbed threads filled with venom. On contact with skin, the nematocysts fire, injecting venom that causes local pain, inflammation, and tissue damage. The venom of the box jellyfish is among the most potent known, affecting the heart, nerves, and skin, and can cause cardiac arrest. Irukandji venom causes a delayed syndrome of severe back, chest, and abdominal pain, raised blood pressure, and anxiety, appearing 20 to 40 minutes after a mild sting. Most other Australian jellyfish have venom that causes local pain and skin irritation that settles over days.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Immediate severe pain at the site of the sting
    \item Red, raised, or whip-like marks on the skin where tentacles touched
    \item Swelling, itching, and blistering
    \item A box jellyfish sting may leave linear welts and cause collapse, breathing difficulty, or cardiac arrest
    \item Irukandji syndrome: delayed severe back, chest, or abdominal pain, sweating, nausea, and anxiety
    \item Bluebottle stings: sharp pain, redness, and swelling that settles over hours
    \item Systemic symptoms in severe stings: nausea, vomiting, headache, and confusion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Skin lesions from jellyfish may be confused with other causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Other marine stings:} stinging fish, sea urchins, and cone shells
    \item \textbf{Coral and anemone injuries:} abrasions and stings
    \item \textbf{Allergic reactions:} hives and swelling from other triggers
    \item \textbf{Bites:} insect and spider bites in beach and coastal settings
    \item \textbf{Thermal burns and abrasions:} from rocks and sand
    \item \textbf{Other causes of chest or back pain:} in Irukandji syndrome, other emergencies must be excluded
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most stings from bluebottles and common jellyfish can be managed at home, but stings in northern Australian waters, from large box jellyfish, or with severe symptoms require urgent medical care. Anyone with a suspected Irukandji sting should seek medical assessment even if the sting itself was mild, because symptoms develop later. Stings to the face, multiple stings, and stings in children warrant medical review.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Collapse, difficulty breathing, or unconsciousness after a sting
    \item A suspected box jellyfish sting in northern waters, with severe pain or welts
    \item Severe back, chest, or abdominal pain developing after a sting, which may indicate Irukandji syndrome
    \item A sting to the eye or mouth
    \item Signs of anaphylaxis: swelling of the face or throat, wheezing, or collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} location, time, and the appearance of the jellyfish if seen
    \item \textbf{Examination:} assessment of the skin marks and vital signs
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} observation for delayed symptoms in northern waters
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure and heart monitoring:} in Irukandji syndrome
    \item \textbf{Assessment of complications:} cardiac, neurological, and skin injury
    \item \textbf{Hospital assessment:} for severe stings and those requiring antivenom
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First aid for bluebottle and most stings:} rinse with seawater, remove tentacles with tweezers or gloved hands, and immerse the area in hot water (no hotter than 45 degrees) for 20 minutes
    \item \textbf{First aid for box jellyfish stings:} pour vinegar over the tentacles for at least 30 seconds, call 000, and give CPR if needed
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} analgesics, and cold packs for non-dangerous stings
    \item \textbf{Antivenom:} for major box jellyfish stings, given in hospital
    \item \textbf{Irukandji syndrome:} hospital care with strong pain relief and blood pressure management
    \item \textbf{Wound care:} cleaning, dressings, and monitoring for infection
    \item \textbf{Tetanus:} ensure immunisation is up to date
    \item \textbf{Observation:} for delayed symptoms in tropical stings
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cardiac arrest and death from major box jellyfish stings
    \item Irukandji syndrome with severe pain, high blood pressure, and, rarely, heart damage
    \item Scarring and skin discolouration from severe stings
    \item Secondary infection of sting wounds
    \item Anaphylaxis in allergic people
    \item Psychological distress after a severe sting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most jellyfish stings in Australia are painful but not dangerous and settle within hours to days with first aid and pain relief. Deaths from box jellyfish are now rare because of awareness, first aid, antivenom, and stinger nets. Irukandji syndrome resolves with hospital care, and complications are uncommon with treatment. Correct first aid improves outcomes substantially, and most people recover fully.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Swim at patrolled beaches, and in the tropics swim inside stinger nets
    \item Wear protective clothing, such as stinger suits, in high-risk waters
    \item Heed warning signs and local advice about jellyfish seasons
    \item Do not touch jellyfish washed up on the beach; tentacles can still sting
    \item Carry vinegar for box jellyfish regions
    \item Learn the correct first aid for Australian jellyfish
    \item Do not rub the sting, rinse with fresh water, or apply alcohol or sand, which can worsen stings
    \item Seek medical care for stings in northern waters or with severe symptoms
\end{itemize}
