\chapter{Bunion Surgery}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bunion surgery, also known as bunionectomy or hallux valgus correction, is an operation to correct a bunion -- the bony prominence that forms at the base of the big toe when the great toe deviates towards the second toe. The procedure aims to realign the bones of the forefoot, relieve pain, restore a functional weight-bearing foot, and allow the person to wear comfortable footwear.

Surgery is generally considered only after conservative measures, such as wider shoes, orthotic devices, and padding, have failed to control symptoms. Numerous surgical techniques exist, ranging from open procedures performed through a single larger incision to minimally invasive approaches using small incisions and specialised instruments. The choice of procedure depends on the severity of the deformity, the presence of arthritis in the joint, the person's age and activity level, and the surgeon's experience. Most bunion operations are performed as day-case procedures, meaning the person is admitted and discharged on the same day.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bunions affect women far more often than men, with female-to-male ratios commonly quoted between 3:1 and 10:1, and bunion surgery is one of the most frequently performed operations on the foot. The deformity typically develops in middle age and later, although adolescent and juvenile forms occur, particularly in children with generalised joint laxity. Roughly 1 in 30 people will undergo bunion surgery at some point in their lifetime. The operation is generally regarded as successful, with high rates of patient satisfaction, but outcomes depend on appropriate patient selection, surgical technique, and adherence to post-operative care.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
A bunion represents a progressive malalignment of the first metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint. The first metatarsal drifts medially while the proximal phalanx of the great toe drifts laterally, producing the characteristic bump and crowding of the lesser toes. The deformity is usually multifactorial, and the following factors contribute:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Footwear:} narrow, pointed, or high-heeled shoes that compress the forefoot are strongly associated with bunion development
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} a family history of bunions is common, and foot shape and laxity are inherited
    \item \textbf{Biomechanics:} excessive pronation of the foot and tightness of the Achilles tendon alter forefoot loading
    \item \textbf{Arthritis:} inflammatory arthropathies such as rheumatoid arthritis can cause progressive joint destruction and deformity
    \item \textbf{Neuromuscular conditions:} cerebral palsy or other conditions can produce abnormal foot posture and tone
\end{itemize}

Over time, the stretched capsule and ligament on the medial side of the joint fail, while the adductor muscles and lateral structures contract, pulling the toe further into the deformed position. Secondary osteoarthritis of the MTP joint (hallux rigidus) may develop, worsening pain.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pain:} aching over the bunion, made worse by walking, standing, or tight shoes
    \item \textbf{Swelling and redness:} localised inflammation over the prominence, often worse after activity
    \item \textbf{Footwear problems:} difficulty fitting into normal shoes and visible wearing of the shoe lining
    \item \textbf{Toe crowding:} the big toe may overlap or underlap the second toe, causing calluses and pain in the lesser toes
    \item \textbf{Reduced motion:} stiffness or restriction of movement at the great toe joint
    \item \textbf{Bursitis:} inflammation of the overlying bursa, with tenderness and occasionally skin reddening
\end{itemize}

Post-operatively, the foot is swollen and painful for the first few days, bruising may spread down the foot and ankle, and the wound is covered by a dressing. Weight-bearing is restricted initially, with walking in a special post-operative shoe or boot.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gout:} acute episodes of severe pain, swelling, and redness at the base of the great toe, often with elevated uric acid
    \item \textbf{Hallux rigidus:} osteoarthritis of the first MTP joint causing stiffness and dorsal pain, typically without lateral deviation of the toe
    \item \textbf{Sesamoiditis:} pain under the first metatarsal head related to inflammation of the sesamoid bones
    \item \textbf{Bursitis:} isolated inflammation of the pre-bunion bursa without significant bony deformity
    \item \textbf{Arthritis:} inflammatory joint disease such as rheumatoid or psoriatic arthritis affecting the forefoot
    \item \textbf{Metatarsalgia:} pain in the lesser metatarsal heads that may accompany or mimic forefoot disorders
    \item \textbf{Stress fracture:} a fracture of the second metatarsal may present as forefoot pain after activity
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with a painful bunion should consult a healthcare professional when pain interferes with daily activities, footwear becomes very difficult to manage, or the toe shows signs of significant deformity or joint stiffness.

After surgery, contact the surgical team or seek medical review if:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain is severe, uncontrolled, or worsening rather than settling
    \item There is heavy bleeding, or the wound splits open
    \item Spreading redness, warmth, or yellow or foul-smelling discharge develops from the wound
    \item Fever or chills occur without another cause
    \item New pain, swelling, or redness develops in the calf, suggesting a possible blood clot
    \item Numbness or tingling in the toes persists beyond the expected period
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:} sudden chest pain, difficulty breathing, or fainting after the procedure, which may indicate a blood clot in the lung (pulmonary embolism).

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} details of pain, footwear, previous treatments, and any symptoms of inflammatory arthritis are recorded
    \item \textbf{Examination:} inspection of the deformity, assessment of toe alignment, joint range of motion, pulses, sensation, and footwear fitting
    \item \textbf{X-ray:} weight-bearing films of the foot measure the intermetatarsal and hallux valgus angles, assess joint degeneration, and guide surgical planning
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound or MRI:} occasionally used to evaluate soft tissues, bursae, or suspected tendon or stress-fracture pathology
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} inflammatory markers, uric acid, or autoimmune serology when arthritis or gout is suspected
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative assessment:} review of general health, cardiovascular and respiratory status, and medicines such as anticoagulants
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conservative care:} wide, low-heeled shoes with a deep toe box; orthotic devices; padding over the bunion; ice and simple analgesics
    \item \textbf{Anaesthesia:} local or regional anaesthesia with sedation is common; general anaesthesia is used in some cases
    \item \textbf{Osteotomy:} cutting and repositioning the first metatarsal to correct alignment, held with screws, pins, or plates
    \item \textbf{Soft-tissue procedures:} release of the contracted lateral structures and tightening of the medial capsule to rebalance the joint
    \item \textbf{Arthrodesis:} fusion of the MTP joint is reserved for severe arthritis or significant deformity
    \item \textbf{Minimally invasive techniques:} small-incision procedures that may reduce soft-tissue trauma, with comparable outcomes when performed by experienced surgeons
    \item \textbf{Post-operative care:} elevation, ice, wound care, and a post-operative shoe or boot with restricted weight-bearing
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple analgesics such as paracetamol and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, with stronger medication for a short period if needed
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy and graded return to activity, with follow-up X-rays to confirm healing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wound infection, delayed healing, or scar tenderness
    \item Bleeding, bruising, or a haematoma that may require drainage
    \item Nerve injury causing numbness, tingling, or altered sensation in the great toe
    \item Stiffness or reduced motion of the great toe joint
    \item Non-union or delayed union of the osteotomy
    \item Recurrence of the bunion or over-correction into a reverse deformity
    \item Deep vein thrombosis related to reduced post-operative mobility
    \item Complex regional pain syndrome, with persistent pain, swelling, and skin changes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The majority of people are satisfied with the results of bunion surgery and report significant pain relief and improved footwear tolerance. Swelling commonly persists for several weeks and the foot may continue to improve for up to six months or longer. Full recovery, including a return to sporting activity, typically takes several months. Results are generally less predictable in people with advanced arthritis, poor circulation, or diabetes, and there is a small risk that the deformity recurs over time. Close adherence to post-operative instructions is strongly associated with better outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wear shoes with a wide, deep toe box and a low heel to reduce pressure on the forefoot
    \item Choose footwear fitted to foot shape rather than fashion trends, and measure feet regularly
    \item Stop smoking well before surgery, as tobacco use impairs bone and wound healing
    \item Control diabetes and other chronic conditions carefully around the operation
    \item Follow weight-bearing, elevation, and wound-care instructions strictly after surgery
    \item Perform recommended foot and toe exercises to maintain joint mobility and strength
    \item Attend follow-up appointments so that healing is monitored and problems are detected early
\end{itemize}
