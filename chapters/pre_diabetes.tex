\chapter{Prediabetes}
\index{Prediabetes}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Educational information; seek professional advice for individual care.}

\section*{What it means}
Prediabetes means blood glucose is higher than the usual range but not high enough for a diagnosis of diabetes. It often causes no symptoms, but it raises the future risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease. A result should be interpreted using the laboratory’s method, the person’s history, and local diagnostic guidance.

\section*{Assessment}
Diagnosis may use an HbA1c, fasting glucose, or oral glucose-tolerance test. Results can be affected by pregnancy, anaemia, kidney disease, haemoglobin disorders, recent blood loss, and some medicines. A clinician can decide whether repeat testing or another test is needed.

\section*{Reducing risk}
If appropriate, gradual changes in eating patterns, regular physical activity, sleep, stress support, and modest weight loss can reduce the risk of progression. Goals should account for culture, food access, disability, medicines, pregnancy, and eating-disorder risk. Do not use supplements or extreme diets as a substitute for medical care.

\section*{Follow-up}
Keep follow-up appointments and repeat testing at the interval recommended by the clinician. Some people may be offered medicines or a structured diabetes-prevention programme. Ask for review sooner if there is increased thirst, frequent urination, unexplained weight loss, blurred vision, or recurrent infections.

\section*{When to seek urgent care}
Prediabetes itself is not usually an emergency, but seek urgent help for confusion, collapse, severe dehydration, vomiting with inability to keep fluids down, or rapidly worsening symptoms.
