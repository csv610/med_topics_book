\chapter{Exercise and Fitness}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Exercise is planned, structured physical activity performed to improve or maintain fitness, and fitness is the ability to carry out daily tasks with energy and without undue fatigue. Fitness has several components: cardiorespiratory endurance, muscular strength and endurance, flexibility, and body composition. Regular exercise improves all of these, and it is one of the most powerful tools for preventing and managing disease. This chapter explains the components of fitness, the types of exercise, and how to build a balanced program.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Physical inactivity is a leading risk factor for illness worldwide, contributing to heart disease, stroke, diabetes, cancer, and depression, and it is estimated to cause a significant proportion of premature deaths. In Australia, a large minority of adults are insufficiently active, and sedentary behaviour has increased with modern lifestyles. The good news is that the health benefits of becoming active are substantial and are available to people of all ages and abilities.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Exercise improves health through multiple mechanisms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cardiorespiratory fitness:} aerobic exercise strengthens the heart, increases lung efficiency, and improves circulation
    \item \textbf{Muscular fitness:} resistance training builds muscle, protects bone, and boosts metabolism
    \item \textbf{Flexibility:} stretching maintains joint range and reduces injury
    \item \textbf{Metabolic effects:} exercise improves blood sugar, cholesterol, blood pressure, and body composition
    \item \textbf{Brain and mood:} activity releases endorphins, reduces stress and anxiety, and improves sleep and cognition
    \item \textbf{Long-term protection:} regular activity reduces the risk of heart disease, stroke, diabetes, several cancers, and dementia
    \item \textbf{The principle of training:} fitness improves through overload, progression, and consistency
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
A balanced exercise program includes several elements.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Aerobic exercise: walking, running, cycling, swimming, and dancing, most days
    \item Resistance training: weights, resistance bands, or body-weight exercises, at least twice weekly
    \item Flexibility and balance: stretching, yoga, tai chi, and balance exercises
    \item Intensity: moderate activity makes you breathe harder, and vigorous activity makes you huff and puff
    \item Duration: aim for at least 2.5 to 5 hours of moderate activity weekly
    \item Variety: mixing activities prevents boredom and works different systems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Exercise should be tailored to health and circumstances.
\begin{itemize}
    \item People with heart disease, diabetes, or other conditions need adapted programs
    \item Some people benefit from supervised exercise, such as cardiac rehabilitation
    \item Injuries and joint conditions favour low-impact options
    \item The program is individualised rather than one-size-fits-all
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with significant health conditions, or who are starting vigorous exercise after being inactive, should check with a doctor. Anyone with chest pain, severe breathlessness, or dizziness during exercise should stop and seek assessment.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, pressure, or discomfort during or after exercise
    \item Collapse, fainting, or severe dizziness
    \item Palpitations with chest pain
    \item Sudden weakness or difficulty speaking
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Fitness can be assessed and tracked.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Health assessment:} a GP review, particularly for people with risk factors
    \item \textbf{Fitness testing:} simple tests of aerobic fitness, strength, and flexibility
    \item \textbf{Goal setting:} specific, measurable, achievable goals
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} tracking activity, fitness, and progress
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to exercise physiologists and physiotherapists for specific needs
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Building fitness is a gradual, structured process.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Start where you are:} build from current activity levels
    \item \textbf{Progress gradually:} increase duration and intensity step by step
    \item \textbf{Combine the components:} aerobic, strength, flexibility, and balance
    \item \textbf{Schedule it:} make exercise part of the routine
    \item \textbf{Recovery:} rest days allow the body to adapt
    \item \textbf{Variety and enjoyment:} the best exercise is the one you keep doing
    \item \textbf{Reassess:} review progress and adjust the plan
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Injuries from doing too much, too soon
    \item Soreness and fatigue, which settle with sensible progression
    \item Burnout and stopping when the program is unsustainable
    \item Overtraining in people who push too hard
    \item The far greater risks of inactivity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Fitness improves with regular exercise, and the health benefits are broad and durable. Cardiorespiratory fitness improves within weeks, strength within months, and the protection against chronic disease builds over years. People who are fit enjoy more energy, better mood and sleep, and a longer, healthier life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Aim for the physical activity guidelines
    \item Include strength training at least twice a week
    \item Add incidental activity throughout the day
    \item Warm up and cool down
    \item Listen to your body and rest when needed
    \item Stay hydrated and eat well to support training
    \item Make exercise a lifelong habit
\end{itemize}
