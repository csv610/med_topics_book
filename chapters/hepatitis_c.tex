\chapter{Hepatitis C}
\index{Hepatitis C}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hepatitis C is a viral infection of the liver caused by the hepatitis C virus, spread mainly through contact with infected blood. It is the most common blood-borne virus in many countries. Most people infected develop chronic hepatitis C, which is silent for years but progressively damages the liver, leading to cirrhosis, liver failure, and liver cancer. The great news is that modern oral treatments cure more than 95\% of people in a short course with few side effects, and some countries have committed to eliminating hepatitis C.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Around 100,000 people are estimated to be living with chronic hepatitis C, with rates highest among people who have ever injected drugs, people in prison, and some Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities. The incidence of new infections is falling. Around 20--30\% of people clear the virus without treatment, and the remainder develop chronic infection. Since highly effective treatments became available, the number of people cured has risen dramatically, and some countries are leaders in hepatitis C elimination efforts.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood-borne transmission:} the vast majority of infections are acquired through sharing injecting equipment
    \item \textbf{Historical transmission:} blood transfusions and blood products before 1992, when screening began
    \item \textbf{Less common routes:} unsterile tattooing or piercing, needle-stick injuries, and rarely mother-to-child or sexual transmission
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} the virus infects liver cells and replicates continuously, driving chronic inflammation and progressive fibrosis
    \item \textbf{Spontaneous clearance:} the immune system clears the virus in around a quarter of people within six months
    \item \textbf{Chronic infection:} persistent infection causes scarring that accumulates over decades
    \item \textbf{Cure:} modern antivirals eliminate the virus, halting liver damage
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Most people have no symptoms at the time of infection
    \item Acute infection may cause fatigue, nausea, and occasionally jaundice
    \item Chronic hepatitis C is usually asymptomatic for years or decades
    \item Fatigue is the most common symptom
    \item Advanced disease: abdominal swelling, bruising, bleeding, weight loss, and confusion
    \item Liver cancer may develop without warning symptoms
    \item Extrahepatic effects: joint pains, skin changes, and kidney problems in some people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Other causes of chronic liver disease: hepatitis B, fatty liver, alcohol, and autoimmune hepatitis
    \item Acute hepatitis from other viruses and medications
    \item Fatigue and other non-specific symptoms have many causes
    \item Antibody testing distinguishes past exposure from current infection
    \item Abnormal liver tests prompt investigation for hepatitis C in people with risk factors
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Testing for hepatitis C is recommended for anyone who has ever injected drugs, received a blood transfusion before 1992, had unsterile tattoos or piercings, or has abnormal liver tests. Treatment is available free of charge and should be started as soon as possible after diagnosis.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or personality change with liver disease
    \item Vomiting blood or passing black stools
    \item Severe abdominal pain or swelling
    \item Sudden severe jaundice
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antibody test:} screening test indicating past or current exposure
    \item \textbf{RNA test (viral load):} confirms current infection and guides treatment
    \item \textbf{Liver function tests:} assess inflammation
    \item \textbf{Liver stiffness measurement and ultrasound:} stage fibrosis and screen for cancer
    \item \textbf{Genotype testing:} largely historical; modern pangenotypic drugs treat all genotypes
    \item \textbf{Cure confirmation:} a negative RNA test 12 weeks after finishing treatment confirms cure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Direct-acting antiviral tablets:} a course of 8--12 weeks cures more than 95\% of people
    \item \textbf{All genotypes treated:} modern pangenotypic regimens simplify treatment
    \item \textbf{Few side effects:} treatment is well tolerated by nearly everyone
    \item \textbf{Access:} treatment is subsidised through the public medicines subsidy and widely available through GPs and community clinics
    \item \textbf{Before treatment:} assessment of liver health and drug interactions
    \item \textbf{After cure:} people with established cirrhosis continue cancer surveillance
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} limit alcohol and maintain a healthy weight
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cirrhosis in around 20--30\% of people with chronic infection over 20--30 years
    \item Liver failure and the need for transplantation
    \item Hepatocellular carcinoma
    \item Extrahepatic conditions, including cryoglobulinaemia and kidney disease
    \item Transmission to others if untreated
    \item Stigma and psychological impact
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Hepatitis C is one of medicine's great success stories: it is curable in more than 95\% of people with a short, well-tolerated course of tablets. Cure eliminates the risk of cirrhosis and liver cancer progression in most people, though those with established scarring continue surveillance. Early treatment prevents liver damage entirely. With the widespread availability of treatment, elimination of hepatitis C in many countries is achievable within the next decade, and people cured can expect a normal life expectancy.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Never share needles, syringes, or other injecting equipment; use sterile equipment each time
    \item Seek help from needle and syringe programs and opiate substitution services
    \item Ensure tattoos and piercings use sterile single-use equipment
    \item Use condoms to reduce the small sexual transmission risk
    \item Get tested if you have any risk factors, even without symptoms
    \item Complete treatment as prescribed and attend the cure test
    \item Limit alcohol and maintain a healthy liver through diet and exercise
\end{itemize}
