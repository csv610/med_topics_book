\chapter{Glioblastoma}
\index{Glioblastoma}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common and most aggressive primary brain cancer in adults. It arises from glial cells, the supportive cells of the brain, and is classified as a grade 4 astrocytoma by the World Health Organization, the highest grade of brain tumour. Glioblastoma grows rapidly, invades surrounding brain tissue, and recurs after treatment. Standard treatment combines surgery to remove as much tumour as safely possible, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy with temozolomide. Despite intensive treatment, glioblastoma has a poor prognosis, but outcomes are improving, and treatment focuses on controlling the tumour, preserving neurological function, and maintaining quality of life. Research into new treatments is active and ongoing.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Glioblastoma is the most common malignant brain tumour in adults, accounting for around half of all primary brain cancers. In many countries, about 1,000--1,500 people are diagnosed each year. It is slightly more common in men than women, and the average age at diagnosis is around 60--64 years, though it can occur at any age, including rarely in children. Most glioblastomas arise spontaneously without a known cause, and only a very small proportion are linked to inherited syndromes or previous brain radiotherapy. The incidence has been stable or slightly rising in recent decades, partly due to improved diagnosis.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The cause of most glioblastomas is unknown, but the tumour is characterised by specific genetic changes.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Origin:} glioblastoma develops from glial cells, most often via a stepwise progression from a lower-grade astrocytoma or directly as a "primary" glioblastoma
    \item \textbf{Rapid growth:} the tumour grows quickly and infiltrates surrounding brain tissue, making complete surgical removal impossible
    \item \textbf{Genetic changes:} common alterations include mutations in the \textit{TP53}, \textit{EGFR}, and \textit{PTEN} genes, and loss of chromosome 10
    \item \textbf{MGMT promoter methylation:} a genetic marker that predicts better response to chemotherapy with temozolomide
    \item \textbf{IDH mutation status:} glioblastomas that carry an IDH mutation (secondary glioblastoma) have a somewhat better prognosis
    \item \textbf{Angiogenesis:} the tumour stimulates the growth of new blood vessels to support its rapid growth
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on the location of the tumour and the speed of its growth.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Headaches, which are often new, persistent, and worse in the morning or with changes in position
    \item Seizures, which can be the first sign in around a quarter of patients
    \item Weakness, numbness, or paralysis on one side of the body
    \item Difficulty with speech, language, or comprehension
    \item Changes in personality, behaviour, or cognition
    \item Visual problems, including double vision or loss of visual field
    \item Nausea and vomiting from raised pressure inside the skull
    \item Balance problems and difficulty walking
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions can produce similar symptoms or brain lesions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Other primary brain tumours, including lower-grade gliomas, meningiomas, and lymphomas
    \item Brain metastases from cancers elsewhere in the body
    \item Stroke, including ischaemic stroke and intracerebral haemorrhage
    \item Brain abscess and other infections
    \item Multiple sclerosis and other inflammatory brain diseases
    \item Migraine and other primary headache disorders
    \item Seizure disorders without an underlying tumour
    \item Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease and other progressive neurological conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with new, persistent, or worsening neurological symptoms -- especially seizures, progressive weakness, speech difficulty, or new headaches with neurological signs -- should be assessed promptly by a doctor.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe headache with vomiting, confusion, or loss of consciousness
    \item New seizures or fits
    \item Sudden weakness, facial drooping, or difficulty speaking, which may indicate stroke or a brain emergency
    \item Sudden loss of vision or severe neurological deterioration
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is made with brain imaging and confirmed by tissue biopsy or surgical resection.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{MRI of the brain:} the key imaging test; glioblastoma typically appears as an enhancing, irregular mass, often with central necrosis
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} often the first test in an emergency setting
    \item \textbf{Surgery or biopsy:} tissue is examined under a microscope to confirm the diagnosis and grade
    \item \textbf{Molecular testing:} tests for MGMT promoter methylation, IDH mutation, and other markers guide treatment and prognosis
    \item \textbf{Neurological assessment:} documentation of symptoms and neurological function
    \item \textbf{Multidisciplinary review:} the case is discussed by a team of neurosurgeons, radiation oncologists, medical oncologists, and pathologists
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is intensive and aims to control the tumour while maintaining quality of life.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} maximal safe resection is the first step, removing as much tumour as possible without damaging essential brain functions
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} targeted radiation to the tumour bed, given over several weeks after surgery
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy:} temozolomide, an oral chemotherapy, is given during and after radiotherapy, particularly when the MGMT marker predicts response
    \item \textbf{Tumour-treating fields:} a wearable device that delivers low-intensity electric fields, which can extend survival in some patients
    \item \textbf{Steroids:} dexamethasone reduces brain swelling and symptoms
    \item \textbf{Antiepileptic medicines:} control seizures if they occur
    \item \textbf{Palliative care:} symptom control and support are integrated from diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Clinical trials:} access to experimental treatments through trials is an option for many patients
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Tumour progression and recurrence, which occurs in most cases despite treatment
    \item Neurological deficits from the tumour or surgery, including weakness, speech problems, and cognitive changes
    \item Seizures and raised intracranial pressure
    \item Side effects of radiotherapy and chemotherapy, including fatigue, nausea, and low blood counts
    \item Side effects of long-term steroids, including weight gain, diabetes, and infection risk
    \item Venous thromboembolism, as brain cancer increases clotting risk
    \item Psychological and financial burden on patients and families
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for glioblastoma remains poor despite advances. With standard treatment, median survival is around 12--15 months, and about 25\% of patients survive two years, with a small proportion surviving longer. Patients with MGMT-methylated tumours respond better to chemotherapy and have a better outlook. Treatment cannot cure glioblastoma in most cases, so care focuses on controlling the tumour, managing symptoms, and maximising quality of life. New treatments, including immunotherapy and targeted therapies, are being studied, and participation in clinical trials offers hope for improved outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item There are no proven measures to prevent glioblastoma, as most cases arise without identifiable causes
    \item Report new or changing neurological symptoms to your doctor promptly
    \item If diagnosed, engage a multidisciplinary team and consider a second opinion or clinical trial
    \item Follow the treatment plan and attend all appointments
    \item Manage symptoms openly with your care team, including pain, fatigue, and emotional distress
    \item Organise practical support, including help with daily tasks and transport to treatment
    \item Seek psychological support for yourself and your family, including counselling and support groups
    \item Discuss advance care planning early with your family and doctors
\end{itemize}
