\chapter{Dairy Foods}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dairy foods are products made from the milk of mammals, most commonly cows, and include milk, cheese, yoghurt, and butter. Dairy is a major food group in the Australian dietary guidelines, valued for its rich supply of calcium, protein, and several vitamins and minerals. This chapter outlines the nutritional benefits and potential drawbacks of dairy foods, the difference between dairy and lactose intolerance, and practical guidance on choosing and consuming dairy as part of a balanced diet.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Dairy consumption varies widely around the world. Australia has one of the higher rates of dairy consumption globally, with most adults consuming milk, cheese, or yoghurt regularly. Worldwide, around two-thirds of adults have some degree of lactose malabsorption, although the proportion is much lower in populations of northern European descent and higher in East Asian, African, and Indigenous Australian populations. Food allergy to cow's milk affects roughly 2--3\% of infants but is usually outgrown by school age, whereas lactose intolerance typically develops in adolescence or adulthood.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nutritional composition:} dairy provides calcium, protein, phosphorus, potassium, iodine, riboflavin, and vitamins A and B12, and is often fortified with vitamin D
    \item \textbf{Lactose:} milk sugar requires the enzyme lactase to be digested; when lactase activity declines, undigested lactose is fermented by gut bacteria, producing gas, bloating, and loose stools
    \item \textbf{Cow's milk protein allergy:} an immune reaction to milk proteins such as casein and whey, distinct from lactose intolerance
    \item \textbf{Fat content:} full-cream, reduced-fat, and skim products differ in energy and saturated fat content, which is relevant to cardiovascular risk and weight management
    \item \textbf{Fermentation:} the bacteria used to make yoghurt and cheese partly digest lactose, which is why many people with lactose intolerance tolerate these foods better
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Most people tolerate dairy without any symptoms
    \item Lactose intolerance typically causes bloating, wind, abdominal cramps, and diarrhoea within hours of consuming milk or ice cream
    \item Cow's milk protein allergy in infants may cause eczema, hives, vomiting, diarrhoea, or, rarely, severe allergic reactions
    \item Excessive consumption of full-fat dairy can contribute to weight gain in some people
    \item Poor calcium intake is associated with low bone density and increased fracture risk in later life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Irritable bowel syndrome, which can be aggravated by dairy but is a distinct disorder
    \item Coeliac disease, which may cause similar gastrointestinal symptoms
    \item Other food intolerances, such as to gluten, fructose, or artificial sweeteners
    \item Gastroenteritis or other acute infections of the gut
    \item Other causes of infant eczema or reflux, including formula intolerance
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People who suspect lactose intolerance or milk allergy should see their doctor or a dietitian for an accurate diagnosis before removing dairy, since unnecessarily avoiding it can lead to low calcium intake. See a doctor promptly if an infant has persistent vomiting, poor weight gain, blood in the stool, or severe eczema.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden difficulty breathing, throat tightness, or swelling of the face or lips after consuming dairy (possible anaphylaxis)
    \item Signs of severe dehydration in a child, such as listlessness or passing little urine
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diet history:} a careful record of foods and symptoms helps distinguish intolerance from allergy
    \item \textbf{Lactose hydrogen breath test:} measures hydrogen in the breath after a lactose drink; a rise indicates malabsorption
    \item \textbf{Elimination and challenge:} removing dairy for a period and reintroducing it under supervision can confirm the diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Allergy testing:} skin-prick tests or specific IgE blood tests may be used when cow's milk protein allergy is suspected
    \item \textbf{Bone density testing:} may be appropriate for people with low calcium intake or fracture risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lactose intolerance:} choose lactose-free milk, small serves of dairy, hard cheeses, and yoghurt with live cultures, or use lactase enzyme supplements
    \item \textbf{Cow's milk protein allergy:} strict avoidance of milk protein, with advice from a dietitian; most children outgrow it
    \item \textbf{Calcium alternatives:} calcium-fortified plant milks, canned fish with bones, tofu, and leafy green vegetables
    \item \textbf{Fat choices:} prefer reduced-fat dairy for most adults while ensuring children under two years receive full-cream products
    \item \textbf{Portion control:} follow the recommended number of daily serves for age and life stage
    \item \textbf{Supplementation:} calcium and vitamin D supplements when dietary intake is inadequate, guided by a doctor
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Low bone mineral density and increased fracture risk from chronic inadequate calcium intake
    \item Nutritional deficiencies of protein, riboflavin, iodine, and B12 if dairy is removed without suitable substitutes
    \item Weight gain and adverse lipid profiles with excessive consumption of full-fat dairy
    \item Growth faltering in infants with unrecognised cow's milk protein allergy
    \item Risk of anaphylaxis in the small minority with severe milk allergy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For most people, dairy foods can be consumed safely as part of a varied diet and contribute importantly to bone health. Lactose intolerance is a lifelong but easily managed condition, and many affected people still tolerate small amounts of dairy. Cow's milk protein allergy resolves in the majority of children by school age. With appropriate substitution, people who avoid dairy can maintain fully adequate nutrition, and the long-term outlook is excellent.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Include 2--4 serves of dairy (or calcium-rich alternatives) daily depending on age
    \item Introduce dairy gradually if you suspect lactose sensitivity, and note your tolerance threshold
    \item Read food labels for hidden milk ingredients such as whey, casein, and milk solids
    \item Choose reduced-fat varieties if managing weight or cholesterol
    \item Store dairy correctly and observe use-by dates to prevent foodborne illness
    \item Seek professional advice before removing dairy from a child's diet
\end{itemize}
