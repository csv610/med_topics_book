\chapter{Enterovirus in Children}
\index{Enterovirus in Children}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Enteroviruses are a large family of common viruses that cause a wide range of infections, particularly in children. They include the viruses that cause hand, foot and mouth disease, as well as many viruses that cause colds, fevers, rashes, and gastrointestinal symptoms. Most enterovirus infections are mild and clear up on their own, but a small number of children develop serious complications, including meningitis, encephalitis, and heart muscle inflammation. This chapter explains how enteroviruses spread, the illnesses they cause, and when to seek medical care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Enterovirus infections are extremely common and occur most often in summer and autumn in temperate climates, and year-round in tropical regions. They spread easily among children in child care, preschools, and schools, and most children have several enterovirus infections in their early years. Hand, foot and mouth disease, caused by coxsackievirus and enterovirus 71, is one of the most recognisable, affecting young children in frequent outbreaks.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Enteroviruses are transmitted by the faecal-oral route and through respiratory droplets.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viruses:} coxsackieviruses, echoviruses, and numbered enteroviruses, including enterovirus 71 and D68
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} contact with infected faeces, saliva, and respiratory secretions, and contaminated surfaces
    \item \textbf{Entry:} the viruses enter through the mouth and multiply in the throat and gut
    \item \textbf{Spread:} most infections stay in the throat and gut, but in some children the virus spreads to the skin, nervous system, heart, or other organs
    \item \textbf{Immunity:} infection produces immunity to that specific virus, but there are many types, so children can be infected repeatedly
    \item \textbf{Incubation:} symptoms appear three to seven days after exposure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features depend on which enterovirus and which organs are involved.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever and a mild cold-like illness
    \item Hand, foot and mouth disease: fever, sores in the mouth, and a blistering rash on the hands, feet, and sometimes buttocks
    \item A rash without other symptoms
    \item Sore throat, runny nose, and cough
    \item Diarrhoea, vomiting, and abdominal pain
    \item Headache and muscle aches
    \item Conjunctivitis
    \item In serious cases: stiff neck, drowsiness, seizures, and breathing difficulty
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other childhood infections cause similar symptoms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Other viral illnesses, including colds, influenza, and chickenpox
    \item Bacterial throat and ear infections
    \item Gastroenteritis from other viruses
    \item Meningitis from other causes
    \item Measles and other rash illnesses
    \item Allergic rashes
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most enterovirus infections need no medical treatment, but medical advice should be sought for a high fever that persists, poor drinking, signs of dehydration, or a child who seems very unwell.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A stiff neck, severe headache, or sensitivity to light
    \item Drowsiness, confusion, or difficulty waking the child
    \item Seizures or fits
    \item Difficulty breathing or fast breathing
    \item Signs of severe dehydration: no wet nappies, sunken eyes, or a sunken fontanelle in a baby
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is usually clinical.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the typical pattern of symptoms and rash
    \item \textbf{Laboratory tests:} swabs, stool, or blood tests can identify the virus, but are usually not needed
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture:} if meningitis is suspected
    \item \textbf{Other tests:} imaging and heart tests for the rare serious complications
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
There is no specific antiviral treatment for enteroviruses, so care is supportive.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fluids:} encourage small, frequent drinks, and use oral rehydration solution for diarrhoea
    \item \textbf{Pain and fever relief:} paracetamol or ibuprofen
    \item \textbf{Sores in the mouth:} cool, soft foods and drinks to keep the child eating and drinking
    \item \textbf{Rest:} the child should rest while feverish
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} for serious complications, including meningitis, encephalitis, and heart involvement
    \item \textbf{When to return:} seek care if the child deteriorates or cannot keep fluids down
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dehydration from refusal to drink
    \item Viral meningitis, which is usually less severe than bacterial
    \item Encephalitis, in rare cases
    \item Heart muscle inflammation (myocarditis)
    \item Febrile seizures
    \item In newborns, more severe generalised infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for enterovirus infections in children is excellent. Most infections are mild and resolve within a week, and even the more serious complications such as viral meningitis usually recover fully with supportive care. Hand, foot and mouth disease is uncomfortable but self-limiting, and children generally make a complete recovery.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wash hands thoroughly, especially after nappy changes and before food
    \item Teach children to wash hands and cover coughs and sneezes
    \item Keep children with active symptoms home from child care and school
    \item Clean toys and surfaces regularly
    \item Avoid sharing cups, cutlery, and towels
    \item Keep the child drinking during illness
    \item Seek medical advice if concerned about any symptoms
\end{itemize}
