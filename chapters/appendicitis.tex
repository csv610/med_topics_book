\chapter{Appendicitis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Appendicitis is inflammation of the appendix, a small finger-shaped pouch attached to the caecum, the first part of the large bowel, situated in the lower right side of the abdomen. It is one of the commonest causes of sudden, severe abdominal pain and is a surgical emergency, because an inflamed appendix can burst (perforate) and spread infection throughout the abdominal cavity. Appendicitis can occur at any age but is most common in children, teenagers, and young adults. When recognised and treated promptly, the condition is managed safely with surgery, and recovery is usually complete.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The lifetime risk of developing appendicitis is approximately one in thirteen, and it is slightly more common in males. The peak incidence is between 10 and 30 years of age, although the condition also occurs in infants and in older adults, in whom symptoms are often less typical and the diagnosis is frequently delayed. Appendicitis accounts for a significant proportion of emergency surgical admissions, and around one in fifteen people who attend hospital with severe abdominal pain is found to have the condition. The incidence has gradually declined in several developed countries, possibly in relation to changes in diet, but appendicitis remains one of the most common reasons for emergency abdominal surgery.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Appendicitis usually begins when the narrow lumen of the appendix becomes obstructed, allowing bacteria that normally live within the bowel to multiply rapidly in the closed space.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Obstruction:} most often by a faecolith (a hard lump of stool), but also by enlarged lymphoid tissue in the wall, a foreign body, or rarely a worm or tumour
    \item \textbf{Bacterial overgrowth:} trapped organisms multiply, inflame the appendiceal wall, and generate pus
    \item \textbf{Rising intraluminal pressure:} swelling and pus increase pressure inside the appendix, progressively compromising its blood supply
    \item \textbf{Ischaemia and necrosis:} loss of blood flow weakens the wall, and mucosal ulceration allows bacterial spread through it
    \item \textbf{Perforation:} the weakened wall ruptures, spilling bacteria and pus into the peritoneal cavity and causing peritonitis or a contained abscess
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The classic presentation is pain that begins around the navel and migrates to the lower right side of the abdomen over 12 to 24 hours, as inflammation spreads to the peritoneum overlying the appendix. Symptoms vary with age.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain that settles in the right lower abdomen and worsens with movement, coughing, or jarring
    \item Loss of appetite, nausea, and vomiting
    \item Fever, usually low-grade at first, that may climb if the appendix perforates
    \item Localised tenderness on deep palpation of the right iliac fossa, with guarding and rebound tenderness in established disease
    \item Constipation or, less often, diarrhoea
    \item In children and older adults: fever, vomiting, and irritability or vague discomfort may dominate and mask the classic pattern
    \item In pregnancy: pain may be felt higher or more laterally because the appendix is displaced by the enlarging uterus
    \item Atypical positions, such as retrocaecal or pelvic appendicitis, can produce back pain, loin pain, or pelvic discomfort
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Many other conditions cause right lower abdominal pain and can be mistaken for appendicitis, particularly in younger patients and women of reproductive age.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Gastroenteritis and mesenteric lymphadenitis, which is common in children
    \item Urinary tract infection or kidney stone
    \item Constipation or functional abdominal pain
    \item In women: ovarian cyst, pelvic inflammatory disease, or ectopic pregnancy
    \item In men: testicular torsion, which may present with abdominal pain
    \item Crohn's disease and other forms of inflammatory bowel disease
    \item Meckel's diverticulitis
    \item Right-sided diverticulitis or gall-bladder disease
    \item Right lower lobe pneumonia, which may produce referred abdominal pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek urgent medical attention for any pain that moves to the right lower abdomen, worsens with movement, or is accompanied by fever, vomiting, or loss of appetite. Do not eat or drink before being assessed, and avoid strong painkillers that could mask the clinical signs of peritonism.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe, or rapidly worsening abdominal pain
    \item A hard, bloated, or exquisitely tender abdomen
    \item Fever with shaking chills, or signs of shock such as fainting, rapid breathing, or confusion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis is based on the history and examination, supported by blood tests and imaging when the clinical picture is uncertain.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the site and migration of pain, tenderness, guarding, rebound tenderness, and systemic features such as fever and tachycardia
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} a raised white cell count and C-reactive protein support an inflammatory process, although they are non-specific
    \item \textbf{Urine test:} to exclude a urinary tract infection, and a pregnancy test in women of reproductive age
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} often the first imaging test, especially in children and pregnant women, with a graded compression technique
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} the most sensitive and specific imaging test, used when the diagnosis remains unclear
    \item \textbf{MRI:} an option during pregnancy when ultrasound is inconclusive
    \item \textbf{Clinical scoring:} tools such as the appendicitis severity score may be used to estimate the likelihood of disease and guide imaging
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Appendicitis is treated as an emergency, because delay increases the risk of perforation. The standard treatment is surgical removal of the appendix.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Appendectomy:} removal of the appendix, usually by laparoscopic (keyhole) surgery and occasionally as an open operation
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} given before surgery and continued afterwards when the appendix has perforated
    \item \textbf{Intravenous fluids and analgesia:} provided before and after surgery to maintain hydration and control pain
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics alone:} a course of antibiotics may settle uncomplicated appendicitis in some adults, although a proportion later require surgery for recurrent symptoms
    \item \textbf{Abscess drainage:} if a walled-off collection of pus has formed, it may be drained percutaneously, with the appendix removed at a planned interval operation
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} wound care, activity advice, and clinical review in the days and weeks after surgery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Delays in treatment are the main driver of complications in appendicitis.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Perforation, which spreads infection into the abdomen and causes peritonitis
    \item An appendiceal abscess, a walled-off collection of pus around the appendix
    \item Sepsis, a life-threatening whole-body response to the infection
    \item A temporary pause in bowel activity (paralytic ileus) after surgery
    \item Wound infection or an intra-abdominal collection requiring drainage
    \item Adhesions (scar tissue) that can later cause bowel obstruction
    \item Rarely, a small risk to fertility in women after severe pelvic infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt surgery the outlook is excellent, and almost all patients recover fully within a few weeks. The principal danger is perforation: patients treated before the appendix ruptures have few complications, whereas perforation substantially increases the risk of intra-abdominal infection, a longer hospital stay, and, rarely, death. Overall, appendicitis is now a very safe condition to treat provided it is recognised and managed early, and recurrence after appendectomy is uncommon.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Appendicitis cannot be reliably prevented, although a diet rich in fibre is associated with a lower risk of the condition. The most important self-care step is not to ignore right lower abdominal pain.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek medical attention early for abdominal pain that is severe, worsening, or moving to the right side
    \item Do not eat, drink, or take laxatives while awaiting assessment
    \item Avoid strong painkillers that could hide the signs of appendicitis
    \item After surgery, follow wound-care and activity advice and attend follow-up appointments
    \item Keep up a balanced, high-fibre diet with plenty of fluids
    \item Report new fever, worsening pain, or changes at the wound site after treatment promptly
\end{itemize}
