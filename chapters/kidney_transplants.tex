\chapter{Kidney Transplants}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A kidney transplant is a surgical operation in which a healthy kidney from a donor is placed into a person whose own kidneys have failed, to take over their filtering work. It is the best treatment for end-stage kidney disease, offering better survival, quality of life, and freedom from dialysis than any alternative. The donor kidney can come from a living donor, often a family member or friend, or from a deceased donor. The recipient takes immunosuppressant medicines for life to prevent rejection. With modern care, most transplanted kidneys function for a decade or more, and most recipients return to active, normal lives.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Around 800 to 1,000 kidney transplants are performed in Australia each year, and around half of the kidneys come from living donors, a higher proportion than in most countries. There are about 1,500 Australians on the waiting list at any time, and many people wait several years for a deceased donor kidney. The major causes of kidney failure requiring transplant are diabetes and high blood pressure. Transplant outcomes in Australia are among the best in the world, with more than 95 per cent of grafts working after one year.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Kidney failure, from diabetes, high blood pressure, glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease, and other causes, destroys the kidney's filtering units, and transplant replaces them with a healthy kidney. The transplanted kidney is placed in the lower abdomen, where its blood vessels are joined to the recipient's iliac vessels and its ureter to the bladder. Because the immune system recognises the donor kidney as foreign, immunosuppressant medicines are needed to prevent rejection, in which immune cells attack the graft. Rejection can be acute, occurring in the first months and usually treatable, or chronic, causing gradual loss of function over years. Balancing immunosuppression against the risk of infection and cancer is the central task of long-term care.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Candidates: people with end-stage kidney disease, whether on dialysis or approaching it
    \item Living donation: a donor kidney from a family member, friend, or altruistic stranger
    \item Deceased donation: a kidney from a person who has died, allocated by matching
    \item The operation: about three hours, with a hospital stay of a week or so
    \item The new kidney often produces urine immediately or within days
    \item Immunosuppressant medicines taken for life
    \item Regular follow-up with blood tests of kidney function and drug levels
    \item Most recipients stop dialysis and return to work, study, and travel
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The suitability for transplant is assessed individually.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dialysis:} haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis as the alternative treatment
    \item \textbf{Conservative care:} for people who decline transplant or dialysis
    \item \textbf{Medical fitness:} heart, lung, and other conditions affect transplant eligibility
    \item \textbf{Donor suitability:} living donors are assessed for their own health
    \item \textbf{Timing:} transplant before or early in dialysis gives better outcomes
    \item \textbf{Alternative options:} paired exchange and other matching schemes
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with advanced kidney disease should be referred for transplant assessment early, before dialysis is needed. Recipients should seek urgent review for fever, pain over the transplant, reduced urine, swelling, or any sign of rejection or infection. Living donors should be followed up after donation, and anyone considering donation should have a full assessment.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever, shaking, or signs of infection after transplant
    \item Sudden reduction in urine output or severe swelling
    \item Pain or tenderness over the transplant
    \item Difficulty breathing or chest pain
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or collapse
    \item Heavy bleeding or a wound that breaks open
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transplant assessment:} blood tests, heart and lung review, and screening for infection and cancer
    \item \textbf{Tissue typing and cross-matching:} matching the donor and recipient
    \item \textbf{Donor evaluation:} kidney function, anatomy, and health of the living donor
    \item \textbf{Blood tests after transplant:} creatinine, drug levels, and markers of rejection
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} assessment of blood flow to the new kidney
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} the definitive test for rejection
    \item \textbf{Long-term monitoring:} kidney function, blood pressure, and drug side effects
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The operation:} transplantation with blood vessels and ureter connected
    \item \textbf{Immunosuppression:} a combination of medicines, adjusted over time
    \item \textbf{Treatment of rejection:} high-dose steroids and other therapies for acute episodes
    \item \textbf{Prevention of infection:} vaccinations, antibiotics, and avoiding infections
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure and diabetes control:} protecting the graft
    \item \textbf{Cancer screening:} regular checks, as immunosuppression raises risk
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} healthy diet, activity, and sun protection
    \item \textbf{Donor care:} follow-up of the living donor's health
    \item \textbf{Psychosocial support:} counselling and peer support
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rejection, both acute and chronic
    \item Infection, including common infections and opportunistic ones
    \item Side effects of immunosuppressants: diabetes, high blood pressure, and bone disease
    \item Increased risk of skin and other cancers
    \item Surgical complications: bleeding, clot, and urine leak
    \item Delayed graft function
    \item Risks to the living donor, which are low with good selection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Kidney transplant is the most successful treatment for kidney failure. Around 95 per cent of grafts function at one year, most last a decade or more, and recipients live longer, with better quality of life, than those on dialysis. Living donor grafts last longest. Chronic rejection and the long-term effects of immunosuppression are the main challenges, and regular care maximises graft survival. For most people, a transplant restores health, energy, and freedom.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Refer early for transplant assessment if you have advanced kidney disease
    \item Consider living donation and discuss it with your kidney team
    \item Register for deceased donation and discuss it with your family
    \item Take immunosuppressant medicines exactly as prescribed
    \item Attend all follow-up visits and blood tests
    \item Protect the graft: control blood pressure, blood sugar, and weight
    \item Use sun protection and attend skin checks
    \item Report fever, pain, or reduced urine urgently
    \item Access support from transplant organisations
\end{itemize}
