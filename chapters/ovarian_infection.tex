\chapter{Ovarian Infection}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Ovarian infection, clinically referred to as oophoritis, is an inflammatory condition of the ovary, most commonly occurring as a component of pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) and frequently coexisting with salpingitis (salpingo-oophoritis). While isolated oophoritis is rare, ovarian infection often progresses to form a complex inflammatory mass known as a tubo-ovarian abscess (TOA), which represents a serious, potentially life-threatening gynaecological emergency. These infections typically arise from the ascending spread of pathogenic microorganisms from the lower genital tract (vagina and cervix) through the uterine cavity and fallopian tubes into the peritoneal cavity. Ovarian infection requires prompt clinical recognition and management to prevent long-term reproductive sequelae, including infertility, chronic pelvic pain, and ectopic pregnancy.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Ovarian infection most commonly affects sexually active females of reproductive age, particularly those aged 15 to 25 years. The epidemiology is closely linked to that of pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) and sexually transmitted infections (STIs), with \textit{Chlamydia trachomatis} and \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae} serving as key sentinel pathogens. Tubo-ovarian abscesses complicate approximately 15\% to 30\% of patients hospitalised for PID. Risk factors include a history of multiple sexual partners, inconsistent barrier contraceptive use, previous episodes of PID, and recent invasive gynaecological procedures (such as intrauterine device insertion or dilation and curettage). In Australia, notification rates for chlamydia and gonorrhoea remain significant, especially among young adults and in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, reflecting a corresponding burden of ascending pelvic infections.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The pathogenesis of ovarian infection typically involves the ascent of microorganisms from the vagina or cervix into the upper genital tract, disrupting the normal mucosal barriers and inciting an inflammatory response.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ascending sexually transmitted pathogens:} Infection is frequently initiated by \textit{Chlamydia trachomatis} or \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae}, which damage the tubal epithelium and facilitate the ascent of other organisms.
    \item \textbf{Polymicrobial vaginal flora:} Secondary invasion by endogenous vaginal pathogens, including anaerobic bacteria (such as \textit{Bacteroides} species, \textit{Peptostreptococcus}), and aerobic bacteria (such as \textit{Escherichia coli}, \textit{Streptococcus} species), is common in mature ovarian infections and abscesses.
    \item \textbf{Iatrogenic and instrumentation factors:} Invasive uterine procedures, including intrauterine device (IUD) insertion, endometrial biopsy, or termination of pregnancy, can breach the protective cervical barrier and introduce pathogens directly into the uterine cavity.
    \item \textbf{Contiguous and haematogenous spread:} Rarely, oophoritis can occur via contiguous spread from adjacent pelvic structures (such as appendicitis or diverticulitis) or haematogenous dissemination (such as systemic tuberculosis or mumps oophoritis).
    \item \textbf{Tissue necrosis and abscess formation:} The inflammatory process leads to exudative tissue damage, agglutination of the fallopian tube and ovary, and subsequent loculation of pus, resulting in a tubo-ovarian abscess (TOA) characterised by tissue necrosis and anaerobic growth.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of ovarian infection ranges from subclinical or mild pelvic discomfort to severe acute abdomen with systemic sepsis.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Lower abdominal or pelvic pain, typically bilateral, constant, and exacerbated by movement or sexual intercourse (dyspareunia).
    \item Abnormal vaginal discharge, which may be purulent, profuse, or malodorous.
    \item Intermenstrual or post-coital vaginal bleeding due to concomitant cervicitis or endometritis.
    \item Pyrexia (fever), chills, rigors, and tachycardia, indicating systemic inflammatory response, particularly common if a tubo-ovarian abscess is present.
    \item Cervical motion tenderness, uterine tenderness, and bilateral adnexal tenderness on bimanual pelvic examination.
    \item Palpable pelvic mass or fullness, suggesting the development of a tubo-ovarian abscess.
    \item Nausea, vomiting, and abdominal guarding or rebound tenderness, indicating secondary pelvic peritonitis or abscess rupture.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Due to the non-specific nature of pelvic pain and systemic symptoms, several acute gynaecological, surgical, and urological conditions must be differentiated from ovarian infection.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ectopic pregnancy:} A critical life-threatening condition presenting with unilateral pelvic pain and vaginal bleeding, differentiated by a positive beta-human chorionic gonadotropin ($\beta$-hCG) test.
    \item \textbf{Ovarian torsion:} Acute, severe, unilateral pelvic pain often accompanied by nausea and vomiting, characterised by compromised ovarian blood flow on Doppler ultrasound.
    \item \textbf{Acute appendicitis:} Presents with migrating right lower quadrant pain, anorexia, and localised peritonitis, requiring differentiation when right-sided adnexal tenderness is prominent.
    \item \textbf{Endometriosis or ruptured ovarian cyst:} Can cause acute pelvic pain and peritoneal signs, but typically lacks systemic symptoms like fever, leukocytosis, or purulent cervical discharge.
    \item \textbf{Urinary tract infection or pyelonephritis:} Characterised by dysuria, urinary frequency, and costovertebral angle tenderness, differentiated via urinalysis and urine culture.
    \item \textbf{Diverticulitis:} Left-sided lower abdominal pain and fever in older patients, differentiated by computed tomography (CT) scan.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients experiencing persistent or worsening lower abdominal pain, abnormal vaginal discharge, or pain during intercourse should seek prompt medical evaluation from a general practitioner or gynaecologist. Early diagnosis and treatment are critical to safeguarding future fertility.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, sudden, or unbearable lower abdominal or pelvic pain.
    \item High fever accompanied by chills, rigors, confusion, or rapid heart rate.
    \item Persistent vomiting or the inability to tolerate oral fluids and medications.
    \item Signs of shock, such as dizziness, lightheadedness, pale or clammy skin, and fainting.
    \item Suspected rupture of a pelvic abscess, indicated by sudden worsening of pain followed by abdominal distension and guarding.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis relies on a combination of clinical criteria, laboratory investigations, and pelvic imaging to confirm upper genital tract inflammation and rule out pregnancy or surgical emergencies.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test:} Serum or urine $\beta$-hCG is mandatory in all reproductive-age females presenting with acute pelvic pain to exclude ectopic pregnancy.
    \item \textbf{Endocervical and vaginal swabs:} Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAAT) for \textit{Chlamydia trachomatis} and \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae}, along with high vaginal swabs for bacterial vaginosis and trichomoniasis.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory markers:} Full blood count (FBC) showing leukocytosis, and elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) or erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), supporting an active inflammatory process.
    \item \textbf{Transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS):} The primary imaging modality, which may demonstrate thickened, fluid-filled fallopian tubes (hydrosalpinx or pyosalpinx), free pelvic fluid, or a complex multilocular adnexal mass characteristic of a tubo-ovarian abscess.
    \item \textbf{Urinalysis and culture:} Used to exclude urinary tract infections and pyelonephritis.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):} Indicated if ultrasound is inconclusive, to better delineate complex pelvic masses or evaluate for suspected abscess rupture.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The primary goals of management are to eradicate the infectious pathogens, resolve symptoms, and prevent long-term reproductive sequelae. Hospitalisation is required for patients who are pregnant, severely ill, unable to tolerate oral therapy, or have a tubo-ovarian abscess.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Empirical antibiotic therapy:} Initiated promptly using broad-spectrum regimens covering \textit{C. trachomatis}, \textit{N. gonorrhoeae}, anaerobes, and Gram-negative bacilli (such as intravenous ceftriaxone, metronidazole, and oral or intravenous doxycycline, modified based on local guidelines).
    \item \textbf{Surgical or radiological drainage:} Indicated for large or ruptured tubo-ovarian abscesses, or where antibiotic therapy fails to produce clinical improvement within 48 to 72 hours; this may involve ultrasound- or CT-guided percutaneous drainage, or laparoscopic drainage.
    \item \textbf{Analgesia and supportive care:} Adequate pain relief with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or paracetamol, intravenous fluid resuscitation for dehydrated or septic patients, and bed rest.
    \item \textbf{Partner notification and treatment:} Sexual partners within the preceding 60 days must be evaluated and treated empirically for chlamydia and gonorrhoea to prevent a cycle of reinfection.
    \item \textbf{Intrauterine device (IUD) management:} While immediate removal is not always mandatory, IUD removal should be considered if there is no clinical improvement after 48 to 72 hours of appropriate antibiotic therapy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic pelvic pain due to peritoneal adhesions and scarring of the pelvic organs.
    \item Tubal factor infertility resulting from bilateral fallopian tube damage, obstruction, or loss of ciliary function.
    \item Ectopic pregnancy due to altered tubal motility and scarring, preventing normal zygote transit.
    \item Abscess rupture leading to generalised peritonitis, septic shock, and multi-organ failure, representing a medical emergency.
    \item Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome, characterised by perihepatitis and the formation of "violin-string" adhesions between the liver capsule and the anterior abdominal wall.
    \item Recurrent pelvic inflammatory disease and ovarian infection due to persistent risk factors or incomplete treatment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of ovarian infection is highly dependent on the timeliness of diagnosis and the prompt initiation of appropriate antimicrobial therapy. With early intervention, most uncomplicated infections resolve completely without long-term reproductive impairment. However, the risk of tubal infertility increases exponentially with each recurrent episode of pelvic infection, rising from approximately 10--12\% after the first episode to over 50\% after three episodes. For patients with a tubo-ovarian abscess, conservative management with antibiotics is successful in 70--80\% of cases, while the remainder require drainage or surgical intervention. Long-term follow-up is essential to monitor for the resolution of inflammatory masses and to address potential issues regarding fertility and chronic pelvic pain.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Practise safe sex by using barrier methods, such as male or female condoms, consistently and correctly.
    \item Undergo regular screening for sexually transmitted infections (STIs), particularly chlamydia and gonorrhoea, if sexually active and under 30 years of age or with new or multiple partners.
    \item Ensure that all sexual partners are tested and treated if an STI is diagnosed, to prevent a cycle of reinfection.
    \item Avoid vaginal douching, as it alters the normal vaginal microbiome and increases the risk of ascending pelvic infections.
    \item Complete the full course of prescribed antibiotics, even if symptoms resolve before the medication is finished.
    \item Attend scheduled follow-up appointments to confirm complete clinical and radiological resolution of the infection.
\end{itemize}
