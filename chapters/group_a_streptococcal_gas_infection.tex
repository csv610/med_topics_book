\chapter{Group A Streptococcal (GAS) Infection}
\index{Group A Streptococcal (GAS) Infection}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Group A streptococcus (GAS), also called \textit{Streptococcus pyogenes}, is a common bacterium that causes a wide range of infections, from mild sore throats (strep throat) and skin infections (impetigo) to serious invasive diseases such as toxic shock syndrome and necrotising fasciitis. GAS is spread through respiratory droplets and skin contact. Most GAS infections are mild and easily treated with antibiotics, but the bacterium can sometimes cause complications such as rheumatic fever and kidney inflammation, and invasive disease is a medical emergency. In many countries, GAS infections are of particular concern in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, where rates of rheumatic heart disease are among the highest in the world.

\section*{Epidemiology}
GAS infections are extremely common. Strep throat affects millions of people worldwide each year, and impetigo is one of the most common skin infections in children. Invasive GAS disease is much rarer, affecting around 1--3 per 100,000 people annually, but it is serious, with a case fatality rate of 10--20\%. Acute rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease follow untreated GAS throat infections and remain a major problem in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities in many countries, where rates are some of the highest recorded globally. GAS infections are more common in winter and in crowded conditions.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
GAS causes disease through direct infection and through immune-mediated complications.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The bacterium:} \textit{Streptococcus pyogenes} is a Gram-positive coccus that produces several toxins and enzymes that damage tissue
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} respiratory droplets from coughing and sneezing, and skin-to-skin contact with infected sores
    \item \textbf{Throat infection:} colonisation of the throat causes tonsillitis and pharyngitis
    \item \textbf{Skin infection:} impetigo and wound infections occur when the bacterium enters broken skin
    \item \textbf{Toxin-mediated disease:} some strains produce toxins that cause scarlet fever and toxic shock syndrome
    \item \textbf{Invasive disease:} the bacterium can enter the bloodstream or deep tissues, causing sepsis and necrotising fasciitis
    \item \textbf{Immune complications:} antibodies formed against the bacterium can cross-react with heart, joint, and kidney tissue, causing rheumatic fever and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The presentation depends on the site and severity of infection.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Strep throat: sudden sore throat, fever, swollen tonsils with pus, tender neck glands, and pain on swallowing
    \item Scarlet fever: strep throat with a fine red rash, a strawberry tongue, and flushed cheeks
    \item Impetigo: itchy, crusted sores, usually around the nose and mouth or on the limbs
    \item Cellulitis: a red, swollen, painful, warm area of skin
    \item Invasive disease: high fever, severe pain, confusion, and shock
    \item Necrotising fasciitis: severe pain out of proportion to skin changes, with rapid tissue destruction
    \item Toxic shock syndrome: fever, rash, low blood pressure, and organ failure
    \item Complications: painful swollen joints and heart damage (rheumatic fever), and blood in the urine and swelling (glomerulonephritis)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Many infections cause similar symptoms and must be distinguished.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Viral sore throats, including those from cold viruses, Epstein-Barr virus, and influenza
    \item Tonsillitis from other bacteria
    \item Other causes of skin infection, including staphylococcal infection
    \item Hand, foot, and mouth disease and other viral rashes
    \item Infectious mononucleosis (glandular fever)
    \item Diphtheria, which is rare but serious
    \item Non-infectious causes of rash and fever
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for a sore throat with high fever that does not settle, or for skin sores that are spreading, painful, or crusted. Anyone with a severe sore throat and rash, or skin infection with fever, should be assessed promptly.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing or swallowing
    \item Severe pain, swelling, or discolouration of a limb with fever, which may indicate necrotising fasciitis
    \item Confusion, low blood pressure, or a faint rash that does not blanch, which may indicate toxic shock or sepsis
    \item Signs of severe dehydration or an inability to keep down fluids
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is clinical, with laboratory confirmation where needed.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Throat swab:} culture or rapid antigen testing confirms strep throat
    \item \textbf{Skin swabs:} identify the bacterium in impetigo and wound infections
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} for suspected invasive disease, including blood cultures and inflammatory markers
    \item \textbf{Rapid molecular tests:} provide quick, accurate results in some settings
    \item \textbf{Complication screening:} urine tests, heart tests, and joint assessment where rheumatic fever or glomerulonephritis is suspected
    \item \textbf{Notification:} invasive GAS disease is notifiable in many countries
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Antibiotics treat GAS infections and prevent complications.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} penicillin or amoxicillin are first-line for throat and skin infections; alternatives exist for allergy
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} paracetamol or ibuprofen for fever and throat pain
    \item \textbf{Impetigo care:} antibiotic creams for small areas, oral antibiotics for widespread infection, and good hygiene
    \item \textbf{Invasive disease:} urgent hospital care with intravenous antibiotics and, for necrotising fasciitis, urgent surgery
    \item \textbf{Complication prevention:} treating strep throat promptly reduces the risk of rheumatic fever, which is why it matters in high-risk communities
    \item \textbf{Long-term prevention:} people who have had rheumatic fever may need regular penicillin injections for years to prevent recurrence
    \item \textbf{Household care:} good hand hygiene and not sharing utensils reduce spread
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute rheumatic fever, which can cause permanent heart valve damage (rheumatic heart disease)
    \item Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, an inflammatory kidney condition
    \item Scarlet fever and its complications
    \item Abscesses, including peritonsillar abscess
    \item Invasive disease: sepsis, toxic shock syndrome, and necrotising fasciitis, which can be fatal
    \item Reactive arthritis
    \item Spread of infection to household contacts
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt antibiotic treatment, most GAS infections resolve completely within days, and complications are largely prevented. Strep throat and impetigo have excellent outcomes. Invasive GAS disease is serious but treatable with rapid hospital care; outcomes depend on how quickly treatment begins. The most significant long-term complication, rheumatic heart disease, is preventable with timely treatment of throat infections and, for those already affected, long-term antibiotic prophylaxis.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Practise good hand hygiene, especially after coughing, sneezing, and touching skin sores
    \item Cover the mouth and nose when coughing and sneezing
    \item Do not share drinking cups, cutlery, or towels with someone who is infected
    \item Treat skin cuts, scrapes, and insect bites promptly and keep them clean
    \item Seek treatment early for sore throats with fever, particularly in communities at high risk of rheumatic fever
    \item Complete the full course of antibiotics as prescribed
    \item Keep children with GAS infections home until they have been on antibiotics for 24 hours and feel better
    \item In high-risk areas, participate in sore throat and skin infection control programs
\end{itemize}
