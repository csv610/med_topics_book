\chapter{Hydrocele Repair (Child)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hydrocele repair in children is a surgical procedure to correct a hydrocele, a fluid collection around the testicle. In most newborn boys, hydroceles resolve on their own within the first year of life and need no treatment. Surgery is considered when a hydrocele persists beyond 12--18 months, is a communicating hydrocele (connected to the abdomen), becomes large, or is associated with an inguinal hernia. The operation is simple, safe, and performed as day surgery, with excellent results and rapid recovery.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hydroceles are present in around 1--5\% of newborn boys, and most close and disappear by 12 months. Around 10--20\% of infant hydroceles are communicating and may persist or be associated with a hernia. Hydrocele repair is one of the most common paediatric surgical procedures. The need for surgery is uncommon, and most boys who have it are aged 1--2 years. Outcomes are excellent, with a very low rate of complications or recurrence.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Newborn hydrocele:} the processus vaginalis, the passage between abdomen and scrotum, fails to close fully
    \item \textbf{Non-communicating hydrocele:} fluid is trapped in the scrotum with a closed passage; most resolve
    \item \textbf{Communicating hydrocele:} the passage remains open, allowing fluid to move in and out; these persist and may accompany a hernia
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} fluid accumulates in the tunica vaginalis around the testicle
    \item \textbf{Indications for surgery:} persistence beyond 12--18 months, communication, size, or an associated hernia
    \item \textbf{The operation:} the passage is tied off and the fluid is drained through a small groin or scrotal incision
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A painless swelling of the scrotum in a baby or young boy
    \item The swelling may vary in size, changing with position in communicating hydroceles
    \item The scrotum may glow when a light is shone through (transillumination)
    \item Most boys are otherwise well with normal feeding and behaviour
    \item A hydrocele that persists past 12--18 months is assessed for surgery
    \item Redness, pain, or vomiting suggests complications requiring urgent care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Inguinal hernia, which contains bowel and may be reducible
    \item Testicular torsion, a sudden painful emergency
    \item Testicular tumours, rare in children but always considered
    \item Infection and trauma
    \item Ultrasound confirms the diagnosis when needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Boys with a scrotal swelling should be assessed by a doctor or paediatrician, and persistent or communicating hydroceles are referred for surgical opinion.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe pain or a tender, red scrotum (possible torsion)
    \item Vomiting with a groin or scrotal swelling (possible strangulated hernia)
    \item A swelling that becomes hard, tense, or very painful
    \item A boy who is very unwell, lethargic, or feverish
    \item An irreducible groin bulge
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Examination:} assessment of the swelling, its size changes, and the groin
    \item \textbf{Transillumination:} confirms fluid
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} confirms the diagnosis and excludes other causes
    \item \textbf{Observation:} most newborn hydroceles are followed up rather than operated on
    \item \textbf{Surgical review:} referral for persistent or communicating hydroceles
    \item \textbf{Preoperative checks:} general health and anaesthetic assessment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observation:} most hydroceles resolve by 12 months without treatment
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} hydrocele repair, performed as day surgery under general anaesthesia
    \item \textbf{Technique:} a small incision in the groin or scrotum; the communication is tied off and fluid is drained
    \item \textbf{Hernia repair:} combined when a hernia is present
    \item \textbf{Recovery:} most boys go home the same day and return to normal activity within days
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review to confirm healing
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple analgesia for the first day or two
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Recurrence of the hydrocele, which is uncommon
    \item Swelling and bruising of the scrotum after surgery
    \item Infection and bleeding, which are rare
    \item Anaesthetic risks, which are very low in healthy children
    \item Injury to the testicle or its blood supply, extremely rare
    \item A missed hernia if not identified
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Hydrocele repair in children is one of the most successful operations in paediatric surgery. Recurrence is uncommon, complications are rare, and most boys recover within days with normal testicular function. The operation prevents the complications of communicating hydroceles, including hernia strangulation. With appropriate timing and surgical care, the long-term outlook is excellent.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Have infant scrotal swellings reviewed at routine child health checks
    \item Follow the specialist's advice on when surgery is needed
    \item Prepare the child for day surgery with clear, age-appropriate information
    \item Follow postoperative care: keep the wound clean and dry
    \item Give simple analgesia as directed
    \item Keep follow-up appointments
    \item Report fever, wound redness, or excessive swelling after surgery
    \item Seek urgent care for any new pain or vomiting
\end{itemize}
