\chapter{Mental illness stigma}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Mental illness stigma refers to the negative attitudes, stereotypes, prejudices, and discriminatory behaviours directed towards individuals who live with mental health conditions. Stigma is a multifaceted concept that operates at three distinct levels: public stigma (societal endorsement of negative stereotypes), self-stigma (the internalisation of these stereotypes by the affected individual), and structural or institutional stigma (systemic policies, funding disparities, and laws that restrict opportunities). Clinically, mental illness stigma is recognized as a profound barrier to public health. It causes substantial psychological distress, damages self-esteem, disrupts social and occupational functioning, and deters individuals from seeking timely psychiatric help, thereby directly contributing to delayed recovery and poorer clinical outcomes.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Mental illness stigma is a pervasive global phenomenon that transcends geographical, cultural, and socioeconomic boundaries. In Australia, research conducted by advocacy groups indicates that approximately 75\% of people living with a mental illness report experiencing stigma or discrimination in the preceding year. While public campaigns have improved understanding of high-prevalence disorders like mild depression and anxiety, stigma remains deeply entrenched against low-prevalence, complex psychiatric conditions, including schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and borderline personality disorder. Stigma affects individuals across all demographics, but its impact is often amplified in specific cohorts, such as rural populations, culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) communities, and Indigenous Australians, where cultural taboos and limited access to mental health literacy exacerbate social exclusion.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The development and persistence of mental illness stigma are driven by social, psychological, and systemic mechanisms rather than physical pathology, though they severely impact physiological well-being.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Misconceptions and lack of knowledge:} Public understanding of psychiatric disorders is often poor. Many people incorrectly attribute mental illness to personal weakness, lack of willpower, or moral failings, rather than recognising them as complex neurobiological and environmental conditions.
    \item \textbf{Media sensationalism:} Historically, media depictions of mental illness have associated psychiatric diagnoses, particularly psychosis, with unpredictability, violence, and criminal behaviour, reinforcing fear and social distance.
    \item \textbf{Social distance and lack of contact:} A lack of personal interaction with individuals who are openly managing their mental health allows negative stereotypes to persist unchallenged.
    \item \textbf{Cultural frameworks:} In many societies, mental illness is associated with family shame, spiritual punishment, or loss of social standing, leading families to hide affected individuals and avoid formal healthcare.
    \item \textbf{Structural inequities:} Systematic underfunding of mental health services compared to physical health services, along with discriminatory practices in insurance cover and workplace regulations, institutionalises stigma.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Stigma manifests in patients through specific cognitive, emotional, and behavioural signs that clinicians must recognise.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Help-seeking avoidance:} Reluctance to seek professional psychiatric help or delayed presentation to medical services due to fear of being labelled mentally ill.
    \item \textbf{Social withdrawal and concealment:} Active concealment of symptoms, medication, and therapy appointments from friends, family, and employers, leading to profound isolation.
    \item \textbf{Low self-esteem and the ``why try'' effect:} Internalisation of public prejudice (self-stigma), causing feelings of shame, hopelessness, and a belief that one is incapable of succeeding in life or holding a job.
    \item \textbf{Workplace and housing difficulties:} Experiencing direct discrimination, such as being denied employment, passed over for promotion, or refused housing tenancy after disclosing a mental health condition.
    \item \textbf{Poor treatment adherence:} Discontinuing psychiatric medication or skipping therapy sessions to avoid the self-perception of being dependent on mental health services.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
In a clinical setting, it is important to distinguish the manifestations of stigma from other related phenomena:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Public stigma vs.\ Self-stigma:} Public stigma involves external societal attitudes and actions, whereas self-stigma is the internalised psychological distress and self-devaluation experienced by the patient.
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic overshadowing:} A phenomenon where physical symptoms reported by a psychiatric patient are incorrectly attributed to their mental illness, resulting in delayed diagnosis of serious co-morbid physical conditions (e.g., cardiovascular disease or cancer).
    \item \textbf{Cultural shame vs.\ Clinical paranoia:} Distinguishing a patient's rational fear of community rejection and family ostracisation from clinical delusions or persecutory paranoia.
    \item \textbf{Malingering or non-compliance:} Differentiating a patient's failure to follow a treatment plan due to the paralyzing fear of stigma from genuine lack of motivation or therapeutic resistance.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Individuals should seek support from a general practitioner, counsellor, or psychologist if the fear of stigma or experiences of discrimination lead to severe distress, social isolation, worsening mental health symptoms, or the avoidance of essential medical care.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Active thoughts of suicide, self-harm, or plans to end one's life.
    \item An acute psychiatric crisis, such as severe panic, psychosis, or deep depression, where personal safety is compromised.
    \item Severe physical distress resulting from neglected physical health, particularly when medical attention was avoided due to fear of judgment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Stigma is not a DSM-5 diagnosis, but its impact is evaluated through clinical assessment and validated scales:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical interview and history:} Inquiring about the patient's experiences with discrimination, fear of disclosure at work or home, and feelings of shame during the routine psychiatric history.
    \item \textbf{Psychosocial assessment:} Evaluating the patient's social support network, family dynamics, and potential barriers to care driven by community stigma.
    \item \textbf{Standardised scales:} Utilizing validated questionnaires, such as the \textbf{Internalized Stigma of Mental Illness (ISMI) scale} or the \textbf{Stigma Scale}, to measure the extent of self-stigma and its impact on the patient's quality of life.
    \item \textbf{Screening for diagnostic overshadowing:} Reviewing the patient's physical health systematically to ensure new somatic complaints are fully investigated rather than dismissed as psychosomatic.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Addressing mental illness stigma requires therapeutic interventions for the individual and structural strategies for the healthcare system and society.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT):} Used to target self-stigma. Cognitive restructuring helps patients challenge internalised negative beliefs, reduce feelings of shame, and rebuild self-worth.
    \item \textbf{Psychoeducation:} Providing patients and their families with clear, biological and environmental explanations of mental illness, dismantling the myth that it represents a personal failure.
    \item \textbf{Peer support programmes:} Group sessions led by peer workers living with similar conditions can reduce isolation and normalise the patient's experiences.
    \item \textbf{Contact-based interventions:} Promoting structured interactions where people with lived experience of mental illness share their recovery stories. This is recognized as the most effective method for reducing public stigma.
    \item \textbf{Clinician education:} Training healthcare professionals to recognise diagnostic overshadowing and to communicate with empathy, avoiding stigmatising terminology in clinical records.
    \item \textbf{Institutional advocacy:} Supporting policy reforms and anti-stigma initiatives led by organisations like SANE Australia and Beyond Blue.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The unresolved impact of stigma is associated with severe individual and societal complications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Worsened psychiatric prognosis due to delayed initiation of evidence-based treatments.
    \item Increased risk of suicide and self-harm, driven by isolation, internalised hopelessness, and lack of support.
    \item Socioeconomic marginalisation, resulting from persistent unemployment, underemployment, financial distress, and housing instability.
    \item Poor physical health outcomes and premature mortality due to diagnostic overshadowing and a reluctance to seek general medical care.
    \item Substance abuse as a coping mechanism to self-medicate untreated psychiatric symptoms.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals affected by self-stigma improves significantly with access to supportive, non-judgmental healthcare environments and evidence-based psychological therapies. On a societal level, public health campaigns have successfully reduced stigma surrounding high-prevalence conditions like depression. However, reducing stigma for complex conditions like schizophrenia remains a major challenge, requiring long-term, contact-based public education and legislative protection against discrimination.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventing stigma and protecting oneself from its effects involves active cognitive and social strategies.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Educate oneself and others using scientific, reputable sources of information about mental health.
    \item Use person-first language (e.g., saying ``a person with bipolar disorder'' instead of ``a bipolar person'') to separate a person's identity from their medical condition.
    \item Challenge stigmatising comments, stereotypes, and media portrayals in social and professional settings.
    \item Focus on strengths, resilience, and personal recovery goals rather than defining oneself solely by a diagnosis.
    \item Connect with mental health advocacy groups to build a supportive community and share coping strategies.
\end{itemize}
