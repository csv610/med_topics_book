\chapter{Anaplasmosis}
\index{Anaplasmosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Anaplasmosis is a bacterial infection usually transmitted by the bite of an infected tick. It is caused by \textit{Anaplasma} bacteria and can range from a mild febrile illness to severe disease affecting several organs. Early symptoms are not specific, so recent tick exposure and the local distribution of tick-borne infections are important clues.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms usually begin within days to two weeks after a tick bite and may include fever, chills, headache, marked tiredness, muscle aches, nausea, vomiting, or confusion. A rash is less common than in some other tick-borne infections. Older adults and people with weakened immunity are at greater risk of severe disease, which can include breathing problems, bleeding, kidney injury, or neurological complications.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek prompt medical assessment after a tick bite or possible tick exposure if fever, headache, muscle aches, or unusual tiredness develops. Contact local emergency services for difficulty breathing, confusion, collapse, severe bleeding, seizures, or rapidly worsening illness.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is based on symptoms, examination, exposure history, and laboratory testing. A complete blood count and liver tests may show low blood cells or raised liver enzymes. Molecular testing or paired antibody tests can help confirm infection, but early tests may be negative. Treatment should not be delayed while waiting for confirmation when the clinical suspicion is substantial.

\section*{Management}
Doxycycline is the usual first-line treatment for suspected anaplasmosis in adults and children, as directed by a clinician. Treatment is most effective when started early. Pregnancy, allergy, age, possible co-infection, and local resistance patterns require individual medical advice. Do not self-treat with leftover antibiotics.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use insect repellent and wear long clothing when in tick habitat.
    \item Check the skin, scalp, clothing, and equipment after outdoor activity and remove attached ticks safely.
    \item Seek advice about fever after tick exposure, even if no tick was noticed.
    \item Take the full prescribed course and return for review if symptoms do not improve within the expected time.
\end{itemize}
