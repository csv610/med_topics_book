\chapter{Detox}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
"Detox" is a term used in two very different ways. In medicine, detoxification (detox) refers to the supervised medical process of withdrawing from alcohol or other drugs safely, managing withdrawal symptoms, and reducing the risks of stopping use suddenly. In popular culture, "detox" refers to diets, fasts, or products claimed to "cleanse" the body of toxins. The medical evidence does not support the idea that the body needs special products to remove toxins: the liver, kidneys, and lungs do this constantly. This chapter explains both meanings, the reality of how the body detoxifies itself, and the proper medical approach to withdrawal from substances.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Alcohol and drug withdrawal requiring medical detox is common in Australia. Alcohol is the most frequently withdrawn substance, and withdrawal can be dangerous or even fatal when severe. Opioid withdrawal affects people who use prescription or illicit opioids, and benzodiazepine and stimulant withdrawal also occur. Many people attempt detoxification without medical support, which carries risks of complications and relapse. "Detox" diets and products are widely marketed and used, but they are not supported by evidence and are not needed for health.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Substance dependence:} the brain adapts to regular alcohol or drug use, and stopping suddenly causes rebound effects
    \item \textbf{Alcohol withdrawal:} the brain compensates for alcohol's sedating effect; when alcohol is removed, overactivity causes shaking, sweating, anxiety, and in severe cases seizures and delirium
    \item \textbf{Opioid withdrawal:} removal of opioids causes flu-like symptoms as the brain's pain and reward systems rebound
    \item \textbf{Benzodiazepine withdrawal:} can cause anxiety, insomnia, and seizures, and needs slow tapering
    \item \textbf{Body's own detoxification:} the liver processes and removes metabolic waste and toxins continuously, and the kidneys filter blood; detox diets do not improve this
    \item \textbf{Risks of unsupervised withdrawal:} severe withdrawal can be life-threatening
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Alcohol withdrawal:} sweating, tremor, nausea, anxiety, agitation, insomnia, raised heart rate and blood pressure; severe cases progress to seizures, hallucinations, and delirium tremens
    \item \textbf{Opioid withdrawal:} yawning, runny nose, muscle aches, cramps, diarrhoea, sweating, and agitation
    \item \textbf{Benzodiazepine withdrawal:} anxiety, insomnia, tremor, and risk of seizures
    \item \textbf{Detox diet claims:} marketers claim symptoms such as fatigue, headaches, and skin problems are due to "toxins", but these usually reflect dehydration, poor diet, or unrelated conditions
    \item \textbf{Risky detox practices:} severe calorie restriction, fasting, laxatives, and colon cleansing can cause harm
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Withdrawal from different substances, which needs different management
    \item Underlying mental health conditions, which can cause similar symptoms
    \item Physical illness causing fatigue, tremor, or gastrointestinal symptoms
    \item Detox diet symptoms versus actual deficiency or illness
    \item Medication interactions and side effects
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who wants to stop alcohol or drug use, especially after prolonged or heavy use, should seek medical advice before stopping. Medical detox may be needed, and it is safest under professional supervision. Anyone who has stopped drinking heavily and develops severe symptoms should seek urgent care.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seizures, hallucinations, severe confusion, or agitation during alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal
    \item Chest pain, irregular heartbeat, or collapse
    \item Signs of severe dehydration or inability to keep down fluids
    \item Any concern that a person in withdrawal is unsafe
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Assessment of substance use:} what, how much, how long, and when it was last used
    \item \textbf{Withdrawal scales:} validated tools such as the CIWA-Ar assess alcohol withdrawal severity
    \item \textbf{Physical examination and observations:} monitoring vital signs during withdrawal
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} liver function, electrolytes, and other tests depending on the substance
    \item \textbf{Screening for co-occurring conditions:} mental health and medical problems
    \item \textbf{Assessment of detox diet harm:} review of weight, nutrition, and symptoms in people using detox products
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Medically supervised withdrawal:} medications such as benzodiazepines for alcohol withdrawal, and support for opioid withdrawal with options including buprenorphine or methadone
    \item \textbf{Safe environment:} detoxification may occur at home with support, in an outpatient program, or in hospital depending on risk
    \item \textbf{Hydration and nutrition:} fluids, electrolytes, vitamins (especially thiamine in alcohol withdrawal), and a balanced diet
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular observation of vital signs and withdrawal symptoms
    \item \textbf{Follow-up treatment:} detox is only the first step; ongoing counselling, rehabilitation, and relapse prevention are essential
    \item \textbf{For detox diets:} the evidence-based advice is that a healthy balanced diet, hydration, sleep, and exercise support health; special "cleanses" are unnecessary and can be harmful
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe alcohol withdrawal, including seizures, delirium tremens, and death
    \item Relapse after unsupervised detox without follow-up treatment
    \item Overdose risk if tolerance is reduced after a period of abstinence
    \item Nutritional deficiencies, dehydration, and electrolyte disturbance
    \item Harm from detox diet practices, including malnutrition, laxative misuse, and interaction with medicines
    \item Progression of underlying medical problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Medically supervised detoxification is safe and effective, and withdrawal symptoms are well controlled with appropriate treatment. However, detox alone does not cure addiction: lasting recovery requires ongoing treatment, including counselling, rehabilitation, and support. Most people who complete detox and engage in follow-up treatment achieve sustained recovery. For the healthy body, no special detox is needed, and the best outcomes come from a balanced diet, hydration, exercise, and good sleep.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek medical advice before stopping alcohol or drugs after regular use
    \item Never attempt unsupervised withdrawal from alcohol or benzodiazepines after heavy use
    \item Engage with counselling and support services for ongoing recovery
    \item Avoid restrictive detox diets, fasting, and colon cleanses
    \item Eat a balanced diet, drink water, exercise, and sleep well to support health
    \item Be wary of products and programs that promise to "flush toxins"
    \item Seek professional help for underlying mental health and substance use issues
\end{itemize}
