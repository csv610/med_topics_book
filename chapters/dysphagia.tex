\chapter{Dysphagia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dysphagia is the medical term for difficulty swallowing. It can range from a mild sensation of food sticking in the throat to a complete inability to swallow, and it can affect any stage of swallowing, from moving food through the mouth to propelling it down the oesophagus. Dysphagia is a symptom of an underlying condition, not a disease itself, and its causes include neurological disorders, structural problems, and muscle diseases. It is important to identify and manage dysphagia because it can lead to choking, malnutrition, dehydration, and pneumonia.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Difficulty swallowing is common, particularly in older people and in people with neurological conditions. It affects a large proportion of people after stroke, most people with motor neurone disease, and many people with Parkinson's disease and dementia. It is also common after treatments for head and neck cancer, including radiotherapy. Dysphagia is associated with increased hospital admissions, poorer nutrition, and higher mortality, making early recognition important.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Swallowing is a complex process involving the brain, nerves, and over 30 muscles of the mouth, throat, and oesophagus, and it can break down at any point.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Oropharyngeal dysphagia:} difficulty starting to swallow or moving food from the mouth to the throat, caused by stroke, neurological disease, head injury, or weakness of the mouth and throat muscles
    \item \textbf{Oesophageal dysphagia:} difficulty moving food down the oesophagus, caused by narrowing, spasm, or motility disorders
    \item \textbf{Structural causes:} strictures, rings, tumours, or an enlarged thyroid compressing the oesophagus
    \item \textbf{Motility disorders:} achalasia, in which the oesophagus fails to relax, and other oesophageal motility problems
    \item \textbf{Neurological causes:} stroke, Parkinson's disease, motor neurone disease, multiple sclerosis, and dementia
    \item \textbf{Medications:} dry mouth from medicines can worsen swallowing, and certain medicines can cause oesophageal injury
    \item \textbf{Ageing:} swallowing function declines with age, increasing the risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms depend on the level and cause of the swallowing difficulty.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Coughing or choking during or after eating or drinking
    \item A sensation of food sticking in the throat or chest
    \item Difficulty chewing or starting a swallow
    \item Food or drink coming back up, or nasal regurgitation
    \item A wet, gurgly voice after swallowing
    \item Drooling, pooling of food in the mouth, or pocketing food in the cheek
    \item Weight loss, poor appetite, and dehydration
    \item Recurrent chest infections or pneumonia
    \item Pain on swallowing in some conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Dysphagia must be distinguished from other symptoms that may be confused with it.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Globus sensation, the persistent feeling of a lump in the throat with normal swallowing
    \item Heartburn and reflux, which can cause a similar sensation
    \item Odynophagia, or pain on swallowing, which may occur with or without difficulty
    \item Anxiety, which can cause throat symptoms
    \item Hoarseness and voice problems, which are separate but often coexist
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with new, persistent, or progressive difficulty swallowing should see a doctor promptly. Sudden difficulty swallowing requires urgent assessment, and progressive difficulty with weight loss requires investigation to exclude serious causes such as cancer.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden complete inability to swallow or severe choking
    \item Difficulty breathing
    \item Signs of stroke, including sudden weakness, facial drooping, or slurred speech
    \item Coughing up food or blood
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The investigations chosen depend on whether the problem is in the throat or the oesophagus.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} assessment of the onset, pattern, and associated symptoms
    \item \textbf{Speech pathology assessment:} observation of eating and drinking to identify oropharyngeal problems
    \item \textbf{Videofluoroscopy:} a moving X-ray of swallowing, using barium-coated food and drink
    \item \textbf{Fibre-optic endoscopic evaluation:} a thin camera passed through the nose to watch swallowing
    \item \textbf{Endoscopy:} examination of the oesophagus to identify strictures, tumours, or inflammation
    \item \textbf{Oesophageal manometry:} measurement of the pressures and coordination of oesophageal contractions
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT scans for suspected tumours or other structural causes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management depends on the cause and severity and involves a team of specialists.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diet modifications:} thickened fluids, softer foods, and specific textures that are easier to swallow
    \item \textbf{Swallowing therapy:} exercises and techniques taught by a speech pathologist to strengthen and coordinate swallowing
    \item \textbf{Safe swallowing strategies:} positioning, smaller mouthfuls, and timing techniques
    \item \textbf{Treatment of the cause:} dilatation of strictures, treatment of reflux, medication for neurological disease, or surgery for specific conditions
    \item \textbf{Feeding support:} enteral feeding through a nasogastric tube or gastrostomy when oral intake is unsafe or inadequate
    \item \textbf{Dental care:} good oral hygiene to reduce the risk of aspiration pneumonia
    \item \textbf{Nutritional support:} dietitian input to maintain weight and hydration
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Aspiration pneumonia from food or fluid entering the lungs
    \item Choking episodes
    \item Malnutrition, dehydration, and weight loss
    \item Reduced quality of life and loss of the social pleasure of eating
    \item Depression and social isolation
    \item Increased dependence on carers
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for dysphagia depends on the cause. Dysphagia after stroke often improves substantially in the early months with therapy. In progressive neurological conditions, swallowing declines over time and feeding support becomes necessary, but good planning allows comfort and safety. Structural causes such as strictures are often successfully treated. Early assessment by a speech pathologist improves outcomes and reduces complications.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sit upright while eating and remain upright for a period afterwards
    \item Take small mouthfuls and chew thoroughly
    \item Eat in a calm environment without distraction
    \item Follow the food and fluid textures recommended by the speech pathologist
    \item Maintain good mouth care after meals
    \item Report any coughing, choking, or weight loss promptly
    \item See a doctor early for any new swallowing difficulty
\end{itemize}
