\section*{Definition and Overview}
The visual inspection of urine, historically termed uroscopy, represents one of the oldest diagnostic modalities in the history of medicine. In the modern clinical framework, a urine colour chart infographic serves as an essential, user-friendly tool that translates complex physiological and biochemical states into accessible visual data. The baseline colour of human urine is primarily determined by the concentration of urochrome (also known as urobilin), a yellow pigment generated from the natural breakdown of hemoglobin during the phagocytosis of senescent erythrocytes. Under normal physiological conditions, urine colour ranges from pale, straw-like yellow to deep amber, reflecting the hydration status of the individual. However, deviations from this spectrum---resulting in red, pink, orange, green, blue, brown, or black urine---can serve as critical clinical indicators of systemic disease, toxic exposures, pharmacological side effects, or dietary variations.

The clinical significance of urine colour monitoring lies in its capacity to provide immediate, non-invasive insights into renal function, metabolic pathways, and hepatobiliary integrity. For instance, the concentration of urine is a direct reflection of the renal tubule's capacity to concentrate or dilute urine under the influence of antidiuretic hormone (ADH), which is modulated by intravascular volume. When an individual is dehydrated, the kidneys conserve water, resulting in highly concentrated urine rich in urochrome, presenting as a dark yellow or amber colour. Conversely, overhydration suppresses ADH secretion, leading to the excretion of large volumes of dilute, near-colourless urine. Beyond simple hydration monitoring, abnormal urine colours (chromaturia) are frequently the initial presenting symptom of underlying pathologies. Hematuria (the presence of blood in the urine) can stain urine pink or red and may indicate malignant neoplasms of the urological tract, nephrolithiasis, or severe glomerular disease. Bilirubinuria, which imparts a dark brown or ``cola'' colour, is a hallmark of obstructive biliary disease or acute hepatocellular injury. Given this wide range of diagnostic implications, the urine colour chart infographic is not merely a novelty for personal health monitoring but is a clinically validated tool utilized in sports science, occupational health, military operations, and primary care settings to screen for dehydration and detect early signs of severe pathological processes.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of urine colour variation is multifaceted, as it encompasses both benign physiological fluctuations (such as hydration status and dietary habits) and pathological conditions. Hydration status, the most common driver of urine colour changes, varies widely across global demographics. Public health surveys indicate that inadequate daily water intake is highly prevalent, with some clinical studies estimating that up to 50\% to 60\% of children and adolescents, and approximately 40\% of adults in developed countries, do not meet recommended daily hydration guidelines. This leads to a high baseline prevalence of concentrated, dark yellow to amber urine in the general population, particularly among manual labourers, agricultural workers, military personnel, and athletes operating in arid or high-temperature environments.

Pathological causes of abnormal urine colour, such as hematuria, have distinct epidemiological profiles. The prevalence of microscopic hematuria in the general population ranges from 2.5\% to 20.0\%, depending on the specific age cohort, screening methods, and diagnostic thresholds. Macroscopic or gross hematuria (which visibly colors the urine pink, red, or tea-coloured) is less common but carries a significantly higher clinical risk; approximately 10\% to 20\% of adult patients presenting with gross hematuria are subsequently diagnosed with a urological malignancy, such as transitional cell carcinoma of the bladder or renal cell carcinoma. The incidence of hematuria increases markedly with age, particularly in males over the age of 50, where it is frequently associated with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) or prostate cancer. In contrast, in younger female populations, red or pink urine is most commonly secondary to acute uncomplicated cystitis, which has an annual incidence exceeding 10\% in adult women.

Other pigmenturias have rarer but well-defined epidemiological patterns. Bilirubinuria, manifesting as dark, tea-coloured urine, is closely tied to the epidemiology of hepatobiliary disease. In developed nations, this is frequently secondary to cholelithiasis (gallstones), which affects approximately 10\% to 15\% of the adult population, or alcoholic and non-alcoholic fatty liver diseases. In developing regions, viral hepatitis (specifically Hepatitis A, B, and C) remains a major etiology of hepatocellular jaundice and bilirubinuria. Extremely rare conditions, such as alkaptonuria (an autosomal recessive metabolic disorder presenting with black urine), have a global prevalence estimated at 1 in 250,000 to 1 in 1,000,000 live births, though geographical clusters exist in countries like Slovakia and the Dominican Republic. Similarly, blue or green urine caused by genetic disorders like familial benign hypercalcemia (blue diaper syndrome) is confined to case reports, whereas iatrogenic green urine secondary to intravenous infusions of propofol or amitriptyline occurs in a small percentage of patients undergoing intensive care or general anesthesia.

\section{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The etiologies underlying urine colour variations are diverse, spanning physiological, pathological, pharmacological, and dietary domains. The pathophysiological mechanisms are characterized by the renal clearance of endogenous or exogenous pigments. Below is a detailed breakdown of these causes and their underlying pathways:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Urochrome Concentration (Pale Yellow to Dark Amber)}: Under normal physiological regulation, the posterior pituitary gland secretes antidiuretic hormone (ADH, or vasopressin) in response to increased plasma osmolality or decreased blood volume. ADH binds to V2 receptors on the basolateral membrane of the renal collecting duct cells, initiating a cAMP-mediated signaling cascade that promotes the insertion of aquaporin-2 water channels into the apical membrane. This facilitates the reabsorption of water back into the renal interstitium, concentrating the non-reabsorbable solute urochrome (urobilin), which is derived from the breakdown of bilirubin. In states of dehydration, this mechanism is highly active, producing dark, concentrated amber urine. When free water intake is abundant, plasma osmolality falls, ADH secretion is suppressed, and water is excreted, resulting in dilute, clear urine.

  \item \textbf{Gross Hematuria (Pink to Bright Red)}: The presence of intact erythrocytes in the urine causes a pink, red, or smoky brown appearance. The pathophysiology depends on the site of entry into the urinary tract. Glomerular hematuria involves disruption of the glomerular filtration barrier (consisting of fenestrated endothelial cells, the glomerular basement membrane, and podocyte foot processes) due to inflammatory or immunologic processes (e.g., IgA nephropathy, post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis). This allows red blood cells to squeeze into Bowman's space, often resulting in dysmorphic RBCs and RBC casts. Post-glomerular hematuria occurs when mucosal integrity is breached anywhere from the renal pelvis to the urethra, commonly caused by mechanical trauma from nephrolithiasis, infectious ulceration in cystitis, or neoangiogenesis and friable blood vessel rupture within urothelial or renal cell carcinomas.

  \item \textbf{Pigmenturia: Hemoglobinuria and Myoglobinuria (Red-Brown to Cola-Coloured)}: Free hemoglobin and myoglobin are potent pigment compounds that can filter through the glomerulus. Hemoglobinuria occurs during severe intravascular hemolysis (e.g., glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency crises, mismatched blood transfusions, or mechanical trauma from prosthetic heart valves). When the binding capacity of plasma haptoglobin is saturated, free hemoglobin dimers pass through the glomerular filter. Myoglobinuria arises from rhabdomyolysis, where skeletal muscle necrosis (triggered by crush injuries, prolonged immobilization, statin toxicity, or extreme physical exertion) releases large quantities of intracellular myoglobin into the circulation. Both pigments contain heme groups that can damage renal tubular cells through direct cytotoxicity, lipid peroxidation, and cast obstruction, leading to acute tubular necrosis (ATN).

  \item \textbf{Bilirubinuria (Dark Brown or Tea-Coloured)}: Under normal conditions, senescent red blood cells are destroyed in the reticuloendothelial system, releasing heme, which is metabolized into unconjugated bilirubin. Unconjugated bilirubin is hydrophobic, bound to albumin, and cannot pass through the glomerular membrane. In the liver, it is conjugated with glucuronic acid by the enzyme uridine diphosphate-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT), rendering it water-soluble. Conjugated bilirubin is normally excreted into the bile ducts and gastrointestinal tract. However, in obstructive jaundice (e.g., cholelithiasis, pancreatic head tumors) or hepatocellular injury (e.g., acute viral or drug-induced hepatitis), conjugated bilirubin regurgitates into the systemic circulation. Because it is water-soluble, it is filtered by the kidneys, imparting a dark brown, tea, or cola colour to the urine.

  \item \textbf{Dietary Pigments (Red, Pink, or Orange)}: Consumption of specific foods can temporarily alter urine colour. Ingestion of beets contains anthocyanins, which can cause pink or red urine (beeturia) in approximately 10\% to 14\% of the population. This occurs when stomach acid is insufficient to decolorize the pigment, or in individuals with altered gut transit times, leading to absorption in the colon and subsequent renal excretion. Similarly, large quantities of blackberries, rhubarb, or carrots (containing beta-carotene) can stain urine red, orange, or deep yellow, respectively.

  \item \textbf{Pharmacological and Chemical Agents (Various Colours)}: Exogenous drugs and their metabolites are frequently cleared via the kidneys, altering urine colour. Rifampicin, a lipophilic antibiotic used in tuberculosis treatment, is metabolized into an orange-red compound that colours all bodily fluids, including urine, sweat, and tears. Phenazopyridine, a urinary tract analgesic, is excreted as an azo dye, causing a bright, fluorescent orange-red discoloration. Amitriptyline (an antidepressant), propofol (an anesthetic), and methylene blue (used to treat methemoglobinemia) can impart a blue-green hue to the urine. Metronidazole (an antimicrobial) and senna (a stimulant laxative) can cause urine to turn dark brown or reddish-brown.

  \item \textbf{Infectious Pathogens (Green, Purple, or Milky)}: Specific bacterial infections alter urine appearance through pigment production or biochemical reactions. \textit{Pseudomonas aeruginosa} infections can produce pyocyanin (a blue-green pigment) and pyoverdine (a yellow-green fluorescent pigment), which combine to colour the urine green. Purple urine bag syndrome (PUBS) occurs in catheterized patients, where dietary tryptophan is metabolized by gut bacteria to indole, absorbed, conjugated in the liver to indoxyl sulfate, and excreted in urine. In the presence of alkaline urine, bacterial enzymes (such as sulfatases and phosphatases produced by \textit{Providencia}, \textit{Klebsiella}, or \textit{Proteus} species) convert indoxyl sulfate into indigo (blue) and indirubin (red), which react with the plastic catheter bag to produce a striking purple colour.

  \item \textbf{Metabolic and Genetic Anomalies (Black or Blue)}: Alkaptonuria is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the HGD gene, leading to a deficiency of homogentisate 1,2-dioxygenase. This blocks the tyrosine degradation pathway, causing an accumulation of homogentisic acid (HGA). HGA is rapidly cleared by the kidneys; upon voiding and exposure to atmospheric oxygen, HGA undergoes oxidation and polymerization, turning the urine black. Familial benign hypercalcemia (blue diaper syndrome) is a rare metabolic defect in intestinal tryptophan transport, leading to bacterial conversion of tryptophan to indican, which is cleared by the kidneys and oxidizes to indigo blue upon exposure to air, staining diapers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of urine colour alterations ranges from completely asymptomatic, benign discoloration to severe systemic symptomatology indicating acute, life-threatening pathologies. The characterization of clinical features requires evaluating the specific colour variant alongside associated urinary, constitutional, and systemic signs:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Colour Characterization and Semiology}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Straw Yellow to Pale Yellow}: Indicates optimal hydration. The patient is typically asymptomatic with stable vital signs.
    \item \textbf{Dark Amber to Copper}: Suggestive of mild-to-severe dehydration. May be accompanied by clinical signs of hypovolemia, such as dry mucous membranes, decreased skin turgor, orthostatic hypotension, tachycardia, oliguria, and systemic fatigue.
    \item \textbf{Fluorescent Orange or Red-Orange}: Frequently associated with medication ingestion (e.g., phenazopyridine or rifampicin). The urine is typically clear without sediment, and the patient lacks associated dysuria or flank pain unless the underlying disease (e.g., UTI or tuberculosis) is symptomatic.
    \item \textbf{Pink, Bright Red, or Clotted (Gross Hematuria)}: Visual detection of blood. It is clinically divided into painful and painless hematuria. Painful gross hematuria is typically associated with passage of clots, nephrolithiasis, or acute bacterial cystitis. Painless gross hematuria is a classic presentation of bladder or renal malignancies, especially in older adults, and must always be treated as a malignant process until proven otherwise.
    \item \textbf{Tea-Coloured, Cola-Coloured, or Dark Brown}: Suggestive of bilirubinuria, myoglobinuria, or hemoglobinuria. Bilirubinuria is characterized by urine that produces a yellow foam when shaken. Myoglobinuria is often associated with severe muscle tenderness, swelling, and systemic weakness.
    \item \textbf{Blue, Green, or Teal}: Often iatrogenic or secondary to \textit{Pseudomonas} infection. If infectious, it is usually accompanied by cloudy urine, a foul or sweetish odour, and systemic signs of infection.
    \item \textbf{Purple (Purple Urine Bag Syndrome)}: Exclusively observed in catheterized, often chronically ill or institutionalized patients. The urine inside the drainage bag and tubing turns purple, which can cause significant caregiver distress, although the patient may remain minimally symptomatic.
    \item \textbf{Milky or Cloudy (Chyluria or Pyuria)}: Indicates the presence of lipids/lymph (chyluria, due to lymphatic-urinary fistulas often caused by filariasis) or pus/white blood cells (pyuria, due to severe urinary tract infection).
  \end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Associated Urinary Symptoms}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dysuria, Urgency, and Frequency}: Strongly indicate localized inflammation of the bladder trigone or urethra, most commonly due to a bacterial urinary tract infection or chemical irritation.
    \item \textbf{Renal Colic}: Severe, acute, unilateral flank pain radiating to the groin or labia/scrotum. It is typically paroxysmal and associated with macroscopic or microscopic hematuria due to ureteral obstruction by a calculus.
    \item \textbf{Obstructive Voiding Symptoms}: Hesitancy, straining, weak stream, post-void dribbling, and sensation of incomplete bladder emptying, commonly associated with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) or urethral strictures presenting with hematuria.
  \end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Constitutional and Systemic Signs}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fever, Rigors, and Chills}: Suggestive of upper urinary tract involvement (pyelonephritis) or systemic urosepsis.
    \item \textbf{Jaundice and Scleral Icterus}: Yellowing of the skin and conjunctival membranes, indicating hyperbilirubinemia. Often accompanied by pruritus (itching) due to bile salt deposition in the skin and pale, acholic (clay-coloured) stools.
    \item \textbf{Myalgia, Weakness, and Muscle Swelling}: Classic features of rhabdomyolysis, which can lead to pigment-induced acute kidney injury.
    \item \textbf{Unintentional Weight Loss, Cachexia, and Night Sweats}: Red flag signs pointing towards renal, urothelial, or systemic malignancies.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When evaluating a patient with abnormal urine colour, clinicians must systematically differentiate between benign dietary/pharmacological causes and pathological conditions. The differential diagnosis includes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Gross Hematuria vs. Pseudohematuria}: Gross hematuria is defined by the presence of red blood cells in the urine ($>3$ RBCs per high-power field on microscopy). Pseudohematuria represents discoloration of the urine without intact RBCs, which can be caused by food dyes (beets, blackberries), medications (rifampicin, phenazopyridine), or endogenous pigments (free hemoglobin, myoglobin, porphyrins).

  \item \textbf{Myoglobinuria vs. Hemoglobinuria}: Both cause red-brown or cola-coloured urine and yield a positive result for blood on a standard urine dipstick (due to the pseudoperoxidase activity of the heme group). Under microscopy, however, both lack intact RBCs. They are differentiated by clinical history (trauma or extreme exercise for myoglobinuria; transfusion history or anemia for hemoglobinuria), serum creatine kinase (dramatically elevated in myoglobinuria), plasma haptoglobin (profoundly depressed in hemolytic anemia/hemoglobinuria), and visual inspection of plasma (pink in hemoglobinuria, clear in myoglobinuria).

  \item \textbf{Obstructive vs. Hepatocellular Jaundice (Bilirubinuria)}: Bilirubinuria indicates conjugated hyperbilirubinemia. Obstructive jaundice (extrahepatic biliary blockage due to gallstones, strictures, or pancreatic cancer) is characterized by elevated alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), and dilated bile ducts on ultrasound. Hepatocellular jaundice (hepatitis, toxins) shows marked elevations in alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) with normal biliary duct caliber.

  \item \textbf{Alkaptonuria vs. Acute Intermittent Porphyria}: Both conditions present with urine that darkens upon standing or exposure to environmental factors. In alkaptonuria, the urine turns dark brown or black due to the oxidation of homogentisic acid, particularly in alkaline environments. In acute intermittent porphyria, the urine turns a deep red, purple, or ``port-wine'' colour when exposed to light, due to the photo-oxidation of porphobilinogen to porphobilin.

  \item \textbf{Pseudomonas Urinary Tract Infection vs. Methylene Blue Ingestion}: Both can cause blue-green urine. A \textit{Pseudomonas} infection is diagnosed via positive urine culture, pyuria, and clinical symptoms of infection (fever, dysuria). In contrast, methylene blue ingestion or administration (often during surgical procedures or for the treatment of methemoglobinemia) is confirmed by medication history and is not associated with inflammatory urinary markers.

  \item \textbf{Severe Dehydration vs. Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)}: Highly concentrated, amber urine indicates that the renal tubules are functional and actively conserving water in response to dehydration. In patients with advanced CKD, the kidneys lose their concentrating ability, resulting in a fixed urine specific gravity of approximately $1.010$ (isosthenuria) regardless of the patient's hydration status. Thus, a dehydrated CKD patient may present with pale, dilute-looking urine despite severe volume depletion.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
While minor changes in urine colour are frequently benign and resolve with increased fluid intake or the cessation of certain foods and medications, persistent or distinct colour changes can be the first sign of a serious medical condition. Immediate medical evaluation is required under the following circumstances:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Painless Gross Hematuria}: Any sudden appearance of pink, red, or cola-coloured urine that is not accompanied by pain during urination requires prompt, comprehensive urological evaluation. In adults, this is a primary sign of bladder, ureteral, or kidney cancer, and must never be ignored.
  \item \textbf{Jaundice with Tea-Coloured Urine}: If dark, tea- or cola-coloured urine is accompanied by yellowing of the skin or the whites of the eyes (sclera), pale or clay-coloured stools, and persistent itching, it indicates severe hepatobiliary dysfunction or biliary obstruction. The patient should seek prompt medical evaluation to prevent acute liver injury or ascending cholangitis.
  \item \textbf{Severe Unilateral Flank Pain}: If red or pink urine is accompanied by sudden, severe, cramping pain in the back, side, or lower abdomen that radiates to the groin, it is highly suggestive of acute ureteral obstruction by a kidney stone (nephrolithiasis).
  \item \textbf{Suspected Rhabdomyolysis}: If dark brown or cola-coloured urine appears after a fall, traumatic muscle injury, prolonged immobilization, or extreme physical exertion, and is accompanied by severe muscle pain, swelling, and profound weakness, immediate medical attention is required to prevent pigment nephropathy and acute renal failure.
  \item \textbf{Signs of Systemic Infection (Urosepsis)}: If abnormal urine colour (such as cloudy, foul-smelling, or green urine) is accompanied by a high fever ($>38.5^\circ$C), shaking chills, rapid heart rate (tachycardia), rapid breathing (tachypnea), confusion, or severe dizziness (hypotension), the patient must be transported immediately to an emergency department.
\end{itemize}

For life-threatening symptoms, such as an inability to urinate accompanied by severe abdominal pain, signs of septic shock, or altered consciousness, call emergency services immediately:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{local emergency services} in many countries
  \item \textbf{911} in the United States and Canada
  \item \textbf{112} in the European Union and India
  \item \textbf{999} in the United Kingdom
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnostic workup of a patient presenting with abnormal urine colour is systematic, beginning with a detailed history and physical examination, followed by laboratory, imaging, and endoscopic evaluations:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Clinical History and Physical Examination}: The clinician must document all current medications, dietary intake (beets, berries, food dyes), history of vigorous exercise, occupational heat exposure, prior urological conditions, and systemic symptoms. Physical examination focuses on assessing hydration status (mucous membranes, skin turgor), examining for scleral icterus or jaundice, palpating the abdomen for hepatomegaly or masses, and checking for costovertebral angle tenderness (CVA tenderness) and suprapubic pain.

  \item \textbf{Urinalysis and Microscopy}: The cornerstone of investigation.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dipstick Analysis}: Evaluates chemical parameters. A positive blood/heme test with the absence of red blood cells under microscopy indicates free hemoglobinuria or myoglobinuria. The presence of leukocyte esterase and nitrites suggests a bacterial infection. Bilirubin on the dipstick indicates conjugated hyperbilirubinemia, while elevated urobilinogen can indicate hemolytic disorders.
    \item \textbf{Specific Gravity (SG)}: Reflects urine concentration. An SG $>1.025$ suggests dehydration, whereas an SG fixed at approximately $1.010$ indicates a loss of renal concentrating ability (isosthenuria).
    \item \textbf{Microscopic Examination}: Essential to confirm true hematuria ($>3$ RBCs per high-power field). The presence of dysmorphic RBCs or red blood cell casts indicates a glomerular source of bleeding (e.g., glomerulonephritis). White blood cells (pyuria) and bacteria confirm infection.
  \end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Serum Chemistry and Hematology}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Renal Function Panel}: Serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and electrolytes are evaluated to rule out acute kidney injury (AKI) or chronic kidney disease (CKD).
    \item \textbf{Liver Function Tests (LFTs)}: Total and direct bilirubin, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) are measured to localize hepatobiliary pathology.
    \item \textbf{Muscle Breakdown Markers}: Serum creatine kinase (CK) levels are measured if myoglobinuria or rhabdomyolysis is suspected; a CK level five times the upper limit of normal confirms the diagnosis.
    \item \textbf{Complete Blood Count (CBC)}: Evaluates for leukocytosis (infection) and anemia (which may result from chronic hematuria or hemolysis).
    \item \textbf{Hemolytic Profile}: If hemoglobinuria is suspected, tests include serum haptoglobin (decreased), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH, elevated), and a peripheral blood smear to look for schistocytes.
  \end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Microbiological Studies}: A urine culture and sensitivity test must be performed if a urinary tract infection is suspected, particularly to identify organisms like \textit{Pseudomonas aeruginosa} or \textit{Proteus} species in cases of green or purple urine.

  \item \textbf{Imaging Modalities}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Renal and Urinary Tract Ultrasonography}: A non-invasive first-line test to screen for hydronephrosis, nephrolithiasis, BPH, and large renal or bladder masses.
    \item \textbf{Abdominal and Pelvic CT Urography (CTU)}: The gold standard imaging technique for evaluating hematuria. It provides high-resolution, contrast-enhanced visualization of the renal parenchyma, collecting systems, and ureters, allowing for the detection of small urothelial tumors and renal calculi.
    \item \textbf{Hepatobiliary Imaging}: Right upper quadrant ultrasound, magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP), or CT of the abdomen is indicated if bilirubinuria points to biliary tract disease or hepatic injury.
  \end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Cystoscopy}: Direct endoscopic visualization of the urethra and bladder mucosa. This is indicated for any patient over the age of 35 presenting with unexplained hematuria, or younger patients with risk factors for urothelial malignancy (e.g., smoking history, occupational exposures to aromatic amines), as it is the most sensitive method for detecting small bladder tumors.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management strategies for abnormal urine colour are directed entirely at treating the underlying cause, ranging from simple hydration adjustments to complex surgical interventions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Fluid Resuscitation and Hydration Support}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dehydration}: For patients with dark yellow or amber urine caused by volume depletion, management involves increasing fluid intake. Mild-to-moderate dehydration is treated with oral rehydration solutions. Severe dehydration, especially when accompanied by hemodynamically unstable vital signs or persistent vomiting, requires intravenous administration of isotonic crystalloids (e.g., normal saline or lactated Ringer's).
    \item \textbf{Rhabdomyolysis}: Requires aggressive intravenous hydration (typically $200$ to $300$ mL/hour) to maintain high urine output ($>200$ mL/hour). This dilutes intratubular myoglobin, prevents cast formation, and minimizes renal tubular injury. In some protocols, sodium bicarbonate is added to the intravenous fluids to alkalinize the urine (pH $>6.5$), which increases the solubility of myoglobin and prevents its precipitation in the renal tubules.
  \end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Pharmacotherapy}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antibiotic Therapy}: Indicated for urinary tract infections. Uncomplicated cystitis is treated with short courses of nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, or fosfomycin. Green urine caused by \textit{Pseudomonas aeruginosa} requires targeted antipseudomonal agents such as ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, ceftazidime, or piperacillin-tazobactam, adjusted based on culture susceptibility.
    \item \textbf{Immunosuppressive and Corticosteroid Therapy}: Utilized in cases of glomerular hematuria secondary to autoimmune glomerulonephritis (e.g., lupus nephritis or IgA nephropathy) to reduce renal inflammation and halt disease progression.
  \end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Surgical and Interventional Procedures}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urolithiasis}: Nephrolithiasis causing hematuria and obstruction may require shockwave lithotripsy (ESWL), ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy, or percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) to fragment and remove obstructing stones.
    \item \textbf{Urological Malignancies}: For bladder tumors detected during investigation of painless gross hematuria, transurethral resection of the bladder tumor (TURBT) is performed for both diagnosis and initial treatment, followed by intravesical therapy or cystectomy depending on the stage. Renal cell carcinoma is managed with partial or radical nephrectomy.
    \item \textbf{Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia}: Hematuria secondary to BPH is managed medically with alpha-blockers (e.g., tamsulosin) and 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors (e.g., finasteride), or surgically via transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) or laser enucleation.
    \item \textbf{Biliary Obstruction}: Obstructive jaundice causing bilirubinuria is managed via Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) with sphincterotomy and biliary stent placement to restore bile flow, or via cholecystectomy for symptomatic gallstones.
  \end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Discontinuation of Offending Agents}: If a medication is identified as the source of abnormal urine colour (e.g., rifampicin, phenazopyridine, amitriptyline), the clinician must evaluate the necessity of the drug. If the discoloration is a benign side effect (such as orange urine from rifampicin during tuberculosis treatment), the patient is reassured and advised to continue the medication. If the discoloration is a sign of drug-induced toxicity (e.g., statin-induced rhabdomyolysis), the offending agent must be discontinued immediately.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The complications associated with urine colour changes are primarily linked to the underlying pathologies that produce abnormal pigments. Untreated or delayed management can lead to significant morbidity and mortality:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)}: A major complication of pigmenturia. Intratubular precipitation of myoglobin or free hemoglobin, combined with renal vasoconstriction and direct heme-induced tubular cytotoxicity, causes pigment nephropathy (acute tubular necrosis). AKI can also result from severe, untreated dehydration or bilateral urinary tract obstruction (e.g., large bladder tumors or bilateral ureteral calculi).

  \item \textbf{Hemorrhagic Complications}: Severe, ongoing hematuria can lead to significant blood loss, presenting as acute anemia, orthostatic instability, or hemorrhagic shock in extreme cases. Additionally, blood clots within the bladder can lead to acute urinary retention, causing severe pain and bladder distension requiring urgent catheterization.

  \item \textbf{Urosepsis and Septic Shock}: Untreated lower urinary tract infections or pyelonephritis (which may present with cloudy, green, or purple urine) can progress to systemic bacteremia. Urosepsis is characterized by severe systemic inflammation, hypotension, multi-organ failure, and a high mortality rate if antibiotic therapy is delayed.

  \item \textbf{Progression of Undiagnosed Malignancies}: Attributing painless hematuria to a benign cause (such as a UTI or dietary factor) without proper diagnostic evaluation can lead to delayed diagnosis of bladder or renal cancers. This delay allows the tumor to progress from muscle-invasive or localized stages to advanced, metastatic disease, significantly reducing the chances of curative treatment.

  \item \textbf{Hepatic Encephalopathy and Liver Failure}: In cases where tea-coloured urine is caused by progressive biliary obstruction or acute hepatocellular failure, accumulation of toxic metabolites (such as ammonia) in the circulation can cross the blood-brain barrier, leading to cognitive impairment, asterixis, coma, and death.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals experiencing changes in urine colour is highly variable and depends entirely on the specific underlying etiology, the patient's comorbidities, and the speed of clinical intervention:

For physiological dehydration, the prognosis is excellent. Rehydration with oral or intravenous fluids yields rapid normalization of urine colour and specific gravity within hours, with no long-term sequelae. Similarly, dietary chromaturia (beets, blackberries) and medication-induced colour changes (rifampicin, phenazopyridine) are completely benign and self-limiting, resolving within 24 to 48 hours after the substance is cleared from the body.

For infectious causes, such as bacterial cystitis or \textit{Pseudomonas} infections, the prognosis is highly favorable, with resolution of symptoms and abnormal urine colour within days of initiating appropriate antimicrobial therapy. However, the prognosis for patients with purple urine bag syndrome is often dictated by the patient's overall clinical state, as it frequently occurs in frail, elderly, or institutionalized patients with chronic urinary catheterization and multiple comorbidities.

For pathological causes like gross hematuria, the prognosis depends on the underlying diagnosis. If the bleeding is caused by nephrolithiasis or BPH, the prognosis is excellent with standard medical or surgical therapies. However, if gross hematuria is the presenting sign of a urological malignancy, the prognosis is directly correlated with the stage and grade of the tumor at the time of diagnosis. Localized bladder or renal cancers have a high 5-year survival rate ($>70\%$ to $90\%$ with radical resection), whereas metastatic urothelial carcinoma carries a poor prognosis, with 5-year survival rates dropping below $15\%$.

In cases of rhabdomyolysis and myoglobinuria, the prognosis is favorable if aggressive intravenous hydration is initiated early. Up to 80\% of patients recover full renal function without requiring temporary renal replacement therapy (hemodialysis), provided they receive prompt treatment before severe acute tubular necrosis occurs. If treatment is delayed and oliguric AKI develops, the recovery period can span weeks to months, and some patients may develop chronic kidney disease.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventative strategies and self-care measures are essential to maintain urinary tract health, optimize hydration, and facilitate early detection of serious medical conditions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Fluid Intake Optimization}: The primary preventative measure for concentrated urine and dehydration-related health issues (such as nephrolithiasis). Individuals should aim to consume approximately 2.0 to 3.0 liters of fluid daily, primarily water. Fluid intake must be scaled upward in response to high ambient temperatures, physical exercise, and occupational heat stress to compensate for sweat loss.

  \item \textbf{Utilization of Urine Colour Charts}: Individuals should use a urine colour chart infographic as a daily self-monitoring tool. The target colour for optimal hydration is pale straw-yellow or clear. If the urine appears dark yellow or amber, it serves as an immediate visual cue to increase fluid consumption.

  \item \textbf{Gradual Physical Conditioning}: To prevent exertional rhabdomyolysis, individuals initiating new exercise regimens should gradually increase intensity. Avoid sudden, extreme muscular exertion, especially in hot and humid conditions, and ensure adequate hydration before, during, and after physical exercise.

  \item \textbf{Dietary and Medication Education}: Patients starting new medications (such as rifampicin, sulfasalazine, or phenazopyridine) should be educated by their healthcare providers that orange or reddish-brown urine is an expected, benign side effect. Keeping a food and medication log can help clarify if a change in urine colour is related to recent consumption of beets, blackberries, or specific drugs.

  \item \textbf{Catheter Care and Hygiene}: In patients with chronic indwelling urinary catheters, preventing catheter-associated urinary tract infections (which can lead to purple urine bag syndrome or pyuria) requires strict adherence to aseptic catheter care. This includes daily cleansing of the urethral meatus, maintaining a closed drainage system, keeping the drainage bag below the level of the bladder, and securing prompt removal of the catheter when it is no longer clinically indicated.

  \item \textbf{Avoidance of Bladder Irritants and Carcinogens}: Smoking is the single most significant risk factor for bladder cancer. Stopping smoking significantly reduces the long-term risk of developing urothelial malignancies that present with gross hematuria. Additionally, avoiding exposure to occupational chemicals (such as aniline dyes and aromatic amines) is essential.

  \item \textbf{Regular Medical Screenings}: Adults over the age of 50, particularly males, should undergo regular health checkups. Routine urinalysis during annual physical exams can detect microscopic hematuria or proteinuria early, allowing for timely diagnosis and management of renal disease or silent urological malignancies.
\end{itemize}
