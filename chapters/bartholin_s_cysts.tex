\chapter{Bartholin's Cysts}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A Bartholin's cyst is a fluid-filled swelling that forms near the opening of the vagina, at the back of the labia majora. The two Bartholin's glands, one on each side of the vaginal opening, produce fluid that helps lubricate the vagina. When the duct of one of these glands becomes blocked, fluid accumulates and forms a cyst. The cyst may become infected and form an abscess. Bartholin's cysts are benign and usually not serious, but they can be painful and may need treatment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bartholin's cysts are common and occur only in women, most often during the reproductive years, typically between ages 20 and 40. They are rare before puberty and after the menopause. Around 2 in 100 women will develop a Bartholin's cyst or abscess at some point in their lifetime. Abscesses are more common than simple cysts and often recur. Women with a history of sexually transmitted infections or previous cysts have a higher risk.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blocked duct:} the duct draining a Bartholin's gland becomes obstructed, and mucus-like fluid builds up behind the blockage
    \item \textbf{Infection:} bacteria, often from the normal vaginal and bowel flora, may infect the cyst and turn it into an abscess
    \item \textbf{Causative organisms:} \textit{Escherichia coli}, \textit{Staphylococcus}, \textit{Streptococcus}, and sometimes sexually transmitted bacteria such as \textit{Chlamydia} or \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae}
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} the blockage is often spontaneous and not clearly explained; injury to the area is an occasional trigger
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A painless, slow-growing lump on one side of the vaginal opening (typical of a simple cyst)
    \item Pain, tenderness, swelling, and redness that worsen with infection (abscess)
    \item Discomfort when walking, sitting, or during intercourse
    \item Difficulty or pain when urinating if the swelling is large
    \item Fever and feeling unwell may accompany a significant abscess
    \item Cysts range in size from a pea to several centimetres across
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inguinal hernia:} a bulge in the groin that may extend into the labia
    \item \textbf{Lipoma} or other benign soft-tissue lump
    \item \textbf{Sebaceous cyst} or epidermal inclusion cyst of the vulva
    \item \textbf{Vulval or vaginal cancer:} rare, but should be considered, especially in women over 40 with a hard or irregular mass
    \item \textbf{Varicosity} (varicose vein) of the vulva
    \item \textbf{Endometriosis deposit} or Gartner's duct cyst in the vaginal wall
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if you notice a lump near the vaginal opening, especially if it is painful, growing, or not resolving on its own within a few days.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The swelling is very painful, tense, or rapidly enlarging
    \item You develop a high fever, chills, or feel generally unwell
    \item You cannot pass urine because of the swelling
    \item The area becomes red and spreads, suggesting spreading infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} a pelvic examination is usually sufficient to make the diagnosis; the swelling is found at the expected position of a Bartholin's gland
    \item \textbf{Assessment of the mass:} the doctor checks whether the lump is tender, fluctuant (fluid-filled), or hard and irregular, which may need further investigation
    \item \textbf{Swab and culture:} a swab of pus taken at the time of drainage may be sent to identify bacteria and guide antibiotics
    \item \textbf{Sexually transmitted infection testing:} tests for chlamydia and gonorrhoea may be offered because these bacteria can be involved
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} rarely needed, but considered in older women or when a cyst is hard, non-tender, or persistent after treatment, to exclude cancer
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observation:} small, painless cysts may need no treatment and sometimes resolve on their own
    \item \textbf{Warm baths and analgesics:} sitting in a warm bath and simple pain relief may ease discomfort while the cyst settles
    \item \textbf{Drainage:} an infected cyst or abscess usually requires incision and drainage in a clinic or minor-operations setting
    \item \textbf{Catheter placement:} a small balloon catheter may be left in place for a few weeks after drainage to keep the duct open
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} used if there is spreading cellulitis, fever, or signs of a more severe infection
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} for recurrent cysts, marsupialisation (stitching the duct open) or removal of the whole gland may be offered
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} a review appointment to confirm healing and to check the mass has not recurred
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Recurrence of the cyst or abscess -- occurs in a significant proportion of cases, especially with simple drainage alone
    \item Abscess formation and spreading skin infection (cellulitis)
    \item Scarring or persistent sinus after repeated drainage
    \item Bleeding or bruising after surgery
    \item Rarely, infection spreading into the bloodstream (sepsis)
    \item Malignancy in the gland is very rare, but hard, persistent masses should always be investigated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most Bartholin's cysts and abscesses resolve well with drainage and simple measures, and pain typically settles quickly. Recurrence is common, particularly after simple incision and drainage, but marsupialisation reduces the chance of recurrence. Full recovery is expected in the vast majority of women, and the condition does not affect fertility or general health.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Practise good vulval hygiene without over-cleaning or using harsh soaps and douches
    \item Wear loose, breathable underwear and avoid tight-fitting clothing
    \item Use barrier protection (condoms) and consider regular screening for sexually transmitted infections
    \item Take warm sitz baths for comfort if a tender lump develops
    \item Avoid squeezing or trying to drain the cyst yourself
    \item See a doctor promptly for any new, painful, or enlarging vulval lump, especially after menopause
\end{itemize}
