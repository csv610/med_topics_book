\chapter{Vaginal infection}
\index{Vaginal infection}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A vaginal infection (vaginitis) is inflammation of the vagina that most commonly produces abnormal discharge, itching, soreness, or an unpleasant odour. The healthy vagina is not sterile; it is home to a balanced community of bacteria, the most important of which are \textit{Lactobacillus} species. These helpful bacteria produce lactic acid and hydrogen peroxide, keeping the vaginal environment mildly acidic (a pH of about 3.8 to 4.5), which discourages harmful organisms from multiplying. A vaginal infection develops when this balance is disturbed, allowing an organism such as a bacterium, fungus, or parasite to overgrow, or when a sexually transmitted infection (STI) is acquired.

The most common vaginal infections are bacterial vaginosis, vulvovaginal candidiasis (vaginal thrush), and trichomoniasis. Sexually transmitted infections such as chlamydia and gonorrhoea can also cause symptoms in the vagina and cervix. Most vaginal infections are easily treated and do not cause lasting harm, but some, especially when untreated, can spread into the pelvis, cause complications in pregnancy, or increase the risk of acquiring other infections. This chapter focuses on the recognition, diagnosis, and management of the common vaginal infections and the symptoms that should prompt a medical review.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Vaginal infections are extremely common and affect women of all ages, though they are most frequent during the reproductive years. Bacterial vaginosis is estimated to affect roughly 1 in 3 women at some point, and it is the most common cause of abnormal vaginal discharge in women of childbearing age. Vaginal thrush is diagnosed so often that many women recognise its symptoms themselves, with about 75 percent of women experiencing at least one episode in their lifetime and a substantial minority having recurrent attacks.

Trichomoniasis, caused by the parasite \textit{Trichomonas vaginalis}, is one of the most common curable STIs worldwide, with hundreds of millions of new cases each year, although it is much less commonly diagnosed in many countries than in some other regions. Chlamydia and gonorrhoea remain the most frequently notified bacterial STIs in many countries, with notification rates highest in people aged 15 to 29. Rates of vaginal infection are influenced by hormonal stage, sexual activity, antibiotic use, and hygiene practices. After the menopause, falling oestrogen levels thin the vaginal lining and raise the pH, making dryness, soreness, and atrophic changes more likely, while the overall risk of infective vaginitis tends to fall.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\subsection*{Bacterial vaginosis}
Bacterial vaginosis is not a single infection but an imbalance of the vaginal ecosystem. The numbers of protective \textit{Lactobacillus} bacteria fall, while other organisms, particularly \textit{Gardnerella vaginalis} and various anaerobes, multiply dramatically. The result is a thin, grey-white, fishy-smelling discharge. Factors that disturb the ecosystem include douching, use of scented intimate products, new or multiple sexual partners, and cigarette smoking. It is not classically considered an STI, but sexual activity influences it.

\subsection*{Vulvovaginal candidiasis (thrush)}
Thrush is caused by the yeast \textit{Candida albicans} in around 90 percent of cases, with other \textit{Candida} species causing the remainder. \textit{Candida} commonly lives harmlessly in the bowel and vagina, but it overgrows when the environment favours it, such as during antibiotic courses (which kill protective bacteria), in pregnancy, with uncontrolled diabetes, or when immune defences are impaired.

\subsection*{Trichomoniasis}
Trichomoniasis is a sexually transmitted infection caused by the single-celled parasite \textit{Trichomonas vaginalis}. It infects the vagina and urethra and is almost always passed on through sex. The parasite provokes a marked inflammatory response, producing a frothy, sometimes yellow-green, offensive-smelling discharge.

\subsection*{STIs of the cervix and vagina}
Chlamydia (\textit{Chlamydia trachomatis}) and gonorrhoea (\textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae}) are bacteria that infect the cervix, urethra, and other mucosal surfaces. They may produce discharge, bleeding, and pain, but are frequently silent. Untreated, they can ascend into the upper genital tract and cause pelvic inflammatory disease.

\subsection*{Other causes}
Non-infective irritation from perfumed soaps, bubble bath, lubricants, spermicides, and tight synthetic clothing can produce symptoms that mimic infection. Allergic or contact dermatitis of the vulva, low oestrogen states (atrophic vaginitis), and foreign bodies such as retained tampons can also cause discharge and irritation, and a retained tampon or condom carries a risk of the serious toxic shock syndrome.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\subsection*{Symptoms common to most vaginal infections}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Discharge:} a change in amount, colour, or consistency of vaginal discharge
    \item \textbf{Itching and soreness:} especially around the vulva (the external area)
    \item \textbf{Odour:} an unpleasant or fishy smell, which may worsen after sex
    \item \textbf{Irritation with sex or urination:} pain or burning during intercourse or passing urine
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Features that help distinguish the common types}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bacterial vaginosis:} thin, white or grey, uniform discharge with a fishy odour; usually little itching
    \item \textbf{Vaginal thrush:} thick, white, cottage-cheese-like discharge with intense itching, redness, and swelling of the vulva; a burning feeling is common
    \item \textbf{Trichomoniasis:} frothy, yellow-green, sometimes bubbly discharge with a strong smell, vulval soreness, and irritation of the urinary tract
    \item \textbf{Chlamydia and gonorrhoea:} often no symptoms; when present, a change in discharge, bleeding between periods or after sex, and lower abdominal discomfort
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Physiologic (normal) vaginal discharge, which varies with the menstrual cycle
    \item Atrophic vaginitis after the menopause
    \item Contact or allergic dermatitis of the vulva from soaps, perfumes, or synthetic underwear
    \item Vulval conditions such as lichen sclerosus or vulvodynia
    \item Urinary tract infection or urethritis
    \item Pelvic inflammatory disease when pain is prominent
    \item Foreign body such as a retained tampon or condom
    \item Cervical polyps, ectopy, or rarely malignancy when there is persistent abnormal bleeding or discharge
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor or sexual health clinic if you notice a new or unusual discharge, persistent itching or soreness, a strong odour, or pain with sex, or if symptoms do not settle with over-the-counter thrush treatment. It is also wise to seek review after unprotected sex with a new partner, as several infections cause no symptoms yet remain transmissible.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe lower abdominal or pelvic pain, especially with fever
    \item Heavy or prolonged vaginal bleeding
    \item Fainting, dizziness, or signs of a severe general illness
    \item Symptoms of toxic shock syndrome, including sudden high fever, a sunburn-like rash, confusion, and vomiting, which can complicate a retained tampon
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical history:} onset, timing, discharge character, odour, itching, pain, sexual history, contraceptive use, medicines, and pregnancy status
    \item \textbf{Vaginal examination:} inspection of the vulva and vagina to assess discharge and redness, always with informed consent
    \item \textbf{pH testing and microscopy:} a swab of discharge tested for acidity and examined under a microscope to identify bacteria, yeast, or the parasite \textit{Trichomonas vaginalis}
    \item \textbf{Whiff test:} adding a drop of alkali to a sample releases a fishy odour in bacterial vaginosis
    \item \textbf{Molecular tests (PCR):} highly sensitive tests for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and trichomoniasis, which can be performed on self-collected swabs or urine
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} not usually needed for common vaginal infections, but HIV and syphilis testing is offered when an STI is found or suspected
    \item \textbf{Referral:} a gynaecologist or sexual health physician may be involved for recurrent, persistent, or complicated infections
\end{itemize}

The accuracy of self-diagnosis is poor: studies show that many women who treat themselves for thrush actually have bacterial vaginosis or another condition, which is why a confirmed diagnosis matters, particularly for recurrent symptoms. A simple symptom-based approach is often sufficient in mild, typical first episodes, but a swab test is wise before starting treatment when symptoms are unusual, severe, or recurrent, or when there is any possibility of pregnancy.

\section*{Management}
\subsection*{Bacterial vaginosis}
Oral or vaginal metronidazole (or clindamycin cream) is the standard treatment and usually clears the infection within a week. Avoiding douching and scented products reduces recurrence. Partners do not require treatment, as bacterial vaginosis is not directly transmitted.

\subsection*{Vaginal thrush}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antifungal creams and pessaries:} clotrimazole and miconazole are effective for most mild cases and are available over the counter
    \item \textbf{Oral tablets:} fluconazole as a single dose is simple and effective
    \item \textbf{Recurrent thrush:} may need longer courses, maintenance therapy, or treatment of an underlying cause such as poorly controlled diabetes
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} only certain antifungals are safe in pregnancy, so medical advice should be sought
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Trichomoniasis, chlamydia, and gonorrhoea}
These require prescription treatment: metronidazole for trichomoniasis, and antibiotics such as doxycycline or azithromycin for chlamydia, while gonorrhoea is usually treated with an injection plus an oral antibiotic. Sexual partners in the previous few weeks must be tested and treated to prevent reinfection, and a test of cure is often arranged. Anyone diagnosed with an STI should also be offered screening for HIV and other infections.

\subsection*{General measures}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Avoid douching and intimate perfumed products
    \item Wear cotton underwear and avoid tight clothing during episodes
    \item Use barrier protection (condoms) to reduce the risk of sexually transmitted infection
    \item Complete the full course of prescribed treatment even if symptoms improve
    \item Return for review if symptoms persist after treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ascending infection leading to pelvic inflammatory disease, which can cause chronic pelvic pain and fallopian-tube damage
    \item Infertility and ectopic pregnancy risk after untreated chlamydia or gonorrhoea
    \item Pregnancy complications, including preterm birth, low birth weight, and infection of the baby during delivery
    \item Increased susceptibility to HIV and other STIs
    \item Toxic shock syndrome from a retained tampon or foreign body
    \item Recurrent symptoms and significant impact on quality of life and intimate relationships
\end{itemize}

Vaginal infections during pregnancy deserve particular attention. Bacterial vaginosis has been associated with an increased risk of preterm birth and late miscarriage, so symptomatic infection is treated during pregnancy. Chlamydia and gonorrhoea can infect the baby's eyes or lungs during delivery, and are screened for in early pregnancy and treated promptly. Thrush is more common and often more severe in pregnancy, and only certain antifungal medicines are considered safe, which is why pregnant women should not self-treat with the products they used before pregnancy.

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for most vaginal infections is excellent. Bacterial vaginosis and thrush usually respond quickly to treatment, though recurrences are common and some women need repeated or maintenance courses. Trichomoniasis, chlamydia, and gonorrhoea are cured reliably with the correct antibiotics, provided partners are treated to prevent reinfection. The main threat to long-term health comes from delayed treatment, which allows infection to spread to the upper genital tract. Prompt diagnosis, a completed course of treatment, and simple hygiene measures give the best chance of quick resolution and few complications.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use condoms consistently to reduce the risk of STIs
    \item Avoid douching and vaginal deodorants, which disturb the healthy balance of vaginal bacteria
    \item Wash the vulva with plain water or a very mild, unperfumed cleanser rather than harsh soap
    \item Wear cotton underwear and change out of wet swimwear or exercise clothing promptly
    \item Wipe from front to back after using the toilet
    \item Complete any antibiotic course as prescribed and seek advice about recurrent thrush
    \item Attend regular cervical screening and STI checks if sexually active, especially with new partners
\end{itemize}
