\chapter{Laparoscopic cholecystectomy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is a minimally invasive surgical procedure performed to remove the gallbladder, a small pear-shaped organ located beneath the liver that stores bile. It is the gold standard treatment for symptomatic gallstones (cholelithiasis) and associated biliary diseases. The procedure is performed under general anaesthesia using a laparoscope—a thin tube equipped with a light and camera—inserted through several small abdominal incisions (keyhole surgery). This approach has largely replaced open cholecystectomy due to its associated benefits, including reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, minimal scarring, and a faster return to normal activities.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Gallstone disease is highly prevalent in Western societies, affecting approximately 10\% to 15\% of the adult population. In Australia, cholecystectomy is one of the most frequently performed elective abdominal operations, with over 50,000 procedures completed annually across public and private healthcare sectors. The incidence of gallstones increases with age, peaking in the fifth and sixth decades of life. There is a distinct female predisposition, with women being two to three times more likely to develop gallstones than men, primarily driven by oestrogen-mediated increases in biliary cholesterol secretion. Other high-risk groups include individuals with a body mass index (BMI) greater than 30, pregnant women, patients undergoing rapid weight loss, and those with a family history of cholelithiasis.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The primary indication for a laparoscopic cholecystectomy is symptomatic gallbladder disease, which arises from physical obstructions or inflammatory processes within the biliary system.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cholelithiasis:} The formation of gallstones, which typically consist of cholesterol, bilirubin, and calcium salts. Biliary colic occurs when a gallstone temporarily obstructs the cystic duct, causing transient gallbladder distension and visceral pain.
    \item \textbf{Acute cholecystitis:} Prolonged obstruction of the cystic duct leads to chemical and bacterial inflammation of the gallbladder wall, causing persistent right upper quadrant pain, fever, and leukocytosis.
    \item \textbf{Choledocholithiasis:} Migration of gallstones into the common bile duct, which can cause biliary obstruction, jaundice, and ascending cholangitis.
    \item \textbf{Gallstone pancreatitis:} Obstruction of the hepatopancreatic ampulla (ampulla of Vater) by a migrating gallstone, leading to pancreatic enzyme activation and acute pancreatic inflammation.
    \item \textbf{Biliary dyskinesia:} A functional disorder characterised by impaired gallbladder motility and emptying in the absence of gallstones, diagnosed via a reduced gallbladder ejection fraction on a cholescintigraphy (HIDA) scan.
    \item \textbf{Gallbladder polyps:} Mucosal projections that may require cholecystectomy if they are symptomatic, larger than 10 millimetres, or show features concerning for neoplastic progression.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of patients requiring cholecystectomy varies from recurrent biliary colic to acute systemic illness.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Biliary colic:} Episodic, severe, dull pain located in the right upper quadrant or epigastrium. The pain often radiates to the right shoulder or interscapular region, typically lasts between one and five hours, and is frequently precipitated by fatty meals.
    \item \textbf{Nausea and vomiting:} Common autonomic symptoms accompanying acute episodes of biliary pain.
    \item \textbf{Dyspepsia:} Flatulence, bloating, and postprandial fullness, though these symptoms are non-specific.
    \item \textbf{Right upper quadrant tenderness:} Localised tenderness on abdominal palpation. In acute cholecystitis, this may manifest as a positive Murphy's sign, where deep inspiration during RUQ palpation causes sudden arrest of breathing due to pain.
    \item \textbf{Systemic signs:} Low-grade fever, tachycardia, and mild leukocytosis indicate acute inflammatory progression (acute cholecystitis).
    \item \textbf{Jaundice:} Yellowing of the skin and sclera, accompanied by dark urine and pale stools, indicating biliary tree obstruction (choledocholithiasis).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Prior to surgical referral, other common gastrointestinal and cardiovascular conditions must be excluded.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD):} Can present with burning epigastric or retrosternal pain, often worse when lying flat, and usually responds to acid suppression therapy.
    \item \textbf{Peptic ulcer disease (PUD):} Characterised by burning epigastric pain that is typically related to meals (either relieved or exacerbated by food) and diagnosed via upper endoscopy.
    \item \textbf{Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS):} Presents with chronic abdominal pain, bloating, and altered bowel habits, but lacks systemic signs, localised RUQ tenderness, or abnormal laboratory findings.
    \item \textbf{Acute pancreatitis:} Causes severe, constant epigastric pain radiating to the back, accompanied by marked elevations in serum lipase or amylase levels.
    \item \textbf{Right-sided pneumonia or pleurisy:} Can cause pleuritic chest or right upper quadrant pain, distinguished by respiratory symptoms, cough, and abnormal findings on chest X-ray.
    \item \textbf{Renal colic:} Severe, colicky loin-to-groin pain, typically accompanied by haematuria and confirmed with non-contrast CT pyelogram.
    \item \textbf{Cardiac ischaemia:} Atypical myocardial infarction can present with epigastric pain and nausea, necessitating an electrocardiogram (ECG) and troponin measurement.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients with diagnosed gallstones should seek medical advice if they experience changes in their typical symptoms. Following a laparoscopic cholecystectomy, patients must monitor for signs of potential postoperative complications.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, crushing chest pain or difficulty breathing.
    \item Sudden, intolerable abdominal pain that is not relieved by prescribed analgesia.
    \item High fevers (above 38.5 degrees Celsius) accompanied by rigors or chills.
    \item Progressive yellowing of the skin or eyes (jaundice).
    \item Persistent vomiting and inability to tolerate any oral fluids.
    \item Heavy bleeding, purulent discharge, or rapidly spreading redness around the surgical wounds.
    \item Dizziness, fainting, or sudden swelling and severe pain in one calf muscle.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A combination of laboratory tests and imaging is required to confirm gallbladder disease and plan the surgical approach.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transabdominal ultrasound:} The primary imaging modality. It has high sensitivity and specificity for detecting gallstones, showing mobile, hyperechoic structures with acoustic shadowing, gallbladder wall thickening (greater than 3 millimetres), pericholecystic fluid, and a sonographic Murphy's sign.
    \item \textbf{Liver function tests (LFTs):} Used to screen for biliary obstruction. Elevated bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) suggest common bile duct stones.
    \item \textbf{Full blood count (FBC):} Demonstrates leukocytosis with a neutrophilic shift in acute cholecystitis.
    \item \textbf{Serum lipase:} Measured to exclude secondary biliary pancreatitis.
    \item \textbf{Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP):} A non-invasive MRI protocol used if LFTs are abnormal or the common bile duct is dilated on ultrasound, to rule out choledocholithiasis.
    \item \textbf{HIDA scan (cholescintigraphy):} Indicated when biliary dyskinesia or acute acalculous cholecystitis is suspected but ultrasound findings are equivocal.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is the definitive management strategy for symptomatic gallbladder disease.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgical technique:} The procedure is performed under general anaesthesia with endotracheal intubation. Carbon dioxide pneumoperitoneum is established, and four small ports are placed. The gallbladder is retracted to expose Calot's triangle. Dissection is performed to achieve the ``critical view of safety,'' isolating the cystic duct and cystic artery. These structures are clipped and divided, and the gallbladder is dissected from the liver bed and extracted through an umbilical port.
    \item \textbf{Intraoperative cholangiogram (IOC):} Often performed routinely or selectively by injecting contrast into the biliary tree under fluoroscopy to check for silent common bile duct stones and delineate biliary anatomy.
    \item \textbf{Conversion to open cholecystectomy:} Necessary in 1\% to 5\% of cases due to dense adhesions, severe inflammation, anatomical anomalies, or intraoperative complications. It represents a clinical decision for patient safety rather than a failure of the procedure.
    \item \textbf{Postoperative care:} Emphasises early mobilisation, deep breathing exercises, and prompt management of pain with multimodal analgesia (paracetamol, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and short-acting opioids if required). Many patients are managed as day-cases, while others may require an overnight stay.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While laparoscopic cholecystectomy is highly safe, potential surgical and anaesthetic risks must be discussed.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bile duct injury:} A rare but severe complication (occurring in 0.3\% to 0.5\% of cases) involving transection, occlusion, or stricture of the common bile duct or hepatic ducts, requiring specialised reconstructive surgery.
    \item \textbf{Bile leak:} Occurs from the cystic duct stump, gallbladder bed (ducts of Luschka), or an unrecognised biliary injury, presenting with abdominal pain and peritonitis.
    \item \textbf{Haemorrhage:} Can arise from the cystic artery, liver bed, or trocar insertion sites, occasionally requiring re-operation.
    \item \textbf{Surgical site infection:} Superficial wound infection at port sites, typically treated with local wound care and oral antibiotics.
    \item \textbf{Visceral injury:} Inadvertent damage to the duodenum, stomach, or colon during instrument insertion or dissection.
    \item \textbf{Post-cholecystectomy syndrome:} Persistent gastrointestinal symptoms, including bloating, flatulence, and right upper quadrant pain, which may require further evaluation of the biliary tree or sphincter of Oddi.
    \item \textbf{Venous thromboembolism:} Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or pulmonary embolism (PE) secondary to surgery and immobility.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis following laparoscopic cholecystectomy is excellent. The resolution of biliary pain is achieved in over 90\% of patients with confirmed gallstone disease. The majority of patients return to light activities within one week and resume full physical work and exercise within two to four weeks. The removal of the gallbladder does not impair digestion, as bile now flows directly from the liver into the duodenum. While approximately 5\% to 10\% of patients may experience temporary mild diarrhoea or loose stools postoperatively due to continuous bile flow, this symptom usually resolves spontaneously within a few weeks or months.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Postoperative recovery and long-term wellbeing are supported by adherence to self-care protocols.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Wound management:} Keep port-site dressings clean and dry for the first 48 hours. Showers are permitted, but bathing, swimming, or soaking in water should be avoided until wounds have healed completely.
    \item \textbf{Activity modification:} Avoid heavy lifting (greater than 5 kilograms) and strenuous abdominal exercises for four to six weeks to prevent port-site hernias.
    \item \textbf{Dietary adjustments:} Resume a normal diet gradually. While a low-fat diet is recommended for the first few weeks to minimise bloating and diarrhoea, patients can slowly reintroduce healthy fats as tolerated.
    \item \textbf{Pain management:} Take prescribed pain medications regularly in the initial days rather than waiting for pain to become severe, and manage post-laparoscopy shoulder tip pain (caused by residual carbon dioxide gas) with gentle walking and heat packs.
    \item \textbf{Preventing constipation:} Drink plenty of water and consume high-fibre foods to offset the constipating effects of opioid analgesics.
\end{itemize}
