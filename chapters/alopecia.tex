\chapter{Alopecia (Hair Loss)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Alopecia is the medical term for hair loss, affecting the scalp, face, or body. It ranges from mild, diffuse thinning to complete loss of scalp hair, and it is classified according to whether the hair follicles are permanently destroyed (scarring alopecia) or remain intact and capable of regrowth (non-scarring alopecia). The most common forms are androgenetic (pattern) alopecia, telogen effluvium, and alopecia areata. Most types of alopecia pose no direct threat to physical health, but the psychological and social impact can be substantial, and accurate diagnosis is essential because the causes and treatments differ considerably. The hair follicle is a dynamic structure that cycles through growth, regression, and rest, and disruption of this cycle underlies virtually all forms of hair loss.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hair loss is extremely common and increases with age. Androgenetic alopecia affects the majority of men by middle age, and a substantial minority of women, particularly after the menopause. Telogen effluvium can occur at any age following childbirth, severe illness, surgery, psychological stress, or major weight loss. Alopecia areata affects roughly 1 to 2\% of the population, begins in childhood or young adulthood in many cases, and affects both sexes equally. Traction alopecia is more common in people who habitually wear tight hairstyles, and scarring alopecias are rare but important because they cause permanent hair loss. The condition has a significant genetic component, and a family history of pattern hair loss is a strong predictor.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Hair grows in cycles, and different forms of alopecia disrupt the cycle in different ways:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Androgenetic alopecia:} genetically determined sensitivity of scalp follicles to dihydrotestosterone shortens the growth phase and shrinks the follicles, producing gradual thinning at the crown and temples.
    \item \textbf{Telogen effluvium:} a physiological shock such as illness, childbirth, surgery, or stress pushes a large number of growing hairs prematurely into the resting (telogen) phase, so that they shed together two to three months later.
    \item \textbf{Alopecia areata:} an autoimmune attack on the hair follicle interrupts the growth phase, producing smooth, round patches; the follicle remains alive and regrowth is possible.
    \item \textbf{Traction alopecia:} sustained tension from tight hairstyles damages the follicle at the hairline and areas of maximal pull.
    \item \textbf{Scarring alopecia:} inflammatory conditions such as lichen planopilaris or discoid lupus destroy follicles and replace them with scar tissue, causing permanent loss.
    \item \textbf{Other causes:} thyroid disease, iron deficiency, severe scalp infections such as tinea capitis, and certain medicines including chemotherapy agents.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Features vary with the type of alopecia:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pattern hair loss:} a receding frontal hairline and crown thinning in men; a widening central part with diffuse thinning in women.
    \item \textbf{Telogen effluvium:} diffuse shedding starting weeks to months after a stressor, usually without obvious bald patches and with preserved hair on the scalp.
    \item \textbf{Alopecia areata:} smooth, coin-shaped bald patches that may enlarge, merge, or spread; involvement of all scalp hair (alopecia totalis) or all body hair (alopecia universalis) occurs in severe cases.
    \item \textbf{Traction alopecia:} thinning and broken hairs along the hairline and at points of tight pulling.
    \item \textbf{Scarring alopecia:} shiny, atrophic, often featureless areas where follicles have been destroyed, sometimes with preceding itching, burning, or redness.
    \item \textbf{Associated features:} fatigue, weight change, or temperature intolerance suggest an underlying systemic condition such as thyroid disease.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Conditions that can resemble alopecia include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hair shaft breakage:} from chemical processing, bleaching, or heat styling, causing apparent loss without follicle involvement.
    \item \textbf{Scalp disorders:} psoriasis, seborrhoeic dermatitis, and tinea capitis, a fungal infection that causes scaly, broken-hair patches.
    \item \textbf{Trichotillomania:} the compulsive pulling out of one's own hair, producing irregular patches of broken hair.
    \item \textbf{Systemic disease:} thyroid disorders, iron deficiency, and other hormonal or nutritional problems.
    \item \textbf{Drug-induced shedding:} including chemotherapy and several other medication classes.
    \item \textbf{Syphilis:} a rare cause of patchy, moth-eaten hair loss in secondary disease.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if hair loss is sudden, patchy, or spreading, if it is accompanied by scalp redness, scaling, or scarring, or if it causes significant distress. Prompt attention is also warranted when hair loss occurs with unexplained tiredness, weight change, or other systemic symptoms, and before starting any treatment.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden widespread hair loss with fever or a severe headache.
    \item Scalp swelling, severe pain, or signs of a serious infection.
    \item Difficulty breathing or swallowing that is unrelated to hair loss itself.
    \item Any sudden, severe symptom that causes concern.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis begins with a careful history and scalp examination:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the onset and speed of loss, recent illness or stress, medicines, hair-care practices, and family history of hair loss.
    \item \textbf{Examination:} assessing the pattern of loss, the scalp surface, and the condition of the hair shafts.
    \item \textbf{Hair pull test:} gently tugging a small bundle of hairs to gauge how many shed easily.
    \item \textbf{Trichoscopy:} magnified examination of the scalp and hair with a dermatoscope to distinguish scarring from non-scarring loss.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} iron studies, thyroid function, and other tests as indicated by the clinical picture.
    \item \textbf{Scalp biopsy:} a small skin sample is taken for scarring or inflammatory forms where the diagnosis is uncertain.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on the type and underlying cause:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pattern hair loss:} topical minoxidil for men and women, and oral finasteride or dutasteride for men, are the mainstays; treatment slows progression and stimulates regrowth and is usually long-term.
    \item \textbf{Telogen effluvium:} no specific treatment is needed beyond addressing the trigger, with spontaneous regrowth over months.
    \item \textbf{Alopecia areata:} potent topical steroids, intralesional steroid injections, and systemic treatments for extensive or refractory disease.
    \item \textbf{Traction alopecia:} stopping the causative hairstyle to allow recovery of the follicles.
    \item \textbf{Scarring alopecia:} early specialist treatment with anti-inflammatory agents to limit further destruction, though lost hair will not regrow.
    \item \textbf{Cosmetic options:} wigs, hairpieces, scalp micropigmentation, and medical camouflage for visible loss.
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} counselling for distress, and support groups for people with visible hair loss.
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular review to monitor regrowth and adjust treatment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Complications of hair loss and its causes include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Permanent follicle destruction in scarring and long-standing traction alopecia.
    \item Psychological distress, anxiety, low self-esteem, and social withdrawal.
    \item Sunburn of the exposed scalp.
    \item Untreated underlying conditions, such as thyroid disease or iron deficiency, with their own health consequences.
    \item Side effects of hair-loss treatments, including skin irritation and contact dermatitis.
    \item Occupational and social impact from visibly altered appearance.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook varies by type. Telogen effluvium almost always resolves within several months once the trigger is treated. Pattern hair loss is progressive but often responds to early, sustained treatment, and newer therapies offer improved outcomes. Alopecia areata may regrow spontaneously, although it can recur or become extensive; treatment success is higher for limited disease. Most reversible causes produce good regrowth when the underlying problem is identified early. Emotional wellbeing should be part of every plan, and many people manage hair loss very well with support, treatment, and cosmetic measures.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Not all hair loss is preventable, but several measures help:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Avoid tight hairstyles, harsh chemicals, and excessive heat that damage hair.
    \item Eat a balanced diet with adequate iron, protein, and other nutrients.
    \item Manage stress and maintain good sleep.
    \item Treat underlying conditions, such as thyroid disease or anaemia, under medical guidance.
    \item Handle hair gently when wet and use a wide-tooth comb.
    \item Seek advice early if you notice new, patchy, or accelerating hair loss.
\end{itemize}
