\chapter{Glioma}
\index{Glioma}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A glioma is a type of brain tumour that arises from glial cells, the cells that support and protect the brain's nerve cells (neurons). Gliomas include astrocytomas, oligodendrogliomas, and ependymomas, and they are graded from 1 to 4 according to how quickly they grow and how abnormal the cells look. Low-grade gliomas grow slowly and may be present for years, while high-grade gliomas, such as glioblastoma, grow rapidly. Gliomas can occur at any age, and symptoms depend on the tumour's location and size. Treatment varies from monitoring alone for slow-growing tumours to surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy for more aggressive ones.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Gliomas are the most common primary brain tumours, accounting for around 80\% of malignant brain tumours. In many countries, more than 1,900 people are diagnosed with a brain tumour each year, and gliomas make up the largest group. They are slightly more common in men and most often diagnosed in adults aged 40--70, though some types, including pilocytic astrocytomas and ependymomas, are more common in children. The incidence of gliomas has been fairly stable over recent decades, and most arise without any identifiable cause.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The cause of most gliomas is unknown, but they are defined by the cell type and genetic changes involved.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Origin:} gliomas arise from glial cells -- astrocytes (astrocytomas), oligodendrocytes (oligodendrogliomas), or ependymal cells (ependymomas)
    \item \textbf{Grade:} tumours are graded 1--4 based on growth rate and cell appearance; grade 1--2 are low grade, grade 3--4 are high grade
    \item \textbf{Genetic changes:} specific mutations help classify gliomas and guide treatment, including IDH mutations and 1p/19q codeletion
    \item \textbf{IDH mutation:} found in many low-grade gliomas and secondary glioblastomas, associated with a better prognosis
    \item \textbf{Growth patterns:} high-grade gliomas infiltrate surrounding brain tissue, making complete removal difficult
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} only brain radiotherapy and rare inherited syndromes are established risk factors; mobile phones and other environmental exposures are not proven causes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on the location, size, and growth rate of the tumour.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Headaches, especially new, persistent, or progressive
    \item Seizures, which are often the first sign in low-grade gliomas
    \item Weakness, numbness, or clumsiness on one side of the body
    \item Difficulty speaking, understanding language, or finding words
    \item Changes in personality, mood, or concentration
    \item Visual disturbances and double vision
    \item Balance problems and difficulty walking
    \item Nausea and vomiting from raised intracranial pressure
    \item Fatigue and general decline in function
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Brain imaging findings and symptoms have many potential causes.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Other brain tumours, including meningiomas, metastases, and lymphomas
    \item Stroke and transient ischaemic attack
    \item Brain abscess and other infections
    \item Demyelinating disease such as multiple sclerosis
    \item Cerebral cavernous malformations and vascular lesions
    \item Epilepsy without a structural cause
    \item Migraine and tension headache
    \item Normal pressure hydrocephalus and other structural conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek prompt medical assessment for any new neurological symptoms, especially seizures, progressive weakness, speech difficulty, or new headaches with neurological signs.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe headache with vomiting, confusion, or collapse
    \item A first seizure or fit
    \item Sudden weakness, facial drooping, or speech difficulty (possible stroke)
    \item Sudden loss of vision or rapid deterioration in consciousness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis relies on brain imaging and, in most cases, tissue sampling.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{MRI:} the preferred imaging test, showing the tumour's location, size, and characteristics
    \item \textbf{CT:} often the first test in an emergency
    \item \textbf{Biopsy or resection:} a tissue sample is examined to confirm the diagnosis and grade
    \item \textbf{Molecular testing:} testing for IDH mutation, 1p/19q codeletion, MGMT methylation, and other markers guides treatment and prognosis
    \item \textbf{Neurological examination:} documents the functional impact of the tumour
    \item \textbf{Multidisciplinary review:} specialists discuss the case to plan the best treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is planned according to the tumour grade, molecular profile, location, and the patient's health and preferences.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low-grade gliomas:} may be monitored with regular imaging, or treated with surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy if they grow or cause symptoms
    \item \textbf{High-grade gliomas:} maximal safe surgery, followed by radiotherapy and chemotherapy
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} removal of as much tumour as is safe
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} targeted radiation for high-grade tumours and some low-grade tumours
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy:} temozolomide and other agents, particularly for high-grade disease
    \item \textbf{Symptom management:} steroids for swelling, antiepileptic medicines for seizures, and rehabilitation therapies
    \item \textbf{Clinical trials:} access to new treatments may be available
    \item \textbf{Supportive and palliative care:} integrated throughout to maintain quality of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Tumour progression and transformation of low-grade tumours into high-grade disease
    \item Neurological deficits from the tumour or its treatment
    \item Seizures and raised intracranial pressure
    \item Side effects of radiotherapy, including fatigue and cognitive changes
    \item Side effects of chemotherapy, including nausea and low blood counts
    \item Side effects of steroids, including weight gain and infection risk
    \item Blood clots, which are more common in people with brain tumours
    \item Psychological and practical burden on patients and carers
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for gliomas varies widely with grade. Low-grade gliomas grow slowly, and many people live for many years, sometimes with no treatment needed for long periods; however, most eventually progress and may require treatment. High-grade gliomas have a much shorter outlook, with glioblastoma being the most aggressive. Molecular markers such as IDH status are strong predictors of outcome: IDH-mutated gliomas grow more slowly and respond better to treatment. With multidisciplinary care, many people with low-grade gliomas maintain good function and quality of life for years.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item There is no proven way to prevent gliomas
    \item Report new or changing neurological symptoms promptly
    \item If diagnosed, seek care from a specialist centre with a multidisciplinary brain tumour team
    \item Follow the treatment plan and attend follow-up imaging and appointments
    \item Manage symptoms and side effects openly with your care team
    \item Maintain as much physical activity and independence as possible
    \item Seek psychological support for yourself and family
    \item Consider discussing advance care planning early, even with lower-grade tumours
\end{itemize}
