\chapter{Dengue}
\index{Dengue}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dengue is a viral infection transmitted by the bite of infected Aedes mosquitoes, most commonly Aedes aegypti. The virus causes a febrile illness ranging from mild flu-like symptoms to severe dengue, a dangerous complication characterised by bleeding, plasma leakage, and shock. Dengue is a major global health problem in tropical and subtropical regions, and cases are increasing worldwide. There is no specific antiviral treatment, but early recognition and careful management dramatically reduce the risk of severe disease and death. This chapter covers the transmission, symptoms, and management of dengue.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Dengue is a rapidly expanding mosquito-borne viral disease. The World Health Organization estimates that hundreds of millions of infections occur each year, with some people developing severe dengue. It is endemic in many countries across Asia, the Americas, Africa, and the Pacific. Risk depends on local mosquito populations, weather, travel, urbanisation, and public-health control measures. Climate change may expand the areas suitable for transmission.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Virus:} dengue is caused by one of four related virus serotypes (DENV-1 to DENV-4)
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} the Aedes aegypti mosquito transmits the virus between humans; the mosquito bites mainly during the day
    \item \textbf{Replication:} after inoculation, the virus replicates in immune cells and spreads through the body, causing fever and systemic symptoms
    \item \textbf{Immune response:} infection with one serotype gives lifelong immunity to that serotype but not others; a second infection with a different serotype carries a higher risk of severe dengue through antibody-dependent enhancement
    \item \textbf{Plasma leakage:} in severe dengue, the virus triggers a cytokine storm that increases blood vessel permeability, causing fluid to leak into tissues and leading to shock and bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden high fever, often with severe headache, pain behind the eyes, and muscle and joint pain (earning it the name "break-bone fever")
    \item Nausea, vomiting, rash, and loss of appetite
    \item Symptoms typically begin 4--10 days after a mosquito bite
    \item The critical phase occurs around days 3--7 of illness, when the fever settles but plasma leakage can cause warning signs: severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, bleeding from gums or nose, and lethargy or restlessness
    \item Severe dengue can cause shock, breathing difficulty, and organ failure
    \item Most infections are mild or asymptomatic, especially in children
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Other mosquito-borne viruses such as Zika, chikungunya, and Ross River virus
    \item Malaria, in travellers returning from affected regions
    \item Influenza and other respiratory viruses
    \item Typhoid fever and other bacterial infections
    \item Measles and other causes of fever with rash
    \item Meningitis, which can cause headache and fever
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with fever after travel to a dengue-affected area should see a doctor promptly and mention their travel history. People diagnosed with dengue need close monitoring during the critical phase and should seek immediate medical attention if warning signs develop.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, or bleeding from the gums, nose, or bowel
    \item Drowsiness, confusion, restlessness, or fainting
    \item Difficulty breathing or cold, clammy skin (possible shock)
    \item Vomiting blood or passing black stools
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} dengue can be confirmed by PCR (detecting viral genetic material) in the first week, or by antibody tests (NS1 antigen and IgM/IgG) from day 4--5
    \item \textbf{Full blood count:} falling platelet count and rising haematocrit indicate plasma leakage and warn of severe disease
    \item \textbf{Liver function tests:} mild abnormalities are common
    \item \textbf{Travel history:} essential for diagnosis in non-endemic countries
    \item \textbf{Clinical monitoring:} blood pressure, urine output, and warning signs are monitored closely during the critical phase
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{No specific antiviral:} management is supportive, focused on maintaining hydration and monitoring for complications
    \item \textbf{Hydration:} oral fluids for mild disease; intravenous fluids in hospital for those at risk of plasma leakage
    \item \textbf{Fever and pain relief:} paracetamol is preferred; avoid aspirin and ibuprofen, which increase bleeding risk
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} required for warning signs, severe disease, or high-risk patients
    \item \textbf{Blood products:} platelet or blood transfusion for significant bleeding
    \item \textbf{Careful fluid management:} fluids are given according to strict protocols, as overhydration can cause fluid overload
    \item \textbf{Vaccination:} a dengue vaccine (Qdenga) is available in some countries for people with previous dengue exposure; it is not routinely recommended for local travellers
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe dengue (dengue haemorrhagic fever and dengue shock syndrome)
    \item Plasma leakage causing fluid in the chest or abdomen
    \item Severe bleeding, including from the gut
    \item Organ failure affecting the liver, kidneys, or heart
    \item Shock and death in untreated severe cases
    \item Prolonged fatigue after recovery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With good supportive care, the vast majority of people with dengue recover fully within 1--2 weeks, although fatigue can persist. The case fatality rate of severe dengue is below 1\% with appropriate hospital care but can be much higher without it. Recovery from one serotype leaves lifelong immunity to that serotype; the risk of severe disease on reinfection with another serotype is a key reason for mosquito protection at every exposure. Early recognition of warning signs is the most important factor in a good outcome.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Prevent mosquito bites: use repellents containing DEET or picaridin, wear long sleeves and trousers, and sleep under nets
    \item In affected areas, reduce mosquito breeding by emptying standing water from containers
    \item Travellers to dengue-affected regions should take bite precautions day and night
    \item Seek medical review promptly if fever develops within two weeks of travel
    \item During illness, drink plenty of fluids and rest
    \item Avoid aspirin and ibuprofen if dengue is suspected
    \item Check current dengue risk and vaccine advice before travelling
\end{itemize}
