\chapter{Antihistamines}
\index{Antihistamines}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Antihistamines are medicines that block the action of \textbf{histamine}, a chemical released by the body's immune cells during an allergic reaction. They are used mainly to relieve the symptoms of allergic rhinitis (hay fever), hives (urticaria), and other allergic conditions such as sneezing, itching, a runny nose, and watery eyes. They do not treat asthma and are not a substitute for adrenaline in anaphylaxis, although they are often given alongside adrenaline during recovery from a severe allergic reaction.

There are two broad groups. \textbf{First-generation} antihistamines, such as diphenhydramine, promethazine, and chlorpheniramine, are older, cross into the brain readily, and cause drowsiness; they are also used as short-term sleep aids and to relieve nausea. \textbf{Second-generation} antihistamines, such as loratadine, cetirizine, fexofenadine, and desloratadine, are newer, cause little or no sedation at standard doses, and are preferred for daytime use because they are less likely to impair concentration and driving.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Allergic rhinitis affects a large proportion of the population -- around one in five adults in many countries -- making antihistamines among the most widely used medicines worldwide, many of them purchased without a prescription. Hay fever commonly begins in childhood or early adulthood, often runs in families, and is more prevalent in urban areas with high pollen exposure.

Hives (urticaria) affect people of all ages; acute attacks are very common, and chronic spontaneous urticaria (lasting more than six weeks) affects roughly 1 per cent of the population. Antihistamine use peaks during pollen seasons and is higher in regions with long or intense allergen seasons. Many people self-manage with over-the-counter products before ever seeing a doctor.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Histamine is stored in granules inside \textbf{mast cells} and basophils, which are concentrated in the skin, lungs, and the linings of the nose, eyes, and gut. When an allergen such as grass pollen, dust mite proteins, or a food triggers these cells, they degranulate and release histamine, which then binds to \textbf{H1 receptors} on blood vessels, nerve endings, and glands:

\begin{itemize}
    \item On blood vessels, histamine widens small arterioles and makes capillaries leaky, causing redness, swelling, and fluid accumulation
    \item On sensory nerve endings, it provokes itching and sneezing
    \item On mucosal glands, it stimulates production of clear nasal discharge and watery eyes
    \item In the airways, it contributes to bronchoconstriction, which is why severe allergy can affect breathing
\end{itemize}

Antihistamines compete with histamine for the H1 receptor, so the chemical cannot produce its effects. First-generation drugs also block histamine receptors in the brain, causing sedation, and have anticholinergic effects such as a dry mouth, blurred vision, and urinary retention. Second-generation drugs are designed to stay outside the brain, which explains their lack of sedation.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intended effect:} reduction in sneezing, itching, runny nose, and hives, usually beginning within 30--60 minutes of a dose
    \item \textbf{Drowsiness and sedation:} prominent with first-generation medicines, and significant enough to impair driving, study, and work
    \item \textbf{Anticholinergic effects:} dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, and difficulty passing urine, more common with older drugs
    \item \textbf{Mild headache or fatigue:} occasionally reported with second-generation drugs
    \item \textbf{Odds of overdose:} severe drowsiness in adults, and in small children agitation, flushing, rapid heart rate, and confusion after accidental ingestion of sedating products
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Non-allergic rhinitis, nasal polyps, or sinusitis:} causes of nasal congestion that respond poorly to antihistamines
    \item \textbf{Chronic hives without an identifiable trigger:} which may need specialist assessment rather than simple avoidance
    \item \textbf{Contact dermatitis or eczema:} itchy rashes that resemble allergic hives but need different treatment
    \item \textbf{Anaphylaxis:} a severe, rapidly progressive allergic reaction that requires adrenaline, not an antihistamine
    \item \textbf{Other causes of drowsiness:} sleep deprivation, depression, or sedating medicines, especially in the elderly
    \item \textbf{Food allergy:} which may cause mouth tingling and hives but requires an allergy action plan
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if allergy symptoms are severe, interfering with daily life, or not controlled by over-the-counter antihistamines, or if chronic hives persist beyond a few weeks. Children with eczema or suspected food allergy should have specialist review to guide management.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, wheezing, or a tight throat
    \item Swelling of the face, lips, or tongue, or collapse -- features of anaphylaxis
    \item A child who becomes very drowsy, agitated, or difficult to wake after taking a sedating antihistamine
    \item Suspected overdose -- contact the local poison information service
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical history:} the pattern, timing, and seasonality of symptoms, and possible triggers in the home, workplace, and diet
    \item \textbf{Examination:} inspection of the nasal mucosa, eyes, and skin for the features of allergic disease
    \item \textbf{Trial of treatment:} a short course of a non-drowsy antihistamine to see whether symptoms improve
    \item \textbf{Allergy testing:} skin prick tests or specific IgE blood tests, arranged by an allergist or immunologist, to identify culprit allergens
    \item \textbf{Review of other medicines:} checking for interactions with sedatives, alcohol, and medicines that worsen anticholinergic effects, particularly in older people
    \item \textbf{Assessment for complications:} examination for asthma, sinusitis, and sleep disturbance that commonly accompany allergic rhinitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Non-drowsy (second-generation) antihistamines:} the usual first choice for daytime symptoms; taken as a regular daily dose for chronic symptoms
    \item \textbf{Dose before exposure:} for predictable triggers, such as the pollen season or a known pet exposure, taking the medicine beforehand provides the best cover
    \item \textbf{Regular daily dosing:} for chronic hives, continuous daily use is more effective than taking the medicine only when symptoms occur
    \item \textbf{Sedating antihistamines:} reserved for night-time symptoms or when sedation is helpful; avoid driving, operating machinery, and alcohol
    \item \textbf{Combination therapy:} intranasal steroid sprays, decongestants, and anti-allergic eye drops often control hay fever better than an antihistamine alone
    \item \textbf{Allergen immunotherapy:} desensitisation injections or tablets, supervised by a specialist, for people whose symptoms are poorly controlled
    \item \textbf{Review:} patients with asthma or recurrent anaphylaxis should carry an adrenaline autoinjector as directed by their specialist
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Drowsiness and slowed reaction times, leading to falls, workplace injury, or road crashes with first-generation drugs
    \item Cognitive impairment in older people, occasionally mistaken for dementia or confusion
    \item Worsening of narrow-angle glaucoma, urinary retention, or constipation through anticholinergic effects
    \item Accidental overdose in children, particularly with sedating syrups
    \item Interaction with alcohol and other central nervous system depressants
    \item Rare allergic reactions to the antihistamine itself
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Allergic symptoms are usually well controlled with the right antihistamine, and most people find a product that suits them. Hay fever symptoms typically fluctuate with allergen exposure from year to year, and some children outgrow their allergies as they mature. Chronic hives can persist for months or years, but around half of cases settle within one to two years, often with the help of higher-dose or second-line agents.

Antihistamines control symptoms rather than cure the underlying allergy. Outcomes are generally excellent when the correct medicine is chosen, triggers are avoided, and associated conditions such as asthma and sinusitis are managed.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Identify and avoid triggers where possible: pollen, dust mites, pet dander, mould, and relevant foods
    \item Keep windows closed on high-pollen days, shower after time outdoors, and check the daily pollen count
    \item Use dust mite-proof covers on bedding and wash bedding in hot water regularly
    \item Wash pets and keep them out of the bedroom if you are allergic
    \item Choose a non-drowsy antihistamine for daytime and never drive or operate machinery if drowsy
    \item Store medicines out of reach of children and follow dose instructions exactly
    \item Seek specialist review if symptoms are poorly controlled or hives persist
\end{itemize}
