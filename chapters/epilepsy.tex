\chapter{Epilepsy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Epilepsy is a neurological condition characterised by a tendency to have recurrent, unprovoked seizures. A seizure is a sudden, uncontrolled surge of electrical activity in the brain that briefly disrupts normal function, causing changes in movement, sensation, awareness, or behaviour. A single seizure does not mean a person has epilepsy; the diagnosis is usually made after two or more unprovoked seizures, or when specific test findings predict a high risk of further seizures. Epilepsy affects people of every age and background, and its causes, seizure types, and severity vary enormously. With modern treatment, most people with epilepsy achieve good seizure control and live full, active lives. This chapter explains seizures and epilepsy, how they are diagnosed, and how they are managed.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Epilepsy is one of the most common neurological disorders, affecting around 1 in 100 people at some point in their lives. In Australia, more than 150,000 people live with active epilepsy, and each year a similar number experience a first seizure that does not progress to epilepsy. The condition has a bimodal age pattern, with the highest rates in young children and in people over the age of 65, reflecting the different causes at each end of life. Around one-third of people with epilepsy do not achieve full seizure control with current medicines. Globally, epilepsy accounts for a substantial share of neurological disability, and access to treatment varies widely between countries.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
A seizure occurs when a group of brain cells discharges abnormally and synchronously; epilepsy arises when this tendency becomes established.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic causes:} many epilepsies have a strong genetic component, and specific gene mutations have been identified in some forms
    \item \textbf{Structural brain abnormalities:} tumours, strokes, head injuries, brain infections, and developmental malformations such as cortical dysplasia can create seizure foci
    \item \textbf{Metabolic and immune disorders:} rare metabolic diseases and autoimmune encephalitis can cause seizures
    \item \textbf{Age-related causes:} birth-related brain injury and genetic syndromes dominate in childhood, while stroke, tumours, and neurodegenerative disease dominate in older adults
    \item \textbf{Provoked seizures:} fever, alcohol withdrawal, sleep deprivation, low blood sugar, and certain drugs can trigger seizures without epilepsy being present
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} the abnormal discharge results from an imbalance between excitation and inhibition in the brain, with disturbances in sodium, potassium, and GABA-mediated signalling
    \item \textbf{Classification:} seizures are classified as focal, originating in one area of the brain, or generalised, involving both hemispheres from the start
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features depend on the seizure type.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Focal aware seizures:} unusual sensations, emotions, or movements with awareness preserved, such as a rising feeling in the stomach, déjà vu, or jerking of one limb
    \item \textbf{Focal impaired awareness seizures:} altered awareness with automatic behaviours such as lip-smacking, fumbling, or wandering
    \item \textbf{Generalised tonic--clonic seizures:} loss of consciousness with stiffening then rhythmic jerking of the whole body, often with a cry, incontinence, and a period of confusion and sleepiness afterwards
    \item \textbf{Absence seizures:} brief lapses of awareness, lasting seconds, with staring and no fall, mainly in children
    \item \textbf{Myoclonic seizures:} sudden brief jerks of a limb or the whole body
    \item \textbf{Atonic seizures:} sudden loss of muscle tone causing a drop to the ground
    \item \textbf{Auras:} many people have a warning symptom before a focal seizure, which can act as an early sign to seek safety
    \item \textbf{Post-ictal state:} confusion, fatigue, headache, and muscle soreness in the minutes to hours after a seizure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Not everything that looks like a seizure is epilepsy.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Syncope (fainting):} brief loss of consciousness from reduced blood flow to the brain, often with warning light-headedness
    \item \textbf{Non-epileptic (psychogenic) seizures:} episodes that resemble seizures but are not caused by abnormal electrical activity, often related to stress or trauma
    \item \textbf{Migraine:} some forms cause visual disturbance or confusion
    \item \textbf{Transient ischaemic attacks and stroke}
    \item \textbf{Sleep disorders:} parasomnias, night terrors, and narcolepsy can be confused with nocturnal seizures
    \item \textbf{Low blood sugar, alcohol withdrawal, and drug effects}
    \item \textbf{Febrile convulsions} in young children, which usually do not indicate epilepsy
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who has a first seizure should be assessed by a doctor promptly, and people with known epilepsy should seek review if seizures increase in frequency or severity, or if side effects from medicines appear.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A seizure lasting longer than 5 minutes (status epilepticus)
    \item Repeated seizures without full recovery of consciousness between them
    \item A first-ever seizure in an adult
    \item A seizure with difficulty breathing, injury, or in a person who is pregnant or has diabetes
    \item Seizure in water, or after a head injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detailed history:} the most important step; an eyewitness account of the episode is invaluable in distinguishing seizures from mimics
    \item \textbf{Electroencephalogram (EEG):} records brain electrical activity and can reveal epileptiform discharges, especially when sleep-deprived recording is used
    \item \textbf{Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):} detects structural causes such as tumours, strokes, or malformations
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} glucose, electrolytes, and other tests to exclude provoked causes
    \item \textbf{Computed tomography (CT):} used urgently after a seizure when a bleed or mass is suspected
    \item \textbf{Ambulatory or video EEG:} for capturing episodes or distinguishing epilepsy from non-epileptic seizures
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} in selected childhood and familial epilepsies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antiseizure medicines:} the mainstay of treatment, chosen according to seizure type; many people achieve control with a single medicine
    \item \textbf{Seizure safety:} avoiding triggers such as sleep deprivation and excessive alcohol, and managing risk in bathing, swimming, and driving
    \item \textbf{Driving restrictions:} Australian laws require a seizure-free period, usually 6 months, before driving can resume; this is mandatory and must be discussed with the treating doctor
    \item \textbf{Vagus nerve stimulation:} an implanted device that reduces seizures in some people who do not respond to medicines
    \item \textbf{Epilepsy surgery:} removal of a seizure focus, which can cure selected people with focal epilepsy
    \item \textbf{Ketogenic diet:} a specialised high-fat diet effective in some children with difficult seizures
    \item \textbf{First aid for seizures:} protect the person from injury, place them on their side, do not restrain them, and time the seizure
    \item \textbf{Psychological and social support:} counselling, epilepsy nurse support, and education for school and workplace
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Injury from falls and seizures, including head injury, burns, and fractures
    \item Drowning, a leading cause of death in epilepsy, often in the bath or while swimming unsupervised
    \item Status epilepticus, a prolonged seizure that is a medical emergency with risk of brain damage
    \item Sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP), rare but more common with poorly controlled seizures
    \item Depression and anxiety, which affect a large proportion of people with epilepsy
    \item Side effects of medicines, including drowsiness, dizziness, and effects on bone health with long-term use
    \item Impaired driving, employment, and social participation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Around two-thirds of people with epilepsy become seizure-free with antiseizure medicines, and many can eventually stop treatment after several seizure-free years. Another group achieves good control with combinations of medicines, while roughly one-third continue to have seizures despite treatment; for some of these people, surgery or device therapy is highly effective. The prognosis is influenced by the underlying cause, seizure type, and age of onset. With good seizure control, life expectancy is close to normal, and people with epilepsy can study, work, drive, and raise families. The key to good outcomes is early specialist assessment, correct seizure classification, and honest attention to safety and mental health.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take antiseizure medicines exactly as prescribed and never stop abruptly, as this can trigger status epilepticus
    \item Prioritise sleep and avoid excessive alcohol, both common seizure triggers
    \item Follow driving rules and never drive after a seizure until the doctor confirms it is safe
    \item Shower instead of bathing, and never swim alone
    \item Wear a medical alert bracelet and tell close contacts how to help during a seizure
    \item Keep a seizure diary to help the doctor adjust treatment
    \item Protect the head in sports where head injury is likely, and seek urgent care for head injuries
    \item Speak openly about mental health and seek treatment for depression or anxiety
    \item Prevent head injury in the broader population through seatbelt use, helmets, and safe workplaces
\end{itemize}
