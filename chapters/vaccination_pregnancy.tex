\chapter{Vaccination pregnancy}
\index{Vaccination pregnancy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Vaccination during pregnancy is the administration of safe and recommended vaccines to a pregnant woman to protect both her and her baby. Two vaccines are routinely recommended in every pregnancy in many countries: the influenza (flu) vaccine and the whooping cough (pertussis) vaccine, given in the combination dTpa (diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis) formulation. Other vaccines may be recommended during pregnancy where there is specific risk, including hepatitis B, COVID-19, and, in outbreak situations or travel, hepatitis A, meningococcal, or other vaccines. Vaccination in pregnancy works in two ways. It directly protects the mother from serious illness during pregnancy, when the immune system and lungs are under extra strain, and it protects the newborn baby, because the mother's antibodies cross the placenta and provide passive immunity during the first months of life, when the infant is too young to be vaccinated. Whooping cough and influenza are both particularly dangerous for young infants, making pregnancy vaccination one of the most effective ways to protect them. The vaccines used in pregnancy are inactivated, meaning they do not contain live viruses, and they have been shown over many years and in many studies to be safe for both the mother and the developing baby.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Influenza and whooping cough are common and can be severe in both pregnant women and newborn babies. Pregnant women are at increased risk of severe influenza, with higher rates of hospitalisation and complications such as pneumonia, particularly in the later stages of pregnancy, and influenza in pregnancy is associated with an increased risk of preterm birth. Whooping cough is highly contagious and can be fatal in young babies, who are most at risk before they receive their own first dose at six weeks of age. In many countries, whooping cough epidemics occur in cycles of several years, and the majority of whooping cough deaths in recent decades have occurred in babies under three months of age. Maternal vaccination has been shown to prevent the great majority of infant deaths from whooping cough in this vulnerable window. Despite this, coverage of maternal vaccination varies: influenza vaccination in pregnancy in many countries has historically been around 50--70\%, and pertussis vaccination around 70--85\%, with considerable variation between regions and population groups. This means there is room to improve uptake and protection. Barriers include not being offered the vaccine, uncertainty about safety, and practical difficulties in accessing vaccination; addressing these is an important part of antenatal care.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The vaccines recommended in pregnancy protect against infections caused by specific viruses and bacteria.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Influenza virus:} a respiratory virus that changes each year, causing seasonal epidemics; pregnant women are more vulnerable to severe disease because pregnancy alters immune function and increases the work of the heart and lungs
    \item \textbf{Bordetella pertussis:} the bacterium that causes whooping cough; it is spread by coughing and produces a toxin that damages the airways, causing the characteristic severe coughing bouts
    \item \textbf{Diphtheria and tetanus:} bacterial diseases prevented by the same dTpa vaccine; tetanus can occur through contaminated wounds
    \item \textbf{Hepatitis B:} a virus transmitted by blood and body fluids; vaccination is recommended in pregnancy when the woman is unvaccinated, is at risk of exposure, or is living with hepatitis B, to help prevent transmission to the baby, and all babies receive the hepatitis B vaccine shortly after birth
    \item \textbf{COVID-19:} the SARS-CoV-2 virus can cause severe illness in pregnant women, and vaccination is recommended at any stage of pregnancy, particularly because pregnant women are at higher risk of intensive care admission and preterm birth with COVID-19
    \item \textbf{Other travel and risk vaccines:} hepatitis A, meningococcal, and a few other vaccines may be given in pregnancy when a woman is travelling or otherwise at specific risk, based on individual risk assessment
\end{itemize}

The key mechanism of benefit is antibody transfer. From around the middle of pregnancy, maternal antibodies are actively transported across the placenta to the fetus. Vaccination during pregnancy ensures a high level of these protective antibodies, which then protect the newborn until the infant's own immune system matures and their scheduled vaccinations begin.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Vaccination in pregnancy is preventive, so the relevant clinical features are those of the diseases being prevented. Understanding how serious these infections can be in pregnancy helps explain why the vaccines are so strongly recommended.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Influenza in pregnancy causes fever, cough, sore throat, muscle aches, and fatigue, and can progress to pneumonia and severe breathing difficulty
    \item Whooping cough in a young baby causes episodes of coughing and gagging, sometimes with a whoop, and babies may stop breathing (apnoea) during coughing bouts
    \item Whooping cough in the mother causes prolonged, exhausting coughing that can last many weeks and disrupt sleep and daily life
    \item Tetanus causes painful muscle spasms and stiffness and is a serious, potentially fatal illness
    \item Hepatitis B infection can be silent but can lead to chronic liver disease and liver cancer
    \item COVID-19 in pregnancy causes respiratory illness that may be severe and is associated with an increased risk of preterm birth
\end{itemize}

Vaccine reactions themselves are generally mild and short-lived, most commonly a sore arm, tiredness, or a low-grade fever lasting a day or two.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The conditions that vaccination prevents overlap with other common illnesses in pregnancy, so a diagnosis is confirmed by testing.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Influenza-like illness can be caused by many respiratory viruses, including RSV, rhinovirus, and COVID-19
    \item Whooping cough may be confused with other causes of a persistent cough, including asthma, viral bronchitis, and reflux
    \item Tetanus is now extremely rare, but any deep or dirty wound should prompt assessment of vaccination status
    \item Hepatitis B may be detected during routine antenatal blood screening
    \item Fever in pregnancy may arise from urinary tract infection, other infections, or non-infectious causes
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Pregnant women should be offered influenza and whooping cough vaccination at every opportunity from the recommended time in pregnancy. The pertussis vaccine is usually given between 20 and 32 weeks of pregnancy, and the influenza vaccine can be given at any stage of pregnancy, ideally before the flu season. A woman who has not yet received these vaccines should discuss them with her doctor, midwife, or vaccination provider, and vaccination can still be given after this window if it has been missed, ideally before the baby is born.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, chest pain, or a very high fever during pregnancy
    \item A baby showing signs of whooping cough, including severe coughing, gagging, turning blue, or stopping breathing
    \item Signs of an allergic reaction to a vaccine, which are extremely rare but require urgent care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Before vaccination, a clinician reviews the woman's vaccination history, pregnancy stage, and any medical conditions. Where a vaccine-preventable disease is suspected, testing is arranged.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vaccination history:} confirmation of previous doses of influenza, dTpa, hepatitis B, and COVID-19 vaccines, so that the right doses are given
    \item \textbf{Antenatal blood tests:} screening for hepatitis B and other infections is routine in early pregnancy, and results guide hepatitis B vaccination and care
    \item \textbf{Nasal or throat swabs:} PCR testing for influenza, whooping cough, and COVID-19 if symptoms develop
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} advice about the timing and scheduling of vaccination doses and catch-up vaccination, and a reminder that the dTpa vaccine is recommended in every pregnancy, not just the first
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management centres on vaccination at the recommended times, together with general health measures.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Influenza vaccine:} recommended at any stage of pregnancy, ideally before or during flu season, and safe in all trimesters
    \item \textbf{dTpa (whooping cough) vaccine:} recommended between 20 and 32 weeks of pregnancy for every pregnancy, so that high antibody levels are passed to the baby
    \item \textbf{COVID-19 vaccine:} recommended at any stage of pregnancy, with boosters as advised
    \item \textbf{Hepatitis B vaccine:} offered where indicated by risk or screening results
    \item \textbf{Catch-up vaccination:} missed or delayed vaccines should be given at the next opportunity, including soon after birth
    \item \textbf{Partner and household vaccination:} encouraging others in the household to be vaccinated against whooping cough and influenza reduces the baby's exposure after birth
    \item \textbf{General measures:} good hygiene, handwashing, avoiding contact with people who are unwell, and a healthy lifestyle, which reduce the chance of being exposed to infection in the first place
    \item \textbf{Documentation:} recording each vaccine dose in the antenatal record and on the local Immunisation Register so that the baby's vaccination history is complete
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe influenza in pregnancy, including pneumonia, hospitalisation, and the need for intensive care
    \item Preterm birth and low birth weight associated with severe influenza and COVID-19 in pregnancy
    \item Whooping cough in a newborn, which can cause apnoea, pneumonia, brain damage, and death
    \item Prolonged, exhausting whooping cough illness in the mother, which can last many weeks
    \item Chronic hepatitis B infection and its long-term complications if infection occurs
    \item Missed opportunities to protect the baby because vaccines were not offered or given on time
    \item Unvaccinated mothers also remain vulnerable to tetanus if they sustain a wound during or after pregnancy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Vaccination during pregnancy is highly effective and very safe. Influenza vaccination in pregnancy substantially reduces the risk of influenza illness and hospitalisation in both mother and baby. Pertussis vaccination in pregnancy is more than 90\% effective at preventing infant deaths from whooping cough and also prevents most whooping cough hospitalisations in babies under three months of age. The antibodies a mother passes to her baby from dTpa vaccination last through the critical early weeks, when the baby cannot yet be vaccinated. Extensive safety studies over many decades and in many countries have found no increased risk of miscarriage, birth defects, or adverse pregnancy outcomes associated with influenza or pertussis vaccination. Babies born to vaccinated mothers have the best possible start, protected against two of the most dangerous infections of early infancy.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Have the influenza vaccine every year during pregnancy, at any stage of pregnancy
    \item Have the dTpa vaccine between 20 and 32 weeks of every pregnancy, as recommended
    \item Ask for COVID-19 vaccination and boosters if you have not yet had them
    \item Ensure hepatitis B screening and vaccination are up to date, so that the baby can be protected from birth
    \item Ask household members and carers to be vaccinated against whooping cough and influenza
    \item Practise good hand hygiene and avoid contact with people who have coughs, colds, or fevers
    \item Report any pregnancy and vaccination queries to your doctor or midwife, who can provide accurate, individual advice
    \item Ask your midwife or doctor at every antenatal visit whether you are due for any vaccine, so that no opportunity is missed
\end{itemize}
