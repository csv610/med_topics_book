\chapter{Diabetes Medicines}
\index{Diabetes Medicines}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Diabetes medicines are medications used to control blood glucose levels in people with diabetes. In type 1 diabetes, the body cannot produce insulin, so insulin therapy is essential from diagnosis. In type 2 diabetes, medicines are used alongside lifestyle measures such as healthy eating, exercise, and weight management, and treatment is stepped up over time as the condition progresses. There are several classes of diabetes medicines, including metformin, sulfonylureas, newer injectable agents, and insulin, and the choice depends on the type of diabetes, blood glucose levels, other health conditions, and individual circumstances. This chapter explains the main types of diabetes medicines and how they are used.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Diabetes affects around one in fifteen people, and the vast majority have type 2 diabetes. About 85\% of people with type 2 diabetes are overweight or obese, and rates rise with age. Around 10--15\% of people with type 2 diabetes eventually need insulin. The number of people using diabetes medicines has grown substantially, with metformin the most commonly prescribed drug and newer agents such as GLP-1 receptor agonists now widely used. Diabetes medicines are among the most prescribed medications in many countries through the public medicines subsidy.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Insulin deficiency:} in type 1 diabetes, autoimmune destruction of the insulin-producing beta cells means no insulin is produced
    \item \textbf{Insulin resistance:} in type 2 diabetes, body tissues respond poorly to insulin, and beta cells gradually fail
    \item \textbf{Metformin:} reduces glucose production by the liver and improves insulin sensitivity
    \item \textbf{Sulfonylureas:} stimulate the pancreas to release more insulin
    \item \textbf{GLP-1 receptor agonists:} mimic a gut hormone, increasing insulin release, slowing stomach emptying, and reducing appetite
    \item \textbf{SGLT2 inhibitors:} cause the kidneys to excrete glucose in the urine
    \item \textbf{Insulin:} replaces the body's own insulin, given by injection or pump
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The goal of treatment is to keep blood glucose in the target range to prevent symptoms and complications
    \item Medicines may be started when lifestyle measures alone do not control blood glucose
    \item Blood glucose monitoring, including HbA1c tests every 3--6 months, assesses how well treatment is working
    \item Some medicines cause weight loss, while others (such as sulfonylureas and insulin) may cause weight gain
    \item Hypoglycaemia (low blood glucose) can occur with sulfonylureas and insulin, causing sweating, tremor, confusion, and collapse
    \item Doses may need adjustment during illness, pregnancy, or changes in diet and activity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Type 1 versus type 2 diabetes, which determines the treatment approach
    \item Other conditions requiring insulin or other agents, such as gestational diabetes and steroid-induced diabetes
    \item Secondary causes of hyperglycaemia, including pancreatic disease and medications
    \item Choice between medicine classes, guided by cardiovascular and kidney risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with diabetes should have regular reviews with their doctor and a diabetes educator. Seek medical advice if blood glucose levels are consistently out of range, if symptoms such as thirst, frequent urination, or fatigue develop, or before starting, stopping, or changing any diabetes medicine.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe hypoglycaemia: confusion, drowsiness, seizures, or unconsciousness (treat with fast-acting glucose if able)
    \item Signs of diabetic ketoacidosis: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, rapid breathing, and fruity-smelling breath
    \item Hyperglycaemic hyperosmolar state: extreme thirst, confusion, and drowsiness
    \item Any sudden severe illness with diabetes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood glucose tests:} fasting and post-meal glucose levels
    \item \textbf{HbA1c:} reflects average blood glucose over the previous 2--3 months and guides treatment
    \item \textbf{Kidney and liver function tests:} some diabetes medicines require dose adjustment or are unsuitable in kidney disease
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular risk assessment:} blood pressure, cholesterol, and risk factors guide medicine choice
    \item \textbf{Monitoring for side effects:} regular review for effects such as gastrointestinal upset, weight change, and hypoglycaemia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Metformin:} usually the first medicine for type 2 diabetes, taken with food to reduce stomach upset
    \item \textbf{Sulfonylureas:} such as gliclazide, useful when metformin alone is not enough, with hypoglycaemia risk
    \item \textbf{GLP-1 receptor agonists:} such as semaglutide and dulaglutide, injectable agents that lower glucose and support weight loss
    \item \textbf{SGLT2 inhibitors:} such as empagliflozin and dapagliflozin, which also protect the heart and kidneys
    \item \textbf{DPP-4 inhibitors:} oral agents with a low risk of hypoglycaemia
    \item \textbf{Insulin:} essential in type 1 diabetes and used in type 2 when other agents do not control glucose
    \item \textbf{Combination therapy:} many people need two or more medicines over time
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} healthy eating, physical activity, and weight management remain fundamental at every stage
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hypoglycaemia with sulfonylureas and insulin
    \item Gastrointestinal side effects with metformin and GLP-1 agents
    \item Weight gain with sulfonylureas and insulin
    \item Genital infections with SGLT2 inhibitors
    \item Diabetic ketoacidosis if insulin is missed or doses are incorrect
    \item Long-term complications of poorly controlled diabetes, including eye, kidney, nerve, and heart disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Diabetes medicines, combined with lifestyle measures, are highly effective at controlling blood glucose and preventing complications. Modern treatments also reduce the risk of heart attack, stroke, kidney failure, and premature death. Many people with type 2 diabetes achieve remission with substantial weight loss and can reduce or stop medicines. With regular monitoring and appropriate treatment, most people with diabetes live long, healthy, and active lives.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take diabetes medicines exactly as prescribed, at the right times
    \item Learn how to recognise and treat hypoglycaemia
    \item Monitor blood glucose as advised and keep a record
    \item Attend regular reviews of HbA1c, blood pressure, cholesterol, and kidney function
    \item Eat a healthy diet, stay active, and aim for a healthy weight
    \item Never stop or change insulin or other diabetes medicines without medical advice
    \item See a pharmacist or doctor before taking any new medicine, as interactions can occur
\end{itemize}
