\chapter{Child Mental Health}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Child mental health refers to the emotional, psychological, and social wellbeing of children and adolescents, and to the mental health conditions that can affect them. Good mental health allows a child to learn, play, form friendships, manage emotions, and cope with the normal stresses of growing up. Mental health problems in childhood range from common difficulties such as anxiety, low mood, and behaviour problems to more serious conditions including depression, eating disorders, obsessive--compulsive disorder, and post-traumatic stress disorder. Many mental health problems begin in childhood or adolescence, and early recognition, support, and treatment can make a lasting difference to a young person's life.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Mental health problems are common in childhood and adolescence. Roughly 1 in 7 children and young people experiences a mental health condition, and most adult mental health disorders begin before age 25. Anxiety disorders are among the most common, followed by attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), behaviour problems, and depression, which becomes more frequent in the teenage years. Boys are more often diagnosed with ADHD and behaviour disorders in childhood, while depression and anxiety become more common in girls during adolescence. Bullying, family conflict, academic pressure, and heavy social media use are frequent triggers. Only a minority of affected children receive specialist help, which makes recognition by families, teachers, and general practitioners especially important.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Mental health problems in children arise from a mix of biological, psychological, and social factors:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetics and temperament:} a family history of mental illness and an anxious or sensitive temperament increase risk
    \item \textbf{Brain development:} differences in how brain circuits for emotion, reward, and attention develop contribute to conditions such as ADHD and anxiety
    \item \textbf{Family factors:} parental mental illness, conflict, harsh discipline, or neglect
    \item \textbf{Stressful events:} bullying, bereavement, parental separation, serious illness, or abuse
    \item \textbf{School and social pressures:} exam stress, friendship difficulties, and social media use
    \item \textbf{Physical health:} chronic illness, poor sleep, and substance use can worsen mental health
\end{itemize}

For most children, no single cause is found -- the condition reflects the way these factors interact over time, often beginning in response to a stressor and then persisting.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms vary with age and condition, but common signs include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent sadness, irritability, or tearfulness
    \item Excessive worry, fear, or avoidance of school and social situations
    \item Loss of interest in activities the child used to enjoy
    \item Withdrawal from family and friends
    \item Changes in sleep or appetite, including trouble sleeping and fatigue
    \item Falling grades, difficulty concentrating, or refusal to attend school
    \item Tantrums, aggression, or behaviour problems out of keeping with the child's age
    \item Physical complaints such as headaches and stomach aches without a clear cause
    \item Self-harm, or thoughts of death and suicide in older children
\end{itemize}

Symptoms are generally persistent rather than passing, and they interfere with the child's daily life, relationships, or schoolwork.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Normal developmental phases, such as toddler tantrums or teenage moodiness, compared with a mental health condition
    \item Anxiety, ADHD, and depression, which can look similar in children and require careful assessment
    \item Physical illness, such as an underactive thyroid or anaemia, causing low energy and mood changes
    \item Medication side effects or substance use in adolescents
    \item The effects of bullying, grief, or family stress, which may need support rather than a medical diagnosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if a child's distress is persistent, is affecting school, friendships, or family life, or if you are worried about their behaviour, mood, or sleep. Ask the school counsellor, a mental health service, or your GP for advice if you are unsure where to start.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A child who talks about wanting to die or hurt themselves
    \item A child who has harmed themselves deliberately
    \item Sudden, dangerous behaviour or signs of losing touch with reality
\end{itemize}

Helplines such as Lifeline (13 11 14) and the Kids Helpline (1800 55 1800) provide immediate support and advice.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} a careful discussion with the child and parents about symptoms, their timing, school, friendships, and family circumstances
    \item \textbf{Observation and examination:} how the child behaves, relates, and communicates during the visit
    \item \textbf{Questionnaires:} parent- and child-completed rating scales for anxiety, depression, and behaviour problems
    \item \textbf{School input:} information from teachers about behaviour, concentration, and learning
    \item \textbf{Physical checks:} blood tests may be used to exclude physical causes such as thyroid problems or anaemia
    \item \textbf{Specialist assessment:} referral to a child and adolescent mental health service or paediatrician for complex or severe cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychological therapy:} cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT) and other evidence-based talking therapies are first-line treatment for anxiety and depression
    \item \textbf{Parent support and training:} programs that help parents manage behaviour, reduce conflict, and build positive relationships
    \item \textbf{School-based support:} adjustments, counselling, and wellbeing programs within the school setting
    \item \textbf{Medication:} sometimes used for conditions such as ADHD or severe depression, prescribed and monitored by a specialist doctor
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} regular sleep, physical activity, time outdoors, and reduced screen time
    \item \textbf{Family involvement:} working with parents and carers is central to most child mental health treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Struggles at school, including falling behind academically or leaving school early
    \item Difficulty making and keeping friends; social isolation
    \item Self-harm and risk of suicide -- the most serious complications
    \item Development of further mental health problems in adulthood
    \item Substance use in adolescents as a way of coping
    \item Strain on family relationships and increased stress for parents and siblings
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Many children with mental health problems recover fully, especially when difficulties are identified early and the family is supported. Anxiety and mild depression often respond well to psychological therapy. Conditions such as ADHD and obsessive--compulsive disorder may persist but can be managed well, allowing children to succeed at school and in relationships. Some children carry vulnerability into adult life, so ongoing monitoring and support are important. Early treatment is consistently associated with better long-term outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Spend regular, undivided time with your child and listen without judging
    \item Encourage healthy sleep, exercise, and a balanced diet
    \item Limit screen time and monitor social media use
    \item Keep routines predictable, including around meals and bedtime
    \item Praise effort and behaviour you want to encourage
    \item Take talk of suicide or self-harm seriously and seek help immediately
    \item Look after your own wellbeing -- children learn coping from the adults around them
    \item Talk to your GP early if you have concerns; do not wait for problems to escalate
\end{itemize}
