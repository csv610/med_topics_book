\chapter{Jet Lag}
\index{Jet Lag}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Jet lag is a temporary sleep and performance disturbance that occurs when you travel quickly across several time zones, so that your internal body clock (circadian rhythm) is out of step with the local day-night cycle. It causes difficulty sleeping at the right time, daytime fatigue, poor concentration, and digestive symptoms. Jet lag is not dangerous, but it can spoil the start of a trip and impair performance on business or sporting travel. It is caused by a mismatch between the body clock and local time, and it usually settles as the clock adjusts at a rate of about one day per time zone crossed. Planning with light exposure, sleep timing, and melatonin can minimise its effects.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Jet lag affects most people who travel across three or more time zones, and the effects are worse with eastward travel, more zones crossed, and older age. It is most disruptive for frequent flyers, business travellers, pilots and cabin crew, athletes, and shift workers who also travel. Around nine in ten long-haul travellers report at least some symptoms. The effects last longest after eastward travel, where the body clock must advance, compared with westward travel, where it delays more easily.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The body's circadian clock, located in the brain's suprachiasmatic nucleus, drives daily rhythms of sleepiness, alertness, body temperature, and hormone release, synchronised to light through the eyes. When you travel across time zones, the clock cannot reset instantly, so it continues to run on home time while the environment runs on local time. The result is a mismatch: you feel sleepy when it is daytime locally and alert when it is night, and sleep becomes shallow and fragmented. The clock shifts at about one hour per day, faster for westward travel and slower for eastward, and the symptoms persist until it catches up. Light is the strongest signal for resetting the clock.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty falling asleep or waking at the local time
    \item Daytime fatigue, sleepiness, and a need to nap
    \item Poor concentration, memory, and performance
    \item Irritability and low mood
    \item Headache and a general feeling of being unwell
    \item Digestive symptoms: poor appetite, nausea, and irregular bowel habit
    \item Waking in the night and early waking
    \item Symptoms that are worse after eastward travel and with more time zones crossed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Similar symptoms after travel may have other causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Travel fatigue:} tiredness from the journey itself, which settles within a day or two
    \item \textbf{Sleep deprivation:} inadequate sleep before or during travel
    \item \textbf{Shift work disorder:} for workers whose schedules change regularly
    \item \textbf{Altitude and dehydration:} common travel-related causes of headache and fatigue
    \item \textbf{Travellers' illness:} infection causing fever and fatigue
    \item \textbf{Mood and stress:} anxiety or low mood affecting sleep
    \item \textbf{Insomnia:} if sleep problems persist long after arrival
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Jet lag does not usually need medical care, but people with persistent sleep problems, severe fatigue, or concerning symptoms should seek advice. Frequent travellers and shift workers who are struggling may benefit from specialist sleep assessment. Anyone with fever, chest symptoms, or other signs of illness after travel should be assessed, as should those returning from areas with infectious disease risk.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, difficulty breathing, or collapse during or after travel, which may indicate a blood clot or heart problem
    \item Leg pain, swelling, or redness after a long flight, which may indicate deep vein thrombosis
    \item Confusion, severe headache, or neurological symptoms
    \item High fever after travel to a tropical area
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} travel pattern, time zones crossed, symptoms, and sleep
    \item \textbf{Sleep diary:} tracking sleep timing before and after travel
    \item \textbf{Assessment of other causes:} fatigue, infection, and travel-related illness
    \item \textbf{Sleep studies:} rarely needed, for persistent or severe problems
    \item \textbf{Planning tools:} apps and schedules to plan light exposure and sleep
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Light exposure:} the most powerful tool; seek bright light at the right local times and avoid it at others
    \item \textbf{Timed sleep:} going to bed at the local time and using an alarm to wake
    \item \textbf{Melatonin:} a short course, taken at the local bedtime, can help reset the clock
    \item \textbf{Naps:} a short early-afternoon nap can help, avoiding long naps that delay adjustment
    \item \textbf{Caffeine:} used strategically in the morning but avoided late in the day
    \item \textbf{Meals and hydration:} eating at local mealtimes and drinking plenty of water
    \item \textbf{Gradual adjustment before travel:} shifting sleep times towards the destination in the days before departure
    \item \textbf{Exercise and activity:} staying active during local daytime
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Impaired performance on business, driving, or sport
    \item Poor concentration and increased risk of accidents
    \item Mood changes and irritability
    \item Digestive disturbance
    \item Sleep problems persisting after return
    \item Worsening of sleep disorders in susceptible people
    \item Deep vein thrombosis from long flights, which is a separate risk but more likely with prolonged immobility
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Jet lag is self-limiting and resolves as the body clock adjusts, at roughly one day per time zone crossed, usually within a few days. Westward travel is easier to adapt to than eastward. With planned light exposure, timed sleep, and melatonin, most travellers can reduce the severity and duration of symptoms significantly. Frequent travellers often develop personal strategies that work well for them.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Shift your sleep schedule towards the destination time in the days before travel
    \item Seek sunlight at the right times after arrival to help your clock adjust
    \item Go to bed at the local time, even if you are not sleepy
    \item Use a short course of melatonin at the local bedtime if advised
    \item Take short naps if needed, but avoid sleeping through local daytime
    \item Use caffeine early in the day only
    \item Eat meals at local times and stay hydrated
    \item Keep moving during the flight and after arrival
    \item Give yourself a day or two to adjust before important commitments
    \item For long flights, consider compression stockings and regular movement to reduce clot risk
\end{itemize}
