\chapter{Caesarean Section}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A caesarean section, commonly called a C-section, is a surgical operation in which a baby is delivered through an incision made in the mother's lower abdomen and uterus, rather than through the birth canal. It may be planned in advance (an elective caesarean) because of a known risk to the mother or baby, or performed as an emergency when complications arise during labour.

In most cases the lower uterine segment is used, and the mother remains awake under regional anaesthesia, which allows her to be awake for the birth and to have skin-to-skin contact soon afterwards. A general anaesthetic is sometimes required, particularly in emergencies. Caesarean section is a safe and common operation, but it carries different risks from vaginal birth and is usually recommended only when the benefits outweigh those risks.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Caesarean section is one of the most common operations performed worldwide. In Australia, roughly one in three births is by caesarean section, and rates have risen steadily over recent decades. The procedure is more common in older mothers, in multiple pregnancies such as twins or triplets, and after a previous caesarean. Rates vary widely between countries and hospitals, reflecting differences in clinical practice, maternal age, and the management of high-risk pregnancies.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
A caesarean is performed when vaginal birth is considered unsafe for the mother, the baby, or both. Common reasons include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Failed or slow progress of labour:} when the cervix stops dilating or the baby does not descend despite adequate contractions
    \item \textbf{Fetal distress:} signs that the baby is not tolerating labour, such as an abnormal heart rate pattern
    \item \textbf{Breech or transverse presentation:} the baby lying bottom-first or sideways rather than head-first
    \item \textbf{Placental problems:} placenta praevia (the placenta covering the cervix) or placental abruption
    \item \textbf{Previous caesarean:} some women with a prior caesarean choose or are advised to have another
    \item \textbf{Maternal conditions:} severe pre-eclampsia, active genital herpes, or medical conditions that make labour risky
    \item \textbf{Large baby or obstructed labour:} when the baby is too large to pass safely through the pelvis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The procedure follows a characteristic sequence, and post-operative recovery follows a typical pattern:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{During the operation:} the mother is awake with a spinal or epidural block, or asleep under general anaesthesia; an incision is made across the lower abdomen and uterus, and the baby is delivered within minutes
    \item \textbf{Early recovery:} pain and tenderness at the wound, some vaginal bleeding (lochia) for several weeks, and fatigue
    \item \textbf{Wound:} a dressing over the lower abdominal incision, usually closed with dissolving stitches
    \item \textbf{Mobility:} early movement and breastfeeding are encouraged, but heavy lifting and driving are restricted for several weeks
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal effects:} reduced appetite and constipation are common in the first days after surgery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
After a caesarean, new symptoms must be distinguished from normal recovery by considering complications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wound infection versus the normal redness and soreness of healing
    \item Heavy or infected bleeding versus the usual lochia
    \item Deep vein thrombosis versus ordinary calf aching after surgery
    \item Chest infection or pulmonary embolism versus the breathlessness of post-partum recovery
    \item Endometritis (infection of the uterus) versus ordinary pelvic pain and fever
    \item Urinary infection or retention versus normal post-operative discomfort
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Women should contact their maternity team or seek urgent care for any of the following after a caesarean:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wound pain, redness, swelling, or discharge that is worsening, which may indicate a wound infection
    \item Heavy vaginal bleeding or large blood clots, or a wound that is opening
    \item Fever, chills, or feeling generally unwell
    \item Severe or worsening abdominal pain, or pain on passing urine
    \item Chest pain, shortness of breath, or a swollen, painful calf
    \item A painful, red, or hard area on the breast with fever, which may indicate mastitis
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:} heavy bleeding that soaks a pad quickly, difficulty breathing, chest pain, or collapse.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis of caesarean complications relies on clinical assessment and targeted tests:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} checking the wound, abdomen, uterus, and vaginal loss
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count and inflammatory markers to detect infection or significant blood loss
    \item \textbf{Wound swab:} taken if the incision looks infected
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} used to check the uterus for retained tissue or fluid collections
    \item \textbf{Urine testing:} if a urinary tract infection is suspected
    \item \textbf{Imaging of the legs or chest:} ultrasound or CT if a blood clot is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Care of a woman having a caesarean extends from preparation before the operation through to recovery at home:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Birth planning:} discussion of the reason for the caesarean, the procedure itself, and options for subsequent births
    \item \textbf{Anaesthesia:} usually a spinal or epidural block; general anaesthesia if required
    \item \textbf{Complication prevention:} antibiotics before the skin incision and medicines or devices to reduce the risk of blood clots
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} regular paracetamol and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medicines, with stronger options if needed
    \item \textbf{Wound care:} keeping the dressing clean and dry, and having clips removed if they do not dissolve
    \item \textbf{Support:} midwives and lactation consultants assist with feeding and recovery, and physiotherapy supports early mobilisation
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} a postnatal review to check wound healing, blood loss, and emotional wellbeing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
A caesarean is generally safe, but it carries more risk than an uncomplicated vaginal birth:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection of the wound, uterus, or bladder
    \item Heavy bleeding and anaemia, occasionally requiring blood transfusion
    \item Blood clots in the legs (deep vein thrombosis) or lungs (pulmonary embolism)
    \item Damage to the bladder, ureters, or bowel during surgery, which is uncommon
    \item Adhesions and scar tissue, which can affect future pregnancies
    \item A longer recovery and hospital stay compared with vaginal birth
    \item Slightly higher risks in future pregnancies, including uterine rupture during a later labour
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most women recover well from a caesarean and are usually home within a few days. The wound typically heals within one to two weeks, although full recovery from the surgery takes around six weeks, and many women notice numbness or tingling around the scar for some months. Outcomes for the baby are excellent when the caesarean is performed for an appropriate reason. Future pregnancies are usually managed carefully, and some women are able to have a vaginal birth after a previous caesarean.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rest and accept help while recovering, and avoid heavy lifting and strenuous activity for about six weeks
    \item Keep the wound clean and dry, and report any redness, discharge, or opening of the wound promptly
    \item Take pain relief regularly as advised so you can move and care for your baby comfortably
    \item Move about gently to reduce the risk of blood clots, and do the exercises recommended by your midwife or physiotherapist
    \item Drink plenty of fluids and eat fibre-rich food to avoid constipation
    \item Discuss contraception and plans for any future pregnancy with your doctor
    \item Seek help early if you feel low, anxious, or overwhelmed, as postnatal depression is common and treatable
\end{itemize}
