\chapter{Engorgement}
\index{Engorgement}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Breast engorgement is the painful overfilling of the breasts with milk and fluid that occurs when milk production increases after childbirth. It usually develops two to five days after the baby is born, as the milk "comes in," and it can make the breasts swollen, hard, hot, and tender. Engorgement is common and uncomfortable, but it settles as breastfeeding becomes established and the breast is drained effectively. Good management prevents complications such as blocked ducts and mastitis.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Breast engorgement affects a large proportion of new mothers, with most experiencing some degree of fullness and a significant minority developing severe engorgement. It is more likely when breastfeeding starts late, when feeds are restricted or missed, and in first-time mothers. With good support and feeding practices, engorgement usually resolves within a few days, and it is not a reason to stop breastfeeding.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Engorgement is caused by the sudden increase in milk production and blood flow to the breasts.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Milk coming in:} two to five days after birth, hormone changes trigger a large increase in milk production
    \item \textbf{Fluid accumulation:} increased blood and lymph flow to the breasts add to the swelling
    \item \textbf{Failed drainage:} if the baby is not feeding well or frequently enough, milk accumulates and the pressure rises
    \item \textbf{Effects:} the swollen breast tissue presses on the milk ducts, making milk flow more difficult, which worsens the engorgement
    \item \textbf{Consequences:} severe engorgement can make it hard for the baby to latch, and blocked ducts and mastitis can follow
    \item \textbf{Prevention:} frequent feeding from birth is the most effective way to prevent severe engorgement
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Engorgement is usually easy to recognise.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Breasts that feel full, heavy, hard, and warm
    \item Tenderness and pain
    \item Flattened nipples, which make latching difficult
    \item Slight fever and general malaise in severe cases
    \item Difficulty expressing or feeding
    \item The breasts may appear shiny and stretched
    \item The baby may be unsettled and feed poorly
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Engorgement can be confused with other breast conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Blocked milk duct, with a localised tender lump
    \item Mastitis, an infection of the breast, which causes fever, redness, and flu-like symptoms
    \item Breast abscess, a collection of pus
    \item Physiological breast fullness, which is normal and mild
    \item Other causes of breast pain, including fibrocystic change
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most engorgement is managed at home, but medical advice should be sought if fever develops, if there is a persistent painful lump, if the breast becomes red and hot, or if feeding problems continue.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item High fever with a red, hot, painful breast, which may indicate mastitis
    \item Signs of a breast abscess, with severe pain and swelling
    \item Difficulty feeding the baby with signs of dehydration
    \item A very unwell mother
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Engorgement is diagnosed clinically.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the timing relative to birth and the appearance of the breasts
    \item \textbf{Assessment of feeding:} how the baby is latching and feeding
    \item \textbf{Exclusion of mastitis:} checking for fever, redness, and infection
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} only if an abscess is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management relieves the pain and drains the breast effectively.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Frequent feeding:} feed the baby often, including at night, and do not skip feeds
    \item \textbf{Good positioning:} ensure a deep latch; the baby can be fed lying back, or hand expression can help soften the breast first
    \item \textbf{Cold packs:} applied after feeds to reduce swelling and pain
    \item \textbf{Warmth before feeds:} gentle warmth or a warm shower before feeding can help milk flow
    \item \textbf{Gentle massage:} massage towards the nipple during feeds
    \item \textbf{Hand expression:} express a little milk to soften the areola for latching
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} paracetamol or ibuprofen, which are safe while breastfeeding
    \item \textbf{Support:} lactation consultants and midwives provide practical help
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Blocked milk ducts
    \item Mastitis, with fever and flu-like symptoms
    \item Breast abscess in rare cases
    \item Feeding difficulties and nipple damage from poor latch
    \item Early weaning if the experience is distressing
    \item The baby not gaining weight if feeding is ineffective
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Engorgement settles in the vast majority of women within a few days as milk supply regulates and feeding becomes established. It does not harm the baby or the breast, and it is not a reason to stop breastfeeding. With good support, most women manage the engorgement and continue breastfeeding successfully.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Start breastfeeding early and feed frequently from birth
    \item Feed on demand, including at night
    \item Ensure a good latch with the help of midwives or lactation consultants
    \item Avoid restricting feeds, giving supplements, or substituting with bottles early on
    \item Use cold packs after feeds and warmth before
    \item Wear a well-fitting, supportive bra
    \item Seek help early if feeding is painful or the breasts become engorged
\end{itemize}
