\chapter{Anthrax}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Anthrax is a rare but serious infectious disease caused by the bacterium \textit{Bacillus anthracis}. The organism forms tough, dormant spores that can survive in soil, wool, hair, and animal hides for many years, and infection occurs when spores enter the body through the skin, the lungs, or the digestive tract.

Anthrax primarily affects livestock, but humans can acquire it through contact with infected animals or contaminated animal products such as hides, wool, bone meal, or meat. There are four main clinical forms -- cutaneous (skin), inhalational (lungs), gastrointestinal, and injection anthrax -- and the severity ranges from a mild, self-limiting skin sore to a rapidly fatal bloodstream infection. With early recognition and treatment, most forms respond well to antibiotics, but inhalational and gastrointestinal anthrax are medical emergencies.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Anthrax is rare in developed countries but continues to occur in parts of Africa, Asia, and the Middle East, particularly in rural communities that keep livestock. Cutaneous anthrax is by far the most common form, accounting for the great majority of human cases worldwide.

Those at risk include farmers, butchers, wool sorters, hide and leather workers, veterinarians, and laboratory staff. Because anthrax spores are highly stable and could potentially be dispersed deliberately, the disease is also regarded as a bioterrorism agent. Sporadic outbreaks in livestock can occasionally lead to clusters of human cases, which is why suspected animal deaths are investigated promptly.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Infection follows exposure to the spores of \textit{Bacillus anthracis}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cutaneous anthrax:} spores enter through a break in the skin, often on the arms, hands, or face, during handling of infected animals or their products
    \item \textbf{Inhalational anthrax:} spores are breathed in, usually from contaminated dust, hides, or wool, and are carried to the lungs and lymph nodes
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal anthrax:} spores are swallowed in undercooked meat from infected animals
    \item \textbf{Injection anthrax:} a rare form seen in people who inject contaminated drugs
\end{itemize}

Once inside the body, spores germinate into actively dividing bacteria that produce powerful toxins. These toxins damage tissues, cause swelling and bleeding, and overwhelm the immune system. In inhalational anthrax, spores are taken up by immune cells in the lungs and transported to lymph nodes in the chest, where the bacteria multiply before spreading through the bloodstream -- a process that explains the sudden, severe deterioration typical of this form.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on the form of the disease:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cutaneous:} a small, painless, itchy bump that develops within days, then forms a fluid-filled blister that breaks down into a black-centred sore (eschar), often with marked surrounding swelling
    \item \textbf{Inhalational:} begins like a cold or flu with fever, headache, muscle aches, and a dry cough, then progresses over days to severe breathlessness, chest pain, and shock
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal:} severe abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, bloody diarrhoea, and fever
    \item \textbf{Injection:} a deep, often painless swelling at the injection site that becomes red, blistered, or blackened, with fever and a feeling of being unwell
\end{itemize}

The black eschar of cutaneous anthrax is distinctive, but it is not the only cause of such skin lesions, so laboratory confirmation is needed.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions can look similar to anthrax:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cutaneous:} spider bites, staphylococcal or other bacterial skin ulcers, and infected hair follicles (furuncles)
    \item \textbf{Inhalational:} influenza and other viral respiratory infections, community-acquired pneumonia, and glandular fever
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal:} food poisoning, gastroenteritis, and other causes of severe abdominal pain
    \item \textbf{Injection-site infection:} bacterial cellulitis or abscess at the injection site
    \item \textbf{Tick-borne and plague-like illnesses:} in affected regions
\end{itemize}

A history of exposure to animals, animal products, or a deliberate-release event is the key clue that raises suspicion of anthrax.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any person with a suspected anthrax exposure or an illness suggestive of anthrax should be assessed urgently, because early antibiotic treatment greatly improves the outcome. Seek care if:

\begin{itemize}
    \item You have handled sick or dead livestock and developed a skin sore, fever, or cough
    \item A painless sore with a black centre develops, especially on exposed skin
    \item There is fever with severe breathlessness or chest pain after possible exposure
    \item Severe abdominal pain and bloody diarrhoea follow the consumption of undercooked meat
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing or signs of shock
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or collapse
    \item Widespread bleeding, or blood-stained vomit or diarrhoea
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Because anthrax is rare, diagnosis depends on linking the illness to a possible exposure and then confirming it in the laboratory:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} occupation, contact with animals or animal products, travel, and any concerns about deliberate exposure
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} blood cultures can grow \textit{Bacillus anthracis}; PCR and antibody tests are also used
    \item \textbf{Skin swabs:} taken from cutaneous lesions for culture and PCR
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray or CT:} in inhalational disease, which may show widening of the lymph nodes in the chest
    \item \textbf{Spinal fluid testing:} if there are signs of meningitis
    \item \textbf{Public health notification:} suspected cases are reported immediately so that contacts and the source can be investigated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Anthrax is treated urgently, usually in hospital:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} intravenous antibiotics such as penicillin, ciprofloxacin, or doxycycline are the mainstay of treatment; given early, they are very effective, especially in cutaneous disease
    \item \textbf{Combination therapy:} for inhalational, gastrointestinal, or severe disease, several antibiotics are often combined, sometimes with antitoxin antibodies that neutralise the bacterial toxins
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} oxygen, intravenous fluids, and intensive care support for severe disease
    \item \textbf{Skin lesion care:} the eschar is kept clean and covered, and is not routinely removed
    \item \textbf{Drainage or surgery:} occasionally needed for injection anthrax
    \item \textbf{Post-exposure prevention:} people exposed to spores but without symptoms may be offered antibiotics, and sometimes vaccination, to prevent the disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sepsis and shock:} the most serious complication, especially in inhalational disease
    \item \textbf{Meningitis:} the bacteria can reach the lining of the brain, a very serious complication
    \item \textbf{Bleeding problems:} the toxins can cause widespread bleeding
    \item \textbf{Respiratory failure:} in inhalational disease
    \item \textbf{Scarring:} cutaneous anthrax can leave a scar as the eschar heals
    \item \textbf{Death:} untreated inhalational and gastrointestinal anthrax are frequently fatal; early treatment greatly reduces this risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Cutaneous anthrax is the mildest form: with antibiotics, almost all patients recover, and even without treatment most cases eventually heal, although slowly. Inhalational anthrax is the most dangerous form and is fatal in a large proportion of cases when diagnosis and treatment are delayed, but survival is much better with prompt, intensive treatment. Gastrointestinal and injection anthrax are also serious and carry significant risk. Public health measures around livestock and the rapid treatment of any suspected case are what keep outcomes as good as they can be.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vaccinate livestock in areas where anthrax is common
    \item Wear gloves, cover cuts and grazes, and wash hands after handling animals, hides, wool, or meat in affected areas
    \item Do not slaughter or handle animals that have died suddenly without veterinary assessment
    \item Cook meat thoroughly, especially where anthrax is known to occur
    \item Dispose of dead animals safely according to local guidance, and report unusual animal deaths
    \item Workers at high risk may be offered anthrax vaccination
    \item Report suspected human or animal cases immediately to public health authorities
\end{itemize}
