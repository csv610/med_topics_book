\chapter{First Aid Advice}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
This chapter provides practical, trustworthy first aid advice for common emergencies and injuries, and guidance on where to get training. First aid advice changes as guidelines are updated, so it is important to learn from current, accredited sources rather than old habits or folklore. The core messages are simple: check for danger, call 000 for serious emergencies, start CPR for a person not breathing, control bleeding with pressure, and cool burns with water. For anything beyond minor injuries, professional medical review is needed. This chapter answers the most common first aid questions and directs readers to accredited training and information.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Most Australians will encounter an injury or emergency requiring first aid in their lifetime, and the outcomes depend heavily on what bystanders do. Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest affects thousands of Australians each year, and survival is two to three times higher when CPR is started by a bystander. Choking, severe bleeding, burns, and drowning also cause preventable deaths and disability where first aid is delayed or incorrect. First aid training is widespread, with Red Cross, St John Ambulance, and other providers delivering courses to hundreds of thousands of people annually, and workplace requirements keep many certifications current.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Good first aid advice is grounded in how emergencies harm the body.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cardiac arrest:} the heart stops pumping, so the brain is starved of oxygen; CPR maintains a minimum of blood flow until defibrillation restores the rhythm
    \item \textbf{Bleeding:} uncontrolled bleeding depletes circulating blood, causing shock and cardiac arrest; pressure on the wound reduces flow
    \item \textbf{Choking:} a blocked airway prevents breathing; back blows and abdominal thrusts use air pressure to expel the object
    \item \textbf{Burns:} heat continues to damage tissue after the source is removed; cooling with water stops the burn deepening
    \item \textbf{Unconsciousness:} an unresponsive person can lose their airway as the tongue relaxes; the recovery position keeps the airway open
    \item \textbf{Stroke:} a blocked or bleeding vessel in the brain; rapid recognition (FAST) and early hospital care limit brain damage
    \item \textbf{Anaphylaxis:} a severe allergic reaction causes airway swelling and collapse; adrenaline (EpiPen) reverses it
    \item \textbf{The principle:} simple, correct, early actions address the immediate threat to life better than elaborate or delayed ones
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Common first aid situations and their key features.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cardiac arrest: unresponsive, not breathing or only gasping; treat with CPR and an AED
    \item Choking: clutching the throat, unable to speak or breathe, blue lips; back blows and thrusts
    \item Severe bleeding: blood that soaks dressings or spurts; apply firm pressure and call 000
    \item Burns: redness, blistering, or white, waxy skin; cool with running water for 20 minutes
    \item Stroke: face drooping, arm weakness, speech difficulty; call 000 immediately, note the time of onset
    \item Seizure: stiffening and jerking, loss of awareness; protect, time, and recover
    \item Anaphylaxis: hives, swelling of lips and tongue, wheeze, collapse; use adrenaline
    \item Head injury: loss of consciousness, vomiting, confusion; call 000
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Deciding whether something is an emergency is the first act of first aid.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cardiac arrest versus fainting: a person who responds and breathes normally needs the recovery position and monitoring, not CPR
    \item Mild choking (coughing, able to breathe) versus severe choking (silent, blue), which needs back blows and thrusts
    \item Minor burns (small, superficial, no blisters or few) versus major burns requiring hospital care
    \item Head injury with a brief episode of unresponsiveness, which still needs medical review
    \item Chest pain in a conscious, breathing person, treated as a possible heart attack with 000
    \item Allergic reactions: mild hives versus anaphylaxis, which is an emergency
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Call 000 for any condition that threatens life or limb: unconsciousness, not breathing, severe bleeding, chest pain, severe burns, choking, stroke symptoms, or anaphylaxis. For other injuries, see a doctor, pharmacist, or urgent care service, and always seek professional review for head injuries, deep wounds, and burns larger than the palm of the hand.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unconsciousness or abnormal breathing
    \item Chest pain, especially with breathlessness, sweating, or nausea
    \item Severe or uncontrollable bleeding
    \item Severe choking
    \item Signs of stroke: facial droop, arm weakness, difficulty speaking
    \item Severe burns, drowning, poisoning, or serious head injury
    \item Anaphylaxis or any suspected life-threatening reaction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Danger check:} make sure the area is safe before helping
    \item \textbf{Response check:} call to and gently shake the person to assess responsiveness
    \item \textbf{Breathing check:} look, listen, and feel for normal breathing for up to 10 seconds
    \item \textbf{Scene information:} note what happened, how long ago, and any medicines or medical conditions
    \item \textbf{Handover:} give accurate information to ambulance officers and emergency staff
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} after any first aid, arrange professional assessment as recommended
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CPR:} 30 compressions to 2 breaths; push hard and fast in the centre of the chest; attach an AED and follow its prompts
    \item \textbf{Recovery position:} for an unconscious person breathing normally, to keep the airway open
    \item \textbf{Bleeding:} direct pressure, elevation, and additional dressings; if bleeding continues despite pressure, call 000
    \item \textbf{Burns:} run cool water over the burn for 20 minutes, remove jewellery and clothing unless stuck, cover with cling film, and do not use ice, creams, or burst blisters
    \item \textbf{Choking:} encourage coughing; if severe, alternate 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts; call 000 if it does not clear
    \item \textbf{Stroke:} use the FAST test (Face, Arms, Speech, Time) and call 000 immediately
    \item \textbf{Anaphylaxis:} give the adrenaline autoinjector if available, lie the person flat if conscious, and call 000
    \item \textbf{Poisoning:} call the Poisons Information Centre 13 11 26; do not induce vomiting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acting on outdated or incorrect advice, which can harm
    \item Delay in calling 000 when the situation is beyond first aid
    \item Infection in wounds and burns that are not properly treated
    \item Brain damage and death from delayed CPR or defibrillation
    \item Injury from lifting or moving casualties incorrectly
    \item Distress in helpers who do not know what to do
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outcomes of emergencies are transformed by correct first aid. Bystander CPR and early defibrillation are the difference between life and death in most cardiac arrests, and prompt pressure, cooling, and positioning improve outcomes across bleeding, burns, and unconsciousness. First aid advice is only as good as its source: accredited providers and the Australian Resuscitation Council keep guidelines current. People who refresh their skills regularly respond faster and more correctly, and their actions measurably improve survival and recovery.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Do a first aid course with an accredited provider and refresh it regularly
    \item Keep current first aid guides in the home and car
    \item Store a well-stocked first aid kit and know how to use its contents
    \item Check first aid advice against current guidelines rather than relying on memory of old practices
    \item Keep emergency numbers, including 000 and the Poisons Information Centre, accessible
    \item Practise using an AED if one is available in your workplace or community
    \item After an emergency, allow yourself time to recover and seek support if affected
\end{itemize}
