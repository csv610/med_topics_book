\chapter{Drugs and Alcohol: How to Help Someone}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Watching a family member, friend, or colleague struggle with drugs or alcohol is distressing, and it is natural to want to help but not know where to begin. This chapter provides practical guidance on how to support someone who is using drugs or alcohol in ways that are harming their health or life. Effective help is built on compassion, good communication, and knowledge of the treatment services available. It is important to remember that a person can only change when they are ready, but the support of people who care about them is one of the strongest influences on recovery.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Substance use problems affect a large number of Australian families. A substantial proportion of adults drink alcohol at levels that put their health at risk, and significant numbers use illicit drugs each year. For every person with a substance use problem, several others are affected: partners, children, parents, and friends who carry worry, financial strain, and the burden of caring. Many people who need help do not seek treatment for years, which makes the role of concerned family and friends especially important in encouraging and supporting change.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Substance dependence develops through the interaction of the drug's effects on the brain, genetic vulnerability, and life circumstances. Repeated use alters the brain's reward, motivation, and memory systems, so that the substance becomes the centre of the person's priorities and stopping becomes increasingly difficult. Understanding that addiction is a chronic brain condition, rather than a moral failing, helps supporters respond with patience rather than blame.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Brain changes:} drugs and alcohol overstimulate reward pathways, and the brain adapts by needing more of the substance to feel normal
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} family history of addiction increases vulnerability
    \item \textbf{Mental health:} depression, anxiety, and trauma commonly drive substance use as a way of coping
    \item \textbf{Social influences:} peer groups, stress, unemployment, and social isolation all contribute
    \item \textbf{Denial and minimisation:} the person often underestimates the problem, which is a core feature of addiction rather than simple stubbornness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The signs that someone may have a substance use problem can be observed by those close to them.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Drinking or using drugs in larger amounts or over longer periods than intended
    \item Repeated unsuccessful attempts to cut down or stop
    \item Spending a great deal of time obtaining, using, or recovering from the substance
    \item Strong cravings and continued use despite harm to health, work, or relationships
    \item Withdrawal symptoms when not using: irritability, anxiety, sweating, shaking, or feeling unwell
    \item Giving up hobbies, sports, or social activities that do not involve the substance
    \item Financial problems, borrowing money, or selling possessions
    \item Mood swings, secrecy, defensiveness, and withdrawal from family life
    \item Physical changes: weight loss, poor appearance, bloodshot eyes, or frequent illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before concluding that drugs or alcohol are the cause of a person's difficulties, other possibilities should be considered.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Mental health conditions such as depression, anxiety, or psychosis, which may be the underlying problem or coexist with substance use
    \item Stress and burnout from work, caregiving, or relationship difficulties
    \item Medical conditions causing fatigue, memory problems, or mood changes
    \item Medication side effects or inappropriate use of prescribed medicines
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Supporters should encourage the person to see a GP for an assessment and to discuss treatment options. Family members can also seek advice on their own behalf: GPs, drug and alcohol services, and counselling agencies offer guidance and support to families, even when the person using substances is not ready to engage.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The person collapses, stops breathing, or cannot be roused
    \item A suspected overdose, with severe drowsiness, slow or irregular breathing, or bluish lips and fingers
    \item Seizures or fits
    \item Severe agitation, confusion, paranoia, or hallucinations that put the person or others at risk
    \item Threats or attempts of self-harm or suicide
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
For the supporter, "diagnosis" is about understanding the situation and the options available.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observation:} noticing patterns of use, behaviour changes, and consequences over time
    \item \textbf{Open conversations:} talking with the person in a calm, non-judgemental way about what you have noticed and how you feel
    \item \textbf{Professional assessment:} a GP or drug and alcohol service can formally assess dependence, withdrawal risk, and coexisting mental or physical illness
    \item \textbf{Family information sessions:} many services offer education for families about addiction and treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Helping someone with a substance problem is a process that requires patience and self-care.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Educate yourself:} learn about addiction, withdrawal, and treatment so your expectations are realistic
    \item \textbf{Communicate with compassion:} raise concerns using "I" statements, describe specific behaviours, and express care rather than accusation
    \item \textbf{Avoid enabling:} do not make excuses, lend money for drugs, or take over responsibilities that protect the person from the consequences of their use
    \item \textbf{Encourage professional help:} offer to accompany the person to a GP or treatment service when they are willing
    \item \textbf{Support during treatment:} attend family sessions, help with practical arrangements, and celebrate progress without judging relapses
    \item \textbf{Know what to do in a crisis:} keep emergency numbers available and learn how to recognise an overdose and administer naloxone if the person uses opioids
    \item \textbf{Look after yourself:} join a support group for families, set boundaries, and seek your own counselling if needed
    \item \textbf{Consider intervention approaches:} structured family interventions can help, but they work best with professional guidance
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Strain and breakdown of family and intimate relationships
    \item Financial hardship and debt affecting the whole household
    \item Emotional and physical exhaustion in carers, sometimes called caregiver burnout
    \item Children in the household experiencing neglect, fear, and long-term developmental effects
    \item Domestic violence and other forms of harm, which require immediate attention and safety planning
    \item The risk of overdose, accidents, and self-harm in the person using substances
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Recovery from substance dependence is a long-term process, and outcomes improve substantially with family involvement and support. Many people make multiple attempts before achieving lasting change, and relapse is common during the early years. A supportive, non-judgemental family environment is one of the strongest predictors of sustained recovery. Even when the person does not change, family members who learn to set boundaries and care for their own wellbeing can reduce the harm to themselves and their children.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Look after your own physical and mental health; you cannot support others from an empty cup
    \item Set clear, consistent boundaries about what behaviour you will accept in your home
    \item Join a family support group such as SMART Recovery Family and Friends, or Al-Anon
    \item Keep the lines of communication open, even during difficult periods
    \item Store naloxone if the person uses opioids, and learn how to use it
    \item Plan for emergencies, including having important phone numbers to hand
    \item Seek legal or financial advice if the person's use is causing serious debt or legal problems
\end{itemize}
