\chapter{Gastroenteritis (Gastro)}
\index{Gastroenteritis (Gastro)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Gastroenteritis, commonly called gastro, is an infection of the stomach and intestines causing vomiting, diarrhoea, and abdominal cramps. It is usually caused by viruses, most commonly norovirus, and in children by rotavirus, although bacteria and parasites can also cause it, often through contaminated food or water. Gastro is extremely common and usually clears on its own within a few days, and the main treatment is replacing fluids to prevent dehydration. It spreads easily between people, especially in households, childcare, schools, and aged care, so hygiene measures are important. This chapter explains the causes, symptoms, treatment, and prevention of gastroenteritis.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Gastroenteritis affects almost everyone at some point, and most people experience it once or more each year. Norovirus causes around 1 in 5 cases of gastro worldwide and spreads rapidly in outbreaks, while rotavirus was the leading cause of severe childhood diarrhoea before vaccination, which has reduced cases dramatically. Bacterial gastro from campylobacter, salmonella, and other organisms is less common but can be more severe. Gastroenteritis causes millions of GP visits and days off work and school in many countries each year, and dehydration from gastro is a leading cause of hospitalisation in young children.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viruses:} norovirus (the most common, causing vomiting and spreading in outbreaks), rotavirus (severe in young children, prevented by vaccination), and adenovirus
    \item \textbf{Bacteria:} campylobacter, salmonella, E.\ coli, and shigella, usually from contaminated food or water
    \item \textbf{Parasites:} giardia and cryptosporidium, from contaminated water
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} person to person, through contaminated hands and surfaces, and through contaminated food and water
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} the organism infects the gut lining, causing inflammation and increased fluid secretion, producing vomiting and diarrhoea
    \item \textbf{Incubation:} from hours (bacterial toxins) to days (some bacteria and parasites)
    \item \textbf{Dehydration:} the main danger, from fluid loss in vomit and stool
    \item \textbf{Recovery:} the immune system clears most infections within a few days
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vomiting, which often comes on suddenly
    \item Watery diarrhoea, sometimes with cramps
    \item Nausea, fever, headache, and muscle aches
    \item Abdominal pain and bloating
    \item Loss of appetite and fatigue
    \item Signs of dehydration: dry mouth, thirst, reduced urine, dark urine, dizziness, and sunken eyes
    \item In young children: reduced wet nappies, drowsiness, and crying without tears
    \item Symptoms usually settle within 2--3 days, though some infections last longer
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Vomiting and diarrhoea have other causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Food poisoning, which is gastroenteritis from contaminated food and is similar
    \item Appendicitis, which can begin with vomiting and abdominal pain
    \item Inflammatory bowel disease and irritable bowel syndrome in chronic cases
    \item Influenza and other infections with gut symptoms
    \item Medication side effects
    \item Coeliac disease and food intolerance in recurring cases
    \item The pattern and severity guide the assessment
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most gastro is managed at home with fluids, but medical care is needed for signs of dehydration, persistent vomiting, blood in the stool, high fever, or illness in a baby, older person, or immunocompromised person.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of severe dehydration: no urine for 6--8 hours, confusion, dizziness on standing, or collapse
    \item Blood in vomit or stool
    \item Severe abdominal pain, especially with fever
    \item A baby who is floppy, drowsy, or has a sunken fontanelle
    \item Persistent vomiting that prevents any fluid intake
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the pattern of symptoms, exposure, and signs of dehydration
    \item \textbf{Stool tests:} for bacteria, viruses, and parasites in severe, persistent, or outbreak cases
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} for dehydration and electrolyte disturbance in severe cases
    \item \textbf{Urine output monitoring:} a practical measure of hydration, especially in children
    \item \textbf{Notification:} some causes are notifiable to public health authorities
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fluids:} the cornerstone of treatment; oral rehydration solution is best, with frequent small amounts for children; continue breast or formula feeds
    \item \textbf{Rest:} the gut needs time to recover; return to food gradually as appetite allows
    \item \textbf{Avoid anti-diarrhoeal medicines:} generally not recommended for infective gastro
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} only for specific bacterial infections, not for viral gastro
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} intravenous fluids for severe dehydration
    \item \textbf{Symptom relief:} paracetamol for fever and aches
    \item \textbf{Prevent spread:} hand washing, staying home from work and school for at least 48 hours after symptoms settle, and not preparing food for others
    \item \textbf{Probiotics:} may modestly shorten some episodes, though evidence is mixed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dehydration and electrolyte disturbance, the most common complication
    \item Kidney injury from severe dehydration
    \item Hospitalisation in young children and older adults
    \item Spread to household members and institutions
    \item Rarely, complications of specific bacteria, including reactive arthritis and haemolytic uraemic syndrome
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Gastroenteritis resolves on its own in almost everyone within a few days, and the only treatment that matters in most cases is fluid replacement. With good rehydration, dehydration and hospitalisation are largely prevented, and serious complications are rare in healthy people. Rotavirus vaccination has prevented countless severe childhood cases. The disease is self-limiting, and the main tasks are managing fluids, recognising dehydration early, and preventing spread to others.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wash hands thoroughly with soap and water, especially after the toilet and before food
    \item Keep children who are unwell home from childcare and school until 48 hours after symptoms settle
    \item Do not prepare food for others while unwell
    \item Disinfect surfaces, taps, and handles during an outbreak in the household
    \item Ensure children receive the rotavirus vaccine on schedule
    \item Follow food safety rules to prevent bacterial and foodborne gastro
    \item Use oral rehydration solution for children and people at risk
    \item Seek early medical care for dehydration or severe symptoms
\end{itemize}
