\chapter{Antibiotics}
\index{Antibiotics}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Antibiotics are medicines that kill bacteria or stop them from reproducing. They act only against \textbf{bacteria} -- not viruses -- so they have no effect on colds, influenza, most sore throats, or other viral infections. Their role is to treat established bacterial infections such as pneumonia, urinary tract infections, strep throat, skin and wound infections, and septicaemia, and to prevent infection during surgery and in people with weakened immune systems.

Antibiotics are grouped into \textbf{classes} according to their chemical structure and mechanism of action, including penicillins, cephalosporins, macrolides, tetracyclines, aminoglycosides, and fluoroquinolones. They can be given by mouth, applied topically to skin or eyes, or delivered intravenously in hospital. The modern antibiotic era began with the discovery of penicillin in the 1920s and the introduction of widespread penicillin use in the 1940s, which transformed the management of infection and remains one of the greatest advances in medicine.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Antibiotics are among the most widely prescribed medicines worldwide. In many countries, the most common reason for a prescription is a respiratory tract infection, yet a large proportion of these are viral and will resolve without antibiotics. Penicillins, particularly amoxicillin, are the most frequently used class, followed by macrolides and cephalosporins.

The scale of use is double-edged. In some communities, antibiotics are overprescribed or obtained without prescription, driving up rates of resistance and side effects; in others, people die from infections that a simple course of antibiotics would have cured because access to medicines is poor or unaffordable. This imbalance -- too much use in some places and too little in others -- is a defining challenge of modern infectious disease medicine.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Antibiotics work by exploiting differences between bacterial cells and human cells, attacking structures or processes that bacteria possess but our own cells do not. The main mechanisms are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cell wall synthesis inhibitors:} penicillins, cephalosporins, and vancomycin weaken the bacterial cell wall so that the organism bursts under osmotic pressure
    \item \textbf{Protein synthesis inhibitors:} macrolides, tetracyclines, and aminoglycosides bind to the bacterial ribosome and stop the production of essential proteins
    \item \textbf{DNA and RNA synthesis inhibitors:} fluoroquinolones and rifampicin interfere with the copying and reading of genetic material
    \item \textbf{Metabolic pathway inhibitors:} sulfonamides and trimethoprim block the bacterial synthesis of folate, which bacteria must produce themselves
\end{itemize}

Bacteria defend themselves through \textbf{resistance}: spontaneous mutation or the exchange of resistance genes between organisms allows them to inactivate the drug, pump it out, or alter its target. Resistance is strongly selected for whenever antibiotics are used broadly or unnecessarily, which is why they should be prescribed as precisely and for as short a duration as clinical need allows.

\section*{Clinical Features}
When antibiotics work, the expected response is improvement in fever, pain, and other signs of infection within 48--72 hours. The features a patient may notice relate both to the infection itself and to the medicine:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Expected effect:} settling fever and improving symptoms of the treated infection over the first days
    \item \textbf{Common side effects:} nausea, stomach upset, diarrhoea, and mild headache
    \item \textbf{Yeast overgrowth:} oral or vaginal thrush can follow a course of antibiotics because normal protective bacteria are suppressed
    \item \textbf{Skin reactions:} a mild, non-spreading rash; some classes, such as penicillins, cause this more often than others
    \item \textbf{Antibiotic-associated diarrhoea:} loose stools during or after treatment, occasionally due to overgrowth of the toxin-producing bacterium \textit{Clostridioides difficile}
    \item \textbf{Allergic reactions:} ranging from hives to -- rarely -- severe swelling, breathing difficulty, and anaphylaxis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When symptoms persist or new symptoms appear during antibiotic treatment, the possibilities to weigh up include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Resistant bacteria}, the \textbf{wrong antibiotic}, or a dose that is too low
    \item The \textbf{wrong diagnosis} -- the illness may not be bacterial, or the bacterial focus may be somewhere else
    \item A \textbf{viral, fungal, or non-infectious cause} mimicking a bacterial infection
    \item A \textbf{drug side effect} (such as diarrhoea or rash) mistaken for worsening infection
    \item A \textbf{genuine allergy} versus a non-allergic drug rash
    \item A \textbf{new infection} superimposed on the one being treated
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact a doctor if there is no improvement after a few days of antibiotics, if symptoms worsen, or if fever persists. Seek advice promptly for severe or bloody diarrhoea, a spreading or blistering rash, yellowing of the skin or eyes, or signs of a severe allergic reaction.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, wheezing, or a tight feeling in the throat
    \item Swelling of the face, lips, or tongue, or collapse -- features of anaphylaxis
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, high fever, rapid breathing, or other signs of sepsis
    \item Severe abdominal pain, or frequent bloody diarrhoea
    \item A rapidly spreading, blistering, or painful rash
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The decision to prescribe an antibiotic is clinical, based on the history and examination, but investigations help confirm the organism and guide the choice of drug:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} identifying the site, onset, and severity of infection and any risk factors for resistance
    \item \textbf{Culture and sensitivity:} swabs, urine, sputum, or blood are sent to identify the bacterium and test which antibiotics are effective
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, C-reactive protein, and procalcitonin help distinguish bacterial from viral infection and gauge severity
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} chest X-ray, ultrasound, or CT where the diagnosis is uncertain, such as suspected pneumonia or an abscess
    \item \textbf{Allergy assessment:} clarification of any reported penicillin or other drug allergy, since many people labelled allergic are not truly allergic
\end{itemize}

Treatment is often started \textbf{empirically} according to the most likely organism and local resistance patterns, then adjusted once culture results return.

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prescribe only for bacterial infection:} the clinician judges whether antibiotics will genuinely help, using validated decision rules for conditions such as sore throat, ear infection, and sinusitis
    \item \textbf{Choose the narrowest effective agent:} a targeted antibiotic, rather than a broad-spectrum drug, preserves the microbiome and reduces resistance pressure
    \item \textbf{Correct dose, route, and duration:} the shortest effective course is now preferred for most infections
    \item \textbf{Delayed (safety-net) prescribing:} for some mild infections, patients are given a prescription to start only if symptoms do not settle in a few days
    \item \textbf{Take as directed:} follow the prescribed dose, timing, and duration; do not share or save tablets, and ask the prescriber what to do if you miss a dose
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} rest, fluids, antipyretics, and analgesia alongside the antibiotic
    \item \textbf{Review:} return if not improving; intravenous therapy and hospital care for severe or systemic infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Antibiotic-associated diarrhoea and \textit{Clostridioides difficile} colitis, which can be severe and recurrent
    \item Allergic reactions, from mild rash to life-threatening anaphylaxis
    \item Oral or vaginal thrush due to disturbance of normal flora
    \item Drug interactions, such as enhanced warfarin effect or reduced efficacy of the oral contraceptive pill with some classes
    \item Organ-specific effects: kidney injury with aminoglycosides, tendon rupture with fluoroquinolones, and prolongation of the heart's QT interval with macrolides
    \item Selection of resistant bacteria, making current and future infections harder to treat
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most bacterial infections resolve completely with the correct antibiotic, with clinical improvement usually apparent within a few days. The outlook depends on the site and severity of the infection, the susceptibility of the organism, the speed of treatment, and the person's underlying health and immune status. Serious infections such as sepsis and meningitis carry significant mortality even with optimal care, whereas uncomplicated community infections such as strep throat or cystitis almost always recover fully.

With responsible use, the risks of side effects and resistance are low. Antibiotics are a precious, finite resource; their continued effectiveness depends on using them only when needed, choosing the right drug, and not extending courses beyond what is necessary.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Do not ask for antibiotics for colds, flu, or viral sore throats
    \item Never share antibiotics with family members or use leftovers from a previous illness
    \item Take the prescribed dose for the prescribed duration; do not extend, stop early, or restart treatment without advice from the prescriber
    \item Reduce the need for antibiotics by preventing infection: hand hygiene, vaccination, and safe food preparation
    \item Tell your doctor about any previous drug allergy before treatment begins
    \item Report troublesome side effects, especially severe diarrhoea or a spreading rash
    \item Ask a clinician for advice about persistent or severe diarrhoea; probiotics are not suitable for everyone
\end{itemize}
