\chapter{Blood Testing}
\index{Blood Testing}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Blood testing is the collection and laboratory analysis of a blood sample to assess a person's health. It is one of the most common medical procedures and is used to screen for disease, help make a diagnosis, monitor an existing condition, or check how well a treatment is working. A small sample is usually taken from a vein in the arm, although blood can also be collected from a fingertip or, in some situations, from a newborn's heel. The sample is sent to a laboratory, where a range of measurements is performed depending on what the clinician has requested. Results are interpreted in the context of the person's symptoms, examination, and medical history, since a single result rarely makes a diagnosis on its own.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Blood testing is among the most frequently performed investigations in medicine, with laboratories processing millions of samples every year. Blood tests are ordered across all age groups and in almost every healthcare setting, from routine check-ups and antenatal care to emergency departments and intensive care units. As populations age and chronic diseases such as diabetes, kidney disease, and heart disease become more common, the demand for blood testing continues to grow. Most tests are safe and well tolerated, though over-ordering can contribute to unnecessary cost, anxiety, and the detection of incidental findings.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Blood tests provide information that helps explain what is happening inside the body:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diagnosis:} tests help confirm or exclude conditions such as anaemia, infection, diabetes, thyroid disease, or kidney and liver problems
    \item \textbf{Screening:} testing healthy people for conditions that may cause no symptoms early on, such as high cholesterol, diabetes, or iron deficiency
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} repeated testing to track chronic disease, such as HbA1c in diabetes or the international normalised ratio (INR) during anticoagulant therapy
    \item \textbf{Treatment monitoring:} checking medicine levels or detecting side effects such as abnormal liver or kidney function
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative assessment:} evaluating fitness for surgery and anaesthesia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Most people experience nothing more than a brief sting when the needle is inserted. Minor and temporary effects are common:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A small bruise (haematoma) at the puncture site
    \item Slight soreness or tenderness for a day or so
    \item Light-headedness or feeling faint, especially in people who are anxious about needles
    \item Rarely, continued oozing from the puncture site if pressure is not held long enough
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
An abnormal result does not, by itself, diagnose a condition. Interpretation is influenced by many factors:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Normal laboratory variation and borderline results that may need repeating
    \item False positives and false negatives, which occur with every test
    \item Medications, diet, exercise, pregnancy, and recent illness affecting results
    \item The time of day, or how long after a meal the sample was taken
    \item Laboratory factors, such as a sample taken into the wrong tube or delayed transport
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Blood testing itself rarely requires urgent care, but contact a doctor or the collection service if:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The puncture site continues to bleed, or swelling and pain worsen over the next day
    \item You feel very unwell, faint, or develop signs of infection such as spreading redness or fever
    \item You have not received your results or you are unsure what they mean
    \item A doctor has asked you to repeat a test or follow up, but you have not been able to do so
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Collapse, fainting that does not recover, or difficulty breathing after a blood test
    \item Any sudden, severe symptom that worries you
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The testing process follows a standard sequence:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Request:} the clinician specifies which tests are needed based on the clinical question
    \item \textbf{Preparation:} some tests require fasting for 8--12 hours, while others have no special requirements
    \item \textbf{Collection:} a trained collector draws the sample, usually from a vein in the arm
    \item \textbf{Laboratory analysis:} the sample is processed using automated analysers and standardised methods
    \item \textbf{Reporting:} results are checked and reported to the requesting clinician, who interprets them in the context of the person's history and examination
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Results are discussed with the patient in plain language, with an explanation of what they mean
    \item Normal results may be reassuring, or may prompt further testing if clinical suspicion remains high
    \item Abnormal results may lead to repeat testing, additional investigations, treatment, or specialist referral
    \item A test result alone is not a diagnosis -- it is always interpreted alongside symptoms and examination findings
    \item Follow-up is arranged so that results are reviewed and acted on in a timely way
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Serious complications from routine blood testing are rare:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Bruising, haematoma, or, very rarely, injury to a nerve or artery near the puncture site
    \item Infection at the puncture site
    \item Prolonged bleeding in people taking anticoagulants or with bleeding disorders
    \item Fainting or falls after the procedure
    \item Anxiety or needle phobia, which can discourage future testing
    \item Harms of interpretation, including false reassurance or unnecessary further investigation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For most people, a blood test is a quick, low-risk procedure with no lasting effects. Bruises settle within a few days, and any discomfort resolves quickly. The value of a blood test lies in the information it provides and the actions that follow -- the test itself does not treat disease. When results are acted on appropriately, blood testing contributes to earlier diagnosis, better monitoring of chronic conditions, and safer prescribing of medicines.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Follow the preparation instructions, such as fasting or holding certain medicines, where given
    \item Drink plenty of water beforehand, and eat beforehand unless fasting is required
    \item Tell the collector if you have fainted at previous tests or have a bleeding tendency
    \item Hold firm pressure over the puncture site for a few minutes after the sample is taken
    \item Wear comfortable clothing with sleeves that roll up easily
    \item Ask about your results if you have not heard back, and keep a record of important results
\end{itemize}
