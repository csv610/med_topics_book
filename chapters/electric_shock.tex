\chapter{Electric Shock}
\index{Electric Shock}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An electric shock occurs when an electric current passes through the body. The effects range from a mild tingling to severe injury and death, depending on the voltage, current, pathway through the body, and duration of contact. Household electricity, power tools, appliances, lightning, and industrial equipment are common sources. Even a shock that seems minor can damage the heart and internal organs, so all significant shocks should be assessed by a doctor. This chapter explains the risks of electric shock, the signs of serious injury, and what to do in an emergency.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Electric shocks are most common in the home and workplace, affecting electricians, construction workers, and people using faulty appliances or doing DIY. Children are at risk from exposed sockets and cords. Most shocks are minor and produce no lasting harm, but each year in many countries some people die from electrocution, and others are left with burns, heart problems, or nerve damage. Lightning strikes are rare but can be fatal.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The damage caused by electricity depends on the characteristics of the current and the body's resistance.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Voltage and current:} higher voltage and current cause more damage, and alternating current is generally more dangerous than direct current
    \item \textbf{Pathway:} current passing through the chest, particularly across the heart, is most dangerous
    \item \textbf{Duration:} the longer the contact, the greater the injury
    \item \textbf{Resistance:} dry skin offers high resistance, so current may cause burns at entry and exit points; wet skin reduces resistance, allowing current deeper into the body
    \item \textbf{Effects:} the current disrupts the heart's rhythm, causes muscles to contract, burns tissues, and can stop breathing
    \item \textbf{Tetany:} muscles contract powerfully and may prevent the person from letting go of the source
    \item \textbf{Delayed effects:} heart rhythm problems can develop after the initial shock
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The signs depend on the severity of the shock.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A tingling or jolt sensation with minor shocks
    \item Burns at the points where current entered and exited the body
    \item Muscle pain, spasms, and weakness
    \item Numbness and tingling from nerve damage
    \item Palpitations or an irregular heartbeat
    \item Confusion, headache, and memory problems
    \item Difficulty breathing
    \item Loss of consciousness, cardiac arrest, and death in severe shocks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The effects of electric shock can be confused with other conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Burns from other causes, including chemical and thermal burns
    \item Fainting or collapse from other causes
    \item Heart rhythm problems unrelated to electricity
    \item Muscle and nerve injuries from other trauma
    \item Panic and anxiety after the event
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any shock from household current, even if it seems mild, warrants medical assessment, particularly if it involved the chest, caused loss of consciousness, or left burns. Shocks during pregnancy, from lightning, or at high voltage require urgent assessment.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Loss of consciousness or cardiac arrest
    \item Difficulty breathing
    \item Chest pain or palpitations
    \item Seizures or fits
    \item Burns, especially if extensive or over the heart area
    \item Any shock from lightning, high-voltage, or industrial current
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment focuses on the heart and the injuries.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the source, voltage, duration, and pathway of the shock
    \item \textbf{Examination:} burns, neurological function, and signs of internal injury
    \item \textbf{ECG:} essential to check for heart rhythm disturbances
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} muscle enzymes and kidney function if muscle damage is suspected
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} observation in hospital for heart rhythm problems after significant shocks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Emergency management and treatment follow the severity of the injury.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Safety first:} switch off the power or use a non-conductive object to separate the person from the current before touching them
    \item \textbf{Emergency care:} CPR if the person is unresponsive and not breathing, and an ambulance
    \item \textbf{Burn care:} cooling and dressing of burns, with review by a burns service for significant injuries
    \item \textbf{Heart monitoring:} hospital observation and treatment of any rhythm disturbance
    \item \textbf{Management of complications:} treatment of nerve, muscle, and internal injuries
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review for late effects on the heart and nervous system
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cardiac arrest and death
    \item Heart rhythm disturbances, which may appear hours later
    \item Burns, including deep burns to internal tissues
    \item Nerve damage with numbness, weakness, and chronic pain
    \item Muscle damage leading to kidney injury
    \item Falls and other injuries caused by the shock
    \item Psychological effects, including anxiety about electricity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the severity of the shock. Most household shocks cause no lasting harm, and people recover fully. Severe shocks, lightning strikes, and high-voltage injuries can be fatal or cause lasting heart, nerve, and muscle damage. Prompt assessment and monitoring give the best chance of detecting and treating complications.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Never touch electrical appliances with wet hands or while standing in water
    \item Use safety switches and have wiring checked by a licensed electrician
    \item Cover unused power points where young children are present
    \item Use appliances and power tools in good condition, with damaged cords replaced
    \item Stay indoors and away from windows during thunderstorms
    \item If caught outside in a storm, avoid trees, water, and metal objects
    \item Follow safe-work practices for anyone working with electricity
\end{itemize}
