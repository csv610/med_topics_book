\chapter{Grazes}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A graze, also called an abrasion, is a superficial wound in which the top layers of skin are scraped away by friction against a rough surface. Grazes are very common injuries, particularly in children, and typically result from falls on roads, footpaths, or playgrounds, or from skidding against a surface. They are usually shallow and heal quickly, but they can be painful because nerve endings in the damaged skin are exposed, and they carry a risk of infection because dirt and grit are often ground into the wound. Most grazes can be treated at home with cleaning, antibiotic ointment, and a dressing, and they heal within one to two weeks, sometimes leaving a scar.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Grazes are among the most common injuries seen in emergency departments and general practice. They are most frequent in children, who are prone to falls while running and playing, and in people engaged in sport, cycling, skateboarding, and similar activities. Road abrasions from falls off bicycles and motorbikes can be more extensive and severe. Most grazes are minor and treated at home, but deeper or contaminated grazes account for a significant number of presentations for wound care and infection prevention.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Grazes result from friction that removes the surface layers of the skin.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} sliding or skidding against a rough surface scrapes away the epidermis and sometimes part of the dermis
    \item \textbf{Depth:} superficial grazes affect only the epidermis; deeper grazes expose the dermis and bleed from damaged capillaries
    \item \textbf{Pain:} the dermis is rich in nerve endings, so grazes hurt, especially when the nerve endings are exposed
    \item \textbf{Contamination:} dirt, grit, and bacteria are often ground into the wound, increasing infection risk
    \item \textbf{Bleeding:} oozing from the wound is normal and helps clean it
    \item \textbf{Healing:} the skin regenerates from the edges and base of the wound, and new skin grows within days
    \item \textbf{Scarring:} deeper grazes can leave scars, especially if infected or repeatedly injured
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
A graze has a characteristic appearance and symptoms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A raw, reddened area where the top layer of skin is missing
    \item Oozing of clear fluid or slight bleeding
    \item Pain, burning, or stinging, which is often worse when the wound is touched or cleaned
    \item Embedded dirt, grit, or gravel in the wound
    \item Surrounding redness that usually settles within a day
    \item Scabbing as the wound dries and heals
    \item Larger or deeper grazes can cover significant areas, such as the whole knee or elbow
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other types of wounds and conditions should be distinguished from a simple graze.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Lacerations (cuts), which are deeper and may need stitches
    \item Burns, which can appear similar but need different care
    \item Puncture wounds, which carry a higher risk of deep infection
    \item Skin infections, which may develop in a graze
    \item Contact dermatitis from dressings or antiseptics
    \item Underlying fractures, which should be considered if there is significant pain, swelling, or an inability to bear weight
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most grazes can be treated at home. See a doctor if the graze is deep or large, if dirt is deeply embedded and cannot be removed by washing, if it does not stop oozing, or if there are signs of infection.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A graze with heavy bleeding that does not stop with pressure
    \item Signs of infection spreading: increasing pain, redness spreading beyond the wound, heat, pus, fever, or red streaks
    \item A graze from a rusty or dirty object with uncertain tetanus vaccination status
    \item A graze associated with a fracture, a head injury, or loss of consciousness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Grazes are diagnosed by examination, and tests are rarely needed.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Examination:} assessing the depth, size, and contamination of the wound
    \item \textbf{Assessment of foreign material:} checking for embedded grit that needs removal
    \item \textbf{Checking tetanus status:} confirming the person's vaccination history, as deep or dirty wounds may require a booster
    \item \textbf{Assessing for other injuries:} checking for fractures or other wounds sustained in the same fall
    \item \textbf{Review of healing:} follow-up to ensure the wound heals without infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Good wound care promotes healing and prevents infection.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cleaning:} rinse the wound thoroughly with clean water or saline to remove dirt and grit; antiseptic may be used
    \item \textbf{Removing embedded material:} wash out loose debris; stubborn grit may need professional removal
    \item \textbf{Antibiotic ointment:} a thin layer of antiseptic or antibiotic ointment helps prevent infection
    \item \textbf{Dressing:} cover with a sterile non-stick dressing to keep the wound moist, which speeds healing
    \item \textbf{Daily care:} change the dressing daily, cleaning the wound each time
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} paracetamol or ibuprofen if needed
    \item \textbf{Tetanus vaccination:} a booster is given if it is due and the wound is dirty or deep
    \item \textbf{Watching for infection:} monitoring for increasing redness, pain, swelling, or discharge
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection, which can spread to surrounding skin (cellulitis) and, rarely, deeper tissues
    \item Scarring, especially with deep grazes or poor healing
    \item Pigmentation changes in the healed skin
    \item Tetanus, which is very rare in Australia because of vaccination
    \item Embedded foreign material causing a "tattoo" if not removed
    \item Delayed healing in people with diabetes, poor circulation, or weakened immunity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most grazes heal quickly and completely. Superficial grazes heal within a few days to a week, and deeper ones within one to three weeks. With proper cleaning and dressing, infection is uncommon, and scarring is usually minimal. Grazes on areas that move, such as joints, may take a little longer and benefit from keeping the area flexible. Most people need no medical follow-up, and the main self-care priorities are keeping the wound clean and watching for infection.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Clean grazes promptly and thoroughly with clean water
    \item Keep the wound covered with a clean, non-stick dressing
    \item Change the dressing daily and keep the wound moist
    \item Do not pick at scabs, which delays healing and increases scarring
    \item Protect healing skin from the sun to reduce pigmentation changes
    \item Wear appropriate protective gear, such as knee and elbow pads and helmets, during sport and cycling
    \item Ensure tetanus vaccinations are up to date
    \item See a doctor if the wound shows signs of infection or does not heal
    \item People with diabetes should have even minor grazes reviewed if healing is slow
\end{itemize}
