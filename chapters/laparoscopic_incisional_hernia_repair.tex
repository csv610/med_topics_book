\chapter{Laparoscopic incisional hernia repair}
\index{Laparoscopic incisional hernia repair}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An incisional hernia occurs when intra-abdominal tissue or organs protrude through a defect or weakness in a previous surgical scar. Laparoscopic incisional hernia repair is a minimally invasive surgical procedure designed to repair this abdominal wall defect using specialised keyhole instruments and a laparoscope (a thin, lighted camera). The procedure involves reducing the herniated contents back into the peritoneal cavity, clearing adhesions, and closing the defect or reinforcing the weakened abdominal wall with a synthetic or biosynthetic mesh. Laparoscopy offers significant clinical benefits over open repair, including reduced postoperative pain, lower surgical site infection rates, shorter hospital stays, and faster return to daily activities. It is a critical intervention to alleviate symptoms, prevent progression, and avoid life-threatening complications such as bowel obstruction or strangulation.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Incisional hernias represent a common complication of abdominal surgery, occurring in approximately 10\% to 20\% of patients undergoing laparotomy, and rising up to 30\% in high-risk cohorts or where surgical site infections occurred. Laparoscopic repair is increasingly selected for small-to-moderate primary defects (typically between 2 cm and 10 cm in width). In many countries and globally, the prevalence of incisional hernias is higher in older adults, with peak incidence in the sixth and seventh decades of life, owing to age-related changes in collagen synthesis and cumulative abdominal surgeries. There is a slight female predominance for certain types of abdominal hernias, though incisional hernias are generally distributed equally between sexes, reflecting the underlying rates of laparotomies. Key risk factors that significantly elevate incidence include obesity, diabetes, chronic respiratory diseases causing cough, smoking, and previous postoperative wound infections.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
An incisional hernia develops when the structural integrity of the fascial closure is compromised, leading to mechanical failure of the abdominal wall.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgical wound failure:} Poor fascial healing, excessive suture tension, or inappropriate suture selection during the primary abdominal closure.
    \item \textbf{Patient comorbidities:} Chronic conditions such as diabetes mellitus, malnutrition, chronic kidney disease, or long-term corticosteroid use that impair collagen synthesis and tissue repair.
    \item \textbf{Increased intra-abdominal pressure:} Chronic coughing (e.g. from chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), severe constipation, urinary straining due to prostatic hypertrophy, or ascites that apply continuous mechanical stress to the scar.
    \item \textbf{Obesity:} Excess adipose tissue increases intra-abdominal pressure and compromises wound healing by impairing local microvascular perfusion.
    \item \textbf{Surgical site infection:} Postoperative wound infections degrade fascial tissue through bacterial enzymatic activity, dramatically increasing the risk of subsequent herniation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of an incisional hernia is characterised by both localised symptoms and signs of gastrointestinal compromise.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visible bulge:} A soft bulge or protrusion along or adjacent to a previous surgical scar, which typically becomes more prominent with standing, coughing, or straining.
    \item \textbf{Localised discomfort:} Dull, aching pain or a feeling of heaviness at the hernia site, often exacerbated by physical exertion or prolonged standing.
    \item \textbf{Reducibility:} The ability to gently push the bulging mass back into the abdominal cavity, either manually or when lying flat.
    \item \textbf{Skin changes:} Overlying skin may become thin, discoloured, or ulcerated in chronic, large hernias due to pressure and friction.
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal symptoms:} Nausea, vomiting, abdominal bloating, and severe localised pain, indicating potential incarceration or strangulation of bowel loops.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
It is essential to differentiate an incisional hernia from other abdominal wall masses and sources of localised pain.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diastasis recti:} Separation of the rectus abdominis muscles without a true fascial defect, presenting as a midline bulge during straining.
    \item \textbf{Abdominal wall haematoma or seroma:} A collection of blood or fluid within the surgical scar, typically appearing in the early postoperative period without a fascial gap.
    \item \textbf{Postoperative abscess:} A localised infectious collection characterised by fluctuance, erythema, warmth, and systemic signs like fever.
    \item \textbf{Suture granuloma:} A foreign-body inflammatory reaction to suture material, presenting as a firm, localised nodule under the skin.
    \item \textbf{Abdominal wall neoplasm:} Benign or malignant soft tissue tumours (e.g. desmoid tumours, lipomas, or metastatic lesions) mimicking a hernia bulge.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients with a known or suspected incisional hernia should consult their surgeon or general practitioner for an elective evaluation. Immediate emergency medical attention must be sought if signs of hernia incarceration, bowel obstruction, or strangulation occur.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Incarcerated hernia:} A hernia bulge that cannot be pushed back in and is accompanied by severe, constant pain.
    \item \textbf{Signs of strangulation:} Severe localised pain, redness, warmth, or purple or dark discolouration of the skin overlying the bulge.
    \item \textbf{Bowel obstruction:} Nausea, repetitive vomiting, severe abdominal cramping, and an inability to pass wind or open the bowels.
    \item \textbf{Systemic toxicity:} High fever, rapid heart rate, dizziness, or confusion alongside a painful hernia bulge.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is primarily clinical, supplemented by imaging to delineate the defect size, hernia contents, and to plan the surgical approach.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} Palpation of the abdominal wall while the patient is both supine and standing, using coughing manoeuvres (Valsalva) to elicit the bulge.
    \item \textbf{Abdominal CT scan:} The gold standard imaging modality, providing detailed anatomical views of the defect size, contents (e.g. omentum or bowel), and rectus muscle quality.
    \item \textbf{Abdominal ultrasound:} A non-invasive, dynamic imaging option useful for identifying fascial defects and verifying reducibility when CT is contraindicated.
    \item \textbf{Routine blood tests:} Pre-operative evaluation including full blood count, electrolytes, and coagulation profiles to assess surgical fitness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Surgical repair is the definitive management, with laparoscopic approaches preferred for appropriate defects to minimise wound complications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Laparoscopic reduction and adhesiolysis:} Carefully freeing intra-abdominal adhesions to the abdominal wall and reducing the herniated contents back into the peritoneal cavity.
    \item \textbf{Mesh reinforcement:} Placement of a synthetic (e.g. polypropylene, polyester) or composite mesh extending at least 3--5 cm beyond the margins of the defect, secured with tacks, sutures, or fibrin glue.
    \item \textbf{Defect closure (IPOM-plus):} Closure of the fascial defect prior to mesh placement to improve abdominal wall function and reduce seroma formation.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological pain control:} Multimodal postoperative analgesia using paracetamol, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and short-term oral opioids.
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} Temporary restriction of heavy lifting (typically greater than 5 kg) and avoidance of strenuous abdominal exercises for 6--8 weeks post-surgery.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Seroma or haematoma formation:} Accumulation of fluid or blood in the dead space of the previous hernia sac, usually resolving spontaneously.
    \item \textbf{Enterotomy:} Inadvertent bowel injury during adhesiolysis, requiring immediate intra-operative repair and potentially conversion to open surgery.
    \item \textbf{Mesh infection:} A serious complication that may require long-term antibiotics or complete surgical removal of the mesh.
    \item \textbf{Chronic pain:} Persistent discomfort at the repair site, often related to tack placement or nerve irritation by anchoring sutures.
    \item \textbf{Hernia recurrence:} Return of the hernia, which can occur in 5\% to 10\% of laparoscopic repairs depending on patient factors and defect size.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for patients undergoing laparoscopic incisional hernia repair is generally excellent. Minimally invasive techniques have reduced the overall complication rate and shortened hospital stays to 1--3 days compared to open repairs. Patients typically experience a significant improvement in quality of life, physical function, and cosmetic appearance. Most individuals return to light activities within 1--2 weeks and full physical function within 6--8 weeks. The long-term recurrence rate is low when reinforced with adequate mesh, though ongoing optimisation of risk factors (such as weight management and smoking cessation) remains crucial to ensure a durable repair.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Weight management:} Maintaining a healthy body mass index (BMI) to reduce mechanical stress on the abdominal wall.
    \item \textbf{Smoking cessation:} Quitting smoking to enhance collagen synthesis, tissue perfusion, and general wound healing.
    \item \textbf{Proper lifting techniques:} Avoiding heavy lifting and using leg muscles rather than straining the abdominal wall when lifting objects.
    \item \textbf{Regular gentle exercise:} Engaging in walking and core-strengthening activities as advised by a physiotherapist post-recovery.
    \item \textbf{Preventing constipation:} Consuming a high-fibre diet and drinking plenty of water to avoid straining during bowel movements.
\end{itemize}
