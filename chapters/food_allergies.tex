\chapter{Food Allergies}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A food allergy is an immune reaction to a food protein that the body mistakenly treats as a threat. It is different from food intolerance, which does not involve the immune system. Food allergies range from mild reactions, such as hives and tingling, to severe anaphylaxis, which is life-threatening and requires immediate treatment with adrenaline. The most common triggers are peanuts, tree nuts, milk, eggs, wheat, soy, fish, and shellfish, and peanuts and tree nuts are the most common causes of severe reactions in Australia. Food allergy is most common in young children, and some allergies, such as to milk and egg, are often outgrown, while others, such as peanut and seafood allergy, tend to persist. This chapter explains the types of food allergy, the symptoms, and how they are diagnosed and managed.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Food allergy is common and increasing in Australia, which has one of the highest rates in the world. Around 1 in 10 infants has a food allergy, with egg and peanut the most common triggers in early childhood. Around 3% of adults have a confirmed food allergy. Hospital admissions for anaphylaxis have risen sharply in recent decades, and food is the most common trigger, followed by insect stings and medicines. Peanut allergy affects around 3% of infants and persists into adulthood in most. Introduction of foods early in infancy, under guidance, is now recommended to reduce the risk of developing allergy, a change that has followed landmark research.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The immune response:} in food allergy, the immune system produces immunoglobulin E (IgE) antibodies against a food protein
    \item \textbf{Immediate (IgE-mediated) allergy:} on re-exposure, the antibodies trigger the release of histamine and other chemicals, causing symptoms within minutes to 2 hours
    \item \textbf{Anaphylaxis:} the severe end of the reaction, in which chemicals are released throughout the body, causing airway swelling, breathing difficulty, and a drop in blood pressure
    \item \textbf{Non-IgE-mediated allergy:} a delayed reaction involving other immune cells, causing gut symptoms such as vomiting, diarrhoea, and reflux, hours after eating
    \item \textbf{Common allergens:} peanut, tree nuts, egg, cow's milk, wheat, soy, fish, and shellfish
    \item \textbf{The "allergic march":} children with food allergy often develop eczema, asthma, and allergic rhinitis later
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} a family history of allergy, early-life eczema, and delayed introduction of allergenic foods
    \item \textbf{Why reactions vary:} the amount eaten, the form of the food, exercise around the meal, and the individual's sensitivity all influence severity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Immediate reactions: hives, swelling of the lips, face, or tongue, tingling in the mouth, vomiting, and abdominal pain
    \item Respiratory symptoms: wheeze, cough, difficulty breathing, and a hoarse voice
    \item Anaphylaxis: difficulty breathing, throat tightness, dizziness, collapse, and loss of consciousness, occurring rapidly
    \item Skin symptoms: itching, flushing, and eczema flare-ups
    \item Delayed reactions: vomiting, diarrhoea, poor growth, and blood in the stool in non-IgE-mediated allergy
    \item The time course: immediate reactions occur within minutes to 2 hours; delayed reactions within hours to days
    \item The range of severity: the same person can have mild reactions on one occasion and severe on another
    \item Oral allergy syndrome: mild tingling and itching of the mouth and lips with raw fruits and vegetables in people with pollen allergy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Not every reaction to food is an allergy.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Food intolerance, such as lactose intolerance, which causes gut symptoms without immune involvement
    \item Coeliac disease, an autoimmune reaction to gluten with gut and other symptoms
    \item Food poisoning and infectious gastroenteritis
    \item Irritable bowel syndrome, with symptoms after certain foods
    \item Histamine reactions from fish and other foods
    \item Eczema, asthma, and allergic rhinitis, which can coexist and confuse the picture
    \item The distinction is made by testing and specialist assessment
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who has had a reaction to food, or whose child has had one, should see a doctor for assessment and testing. Diagnosis before the next exposure is important, because reactions can be more severe the next time.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, wheezing, or throat tightness after eating
    \item Swelling of the tongue or face, hoarse voice, or difficulty swallowing
    \item Dizziness, collapse, or loss of consciousness
    \item Vomiting with any breathing difficulty
    \item Any suspected anaphylaxis, and give the adrenaline autoinjector immediately if available
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the most important diagnostic tool; the food, the amount, the timing, and the symptoms are documented
    \item \textbf{Skin prick testing:} small drops of allergen extracts placed on the skin are pricked, and a wheal indicates sensitisation
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} specific IgE levels to foods
    \item \textbf{Interpretation:} positive tests show sensitisation, which does not always mean clinical allergy; a specialist correlates tests with the history
    \item \textbf{Oral food challenge:} the gold standard, conducted in hospital, in which the food is eaten under observation to confirm or exclude allergy
    \item \textbf{Anaphylaxis plan:} for those with confirmed allergy, an ASCIA action plan and prescribed adrenaline
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Strict avoidance:} the mainstay; reading labels, informing restaurants, and avoiding cross-contamination
    \item \textbf{Adrenaline autoinjectors:} prescribed for those at risk of anaphylaxis, with training in how and when to use them
    \item \textbf{ASCIA action plan:} a written plan detailing recognition of reactions and treatment steps, shared with schools and carers
    \item \textbf{Antihistamines:} for mild symptoms only, never instead of adrenaline for anaphylaxis
    \item \textbf{Management of non-IgE allergy:} dietary elimination under dietitian supervision, with reintroduction as tolerated
    \item \textbf{Oral immunotherapy:} increasingly available, in which small amounts of peanut protein are given under specialist supervision to build tolerance
    \item \textbf{Reassessment:} children with egg and milk allergy are re-tested over time, as many outgrow them
    \item \textbf{School and childcare plans:} communication and staff training to keep children safe
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Anaphylaxis, which can be fatal without prompt adrenaline
    \item The anxiety of living with food allergy, which affects the whole family
    \item Nutritional problems if elimination diets are not properly managed
    \item Social restriction and bullying in school settings
    \item Adverse food reactions from accidental exposure despite precautions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Outcomes have improved markedly with better diagnosis and management. Most children outgrow egg and milk allergy by school age, and some outgrow peanut and other allergies, though peanut, tree nut, and seafood allergies often persist. People with food allergy who carry and use adrenaline, follow their action plan, and avoid their triggers have an excellent outlook and live full lives. Oral immunotherapy is expanding treatment options, and research continues to improve therapies. The key to a good outcome is specialist diagnosis, education, and preparedness.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Introduce common allergens such as peanut and egg to infants from around 6 months of age, when solids are started, under the current national guidelines, which reduce the risk of allergy
    \item For a child with severe eczema or a family history of allergy, seek advice before introducing allergenic foods
    \item Read food labels carefully and learn the alternative names for the allergen
    \item Tell restaurants, schools, and family about the allergy and the action plan
    \item Carry the adrenaline autoinjector at all times and check its expiry date
    \item Practise using a training device so the autoinjector is used correctly in an emergency
    \item Never deliberately test a food that has caused a reaction
    \item Attend allergy review appointments and specialist follow-up
\end{itemize}
