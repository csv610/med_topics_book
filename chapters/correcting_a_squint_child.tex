\chapter{Correcting a Squint (Child)}
\index{Correcting a Squint (Child)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A squint (strabismus) in a child is a condition in which the eyes do not look in the same direction, with one eye turning inwards, outwards, upwards, or downwards. Correcting a childhood squint is important not just for appearance but because the developing brain can suppress vision from the misaligned eye, leading to permanent reduced vision (amblyopia or "lazy eye"). Treatment aims to straighten the eyes, allow normal development of binocular vision, and prevent amblyopia. Options include glasses, patching, eye drops, and surgery, and treatment is most effective when started early.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Squints affect around 2--4\% of children, making strabismus one of the most common childhood eye conditions. The most frequent type is a convergent squint (esotropia), often associated with long-sightedness and appearing between the ages of two and four. Squints also occur in infancy and can be associated with prematurity, family history, neurological conditions, or cataract. Early detection through routine child health checks and vision screening is important, because untreated squint is a leading cause of preventable amblyopia.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Childhood squints arise from problems with eye alignment, focusing, or the brain's control of eye movement.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Refractive error:} uncorrected long-sightedness causes the eyes to over-converge
    \item \textbf{Infantile (congenital) esotropia:} a large, constant inward turn present in the first year of life
    \item \textbf{Accommodative esotropia:} convergent squint driven by the effort of focusing
    \item \textbf{Intermittent exotropia:} an outward drift, often worse when tired or daydreaming
    \item \textbf{Nerve or muscle weakness:} cranial nerve palsy or abnormal muscle action
    \item \textbf{Amblyopia:} the brain suppresses the image from the turned eye, and that eye loses vision
    \item \textbf{Neurological or genetic conditions:} cerebral palsy, Down syndrome, and other disorders increase the risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
A squint may be noticed by parents, or detected at a screening examination.

\begin{itemize}
    \item One eye appearing to turn inwards or outwards, constantly or intermittently
    \item The eyes appearing aligned in photographs but misaligned in person (or vice versa)
    \item Squinting or closing one eye in bright light
    \item Head tilting or turning to compensate
    \item Poor depth perception or clumsiness
    \item Eye rubbing or apparent difficulty seeing
    \item In babies: a constant turn after three months of age (all babies have intermittent misalignment in the early weeks)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
An apparent misalignment is not always a true squint.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Pseudostrabismus: prominent skin folds or a broad nasal bridge making the eyes appear crossed
    \item Refractive error without true misalignment
    \item Nystagmus or other eye movement disorders
    \item Cataract, retinoblastoma, or other eye disease causing the eye to drift
    \item Neurological conditions affecting eye movement control
    \item A transient intermittent squint that does not need surgery but needs monitoring
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any child with a constant squint after three to four months of age, or with any squint associated with vision concerns, should be assessed by an optometrist or ophthalmologist. Early referral is essential because treatment of amblyopia is most successful in the preschool years.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A sudden new squint or double vision in an older child
    \item A squint with headache, vomiting, or drowsiness
    \item A white or yellow glow visible in the pupil (possible retinoblastoma, a rare eye cancer)
    \item Eye injury or sudden loss of vision
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Children are examined by a specialist eye team, using methods adapted to age.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} age of onset, whether the squint is constant or intermittent, family history, and birth history
    \item \textbf{Visual acuity testing:} picture or letter charts matched to the child's age
    \item \textbf{Cover tests:} reveal the direction and size of the misalignment
    \item \textbf{Refraction:} drops are used to relax the focusing muscles and measure the glasses prescription
    \item \textbf{Examination of the back of the eye:} to exclude cataract, retinoblastoma, and other disease
    \item \textbf{Orthoptic assessment:} measurement of binocular function
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a paediatric ophthalmologist for diagnosis and management planning
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is planned by a paediatric ophthalmologist and orthoptist.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Glasses:} the first-line treatment for many squints, especially accommodative esotropia; the glasses often straighten the eyes without surgery
    \item \textbf{Patching:} the good eye is covered for several hours a day to force the amblyopic eye to work
    \item \textbf{Atropine drops:} blur the vision in the good eye as an alternative to patching
    \item \textbf{Orthoptic exercises:} for intermittent and control issues
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} eye muscles are repositioned under general anaesthetic when glasses and occlusion are insufficient
    \item \textbf{Post-operative care:} eye drops and follow-up visits, with further glasses or patching as needed
    \item \textbf{Ongoing monitoring:} alignment and vision are checked over years, as the squint can recur
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Amblyopia (lazy eye) with permanent reduced vision if treatment is delayed
    \item Loss of binocular vision and depth perception
    \item The squint recurring after treatment
    \item Double vision (uncommon in children, more common after adult surgery)
    \item Over- or under-correction after surgery
    \item Anaesthetic risks and post-operative redness or infection
    \item Psychosocial effects of teasing or low self-esteem
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for childhood squint is very good when treatment starts early. Glasses alone correct many convergent squints, and patching restores useful vision in most children with amblyopia. When surgery is needed, it successfully straightens the eyes in the majority of children, and early surgery gives the best chance of developing binocular vision. Because the eyes can drift again, children need ongoing review through childhood. Untreated squint leads to permanent loss of vision in the affected eye, which is why screening and early referral matter so much.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend routine child health checks and vision screening
    \item See a GP if you are concerned about your child's eyes at any age
    \item Ensure glasses are worn as prescribed and kept in good condition
    \item Follow the patching or eye drop schedule exactly, even if the child resists
    \item Keep all follow-up appointments, even when the eyes look straight
    \item Encourage a child with glasses or a patch and offer positive support
    \item Protect the eyes from injury with safe toys and, when older, protective eyewear for sport
\end{itemize}
