\chapter{Inguinal Hernia Repair (Child)}
\index{Inguinal Hernia Repair (Child)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Inguinal hernia repair in a child is an operation to fix a hernia in the groin, a condition that is present at birth or develops in infancy. In children, the hernia is caused by a small opening in the abdominal wall that fails to close after the testicle descends in boys, allowing abdominal contents to bulge into the groin or scrotum. Because of the risk of the hernia becoming trapped and strangulated, repair is recommended soon after diagnosis, even in babies. The operation is short and safe, is performed under general anaesthesia as day surgery, and has an excellent outcome, with a very low risk of recurrence.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Inguinal hernia is one of the most common surgical conditions of childhood, affecting around 3 to 5 per cent of full-term babies and a higher proportion of babies born prematurely, in whom rates reach 10 to 30 per cent. Boys are affected four to ten times more often than girls. The hernia may be present at birth or appear in the first months of life, and up to a third are present on both sides. A family history of hernia is common. Strangulation is most likely in the first year of life, which is why early repair is advised.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
During development, the testicles form inside the abdomen and descend through the inguinal canal into the scrotum, bringing with them a finger-like pouch of abdominal lining called the processus vaginalis. This pouch normally closes off after descent. When it fails to close, abdominal contents, usually a loop of bowel or fatty tissue, can pass through it to produce a hernia. The same pathway exists in girls, carrying the round ligament. A patent opening allows intermittent bulging with crying, coughing, or straining. The main danger is incarceration, in which the contents become trapped and their blood supply is compromised, leading to strangulation of the bowel, which is an emergency.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A bulge in the groin, sometimes extending into the scrotum or labia
    \item The bulge may appear when the baby cries, strains, or stands, and disappear when calm or lying
    \item The bulge may be reducible with gentle pressure
    \item Often painless, but older children may feel discomfort
    \item An incarcerated hernia is firm, tender, and non-reducible, with the baby irritable, vomiting, or refusing feeds
    \item A swollen, red, or discoloured groin suggests strangulation
    \item In premature babies the hernia may appear later, after discharge from hospital
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of a groin or scrotal swelling in a child should be distinguished from a hernia.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hydrocele:} a fluid collection in the scrotum that is usually soft and transilluminates
    \item \textbf{Undescended or retractile testicle:} a testicle that is not in the scrotum
    \item \textbf{Enlarged lymph nodes:} in the groin from infection
    \item \textbf{Testicular torsion:} sudden severe scrotal pain requiring urgent surgery
    \item \textbf{Epididymitis:} inflammation of the testicle attachments
    \item \textbf{Cysts and rare tumours:} other groin or scrotal masses
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
A groin bulge in a child should be assessed promptly by a doctor, as repair is recommended soon after diagnosis to prevent complications. Parents should seek immediate care if the bulge becomes firm, tender, or non-reducible, or if the baby is irritable, vomiting, or unwell.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A firm, tender, non-reducible groin bulge, which may indicate incarceration
    \item Vomiting, abdominal distension, or a very irritable baby with a groin bulge
    \item A red, swollen, or discoloured groin or scrotum
    \item Signs of severe illness: lethargy, fever, or poor feeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the bulge is usually confirmed clinically, with observation during crying or standing
    \item \textbf{Reducibility testing:} gentle reduction of the hernia by the doctor
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} helpful when the diagnosis is uncertain, or to distinguish a hernia from a hydrocele
    \item \textbf{Urgent surgical review:} for suspected incarceration
    \item \textbf{Preoperative assessment:} including fitness for anaesthesia and review in premature infants
    \item \textbf{Consideration of the other side:} the surgeon checks for a hernia on both sides
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Early repair:} surgery shortly after diagnosis, usually within weeks, to reduce the risk of incarceration
    \item \textbf{Operation:} a small groin incision, with the hernia sac separated and tied off at the internal ring; no mesh is needed in children
    \item \textbf{Contralateral exploration:} the other side is often checked and repaired at the same time if a defect is present
    \item \textbf{Anaesthesia:} general anaesthesia, with day surgery for most children
    \item \textbf{Reduction of incarceration:} the doctor may reduce a trapped hernia before surgery, or it may be reduced under anaesthesia
    \item \textbf{Emergency surgery:} for incarcerated or strangulated hernias, with the bowel checked
    \item \textbf{Aftercare:} simple analgesia, wound care, and avoidance of rough play for a week or two
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Incarceration and strangulation of the bowel, which can occur rapidly in infants
    \item Damage to the testicle from strangulation or surgery
    \item Recurrence of the hernia, which is uncommon in children
    \item Wound infection, bleeding, and bruising
    \item Anaesthesia-related risks in premature or unwell babies
    \item Rarely, injury to the spermatic cord structures
    \item Effects on fertility, which are extremely rare with modern techniques
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outcome of childhood inguinal hernia repair is excellent. The operation is safe, recurrence rates are low, in the order of 1 per cent, and most children go home the same day and recover quickly. The main risk lies not in the operation but in delaying it, because incarceration and strangulation are most dangerous in young infants. Timely repair prevents these complications, and children return to normal activity within days.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Report any groin or scrotal bulge in your child to a doctor promptly
    \item Do not attempt to reduce a firm or painful bulge yourself
    \item Seek urgent care for a bulge associated with irritability, vomiting, or discolouration
    \item Follow pre-procedure fasting advice for anaesthesia
    \item After surgery, give pain relief as advised and keep the wound clean and dry
    \item Avoid vigorous activity and contact sport for the period advised
    \item Attend follow-up to confirm healing and check the other side
    \item Parents of premature babies should be alert for hernias appearing after discharge
\end{itemize}
