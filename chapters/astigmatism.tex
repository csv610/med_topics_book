\chapter{Astigmatism}
\index{Astigmatism}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Astigmatism is a very common refractive error of the eye in which the front surface of the eye (the cornea) or the lens is not perfectly spherical but is shaped more like a rugby ball than a basketball. As a result, light entering the eye is not focused onto a single point on the retina, so vision appears blurred or distorted at all distances. Astigmatism frequently occurs together with short-sightedness (myopia) or long-sightedness (hyperopia). It is usually corrected easily with glasses or contact lenses, and most people with astigmatism enjoy normal, comfortable vision once the correction is worn. Regular eye examinations detect astigmatism early, which matters most in childhood, where significant uncorrected astigmatism can interfere with visual development.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Astigmatism is one of the most common eye conditions worldwide. It can be present from birth and often runs in families. Roughly one in every three or four people has a degree of astigmatism significant enough to affect vision, and mild degrees are even more common. It occurs in both sexes and at all ages. Many children have a small amount of astigmatism that needs no correction, but larger amounts can cause blurred vision and should be detected early, because uncorrected astigmatism in childhood can lead to amblyopia (a "lazy eye"). The prevalence tends to rise in later life as the lens ages and cataractous changes develop.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Astigmatism occurs when the cornea or lens curves more steeply in one meridian than another, so the eye has two different focal points instead of one. Regular astigmatism has a smooth, symmetric corneal shape, whereas irregular astigmatism arises when the curvature is uneven or scarred.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Corneal curvature:} the most common cause is a cornea shaped more like a rugby ball than a sphere
    \item \textbf{Lenticular astigmatism:} less common, due to an irregularly shaped lens
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} the tendency to astigmatism runs in families and may be present at birth
    \item \textbf{Ageing:} lens changes and early cataract formation can alter or increase astigmatism
    \item \textbf{Injury or surgery:} scarring of the cornea after trauma, infection or eye surgery can cause irregular astigmatism
    \item \textbf{Keratoconus:} a progressive thinning and bulging of the cornea that creates increasing, often irregular, astigmatism
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Many people with mild astigmatism do not notice any symptoms. With more significant astigmatism, the main symptom is blurred or distorted vision at any distance.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Blurred, fuzzy or wavy vision that does not fully clear by squinting or moving closer
    \item Eye strain, soreness or fatigue, especially after reading or screen work
    \item Frequent headaches, particularly across the forehead
    \item Difficulty seeing clearly at night or in dim light
    \item Squinting or tilting the head to try to focus
    \item In children: poor school performance, avoiding reading, or rubbing the eyes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because astigmatism causes blurred vision, other causes of blurred vision must be considered.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Myopia and hyperopia:} short- and long-sightedness, which may coexist with astigmatism
    \item \textbf{Presbyopia:} age-related loss of near focusing, usually after age 40
    \item \textbf{Cataract:} clouding of the lens causing gradual blurring at any distance
    \item \textbf{Keratoconus:} progressive corneal bulging with increasingly distorted vision
    \item \textbf{Corneal scarring or infection:} may cause irregular astigmatism or blurred vision
    \item \textbf{Retinal disease:} problems at the back of the eye that reduce visual clarity
    \item \textbf{Amblyopia:} reduced vision because the brain does not use an eye properly, most often in children
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See an optometrist or GP if vision is blurred at any distance, if there is persistent eye strain or headache with close work, or if a child squints, rubs the eyes or appears not to see clearly.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden loss of vision or a sudden change in sight
    \item Eye pain with redness, light sensitivity or nausea
    \item Flashes of light, a shower of floaters or a shadow across the vision
    \item An injury to the eye with loss of vision
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Astigmatism is diagnosed during a routine eye examination, which should be performed regularly in children and at intervals in adults.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visual acuity testing:} reading an eye chart to measure clarity of vision at distance and near
    \item \textbf{Retinoscopy:} shining a light into the eye to estimate the refractive error, useful in young children
    \item \textbf{Autorefraction:} a machine-based measurement of how the eye focuses light
    \item \textbf{Subjective refraction:} trying a series of lenses to find the prescription that gives the clearest vision
    \item \textbf{Keratometry and corneal topography:} mapping the curve of the cornea, especially if keratoconus is suspected
    \item \textbf{Eye health checks:} examination of the front and back of the eye to exclude other disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Astigmatism is corrected with lenses, and most people manage very well.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Glasses:} the simplest and safest correction for all ages, including children
    \item \textbf{Contact lenses:} soft or rigid gas-permeable lenses; rigid lenses can smooth out irregular astigmatism
    \item \textbf{Refractive surgery:} laser procedures such as LASIK or PRK reshape the cornea in suitable adults
    \item \textbf{Regular eye tests:} the prescription can change over time and should be checked periodically
    \item \textbf{Children:} prompt correction with glasses prevents amblyopia, with follow-up until vision stabilises
    \item \textbf{Keratoconus:} if present, needs specialist care, which may include special contact lenses, cross-linking or other procedures
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Astigmatism itself is not a disease and does not harm the eye, but uncorrected astigmatism can cause problems, especially in childhood.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Amblyopia:} in children, blurred images from one eye can cause the brain to suppress that eye permanently
    \item \textbf{Strabismus:} a squint can develop alongside significant refractive error in children
    \item \textbf{Chronic headaches and eye strain:} from straining to focus, especially during close work
    \item Reduced reading speed and school performance in uncorrected children
    \item Difficulty driving at night or performing detailed visual tasks without correction
    \item Complications of refractive surgery, including dry eyes, glare or under-correction, are uncommon but possible
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Astigmatism is a lifelong but stable condition for most people, and it is easily and safely corrected. Once glasses or contact lenses are worn, vision is usually clear and normal. The amount of astigmatism may change slowly over time, and prescriptions are typically updated at routine eye tests. Children corrected early have an excellent visual outcome, with amblyopia preventable in the great majority of cases. Adults who choose refractive surgery often reduce or remove their need for glasses, although outcomes vary and depend on the individual eye.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Astigmatism cannot be prevented, as it is largely determined by the shape of the eye a person is born with. However, its effects can be minimised.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Have children's eyes tested early, and again at school entry and at intervals thereafter
    \item Have regular eye examinations in adulthood, every 1 to 2 years or as recommended
    \item Wear the prescribed glasses or contacts as advised; children's prescriptions need review as they grow
    \item Take regular breaks from close screen work to reduce eye strain
    \item Protect the eyes from injury with appropriate safety eyewear
    \item Seek review promptly if vision changes or worsens at any age
\end{itemize}
