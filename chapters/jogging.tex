\chapter{Jogging}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Jogging is steady, moderate-intensity running at a comfortable pace, and it is one of the most popular and accessible forms of physical activity. Regular jogging improves heart and lung fitness, helps control weight, strengthens bones and muscles, reduces stress, and lowers the risk of heart disease, diabetes, stroke, and many cancers. It requires little equipment beyond good shoes and can be done almost anywhere. To gain the benefits safely, joggers should build up gradually, warm up and cool down, and listen to their bodies, as overuse injuries such as shin splints and runner's knee are common when training increases too quickly.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Physical inactivity is a leading risk factor for chronic disease, and around half of Australian adults do not meet recommended activity levels. Jogging is among the most common forms of exercise for people who are active, with participation highest among adults aged 20 to 50. Studies of large running populations show that even modest amounts of jogging, such as 20 to 30 minutes several times a week, are associated with substantially lower mortality. Overuse injuries affect a significant proportion of runners each year, most often the knee, shin, Achilles tendon, and foot.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Jogging delivers health benefits through its effects on the heart, circulation, metabolism, and muscles. It increases the heart's pumping efficiency, improves blood pressure and cholesterol, improves glucose handling, and supports a healthy body weight by burning kilojoules. Weight-bearing activity strengthens bones and reduces the risk of osteoporosis. The main risk of jogging is injury to the musculoskeletal system, which occurs when training load exceeds the tissue's capacity to adapt, particularly with sudden increases in distance, speed, or frequency, poor footwear, muscle imbalance, or running on hard surfaces. Most overuse injuries respond to rest, gradual return, and correction of the underlying factors.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Improved fitness, energy, and mood with regular jogging
    \item Benefits seen from as little as 20 to 30 minutes, three times a week
    \item Common overuse injuries: shin splints, runner's knee, Achilles tendonitis, and plantar fasciitis
    \item Pain that comes on with running and settles with rest often indicates overuse
    \item Sharp pain, swelling, or pain at night suggests a more significant injury
    \item Blisters, chafing, and sun exposure are common minor problems
    \item Heat exhaustion and dehydration in hot weather
    \item Gradual improvement in pace and endurance with consistent training
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Pain during or after jogging may have various causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Muscle strains:} calf, hamstring, and groin injuries
    \item \textbf{Shin splints:} pain along the shin bone from overuse
    \item \textbf{Patellofemoral pain (runner's knee):} pain around the kneecap
    \item \textbf{Achilles tendonitis and rupture:} pain or a snap at the back of the ankle
    \item \textbf{Stress fractures:} bone pain that persists and is tender to pressure
    \item \textbf{Plantar fasciitis:} heel pain on the first steps of the day
    \item \textbf{Other conditions:} compartment syndrome and referred pain from the hip or back
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Medical assessment is appropriate for pain that is severe, persistent, or worsening, for pain with swelling, bruising, or an inability to bear weight, and for symptoms that do not settle with rest. Anyone with chest pain, palpitations, or unusual breathlessness while jogging should stop and seek assessment. People starting to jog with existing health conditions should discuss it with their doctor.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, pressure, or tightness while running
    \item Collapse, fainting, or confusion
    \item Sudden severe breathlessness or a racing, irregular heartbeat
    \item Heat stroke: confusion, very high body temperature, or collapse in hot weather
    \item A sudden snap with severe pain, which may indicate a tendon rupture
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} training pattern, footwear, surfaces, and the nature of any pain
    \item \textbf{Examination:} assessment of the painful area and biomechanics
    \item \textbf{Running assessment:} gait and technique review, sometimes by a physiotherapist
    \item \textbf{X-rays and scans:} for suspected fractures, stress fractures, or tendon injury
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} where health concerns arise, such as anaemia or thyroid problems
    \item \textbf{Health screening:} cardiovascular assessment for people starting exercise with risk factors
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gradual progression:} increasing distance or time by no more than 10 per cent per week
    \item \textbf{Rest and recovery:} rest days between runs and attention to sleep and nutrition
    \item \textbf{Good footwear:} well-fitted running shoes, replaced regularly
    \item \textbf{Warm-up and cool-down:} easy jogging and stretching around runs
    \item \textbf{Treatment of injuries:} rest, ice, compression, and elevation, with physiotherapy for persistent problems
    \item \textbf{Cross-training:} swimming, cycling, and strength work to balance the body
    \item \textbf{Strength and flexibility:} exercises for the legs, hips, and core
    \item \textbf{Returning to running:} gradual return after injury, guided by pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Overuse injuries, which are common and often preventable
    \item Stress fractures from excessive training
    \item Achilles tendon rupture in middle-aged runners
    \item Heat illness and dehydration in hot weather
    \item Cardiac events in people with undiagnosed heart disease, which are rare
    \item Sun damage and skin cancer in outdoor runners
    \item Reduced motivation and stopping exercise after injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The health benefits of jogging are substantial and dose-related: even low amounts reduce the risk of premature death, and regular joggers have lower rates of heart disease, diabetes, and many cancers. Most overuse injuries resolve with rest and gradual return, and injury rates fall with sensible progression and good footwear. With a consistent, gradual approach, jogging is a safe, lifelong activity that most people can sustain into older age.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Start gradually and build up slowly, especially if new to running
    \item Wear well-fitted running shoes and replace them regularly
    \item Run on forgiving surfaces where possible
    \item Include rest days and cross-training
    \item Warm up before and cool down after runs
    \item Stay hydrated and run in the cooler parts of the day in summer
    \item Use sunscreen, a hat, and sunglasses
    \item Run with a friend or carry identification
    \item Seek advice early for persistent pain
    \item Get a health check before starting if you have risk factors or have been inactive
\end{itemize}
