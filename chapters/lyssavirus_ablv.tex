\chapter{Lyssavirus (ABLV)}
\index{Lyssavirus (ABLV)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
local bat lyssavirus (ABLV) is a zoonotic viral pathogen belonging to the genus \textit{Lyssavirus} within the family \textit{Rhabdoviridae}. Closely related to the classical rabies virus, ABLV is endemic in local bat populations, including both megabats (flying foxes) and microbats. First identified in 1996, the virus causes a progressive, fatal encephalomyelitis in both bats and humans. Transmission to humans occurs through bites, scratches, or saliva contact from an infected bat with mucous membranes or broken skin. Although human infection is extremely rare, it is uniformly fatal once clinical symptoms develop. As there is no effective curative treatment for established infection, clinical management focuses on prevention via pre-exposure vaccination for at-risk individuals and prompt post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) following any bat exposure.

\section*{Epidemiology}
ABLV is enzootic across some countries, present in all four common species of flying foxes (black, grey-headed, spectacled, and little red flying foxes) and at least one microbat species (the yellow-bellied sheath-tailed bat). Studies suggest that approximately 1\% of wild bats carry the virus, with the prevalence rising significantly to 5\% to 10\% in sick, injured, or orphaned bats. Since its discovery in 1996, only three human cases of ABLV have been recorded (in 1996, 1998, and 2013), all occurring in Queensland. Each case was fatal and resulted from a bite or scratch from an infected bat, with incubation periods ranging from a few weeks to over two years. Because bats are the only known reservoir, individuals who handle bats, such as veterinarians, wildlife carers, and researchers, are at the highest risk of exposure.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
ABLV is an enveloped, bullet-shaped, single-stranded, negative-sense RNA virus.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral classification:} Belonging to phylogroup I of the genus \textit{Lyssavirus}, ABLV is genetically and antigenically distinct from classical rabies virus but shares close clinical similarities.
    \item \textbf{Route of entry:} The virus enters the body via bat saliva introduced through a bite, scratch, or mucous membrane exposure.
    \item \textbf{Neurotropic spread:} The virus replicates locally in muscle tissue before binding to nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction, entering peripheral nerves.
    \item \textbf{Retrograde axonal transport:} The virus travels along the axons of peripheral nerves to the spinal cord and brain at a rate of approximately 50 to 100 millimetres per day.
    \item \textbf{Encephalomyelitis:} Upon reaching the central nervous system, rapid viral replication causes severe, diffuse neuronal dysfunction, inflammation, and necrosis, followed by centrifugal spread along autonomic nerves to salivary glands, skin, and other organs.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The incubation period in humans typically ranges from 4 weeks to several months, but can extend to over two years. The clinical presentation is virtually identical to classical rabies, progressing through prodromal, acute neurological, and comatose phases.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prodrome:} Non-specific symptoms including fever, headache, malaise, and anxiety, frequently accompanied by localized pain, tingling, or itching at the site of the original bat exposure.
    \item \textbf{Encephalitic (furious) phase:} Fluctuating consciousness, extreme agitation, confusion, hallucinations, and autonomic instability (e.g. hypersalivation, lacrimation, and sweating).
    \item \textbf{Hydrophobia and aerophobia:} Violent, painful spasms of the diaphragmatic, laryngeal, and pharyngeal muscles triggered by attempts to swallow fluids or drafts of air.
    \item \textbf{Paralytic (dumb) phase:} Progressive, ascending flaccid muscle weakness and paralysis, which may dominate the clinical picture in some cases instead of hyperactivity.
    \item \textbf{Coma and death:} Progression to deep coma, respiratory failure, cardiac arrhythmias, and multi-organ failure.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Given the rarity of the disease, ABLV must be differentiated from other viral infections of the central nervous system and neurological disorders.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Classical rabies:} Excluded in patients without a history of overseas travel to rabies-endemic regions or exposure to foreign animals.
    \item \textbf{Viral encephalitis:} Herpes simplex virus (HSV-1), varicella-zoster virus (VZV), or enterovirus infections presenting with fever and altered consciousness, distinguished by CSF PCR.
    \item \textbf{Arboviral encephalitides:} Japanese encephalitis, Murray Valley encephalitis, or Kunjin virus infections, which are also endemic to parts of the affected region and diagnosed via serology or PCR.
    \item \textbf{Tetanus:} Presents with muscle rigidity, trismus, and spasms, but typically lacks sensory prodrome, hydrophobia, or psychiatric features, and is associated with dirty wounds.
    \item \textbf{Guillain-Barré syndrome:} Ascending paralysis resembling paralytic ABLV, but lacks fever, autonomic dysfunction, or encephalopathy, and exhibits albuminocytological dissociation in CSF.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any bite, scratch, or mucous membrane exposure to saliva from an local bat (regardless of species) is a medical emergency requiring immediate first aid and urgent medical evaluation to initiate post-exposure prophylaxis.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, uncontrollable muscle spasms or respiratory distress
    \item Sudden onset of confusion, agitation, or hallucinations following a known or suspected bat exposure
    \item Inability to swallow fluids or severe throat tightness
    \item Progressive muscle weakness starting in the limbs
    \item Seizures or loss of consciousness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis in a living patient is challenging, and negative results do not rule out infection early in the clinical course.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR):} Performed on saliva, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), or skin biopsy samples (typically taken from the nape of the neck) to detect ABLV RNA.
    \item \textbf{Direct fluorescent antibody (DFA) testing:} The gold standard for detecting lyssavirus antigen in brain tissue, typically performed post-mortem or on euthanised bats.
    \item \textbf{Serology:} Detection of neutralizing antibodies in serum or CSF, though antibodies are often absent until late in the clinical illness.
    \item \textbf{Brain biopsy:} Rarely performed ante-mortem due to high risk, but reveals pathognomonic Negri bodies (eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions) under microscopy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
There is no proven curative treatment for ABLV once symptoms appear. Management is primarily supportive and palliative.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immediate post-exposure first aid:} Wash the wound thoroughly with soap and water for at least 15 minutes, apply an antiseptic such as povidone-iodine, and seek immediate medical care.
    \item \textbf{Active immunisation (PEP):} A course of four doses of rabies vaccine (given on days 0, 3, 7, and 14) is highly effective at stimulating neutralizing antibodies before the virus reaches the central nervous system.
    \item \textbf{Passive immunisation (PEP):} Human rabies immunoglobulin (HRIG) is infiltrated into and around the wound on day 0 to provide immediate neutralizing antibodies.
    \item \textbf{Palliative care:} For symptomatic patients, management focuses on comfort, utilizing high-dose sedation (benzodiazepines, barbiturates), analgesics, and a quiet, dark environment to minimize spasms. The "Milwaukee protocol" (induction of therapeutic coma and antiviral administration) has been attempted but is not recommended due to consistent failures.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Uncontrolled generalised seizures and status epilepticus.
    \item Severe autonomic instability leading to cardiac arrest or malignant arrhythmias.
    \item Respiratory failure due to paralysis of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles.
    \item Aspiration pneumonia secondary to pharyngeal spasm and loss of airway reflexes.
    \item Death, which occurs in virtually 100\% of symptomatic individuals.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals who develop clinical signs of ABLV is extremely grave, with a mortality rate of virtually 100\%. Death typically occurs within 1 to 2 weeks of symptom onset, usually from respiratory arrest or cardiovascular collapse. In contrast, the prognosis is excellent for individuals who receive prompt, appropriate first aid and a complete course of post-exposure prophylaxis prior to the onset of symptoms, which provides near-complete protection against the disease.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Avoid touching or handling any bats, including dead ones, unless trained and vaccinated.
    \item Vaccinate individuals at high occupational risk of bat contact using the pre-exposure rabies vaccine.
    \item Contact local wildlife authorities or a veterinarian to handle injured or trapped bats.
    \item In the event of a bat bite or scratch, immediately wash the wound with soap and running water for 15 minutes and apply antiseptic.
    \item Seek immediate emergency medical assessment after any bat contact to begin post-exposure prophylaxis.
\end{itemize}
