\section*{Definition and Overview}
Tay-Sachs disease is a rare, inherited neurological condition in which a fatty substance builds up in the brain and nervous system, progressively destroying nerve cells. It is caused by a genetic fault that leads to a missing or faulty enzyme called hexosaminidase A, which normally helps break down a type of fat called GM2 ganglioside. Without enough of this enzyme, the ganglioside accumulates inside nerve cells, damaging them until they stop working. The disease is named after the two doctors who first described it, and it belongs to a family of conditions called lysosomal storage diseases. There are three main forms: the infantile form, the commonest and most severe, in which a baby who appears healthy for the first months of life progressively loses skills and usually dies in early childhood; the juvenile form, which begins in later childhood; and a late-onset form, which can first appear in adolescence or adulthood and is much milder and slower. Tay-Sachs disease is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern, which means a child must receive a faulty gene from both parents to be affected. The condition cannot yet be cured, but its impact can be profoundly reduced through carrier testing, genetic counselling, and prenatal diagnosis, and care for affected children focuses on comfort, dignity, and support. This chapter explains the condition, how it is inherited, how it is diagnosed, and how affected children and adults are cared for.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Tay-Sachs disease is very rare in the general population. Historically it was most common in people of Ashkenazi Jewish (Central and Eastern European) descent, in whom roughly one person in every twenty-seven is a carrier, and in whom the disease affected about one in 3,600 newborn children before screening programmes were introduced. It is also more common in some French-Canadian communities, particularly in Quebec, and in the Cajun community of Louisiana, because of what is known as the founder effect, where a small founding population passed a specific gene fault down through generations. In the general the population, about one person in every 250 to 300 carries a Tay-Sachs gene fault, and the disease itself affects roughly one in 300,000 or fewer newborns. The numbers have fallen dramatically in communities with carrier screening: since Jewish community screening began in the 1970s, the number of affected births in Ashkenazi Jewish families has dropped by more than ninety percent. The condition affects boys and girls equally, because the faulty gene is on a non-sex chromosome. Tay-Sachs is a good example of how rare genetic disease can be, and also of how effective prevention through carrier detection can be when a community and health services work together.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Tay-Sachs disease is a single-gene disorder, and understanding its mechanism explains the whole disease process.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The HEXA gene:} the disease is caused by changes (mutations) in the HEXA gene on chromosome 15, which carries the instructions for making the enzyme hexosaminidase A
    \item \textbf{Enzyme deficiency:} the faulty gene leads to little or no functional hexosaminidase A, so the breakdown of GM2 ganglioside is blocked
    \item \textbf{Accumulation of ganglioside:} GM2 ganglioside builds up inside nerve cells, particularly in the brain, swelling the cells and eventually killing them
    \item \textbf{Nerve damage and inflammation:} the accumulating fat triggers damage in the nervous system, causing progressive loss of function
    \item \textbf{Autosomal recessive inheritance:} an affected child inherits one faulty copy of the gene from each parent, who are carriers (with one normal and one faulty copy) and are usually completely healthy
    \item \textbf{Variant forms:} different mutations cause different amounts of residual enzyme activity, which is why the infantile, juvenile, and late-onset forms differ so much in severity
\end{itemize}

Because carriers do not have symptoms, the disease can pass silently through generations until two carriers have a child together. Each child of two carrier parents has a one-in-four chance of being affected, a one-in-two chance of being an unaffected carrier, and a one-in-four chance of inheriting neither faulty gene.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features depend on the form of the disease and the age at which it begins. The infantile form is the classic picture:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal early development:} affected babies appear healthy and develop normally for the first three to six months
    \item \textbf{Exaggerated startle response:} a sudden loud noise makes the baby jump unusually violently, one of the earliest and most characteristic signs
    \item \textbf{Developmental regression:} the baby loses skills already gained, such as sitting, rolling, and reaching, in a steady downhill course
    \item \textbf{Cherry-red spot:} a distinctive red area in the centre of the retina, seen when the doctor looks into the eyes, caused by fatty deposits around the retina
    \item \textbf{Weakness and poor muscle tone:} the limbs become floppy, then stiff, as nerve damage progresses
    \item \textbf{Blindness and deafness:} vision and hearing are progressively lost
    \item \textbf{Seizures:} often starting in the second year of life, and sometimes severe or hard to control
    \item \textbf{Swallowing problems:} difficulty feeding and choking become major problems as the disease advances
    \item \textbf{Enlarged head:} the head may grow more quickly than expected because of the swelling of brain cells
    \item \textbf{Late-onset form:} in teenagers and adults this appears as clumsiness, speech problems, weakness, unsteady walking, and sometimes psychiatric symptoms, progressing slowly over years
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A baby who is losing developmental skills needs careful assessment, because several conditions can look like Tay-Sachs disease, especially in the early stages.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Other lysosomal storage diseases:} Gaucher disease, Niemann-Pick disease, GM1 gangliosidosis, and mucopolysaccharidoses can all cause developmental regression and organ enlargement, and are separated by enzyme and DNA testing
    \item \textbf{Spinal muscular atrophy:} causes progressive muscle weakness in infancy but does not cause the eye changes or seizures of Tay-Sachs
    \item \textbf{Other metabolic disorders:} conditions such as mitochondrial disease or certain amino-acid disorders can cause regression and seizures, and are distinguished by metabolic tests
    \item \textbf{Cerebral palsy:} permanent muscle problems from a birth or early brain injury can mimic early weakness, but cerebral palsy is not progressive
    \item \textbf{Structural brain problems:} brain scans may be needed to exclude structural causes of regression
    \item \textbf{Rett syndrome and other developmental disorders:} in girls, a plateau or loss of skills can suggest Rett syndrome, which has its own genetic test
    \item \textbf{Early-onset epilepsy syndromes:} seizures with regression can occur in several childhood epilepsy syndromes that are not storage diseases
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents should seek medical advice if a baby or child who was developing normally begins to lose skills, becomes unusually floppy, has an exaggerated reaction to noise, stops responding to sounds, or starts having seizures. Because these changes can also occur with treatable conditions, early review is important. For children already diagnosed, the care team will guide families on when to seek help for feeding, breathing, or seizures. Parents who are carriers, or who have a family history of the condition, should seek genetic counselling before or during pregnancy.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A seizure lasting longer than a few minutes, or repeated seizures without recovery between them
    \item Severe difficulty breathing, blue lips, or choking that does not clear
    \item Sudden limpness, unconsciousness, or failure to wake
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Tay-Sachs disease can be diagnosed with certainty by simple blood tests, and the same tests allow carrier detection and prenatal diagnosis.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Enzyme testing:} a blood test measures the activity of hexosaminidase A in white blood cells; very low or absent activity confirms the diagnosis in an affected child
    \item \textbf{DNA testing:} genetic testing of the HEXA gene identifies the specific mutations and confirms the diagnosis and the inheritance pattern
    \item \textbf{Eye examination:} an ophthalmologist looks for the cherry-red spot, which strongly supports the diagnosis in the infantile form
    \item \textbf{Carrier testing:} blood or saliva testing shows whether a person carries one faulty copy of the gene; this is widely available and is offered routinely to people of Ashkenazi Jewish descent and others with a family history
    \item \textbf{Prenatal testing:} in a pregnancy where both parents are carriers, the baby can be tested by chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis
    \item \textbf{Pre-implantation diagnosis:} couples who are carriers can consider in-vitro fertilisation with embryo testing to select an unaffected embryo
    \item \textbf{Brain imaging:} MRI scans may be used in assessment but are not needed for diagnosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
There is currently no cure for Tay-Sachs disease, and management focuses on comfort, quality of life, and the treatment of individual problems, delivered by a specialist paediatric palliative-care team.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Palliative and comfort care:} the care team supports the family and keeps the child comfortable, calm, and free of avoidable distress
    \item \textbf{Seizure management:} anti-seizure medicines are used to control epilepsy as it develops, with the dose adjusted as the disease progresses
    \item \textbf{Feeding support:} when swallowing becomes unsafe, feeding tubes can provide nutrition and hydration comfortably, and this decision is made with the family over time
    \item \textbf{Respiratory care:} suctioning, positioning, and treatment of chest infections help manage breathing difficulties
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy and positioning:} gentle therapy keeps the child comfortable, prevents painful joint stiffness, and eases muscle spasm
    \item \textbf{Music, touch, and sensory comfort:} non-medical measures such as soft music, massage, and quiet surroundings matter in a disease that steals sight and hearing
    \item \textbf{Support for parents and siblings:} counselling, respite care, and sibling support are essential parts of care for the whole family
    \item \textbf{Treatment of late-onset disease:} the adult form is managed with physiotherapy, speech therapy, and treatment of any psychiatric symptoms, and progresses much more slowly
    \item \textbf{Research and trials:} families may discuss experimental approaches, including gene-therapy trials, with their specialist team
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pneumonia and chest infections:} aspiration of food and secretions, and poor cough, make lung infection the commonest terminal event in the infantile form
    \item \textbf{Severe seizures:} epilepsy can become frequent and hard to control as the disease advances
    \item \textbf{Choking and aspiration:} swallowing difficulties cause choking episodes and can lead to blocked airways
    \item \textbf{Blindness and deafness:} loss of sight and hearing isolates the child from their surroundings
    \item \textbf{Joint contractures:} stiffness and immobility cause fixed bending of the joints
    \item \textbf{Nutritional failure:} without feeding support, weight and strength decline as swallowing fails
    \item \textbf{Family stress and grief:} the progressive nature of the disease places enormous emotional strain on parents and siblings, and bereavement support is needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis depends on which form of the disease is present. In the infantile form, children typically survive to about three to five years of age, and death is usually caused by pneumonia or the complications of severe neurological decline. This is a devastating outcome, and the role of care is to make the child as comfortable and loved as possible, with the family supported by a palliative team throughout. The juvenile form progresses over several years, and the late-onset form has a very different outlook: adults with it may live for decades, leading active lives with gradually increasing difficulties with movement, coordination, and sometimes mood, supported by physiotherapy and specialist care. Because there is no cure, the most important outcome for families is often prevention of the next affected child: carrier screening, genetic counselling, and reproductive choices allow couples to avoid passing the condition on. Research into gene therapy and enzyme replacement continues, and while these approaches are not yet established treatments, they give reason for cautious hope for future families.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item If you have Ashkenazi Jewish heritage, or a family history of Tay-Sachs, ask your GP about carrier screening, which is a simple blood test
    \item People whose partner is a known carrier should have carrier testing and genetic counselling before or during pregnancy
    \item Couples who are both carriers can discuss prenatal testing, in-vitro fertilisation with embryo selection, or other options with a genetic counsellor
    \item Parents of an affected child should seek a specialist metabolic or palliative-care team early, and accept help from counselling and respite services
    \item Siblings of an affected child should be offered testing and counselling when they are old enough, so they can make informed reproductive choices
    \item Keep immunisations up to date for affected children, because chest infections are a particular risk
    \item Reach out to family-support organisations for practical help, information, and connection with other families
\end{itemize}
