\chapter{Zinc}
\index{Zinc}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Zinc is an essential trace element (or micronutrient) that is fundamental to the proper functioning of all living organisms. In the human body, zinc is the second most abundant trace mineral after iron, and it is the only metal that is represented in all enzyme classes. It serves crucial catalytic, structural, and regulatory roles. Zinc acts as a cofactor for more than 300 enzymes, including carbonic anhydrase, alcohol dehydrogenase, alkaline phosphatase, and RNA polymerase. Furthermore, zinc is integral to the structural stability of thousands of transcription factors known as "zinc finger" proteins, which are vital for gene expression, cell division, growth, and immune function.

Unlike iron, the human body has no specialized storage system for zinc. Consequently, a continuous dietary intake of zinc is necessary to prevent deficiency. The homeostasis of zinc is tightly regulated by complex mechanisms involving gastrointestinal absorption, excretion, and intracellular transport, primarily mediated by two families of transporter proteins: the Zip (Zrt- and Irt-like proteins, SLC39A family) transporters, which import zinc into the cytosol, and the ZnT (Zinc transporters, SLC30A family) transporters, which export zinc out of the cytosol.

This chapter explores zinc in human health, focusing on the pathophysiology, clinical manifestations, diagnosis, and management of both zinc deficiency (hypozincemia) and zinc toxicity (hyperzincemia). Given that zinc deficiency remains a major global public health concern, particularly in developing nations, and that zinc toxicity can occur due to over-supplementation or industrial exposure, understanding the biological dynamics of zinc is vital for clinical practice and public health nutrition.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiological profile of zinc status is highly stratified by geographic region, socio-economic status, dietary patterns, and age groups.
Globally, zinc deficiency affects approximately 17\% to 20\% of the world's population, representing over 1.2 billion people. The prevalence is disproportionately concentrated in low- and middle-income regions. Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and parts of Central and South America experience the highest rates of deficiency, where in some areas up to 30\% of the population may be affected. This geographic variation is primarily driven by diet: populations relying heavily on cereal- and tuber-based staples with low bioavailability of zinc are at the greatest risk.

In high-income nations, such as some countries, the United States, and Western European countries, severe zinc deficiency is rare. However, mild to moderate deficiency is estimated to affect up to 10\% of the population, particularly vulnerable cohorts. These demographics include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Elderly individuals: Up to 30\% of older adults in institutionalized settings have suboptimal zinc intake due to poor dentition, reduced appetite, and altered absorption.
    \item Infants and toddlers: Especially those exclusively breastfed beyond six months without timely introduction of zinc-rich complementary foods.
    \item Pregnant and lactating women: Due to the high physiological demand for zinc to support fetal development and milk production.
    \item Vegetarians and vegans: Because their diets lack meat, which is the primary source of highly bioavailable zinc, and contain high amounts of phytates.
\end{itemize}

Zinc toxicity, on the other hand, is far less common and typically occurs in sporadic, acute episodes or as chronic toxicity due to inappropriate supplementation. Occupational exposure to zinc fumes, resulting in "metal fume fever," is seen in industrial workers, particularly in welding, galvanizing, and smelting sectors.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Zinc homeostasis is maintained via a delicate balance of dietary intake, gastrointestinal absorption, cellular transport, and excretion. Disruptions in any of these pathways lead to clinical states of deficiency or excess.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary Insufficiency:} The primary cause of zinc deficiency globally is inadequate intake of bioavailable zinc. Foods rich in zinc include red meat, poultry, seafood (especially oysters), and dairy. Plant-based diets, while containing zinc, also contain high levels of phytic acid. Phytates bind to zinc in the gastrointestinal tract, forming insoluble complexes that cannot be absorbed.
    \item \textbf{Impaired Gastrointestinal Absorption:} Chronic malabsorptive disorders significantly impair zinc uptake. These include celiac disease, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, short bowel syndrome, and cystic fibrosis. Gastric bypass surgery also reduces the absorptive surface area, predisposing patients to micronutrient deficiencies.
    \item \textbf{Genetic Defects (Acrodermatitis Enteropathica):} This is a rare, autosomal recessive genetic disorder characterized by a mutation in the SLC39A4 gene, which encodes the ZIP4 transporter. ZIP4 is responsible for the active transport of dietary zinc across the apical membrane of enterocytes. The mutation results in a severe, life-threatening systemic zinc deficiency starting in early infancy, typically when transitioning from breast milk to formula or solid foods.
    \item \textbf{Increased Physiological Loss:} Conditions that lead to hyperzincuria (excessive urinary loss of zinc) or other fluid losses can precipitate deficiency. These include poorly controlled diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, thermal burns, exfoliative dermatitis, sickle cell disease, and chronic alcohol consumption (which impairs intestinal absorption and increases urinary excretion).
    \item \textbf{Iatrogenic Causes:} Long-term total parenteral nutrition (TPN) without adequate zinc supplementation, or prolonged treatment with chelating agents like penicillamine, can deplete systemic zinc stores.
    \item \textbf{Zinc Toxicity Pathways (Aetiology of Excess):} Acute zinc toxicity is typically caused by the ingestion of acidic beverages stored in galvanized containers, industrial exposure, or massive accidental ingestion of zinc supplements. Chronic toxicity is almost exclusively caused by long-term, high-dose zinc supplementation (exceeding 40 to 50 mg/day of elemental zinc).
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology of Zinc Deficiency:} Because zinc is required for DNA and RNA synthesis, cell replication, and protein synthesis, tissue systems with high cell turnover are affected first. This explains why the skin, gastrointestinal tract, and immune system show early dysfunction. Zinc deficiency impairs T-helper cell function, decreases natural killer cell activity, and reduces antibody production, leading to severe immunodeficiency. It also alters taste perception (dysgeusia) by affecting carbonic anhydrase VI (gustin) activity.
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology of Zinc Toxicity:} Ingestion of high doses of zinc (typically over 150 mg/day) competitively inhibits the absorption of copper in the enterocytes. Zinc induces the synthesis of metallothionein, an intracellular metal-binding protein in the gut mucosa. Metallothionein has a higher affinity for copper than zinc. It binds copper, trapping it within the enterocytes, which are subsequently sloughed off into the intestinal lumen and excreted. This leads to profound secondary copper deficiency, which causes microcytic anemia, neutropenia, and neurological symptoms similar to subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of zinc imbalance varies from subtle, non-specific symptoms in mild deficiency to classic, pathognomonic triads in severe genetic forms, or acute systemic distress in toxicity.

For zinc deficiency, the clinical features can be categorized by severity:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mild to Moderate Deficiency:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Growth retardation and delayed skeletal maturation in infants and children.
        \item Impaired immune response, manifesting as recurrent minor infections (respiratory and gastrointestinal).
        \item Poor wound healing and dry, scaly skin.
        \item Dysgeusia (distortion of taste) and hyposmia (reduced ability to smell), which can lead to anorexia.
        \item Cognitive impairment, difficulty concentrating, and lethargy.
        \item Night blindness (nyctalopia), as zinc is a cofactor for retinol dehydrogenase, which converts retinol to retinal in the visual cycle.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Severe Deficiency and Acrodermatitis Enteropathica:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Severe periorificial and acral dermatitis. The rash is typically symmetric, erythematous, vesiculobullous, and pustular, located around the mouth, anus, eyes, and on the hands, feet, and elbows.
        \item Profuse, watery diarrhea that is often refractory to standard anti-diarrheal therapies.
        \item Complete alopecia (hair loss) involving the scalp, eyebrows, and eyelashes.
        \item Neuropsychiatric changes, including extreme irritability, emotional lability, and depression.
        \item Severe susceptibility to opportunistic bacterial, fungal, and viral infections due to profound T-cell lymphopenia.
        \item Hypogonadism and delayed sexual maturation in adolescents.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

For zinc toxicity, the clinical features depend on the route and duration of exposure:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute Oral Ingestion:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Severe epigastric pain, nausea, vomiting, and hematemesis.
        \item Watery or bloody diarrhea.
        \item Metallic taste in the mouth.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inhalation Toxicity (Metal Fume Fever):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Fever, chills, profuse sweating, and generalized myalgia.
        \item Non-productive cough, chest tightness, dyspnea, and headache.
        \item Symptoms typically resolve spontaneously within 24 to 48 hours.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chronic Toxicity (Secondary Copper Deficiency):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Progressive fatigue, pallor, and dyspnea on exertion (secondary to microcytic anemia).
        \item Recurrent infections (secondary to neutropenia).
        \item Sensory ataxia, paresthesias in the extremities, spastic gait, and muscle weakness (myeloneuropathy).
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The clinical features of zinc deficiency and toxicity can mimic several other nutritional, dermatological, and hematological disorders. A thorough differential diagnosis is essential.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Essential Fatty Acid Deficiency:} Presents with dry, scaly skin and alopecia, but lacks the characteristic periorificial distribution of zinc deficiency dermatosis and is not associated with dysgeusia.
    \item \textbf{Biotin Deficiency:} Characterized by alopecia and periorificial dermatitis. It can be distinguished by the presence of organic aciduria and specific neurological signs like hypotonia and seizures.
    \item \textbf{Atopic Dermatitis or Candidiasis:} Often confused with the skin lesions of zinc deficiency. However, atopic dermatitis typically affects the flexural surfaces and is highly pruritic, while cutaneous candidiasis shows satellite lesions and responds to antifungal therapy.
    \item \textbf{Vitamin A Deficiency:} Presents with night blindness and xerophthalmia. Serum retinol levels and response to vitamin A supplementation differentiate it from zinc-related visual impairment.
    \item \textbf{Gluten-Sensitive Enteropathy (Celiac Disease):} Causes malabsorption, diarrhea, and weight loss. While celiac disease can cause secondary zinc deficiency, it is diagnosed via anti-tissue transglutaminase (tTG) antibodies and duodenal biopsy showing villous atrophy.
    \item \textbf{Myelodysplastic Syndrome (MDS):} Chronic zinc toxicity-induced copper deficiency presents with anemia and neutropenia, which can be misdiagnosed as MDS. The presence of ring sideroblasts on bone marrow biopsy can occur in both, but elevated serum zinc and low serum copper or ceruloplasmin point to zinc toxicity.
    \item \textbf{Vitamin B12 Deficiency:} Causes subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord, presenting with paresthesias, ataxia, and loss of proprioception, mimicking the myeloneuropathy of zinc-induced copper deficiency. Normal B12 levels and low copper levels establish the correct diagnosis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Zinc imbalances can range from chronic, low-grade deficiencies that require outpatient dietary counselling to acute, life-threatening toxicities and severe genetic crises.

Patients should contact a primary care physician if they experience:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent skin rashes around the mouth, anus, or extremities that do not respond to topical treatments.
    \item Chronic, unexplained diarrhea, particularly in infants or young children.
    \item Unintentional weight loss, loss of appetite, or persistent distortions in taste and smell.
    \item Recurrent infections, indicating potential immunodeficiency.
    \item Gradual onset of numbness, tingling, or weakness in the hands and feet.
\end{itemize}

Immediate emergency medical attention must be sought if a person displays signs of severe acute poisoning or respiratory distress. In many countries, contact emergency services immediately by dialing local emergency services (or 911 in the United States, 999 in the United Kingdom) if the patient exhibits:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ingestion of a large quantity of zinc supplements or industrial zinc compounds.
    \item Severe abdominal pain accompanied by persistent, forceful vomiting or vomiting blood (hematemesis).
    \item Difficulty breathing, severe chest tightness, high fever, or confusion after inhaling fumes in an industrial setting.
    \item An inability to stand or walk due to rapid, progressive loss of coordination and leg weakness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosing zinc deficiency or toxicity requires a combination of clinical assessment, dietary history, and targeted laboratory investigations. Due to homeostatic mechanisms, confirming a mild deficiency can be biochemically challenging.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Serum or Plasma Zinc Concentration:} This is the most common test used to assess zinc status.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Normal range: Typically 10 to 22 micromoles per litre (70 to 150 micrograms per decilitre).
        \item Suboptimal levels: Less than 10 micromoles per litre indicates deficiency.
        \item Elevated levels: Greater than 25 micromoles per litre suggests toxicity or acute contamination.
        \item Limitations: Serum zinc represents less than 0.2\% of total body zinc. Levels are subject to diurnal variation (highest in the morning) and are decreased by acute-phase responses (inflammation, infection, trauma), pregnancy, and hypoalbuminemia (as 60\% of zinc is bound to albumin).
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full Blood Count (FBC):} Essential in suspected chronic zinc toxicity. It typically reveals a microcytic or normocytic anemia and neutropenia (secondary to copper deficiency).
    \item \textbf{Serum Copper and Ceruloplasmin:} Vital markers when investigating zinc toxicity. Chronic zinc excess leads to suppressed serum copper levels (typically less than 10 micromoles per litre) and low ceruloplasmin levels.
    \item \textbf{Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP) Activity:} Since ALP is a zinc-dependent enzyme, its activity is often reduced in severe zinc deficiency. A rise in ALP activity after zinc supplementation can serve as a retrospective confirmation of deficiency.
    \item \textbf{Urinary Zinc Excretion:} A 24-hour urinary zinc collection can aid in diagnosis.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Low excretion (less than 150 micrograms per day) supports a diagnosis of deficiency.
        \item High excretion (greater than 1000 micrograms per day) indicates systemic excess or hyperzincuria due to catabolic states.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic Testing:} For suspected acrodermatitis enteropathica, sequencing of the SLC39A4 gene can identify pathogenic mutations.
    \item \textbf{Bone Marrow Biopsy:} Indicated if hematological abnormalities from suspected zinc-induced copper deficiency mimic myelodysplasia. Findings include cytoplasmic vacuolation of erythroid and myeloid progenitors and ring sideroblasts.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of zinc-related disorders focuses on correcting the underlying zinc imbalance, treating secondary deficiencies, and addressing any underlying systemic diseases.

For Zinc Deficiency:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary Modification:} For mild deficiency, increasing the consumption of bioavailable zinc is the preferred first step. Red meat, seafood (especially oysters), poultry, and eggs should be encouraged. For vegetarians, techniques such as soaking, sprouting, and fermenting grains and legumes can reduce phytate content and improve zinc bioavailability.
    \item \textbf{Oral Zinc Supplementation:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item For moderate deficiency, oral zinc supplements (zinc sulfate, zinc gluconate, or zinc acetate) are prescribed. The typical therapeutic dose for adults is 20 to 50 mg of elemental zinc per day, taken on an empty stomach to maximize absorption (though it may be taken with food to reduce gastrointestinal irritation).
        \item In pediatric diarrhea (especially in developing nations), the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends 20 mg of elemental zinc daily (10 mg for infants under six months) for 10 to 14 days to reduce the duration and severity of the episode.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intravenous zinc supplementation:} Standard practice in patients receiving long-term TPN, where 2.5 to 5 mg of elemental zinc is added to the daily parenteral solution.
    \item \textbf{Management of Acrodermatitis Enteropathica:} This condition requires lifelong, high-dose oral zinc supplementation. Typically, doses of 1 to 3 mg/kg of elemental zinc per day are required. Symptoms improve dramatically within days of initiating treatment.
\end{itemize}

For Zinc Toxicity:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cessation of Zinc Intake:} Immediate discontinuation of all zinc supplements, including multivitamins and zinc-containing denture creams.
    \item \textbf{Supportive Care for Acute Ingestion:} Includes intravenous hydration, antiemetics, and monitoring of gastric mucosa. Gastric lavage may be considered in massive ingestions if performed early.
    \item \textbf{Copper Supplementation:} If chronic zinc toxicity has induced a copper deficiency, copper replacement is mandatory. This can involve oral copper supplementation (2 to 8 mg of elemental copper daily) or, in severe neurological cases, intravenous copper infusion.
    \item \textbf{Chelation Therapy:} In severe, life-threatening cases of acute systemic zinc poisoning, chelating agents such as calcium disodium edetate (CaNa2EDTA) may be administered to enhance urinary excretion of zinc.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Unmanaged zinc imbalances can lead to profound, irreversible physical and neurological complications.

Complications of Untreated Zinc Deficiency:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Severe Growth Retardation and Dwarfism:} Chronic deficiency during critical developmental windows in childhood results in permanent stunting and skeletal abnormalities.
    \item \textbf{Hypogonadism and Infertility:} Zinc is essential for spermatogenesis and testosterone synthesis in males. Severe deficiency leads to testicular atrophy, oligospermia, and erectile dysfunction. In females, it can cause anovulation and menstrual irregularities.
    \item \textbf{Sepsis and Mortality:} Due to severe suppression of cell-mediated immunity, untreated patients are highly vulnerable to life-threatening bacterial pneumonia and gastrointestinal sepsis.
    \item \textbf{Cognitive and Developmental Delays:} Persistent deficiency in utero or during early childhood impairs neurogenesis and synaptic plasticity, leading to permanent learning disabilities.
\end{itemize}

Complications of Untreated Zinc Toxicity (mainly via Copper Deficiency):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Irreversible Myeloneuropathy:} Prolonged copper deficiency damages the dorsal columns and corticospinal tracts of the spinal cord. Even after correcting zinc and copper levels, neurological deficits such as ataxia, spasticity, and paresthesias may persist.
    \item \textbf{Severe Pancytopenia:} Refractory microcytic anemia and neutropenia can lead to severe anemia-related high-output heart failure and life-threatening opportunistic infections due to lack of white blood cells.
    \item \textbf{Corrosive Gastrointestinal Injury:} Acute ingestion of high-concentration zinc solutions (like zinc chloride) can cause esophageal or gastric strictures, perforation, and chronic peritonitis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals with zinc imbalances is generally excellent if the condition is recognized early and managed appropriately.

For zinc deficiency, clinical response to supplementation is often rapid and dramatic:
- Skin lesions associated with acrodermatitis enteropathica or severe deficiency typically show visible healing within 48 to 72 hours of starting zinc therapy.
- Diarrhea and neuropsychiatric symptoms (like irritability) often resolve within a few days.
- Growth velocities in children increase significantly within weeks of starting treatment (catch-up growth).
- However, if severe deficiency occurred during critical fetal or early childhood developmental periods, some degree of cognitive impairment and short stature may remain permanent.

For zinc toxicity, the prognosis depends on the duration of exposure and the severity of secondary copper deficiency:
- Acute symptoms of metal fume fever or mild oral toxicity resolve completely within 24 to 72 hours with supportive care.
- Hematological complications (anemia and neutropenia) secondary to chronic zinc-induced copper deficiency are fully reversible, typically correcting within 4 to 8 weeks after stopping zinc supplements and initiating copper therapy.
- The prognosis for neurological symptoms is guarded. While sensory ataxia and paresthesias may improve, patients with long-standing spasticity and spinal cord degeneration often experience permanent gait impairment and residual neurological deficits.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventing zinc imbalances involves public health interventions, rational supplementation practices, and dietary optimization.

Dietary Prevention Strategies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Balanced Nutritional Intake:} Incorporate diverse dietary sources of zinc. Lean meats, fish, seafood, legumes, nuts, seeds, and whole grains are excellent sources.
    \item \textbf{Optimizing Bioavailability:} For individuals consuming plant-based diets:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Soak beans, grains, and seeds in water for several hours before cooking to activate endogenous phytases, which break down phytic acid.
        \item Consume leavened grain products (like yeast breads) rather than unleavened products (like flatbreads), as fermentation reduces phytate content.
        \item Pair zinc-rich plant foods with organic acids (such as citric acid in citrus fruits or malic acid in apples), which enhance zinc absorption.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Food Fortification:} In areas with high rates of malnutrition, support the consumption of biofortified crops or foods enriched with zinc (such as fortified wheat flour or infant cereals).
\end{itemize}

Safe Supplementation Practices:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adhere to Tolerable Upper Intake Levels (UL):} The UL for elemental zinc in adults is 40 mg/day. Unless prescribed by a physician to treat a documented deficiency, daily supplementation should not exceed this limit.
    \item \textbf{Monitor Zinc-Copper Ratios:} If long-term zinc supplementation (greater than 40 mg/day) is clinically indicated, it should be accompanied by copper supplementation (at a ratio of approximately 10:1 to 15:1 zinc to copper) to prevent copper depletion.
    \item \textbf{Check Household Products:} Be cautious with over-the-counter nasal sprays containing zinc (which can cause irreversible anosmia) and excessive use of zinc-containing denture creams.
\end{itemize}
