\section*{Definition and Overview}
``Threadworms'' and ``worms'' in everyday local health talk usually refer to intestinal helminths that infect humans, most often the pinworm Enterobius vermicularis in children. Broader worm infections---roundworm (Ascaris), hookworm, whipworm, Strongyloides, and tapeworms---occur less often in urban the affected region but remain important in some remote communities, in migrants and travellers, and in people with specific dietary or animal exposures. This chapter places threadworms in the wider context of intestinal worms: how they differ, which symptoms matter, how diagnosis is made, and which treatments and public health measures apply. Accurate naming matters because medicines, isolation needs, and complications differ by species.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Pinworm is ubiquitous in temperate childhood populations worldwide. Soil-transmitted helminths (Ascaris, hookworm, Trichuris) cluster where sanitation is limited and barefoot ground contact is common; local remote community programmes have addressed these historically and continue surveillance. Strongyloides stercoralis is significant in parts of northern the affected region and can persist for decades, flaring with immunosuppression. Tapeworms relate to undercooked beef, pork, or fish, and to close contact with dogs in hydatid disease regions for Echinococcus (a tissue cestode rather than a typical gut ``thread'' worm). Refugees and travellers may harbour mixed infections. Empiric childhood ``worming'' without symptoms is less emphasised than targeted treatment of proven or highly likely pinworm in households.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pinworm:} faecal--oral egg ingestion; nocturnal perianal egg-laying; intense itch; low systemic burden
    \item \textbf{Ascaris:} egg ingestion from contaminated soil or food; larval lung migration can cause cough; adult small-bowel worms may obstruct
    \item \textbf{Hookworm:} larval skin penetration from soil; intestinal blood loss and iron deficiency
    \item \textbf{Whipworm:} egg ingestion; colitis symptoms when burden is high
    \item \textbf{Strongyloides:} skin penetration; autoinfection cycle; hyperinfection syndrome when immunity drops
    \item \textbf{Taenia and other tapeworms:} undercooked meat or fish; segments passed in stool; cysticercosis if T. solium eggs are ingested
    \item \textbf{Hydatid disease:} Echinococcus eggs from canine faeces; cysts in liver or lung rather than simple enteritis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pinworm pattern:} night-time bottom itch, sleep upset, visible white threads at anus
    \item \textbf{Non-specific gut symptoms:} abdominal pain, nausea, diarrhoea, or visible worm segments
    \item \textbf{Nutritional effects:} iron deficiency with hookworm; failure to thrive with heavy soil-transmitted burdens
    \item \textbf{Respiratory clues:} fleeting eosinophilic pneumonia during larval migration (Ascaris)
    \item \textbf{Itchy rash and larva currens:} consider Strongyloides
    \item \textbf{Anal or stool passage of segments:} tapeworm
    \item \textbf{Eosinophilia:} common laboratory clue in many tissue-invasive helminths, less reliable in simple pinworm
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Functional abdominal pain and constipation:} very common mimics in children
    \item \textbf{Giardiasis and other protozoa:} diarrhoea and bloating after water exposure
    \item \textbf{Coeliac disease and inflammatory bowel disease:} chronic symptoms with blood, weight loss, or growth failure
    \item \textbf{Perianal streptococcal disease:} bright red, painful anus rather than nocturnal itch alone
    \item \textbf{Food allergy and lactose intolerance:} meal-related patterns
    \item \textbf{Biliary or surgical abdomen:} when Ascaris migrates into ducts or causes obstruction---emergency pathways
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a GP for persistent perianal itch, visible worms, unexplained iron deficiency, chronic diarrhoea after travel, or eosinophilia. Mention remote residence, refugee background, barefoot soil exposure, and undercooked meat or fish. Do not start steroids or other strong immunosuppressants without Strongyloides risk assessment when relevant.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain, bilious vomiting, or suspected bowel obstruction
    \item Difficulty breathing, severe allergic features, or collapse
    \item Signs of sepsis in a person with known Strongyloides and immunosuppression
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pinworm tape test:} morning perianal tape for microscopy
    \item \textbf{Stool ova, cysts, and parasites:} multiple samples improve detection of many helminths; pinworm still better on tape
    \item \textbf{Full blood count:} eosinophils and anaemia
    \item \textbf{Strongyloides serology:} important before immunosuppression in at-risk people
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} ultrasound or CT for hydatid cysts; plain films sometimes show Ascaris
    \item \textbf{Specialist parasitology:} complex or imported infections
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pinworm:} mebendazole or pyrantel; repeat dose; treat household; hygiene bundle
    \item \textbf{Soil-transmitted helminths:} albendazole or mebendazole regimens per species and guidelines; iron replacement if deficient
    \item \textbf{Strongyloides:} ivermectin first-line in most cases; specialist care for hyperinfection
    \item \textbf{Tapeworms:} praziquantel or niclosamide depending on species; cysticercosis needs neurology-led care, not simple gut worming alone
    \item \textbf{Hydatid:} specialist surgical and antiparasitic pathways---do not aspirate cysts casually
    \item \textbf{Public health:} notification may apply to some infections; sanitation and shoe-wearing reduce soil-transmitted disease
    \item \textbf{Avoid inappropriate chronic ``deworming'':} repeated unnecessary medicines without diagnosis waste effort and delay real causes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pinworm reinfection and family stress:} common
    \item \textbf{Anaemia and impaired childhood growth:} heavy hookworm or mixed infections
    \item \textbf{Intestinal obstruction or biliary migration:} Ascaris
    \item \textbf{Strongyloides hyperinfection:} high mortality if unrecognised during immunosuppression
    \item \textbf{Neurocysticercosis:} seizures and raised intracranial pressure
    \item \textbf{Hydatid rupture:} anaphylaxis and secondary spread
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most pinworm episodes resolve with combined medicine and hygiene. Soil-transmitted helminths respond well to targeted therapy and improved sanitation. Strongyloides and tissue cestodes need more careful long-term planning but are manageable in specialist hands. Early correct diagnosis prevents both under-treatment of dangerous worms and over-attribution of all gut symptoms to ``worms.''

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Handwashing with soap after toilet use, nappy changes, and before food
    \item Keep children's nails short; wash bedding during pinworm treatment weeks
    \item Wear shoes on soil in endemic hookworm areas; improve sanitation where possible
    \item Cook meat and fish thoroughly; practise safe dog deworming and hand hygiene after animal contact in hydatid regions
    \item Drink safe water when travelling; follow travel medicine advice
    \item Tell your doctor about remote living, travel, and refugee history before chemotherapy or transplant
    \item Treat whole households for pinworm when advised, not only the itchiest child
\end{itemize}
