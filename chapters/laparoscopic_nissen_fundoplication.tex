\chapter{Laparoscopic Nissen fundoplication}
\index{Laparoscopic Nissen fundoplication}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Laparoscopic Nissen fundoplication is a minimally invasive surgical procedure considered the gold standard for the treatment of severe or treatment-resistant gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) and hiatus hernias. The procedure involves mobilising the fundus of the stomach and wrapping it 360 degrees around the lower oesophagus to reinforce the lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) barrier. This mechanical reconstruction prevents the retrograde flow of acidic and non-acidic gastric contents into the oesophagus, thereby alleviating chronic symptoms and protecting the mucosal lining from reflux-induced damage.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease is highly prevalent worldwide, affecting approximately 15\% to 20\% of adults in many countries and other Western nations. While the majority of cases are managed effectively with pharmacotherapy, approximately 10\% to 15\% of individuals with chronic GORD require surgical intervention due to refractory symptoms, medication side effects, or structural issues like a large sliding hiatus hernia. The surgery is most commonly performed in adults aged 30 to 60, with a slightly higher prevalence of surgical intervention in males who are more prone to developing complications of GORD, such as Barrett's oesophagus or strictures.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The primary indication for a laparoscopic Nissen fundoplication is the failure of the physiological antireflux barrier at the gastro-oesophageal junction. The key pathophysiological mechanisms and indications include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lower oesophageal sphincter dysfunction:} Chronic hypotonicity or transient inappropriate relaxations of the LOS allow gastric acid and bile to reflux into the oesophagus.
    \item \textbf{Hiatus hernia:} Displacement of the gastro-oesophageal junction above the diaphragm disrupts the anatomical high-pressure zone, significantly impairing the antireflux mechanism.
    \item \textbf{Refractory GORD:} Persistent symptoms of heartburn and acid regurgitation despite maximal medical therapy (such as high-dose proton pump inhibitors).
    \item \textbf{Extra-oesophageal reflux:} Severe atypical symptoms, including chronic cough, asthma exacerbations, or recurrent laryngitis secondary to micro-aspiration of gastric contents.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Patients presenting for laparoscopic Nissen fundoplication typically describe a long history of severe GORD symptoms. Typical clinical features include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Heartburn:} A burning retrosternal sensation that often radiates upwards, frequently exacerbated by lying flat or bending over.
    \item \textbf{Acid regurgitation:} The effortless backflow of bitter or sour gastric contents into the mouth or throat.
    \item \textbf{Dysphagia:} Difficulty swallowing or a sensation of food sticking in the chest, which may indicate a stricture or oesophageal dysmotility.
    \item \textbf{Atypical respiratory symptoms:} Chronic nocturnal cough, wheezing, hoarseness, or sore throat resulting from acid irritation of the airways.
    \item \textbf{Epigastric or chest pain:} Retrosternal pain that can mimic cardiac angina, requiring thorough cardiovascular workup prior to surgical consideration.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before embarking on surgical intervention, it is critical to exclude other gastrointestinal and cardiac pathologies that can mimic reflux symptoms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Oesophageal motility disorders:} Conditions such as achalasia or distal oesophageal spasm, which present with dysphagia and chest pain but require different treatments.
    \item \textbf{Coronary artery disease:} Angina pectoris must be ruled out in any patient presenting with unexplained retrosternal chest pain.
    \item \textbf{Peptic ulcer disease:} Gastric or duodenal ulcers that present with burning epigastric pain.
    \item \textbf{Gastric or oesophageal malignancy:} Neoplastic lesions presenting with dysphagia, early satiety, or weight loss.
    \item \textbf{Eosinophilic oesophagitis:} An allergic inflammatory condition of the oesophagus characterised by dysphagia and food impaction.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should seek medical evaluation if their reflux symptoms are no longer controlled by standard medications, or if they experience warning symptoms suggesting complications.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Haematemesis:} Vomiting fresh blood or dark "coffee-ground" material.
    \item \textbf{Melaena:} Passing black, tarry, foul-smelling stools.
    \item \textbf{Severe chest pain:} Sudden, crushing retrosternal pain radiating to the jaw, neck, or left arm, which may indicate an acute myocardial infarction.
    \item \textbf{Acute dysphagia or aphagia:} Complete inability to swallow food or liquids.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A definitive diagnosis of GORD and a detailed anatomical and functional evaluation are required prior to undertaking a Nissen fundoplication:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (gastroscopy):} Allows direct visualisation of the oesophageal mucosa to assess the severity of oesophagitis, identify Barrett's oesophagus, and diagnose a hiatus hernia.
    \item \textbf{24-hour catheter-based or wireless pH monitoring:} The gold standard test to confirm pathological acid exposure in the distal oesophagus and correlate symptoms with reflux episodes.
    \item \textbf{Oesophageal manometry:} Essential to evaluate the peristaltic function of the oesophagus; poor motility may necessitate a partial wrap (e.g., Toupet fundoplication) instead of a full 360-degree Nissen wrap.
    \item \textbf{Barium swallow:} Provides a detailed anatomical map of the oesophagus and stomach, particularly useful in demonstrating the size and type of a hiatus hernia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management involves careful pre-operative planning, precise surgical execution, and structured post-operative rehabilitation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative optimisation:} Weight reduction in obese patients to lower intra-abdominal pressure, and smoking cessation to reduce pulmonary complications and improve healing.
    \item \textbf{Surgical technique:} The procedure is performed under general anaesthesia using 4 to 5 small abdominal incisions. The crura of the diaphragm are closed (cruroraphy) to repair any hiatus hernia, and the gastric fundus is wrapped 360 degrees around the distal oesophagus over a bougie to prevent a tight wrap.
    \item \textbf{Post-operative dietary progression:} A strict soft or liquid diet for 2 to 4 weeks (avoiding bread, dry meat, carbonated beverages, and raw vegetables) to allow tissue oedema at the wrap site to resolve.
    \item \textbf{Pain management and activity modification:} Adequate analgesia and avoidance of heavy lifting (over 5 kilograms) for at least 6 weeks post-surgery to protect the diaphragmatic repair.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While laparoscopic Nissen fundoplication is safe, there are specific procedural and long-term risks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Post-operative dysphagia:} Temporary swallowing difficulty due to tissue swelling, which resolves in most patients within a few weeks; persistent dysphagia may require endoscopic dilatation or revision.
    \item \textbf{Gas-bloat syndrome:} Inability to belch or vomit due to the newly constructed valve, leading to abdominal distension, flatulence, and discomfort.
    \item \textbf{Wrap migration or slip:} The wrap may slip down onto the stomach or migrate up into the chest, resulting in recurrent reflux or dysphagia.
    \item \textbf{Surgical injury:} Accidental damage to adjacent organs including the spleen, liver, vagal nerves, or oesophageal perforation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis following laparoscopic Nissen fundoplication is excellent, with high rates of patient satisfaction. Approximately 85\% to 90\% of patients experience complete relief of reflux symptoms and remain off daily acid-suppression medication at 10-year follow-up. Long-term outcomes are heavily dependent on patient selection, surgical expertise, and adherence to post-operative dietary guidelines. A small percentage of patients (around 5\% to 10\%) may require revision surgery over their lifetime due to wrap failure or migration.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Patients can optimise their surgical outcomes and prevent long-term complications by adhering to self-care measures:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Post-operative eating behaviour:} Consume small, frequent meals, chew food extremely thoroughly, and eat slowly to avoid bolus impaction.
    \item \textbf{Avoid carbonated drinks:} Exclude fizzy drinks from the diet permanently, as they introduce gas into the stomach that cannot be easily vented.
    \item \textbf{Weight management:} Maintain a healthy weight to minimise intra-abdominal pressure, which reduces the mechanical stress placed on the fundoplication.
    \item \textbf{Proper lifting techniques:} Avoid heavy lifting and intense abdominal straining during the initial recovery period and exercise caution in the long term.
\end{itemize}
