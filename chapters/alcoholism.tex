\chapter{Alcoholism}
\index{Alcoholism}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Alcoholism, also called alcohol use disorder or alcohol dependence, is a chronic brain disease in which a person is unable to control their drinking despite knowing the harm it causes. It is characterised by craving, loss of control, tolerance, and withdrawal symptoms.

Alcoholism is not a weakness of will: it involves lasting changes in brain circuitry, and it is a treatable medical condition. It develops gradually in many people, over years of heavy drinking, but it can also follow a period of binge drinking in younger people. Recognising alcoholism as a medical problem, rather than a moral failing, is essential to encouraging people to seek help.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Alcoholism is common, affecting roughly 1 in 20 to 1 in 30 adults over a lifetime, and it is about twice as common in men as in women. It is a major cause of disability and premature death worldwide.

People with alcoholism have higher rates of depression, anxiety, suicide, injury, and many chronic diseases, and the condition places a heavy burden on families. Most people with alcoholism never receive treatment, partly because of stigma and partly because they do not recognise the problem themselves.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Alcoholism develops when heavy drinking changes the brain's reward, stress, and impulse-control systems.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The reward system:} alcohol releases dopamine, producing pleasure; repeated drinking makes the brain less sensitive to it, so more alcohol is needed
    \item \textbf{The stress system:} withdrawal from alcohol activates stress circuits, producing anxiety and discomfort that drinking relieves
    \item \textbf{Impulse control:} the frontal lobes, which normally restrain behaviour, become less effective at controlling the urge to drink
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} about half of the risk of alcoholism is inherited
    \item \textbf{Environment:} heavy-drinking peers, easy access to alcohol, and cultural norms that encourage drinking
    \item \textbf{Psychological factors:} using alcohol to cope with stress, trauma, or mental illness
    \item \textbf{Early drinking:} starting to drink at a young age increases the lifetime risk
\end{itemize}

This combination produces the typical cycle of alcoholism: drinking to feel pleasure or relieve stress, developing tolerance, experiencing withdrawal when not drinking, and drinking again to stop the withdrawal -- while the ability to stop gradually shrinks.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Craving for alcohol that is hard to resist
    \item Loss of control, such as drinking more than intended
    \item Tolerance -- needing more alcohol to get the same effect
    \item Withdrawal symptoms when not drinking: tremor, sweating, nausea, anxiety, restlessness, and poor sleep
    \item Drinking to avoid withdrawal, including early in the morning
    \item Repeated attempts to cut down or stop that fail
    \item Spending less time on work, family, and hobbies because of drinking
    \item Continuing to drink despite health, legal, or relationship problems
    \item Denial or minimising of the problem by the person affected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hazardous drinking, where the amount drunk is risky but control is intact
    \item Binge drinking, which is episodic rather than persistent
    \item Depression, anxiety, or bipolar disorder, which are commonly associated with heavy drinking
    \item Other addictions, such as to benzodiazepines or opioids
    \item Medical causes of confusion or tremor, such as thyroid disease, low blood sugar, or a neurological disorder
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who is unable to control their drinking, or whose drinking is harming their health or family, should see a GP. Withdrawal from severe alcoholism should never be attempted alone, because it can be dangerous.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seizures or convulsions during withdrawal
    \item Severe confusion, agitation, or hallucinations, which may be delirium tremens
    \item Thoughts of suicide or self-harm
    \item Unconsciousness, vomiting while drowsy, or slow breathing after drinking
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical interview:} a careful history of drinking, attempts to stop, and the effect on health and daily life
    \item \textbf{Screening tools:} such as the AUDIT and CAGE questionnaires
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} checking for liver disease, high blood pressure, and nerve damage
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} liver function, full blood count, and markers of heavy drinking
    \item \textbf{Mental health assessment:} evaluation for depression, anxiety, and suicide risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detoxification:} medically supervised withdrawal for people with significant dependence
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation programmes:} structured residential or day programmes combining therapy and support
    \item \textbf{Psychological therapies:} cognitive behaviour therapy and motivational interviewing to change drinking behaviour
    \item \textbf{Medication:} naltrexone, acamprosate, or other medicines prescribed by a doctor to reduce craving
    \item \textbf{Mutual help groups:} Alcoholics Anonymous, SMART Recovery, and other peer support
    \item \textbf{Family support:} education and counselling for partners and children, such as through Al-Anon
    \item \textbf{Long-term follow-up:} relapse is common, and continuing care greatly improves the odds of recovery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Medical complications: cirrhosis, pancreatitis, brain damage, heart disease, and cancer
    \item Withdrawal emergencies, including seizures and delirium tremens
    \item Injury, accidents, and suicide
    \item Family breakdown, financial ruin, and job loss
    \item Depression and other mental health problems
    \item Harm to children and other family members
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Alcoholism is a chronic condition, and relapse is part of recovery for many people rather than a failure. With treatment, a substantial proportion of people achieve sustained abstinence or major improvement, and recovery is more likely with long-term support, treatment of mental health problems, and the involvement of family.

Untreated, alcoholism often progresses and shortens life. People who engage with treatment and continue with it after detoxification have the best outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low-risk limits:} keep drinking within low-risk limits and take regular alcohol-free days
    \item \textbf{Social circles:} avoid binge drinking and heavy-drinking peer groups that encourage excess
    \item \textbf{Coping:} deal with stress, anxiety, and depression without alcohol
    \item \textbf{Young people:} talk to children and young people about alcohol before they start drinking
    \item \textbf{Early help:} if you recognise a problem, talk to a GP early -- treatment works better before complications set in
    \item \textbf{Families:} seek support for yourself, set boundaries, and encourage treatment without blame
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} after treatment, keep attending meetings, appointments, and follow-up care
\end{itemize}
