\chapter{Full Blood Count}
\index{Full Blood Count}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The full blood count (FBC), also called a complete blood count or full blood examination, is one of the most common and useful blood tests. It measures the number and characteristics of the three main types of blood cells: red blood cells, which carry oxygen; white blood cells, which fight infection; and platelets, which help blood to clot. The test is used to investigate symptoms such as fatigue, infections, bleeding, and bruising, to screen for many conditions, and to monitor treatment and chronic disease. It is a quick, inexpensive test that provides a remarkable amount of information about a person's health. This chapter explains what the full blood count measures and what the results mean.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The full blood count is the most frequently ordered blood test in many countries and worldwide, performed millions of times each year. It is part of routine health checks, pre-operative assessment, pregnancy care, and the monitoring of chronic conditions and cancer treatment. Around one-third of the population will have a full blood count in any year. The most common abnormalities found are anaemia, which affects a large proportion of people, especially women and older adults, and elevations of white cells and platelets from infection and inflammation. Most results are normal, and most abnormalities are minor and easily explained.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Understanding the components of the test explains its uses.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Red blood cells:} haemoglobin carries oxygen from the lungs to the tissues; low levels cause anaemia with fatigue, pallor, and breathlessness
    \item \textbf{Red cell indices:} the size (MCV) and haemoglobin content of red cells help classify anaemia: small cells suggest iron deficiency, large cells suggest B12 or folate deficiency
    \item \textbf{Haematocrit:} the proportion of blood made of red cells, which rises with dehydration and falls in anaemia
    \item \textbf{White blood cells:} fight infection; they rise with infection, inflammation, and some blood disorders, and fall with immune suppression and some treatments
    \item \textbf{White cell types:} neutrophils (bacterial infection), lymphocytes (viral infection), and eosinophils (allergy and parasites)
    \item \textbf{Platelets:} small cells that form clots; low levels cause bleeding and bruising, and high levels increase clotting risk
    \item \textbf{Reticulocytes:} young red cells, whose level shows whether the bone marrow is producing enough red cells
    \item \textbf{The bone marrow:} the factory for blood cells, which responds to the body's demands and is assessed through the blood count
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The test is used in many situations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Investigating fatigue, pallor, weakness, and breathlessness, which suggest anaemia
    \item Investigating fever, infections, and unexplained illness
    \item Investigating bleeding, bruising, or petechiae, which suggest platelet problems
    \item Routine health screening and pre-operative assessment
    \item Monitoring chronic disease, including kidney disease, and cancer treatment
    \item Monitoring medicines that affect blood cells, such as chemotherapy
    \item Pregnancy monitoring, especially for anaemia
    \item Follow-up of abnormal results to confirm resolution
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Abnormal results need interpretation in context.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Low haemoglobin: iron deficiency, B12 or folate deficiency, chronic disease, bleeding, and blood disorders
    \item High white cells: infection, inflammation, stress, and blood cancers
    \item Low white cells: viral infection, medicines, and immune or bone marrow problems
    \item High platelets: infection, iron deficiency, inflammation, and blood disorders
    \item Low platelets: immune disorders, medicines, infection, and bone marrow disease
    \item Abnormal red cell size: iron, B12, folate, liver, and thyroid causes
    \item The full blood count is never diagnostic alone; it is interpreted with the history, examination, and further tests
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
A full blood count is arranged by a doctor, and results should be discussed with the doctor who ordered them. Anyone with persistent fatigue, bleeding, bruising, fever, or other concerning symptoms should seek assessment.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe breathlessness, chest pain, or collapse from profound anaemia
    \item Significant bleeding or bruising that is sudden or severe
    \item High fever with severe illness, especially with a low white cell count
    \item Confusion or signs of infection or bleeding complications
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The test:} a small blood sample from a vein, with results usually available within hours to a day
    \item \textbf{Blood film:} examination of the cells under a microscope when abnormalities need closer assessment
    \item \textbf{Further tests:} iron studies, B12, and folate for anaemia; inflammatory markers for infection; and clotting tests
    \item \textbf{Bone marrow biopsy:} when blood disorders are suspected and the cause is not clear
    \item \textbf{Repeat testing:} to confirm and track results over time
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal results:} reassurance, with the test repeated when clinically indicated
    \item \textbf{Iron deficiency anaemia:} iron supplements and investigation of the cause, including bleeding
    \item \textbf{B12 and folate deficiency:} replacement and treatment of the cause
    \item \textbf{Anaemia of chronic disease:} management of the underlying condition
    \item \textbf{Infection:} treatment according to the organism and site
    \item \textbf{Platelet disorders:} specialist haematology assessment and treatment
    \item \textbf{Blood disorders:} referral to a haematologist for investigation and management
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular repeat counts for chronic conditions and treatments
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Missed diagnosis when abnormal results are not followed up
    \item Unnecessary investigation and anxiety when minor variations are overinterpreted
    \item Progression of anaemia, infection, or blood disorders without treatment
    \item The complications of the underlying condition, such as bleeding or infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The full blood count is a powerful screening and diagnostic tool, and most abnormalities are found and treated early. Anaemia is corrected in most cases with simple treatment, infections resolve with appropriate therapy, and blood disorders, when present, are detected early enough for effective management. The test's value lies in its breadth: one simple sample screens for problems across the blood, bone marrow, immune, and nutritional systems, and it guides monitoring of almost every chronic disease.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discuss the reason for the test and the results with your doctor
    \item Eat a diet with adequate iron, B12, and folate to prevent common anaemias
    \item Attend routine health checks that include the test when recommended
    \item Report fatigue, bleeding, bruising, or recurrent infections
    \item Follow up abnormal results, including repeating tests as advised
    \item Ask for a copy of results and keep your own record
    \item Do not self-treat with iron or vitamin supplements without knowing the cause
\end{itemize}
