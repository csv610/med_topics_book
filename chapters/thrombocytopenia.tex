\chapter{Thrombocytopenia}
\index{Thrombocytopenia}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Educational reference; always seek professional advice for individual care.}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Thrombocytopenia means a lower than normal platelet count in the blood. Platelets (thrombocytes) plug vessel injuries and support clotting; when counts fall, bruising, petechiae, nosebleeds, gum bleeding, and, at very low levels, serious internal haemorrhage become more likely. Normal adult counts are roughly 150--450 $\times$ 10$^9$/L; mild reductions may be found incidentally, while counts under 50 $\times$ 10$^9$/L raise procedural bleeding concern, and counts under 10--20 $\times$ 10$^9$/L carry higher risk of spontaneous bleeding. Causes include reduced production in the bone marrow, increased destruction or consumption in the blood or spleen, dilution, and distributional change. Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), drug-induced thrombocytopenia, infections (including viral illness and sepsis), liver disease with hypersplenism, pregnancy-associated forms, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), thrombotic microangiopathies (TTP/HUS), and marrow disorders such as leukaemia or aplastic anaemia are important categories. This chapter outlines recognition, urgent red flags, investigations, and management principles used in local practice.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Mild thrombocytopenia is a frequent laboratory finding. ITP occurs in children (often post-viral and self-limited) and adults (more often chronic). Drug-induced cases follow antibiotics, anticonvulsants, quinine-containing products, chemotherapy, and many other agents. HIT is uncommon but critical in heparin-exposed inpatients. In pregnancy, gestational thrombocytopenia is relatively common and usually mild, whereas pre-eclampsia, HELLP, and TTP need specialist obstetric and haematology care. People with chronic liver disease, hypersplenism, alcohol excess, and haematological malignancy are over-represented in hospital cohorts. Incidental low counts in well people still require a structured approach to exclude pseudothrombocytopenia and serious disease.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pseudothrombocytopenia:} EDTA anticoagulant clumping artefact---repeat count in citrate tube
    \item \textbf{Impaired production:} aplastic anaemia, leukaemia, myelodysplasia, B12/folate deficiency, marrow infiltration, chemotherapy, alcohol toxicity
    \item \textbf{Immune destruction:} ITP autoantibodies against platelets; drug-dependent antibodies; post-transfusion purpura
    \item \textbf{Consumption:} DIC, TTP, HUS, Kasabach--Merritt phenomenon, major haemorrhage with resuscitation
    \item \textbf{Sequestration:} splenomegaly pools platelets
    \item \textbf{HIT:} immune-mediated platelet activation with paradoxical thrombosis risk despite low counts
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy-related:} gestational dilution/accelerated clearance versus pathological microangiopathy
    \item \textbf{Infection and sepsis:} direct marrow suppression and consumption
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Petechiae and purpura:} pinpoint or larger non-blanching spots, often legs and mucous membranes
    \item \textbf{Easy bruising and prolonged bleeding from cuts:} common soft signs
    \item \textbf{Epistaxis and gingival bleeding:} frequent
    \item \textbf{Menorrhagia and blood in urine or stool:} needs prompt review
    \item \textbf{Wet purpura in the mouth:} raised bleeding risk marker
    \item \textbf{Retinal or intracranial bleeding:} severe emergency manifestations
    \item \textbf{Thrombosis with low platelets:} think HIT or antiphospholipid-related syndromes, not simple ITP alone
    \item \textbf{Systemic clues:} fever, weight loss, night sweats, bone pain, jaundice, or massive spleen change the differential
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{ITP versus secondary immune thrombocytopenia:} lupus, HIV, hepatitis C, lymphoproliferative disease
    \item \textbf{TTP versus ITP:} TTP has microangiopathic haemolysis, neurological or renal features, and is a haematological emergency
    \item \textbf{HIT versus other hospital thrombocytopenia:} timing after heparin, thrombosis, 4Ts score
    \item \textbf{Marrow failure versus peripheral destruction:} film, reticulocytes, other lineage cytopenias, biopsy
    \item \textbf{Liver disease and alcohol:} common combined mechanisms
    \item \textbf{Gestational thrombocytopenia versus pre-eclampsia/HELLP:} blood pressure, proteinuria, liver enzymes, haemolysis markers
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any new unexplained bruising, petechiae, or bleeding deserves medical review and a full blood count. People on heparin, chemotherapy, or with known blood disorders need earlier thresholds for contact.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe headache, confusion, weakness, or neurological change suggesting intracranial bleed
    \item Uncontrolled bleeding, vomiting blood, black stools with collapse, or heavy pregnancy bleeding
    \item Chest pain or limb ischaemia in a heparin-exposed patient (possible HIT thrombosis)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full blood count and blood film:} confirm true thrombocytopenia; look for schistocytes, blasts, dysplasia
    \item \textbf{Repeat in non-EDTA anticoagulant:} exclude clumping artefact
    \item \textbf{Coagulation profile, fibrinogen, D-dimer:} DIC work-up when sick
    \item \textbf{Haemolysis screen:} LDH, haptoglobin, bilirubin, reticulocytes for TTP/HUS/HELLP
    \item \textbf{Viral and autoimmune serology:} HIV, HCV, HBV, ANA as indicated
    \item \textbf{B12, folate, liver enzymes, alcohol history}
    \item \textbf{HIT testing:} immunoassay and functional assays guided by pretest probability
    \item \textbf{Bone marrow examination:} selected unexplained cases, older adults, or multi-lineage abnormalities
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} spleen size; CT if internal bleeding suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Treat the cause:} stop culprit drugs; treat infection; manage liver disease; deliver baby when obstetric syndromes require
    \item \textbf{ITP treatment:} observation if mild; corticosteroids, IV immunoglobulin, thrombopoietin receptor agonists, rituximab, or splenectomy in specialist pathways for significant bleeding or very low counts
    \item \textbf{Platelet transfusion:} for serious bleeding or procedures at critical counts; often futile alone in consumptive disorders like TTP or HIT without addressing mechanism
    \item \textbf{TTP:} urgent plasma exchange and specialist haematology---do not delay for perfect certainty if highly likely
    \item \textbf{HIT:} stop all heparin; use alternative anticoagulation even when platelets are low if thrombosis risk is active
    \item \textbf{Procedural planning:} dental, surgical, and obstetric procedures need count thresholds and liaison
    \item \textbf{Avoidance of antiplatelet agents and NSAIDs:} unless a clinician weighs benefits carefully
    \item \textbf{Education:} bleeding precautions and when to present
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Life-threatening haemorrhage:} intracranial, gastrointestinal, and postpartum
    \item \textbf{Thrombosis in HIT and antiphospholipid disease}
    \item \textbf{Treatment toxicities:} steroid effects, immunosuppression, post-splenectomy infection risk
    \item \textbf{Missed marrow malignancy:} if film and progressive cytopenias are ignored
    \item \textbf{Fetal and maternal risk:} in pregnancy-associated pathologies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Prognosis tracks the cause. Childhood ITP often remits. Adult ITP may become chronic but is usually manageable. TTP and HIT outcomes depend on speed of recognition. Drug-induced thrombocytopenia typically recovers after drug cessation, though recovery timing varies. Marrow failure syndromes need disease-specific therapy. Many people with mild stable thrombocytopenia live normally with monitoring alone.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use medicines only as prescribed; tell clinicians about quinine, over-the-counter products, and alcohol
    \item Avoid contact sports and high-risk trauma when counts are very low, as advised
    \item Use a soft toothbrush and gentle nose care during thrombocytopenic periods
    \item Seek advice before dental extractions or surgery
    \item Keep anticoagulation and heparin exposure documented in hospital records
    \item Attend monitoring blood tests during chemotherapy and new high-risk drugs
    \item Pregnant people should report headaches, visual change, right upper abdominal pain, and swelling promptly
\end{itemize}
