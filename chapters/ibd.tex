\chapter{Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)}
\index{Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is the umbrella term for chronic inflammatory conditions of the digestive tract, principally Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis. In Crohn's disease, inflammation can affect any part of the gut from mouth to anus, often in patches that penetrate the full thickness of the bowel wall. In ulcerative colitis, inflammation is limited to the innermost lining of the large bowel (colon) and rectum, and is continuous from the rectum upwards. IBD follows a relapsing and remitting course, with episodes of active inflammation separated by periods of remission. It is distinct from irritable bowel syndrome, which involves disordered gut function rather than inflammation. Treatment aims to control inflammation, heal the bowel, and maintain remission so that people can live full lives.

\section*{Epidemiology}
IBD affects around one in 250 people, one of the highest rates in the world, and more than 100,000 people live with the condition. It can begin at any age, but most commonly starts in late adolescence and early adulthood, with a second smaller peak in later life. Ulcerative colitis is slightly more common than Crohn's disease, and both conditions affect men and women similarly. Incidence has risen steadily in many countries and other Westernised countries over recent decades, and is increasing in previously low-risk populations as lifestyles change. IBD is associated with a family history in a minority of cases, and people of Jewish descent have a higher risk.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
IBD is thought to result from an inappropriate immune response to the normal bacteria in the gut in genetically susceptible people, triggered by environmental factors. The immune system attacks the lining of the bowel, causing chronic inflammation, ulceration, and tissue damage. More than 200 gene variants have been linked to IBD, most involved in immune regulation and the gut barrier. Environmental factors implicated include cigarette smoking, which worsens Crohn's disease but is protective in ulcerative colitis; the use of antibiotics and other changes to gut bacteria; diet; and possibly early-life factors. The appendix and tonsils have also been linked to risk. The resulting inflammation releases inflammatory chemicals that damage the bowel wall and cause the characteristic symptoms.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent diarrhoea, sometimes with blood, mucus, or pus
    \item Abdominal pain and cramping, often relieved by opening the bowels
    \item Urgency, and in ulcerative colitis, passing frequent small amounts of stool
    \item Weight loss, loss of appetite, and fatigue
    \item Fever during flares of active disease
    \item Anaemia from chronic blood loss
    \item In Crohn's disease: mouth ulcers, anal fissures, fistulas, and strictures
    \item Extraintestinal features: joint pain, skin rashes, eye inflammation, and mouth ulcers
    \item Children may present with poor growth and delayed puberty
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of IBD overlap with several other conditions that must be considered.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infectious gastroenteritis:} bacterial or parasitic infection causing diarrhoea and blood
    \item \textbf{Irritable bowel syndrome:} disordered gut function without inflammation
    \item \textbf{Coeliac disease:} gluten-triggered enteropathy
    \item \textbf{Diverticulitis:} inflammation of colonic pouches
    \item \textbf{Bowel cancer:} especially in older people with new symptoms
    \item \textbf{Microscopic colitis:} inflammation visible only under the microscope
    \item \textbf{Ischaemic colitis:} reduced blood flow to the bowel in older people
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with persistent diarrhoea, blood in the stool, unexplained weight loss, or abdominal pain lasting more than a few weeks should see a doctor. People with known IBD should seek review for new or worsening symptoms, poor response to treatment, or concerning features such as fever or dehydration.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain, especially with a rigid or distended abdomen
    \item Large-volume rectal bleeding or blood with faintness and dizziness
    \item High fever with shaking, which may indicate a complication or infection
    \item Repeated vomiting or inability to keep fluids down
    \item Signs of bowel obstruction: severe cramping, vomiting, and a swollen abdomen
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count for anaemia, inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein and faecal calprotectin
    \item \textbf{Stool tests:} to exclude infection and measure faecal calprotectin, a marker of bowel inflammation
    \item \textbf{Colonoscopy:} direct examination of the large bowel with biopsies, the key diagnostic test
    \item \textbf{Gastroscopy:} assessment of the upper gut in suspected Crohn's disease
    \item \textbf{Capsule endoscopy:} a swallowed camera for the small bowel, where Crohn's disease often hides
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} MRI of the small bowel, CT, or ultrasound to map disease extent and complications
    \item \textbf{Review of symptoms and examination:} including assessment of the abdomen, mouth, joints, and perianal area
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Aminosalicylates:} anti-inflammatory medicines such as mesalazine, mainly for ulcerative colitis
    \item \textbf{Corticosteroids:} used short-term to control flares, with careful monitoring for side effects
    \item \textbf{Immunomodulators:} azathioprine, mercaptopurine, or methotrexate to maintain remission
    \item \textbf{Biological therapies:} medicines such as infliximab, adalimumab, vedolizumab, and ustekinumab that target specific inflammatory pathways
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} removal of diseased bowel segments in Crohn's disease, or removal of the colon and rectum (proctocolectomy) with ileal pouch formation in ulcerative colitis
    \item \textbf{Nutrition:} dietary advice, nutritional supplements, and in children, exclusive enteral nutrition as first-line therapy
    \item \textbf{Symptom support:} antidiarrhoeals, iron and vitamin replacement, and pain management
    \item \textbf{Smoking cessation:} essential in Crohn's disease, where smoking worsens outcomes
    \item \textbf{Coordinated care:} specialist gastroenterology, IBD nurses, dietitians, and psychological support
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Strictures (narrowing) and fistulas (abnormal tracts) in Crohn's disease
    \item Bowel obstruction and perforation
    \item Abscesses in the abdomen or around the anus
    \item Severe bleeding and toxic megacolon in ulcerative colitis
    \item Anaemia, malnutrition, and osteoporosis from chronic inflammation and treatment
    \item Increased risk of bowel cancer, warranting regular surveillance colonoscopy
    \item Side effects of treatment, including infection risk with immunomodulators and biologics
    \item Effects on growth in children and on fertility, pregnancy, and mental health
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
IBD is a lifelong condition, but most people respond well to modern treatment and enjoy long periods of remission. Around half of people with Crohn's disease require surgery within the first 10 years, although surgery is not a cure and inflammation can recur. Ulcerative colitis can be effectively controlled medically in most people, and in those who need surgery, removal of the colon can be curative. Newer biological treatments have improved remission rates and reduced the need for surgery and hospitalisation. With good disease control, most people with IBD work, study, and have families, though they face an increased long-term risk of bowel cancer and benefit from surveillance.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take medicines as prescribed, even when well, to maintain remission
    \item Stop smoking if you have Crohn's disease
    \item Keep a symptom diary and attend regular specialist reviews
    \item Eat a balanced diet, and seek dietetic advice for flares, strictures, or weight loss
    \item Stay physically active and manage stress, which can trigger flares
    \item Attend surveillance colonoscopy as scheduled
    \item Discuss pregnancy planning with the specialist team
    \item Seek support from a local inflammatory-bowel-disease support organisation and mental health services when needed
    \item Protect bone health with calcium, vitamin D, and weight-bearing exercise, especially if taking steroids
\end{itemize}
