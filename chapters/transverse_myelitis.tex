\chapter{Transverse Myelitis}
\index{Transverse Myelitis}
\medskip
\noindent\textit{Educational reference; always seek professional advice for individual care.}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Transverse myelitis is inflammation across part of the spinal cord. It can disrupt movement, sensation, and control of the bladder or bowel on both sides of the body. Causes include autoimmune disease, multiple sclerosis, neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, MOG-antibody disease, infection, cancer, and sometimes no identifiable cause. It is an emergency because spinal-cord compression and other treatable causes must be excluded promptly.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms often develop over hours to days and may include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item weakness or heaviness in the legs, arms, or all four limbs
    \item numbness, altered temperature or touch, or a tight ``band'' around the trunk
    \item back or limb pain, burning, or electric-shock sensations
    \item difficulty passing urine, urinary retention or incontinence, constipation, or sexual dysfunction
    \item difficulty walking, poor balance, or muscle spasms
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
\textbf{Contact local emergency services urgently} for new weakness, rapidly spreading numbness, difficulty walking, loss of bladder or bowel control, or severe back pain with neurological symptoms. Do not wait for symptoms to resolve. Spinal compression, stroke, infection, and severe metabolic problems can look similar.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment includes a detailed neurological examination and urgent MRI of the spinal cord, often with brain imaging. Blood tests may assess inflammation, infection, nutritional deficiency, and antibodies associated with demyelinating disease. Lumbar puncture may examine cerebrospinal fluid when safe and clinically appropriate. The team may also investigate cancer, vascular disease, medication effects, and spinal-cord compression.

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on the cause. Acute immune-mediated disease is often treated in hospital with high-dose intravenous corticosteroids. Plasma exchange or other immune treatment may be considered when severe disease does not respond adequately. Infection, compression, vascular causes, or cancer require different treatments. Rehabilitation may include physiotherapy, occupational therapy, bladder and bowel care, pain management, mobility aids, and psychological support.

\section*{Complications and Outlook}
Recovery varies widely. Some people recover fully, while others have persistent weakness, altered sensation, pain, spasms, bladder or bowel problems, or sexual dysfunction. Improvement may continue for months or longer. Follow-up with neurology and rehabilitation services is important, particularly because a first episode may later be linked to multiple sclerosis, neuromyelitis optica, or another condition.

\section*{Self-Care and Follow-up}
Take prescribed medicines as directed and attend repeat imaging or neurology appointments. Protect numb skin from burns and pressure, use mobility aids safely, prevent falls, and seek help for urinary infection, new weakness, worsening pain, or new visual or neurological symptoms. Rehabilitation goals should be individualised and reviewed regularly.
