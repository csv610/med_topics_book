\chapter{Heart Valve Disease}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Heart valve disease occurs when one or more of the heart's four valves fail to open fully (stenosis) or fail to close properly (regurgitation), disturbing the one-way flow of blood through the heart. The most commonly affected valves are the aortic and mitral valves. Valve disease can be present from birth or develop later in life through degeneration, infection, or other conditions. Mild valve disease is common and often requires no treatment, while severe disease can cause heart failure and requires timely repair or replacement.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Valvular heart disease affects approximately 2--3\% of the adult population, and its prevalence rises sharply with age: aortic stenosis, the most common form, affects around 5--12\% of people over 75. Rheumatic heart disease, still an important cause in Indigenous Australian communities and low-income countries, affects the mitral valve predominantly. Around 20,000 heart valve operations are performed in Australia each decade. As the population ages, the burden of degenerative valve disease is increasing, and detection is often incidental during examination for other reasons.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Aortic stenosis:} progressive calcification and stiffening of the valve with age, or a congenital bicuspid valve
    \item \textbf{Mitral regurgitation:} leakage due to valve prolapse, ischaemic damage after heart attack, or rheumatic disease
    \item \textbf{Aortic regurgitation:} failure of the aortic valve to close, from valve degeneration, aortic dilation, or infection
    \item \textbf{Mitral stenosis:} usually a late consequence of rheumatic fever
    \item \textbf{Infective endocarditis:} infection damages valve tissue
    \item \textbf{Congenital defects:} malformed valves present from birth
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} stenosis obstructs flow and overloads the chamber behind the valve; regurgitation allows blood to leak backwards, also overloading the heart; both eventually cause heart failure, arrhythmia, and reduced cardiac output
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A heart murmur is often the first sign, detected incidentally
    \item Breathlessness on exertion, progressing to breathlessness at rest
    \item Fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, and weakness
    \item Chest pain and fainting, particularly with severe aortic stenosis
    \item Palpitations from associated arrhythmias, especially atrial fibrillation
    \item Swelling of the ankles and legs with advanced disease
    \item Many people remain symptom-free for years despite significant valve disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Coronary artery disease can cause identical symptoms and often coexists
    \item Heart failure from other causes, such as cardiomyopathy and hypertension
    \item Innocent murmurs, which are common and harmless
    \item Asthma and chronic lung disease cause breathlessness
    \item Anaemia and thyroid disease cause fatigue and breathlessness
    \item Echocardiography reliably distinguishes valve disease from these alternatives
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with a known murmur, breathlessness, chest pain, fainting, or palpitations should have a medical assessment. Pregnant women with known valve disease should be managed by a specialist team.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, pressure, or severe breathlessness
    \item Fainting or collapse, especially with exertion
    \item Palpitations with dizziness or breathlessness
    \item Fever with a known valve condition (possible endocarditis)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Echocardiogram:} the cornerstone of diagnosis, defining valve anatomy, severity of stenosis or regurgitation, and heart function
    \item \textbf{Transoesophageal echocardiogram:} detailed imaging of valve structure, used in planning surgery
    \item \textbf{Electrocardiogram:} detects hypertrophy, strain, and rhythm disturbances
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} shows heart size and lung congestion
    \item \textbf{Cardiac MRI and CT:} quantify regurgitation and plan interventions
    \item \textbf{Cardiac catheterisation:} measures pressures and checks coronary arteries before surgery
    \item \textbf{Regular surveillance:} echocardiography at intervals guided by severity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} mild and moderate disease is followed with regular clinical review and echocardiography
    \item \textbf{Medication:} treats symptoms and complications; diuretics for congestion and medicines to control blood pressure and rhythm
    \item \textbf{Endocarditis prevention:} dental hygiene, infection treatment, and antibiotics before selected procedures for high-risk patients
    \item \textbf{Valve repair:} preferred for many regurgitant valves, preserving the patient's own tissue
    \item \textbf{Valve replacement:} mechanical valves (requiring lifelong blood thinners) or tissue valves (from animals or human donors)
    \item \textbf{Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI):} a less invasive approach for aortic stenosis in suitable patients
    \item \textbf{Timing:} surgery is offered before irreversible heart damage occurs
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive heart failure from valve overload
    \item Arrhythmias, particularly atrial fibrillation, which increases stroke risk
    \item Infective endocarditis on the diseased valve
    \item Sudden cardiac events, including death, in untreated severe aortic stenosis
    \item Stroke and bleeding related to anticoagulation after mechanical valve replacement
    \item Structural valve deterioration over 10--20 years with tissue valves
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Untreated severe valve disease, particularly aortic stenosis, carries a poor outlook once symptoms develop, which is why timely surgery is so important. With modern valve surgery and transcatheter techniques, outcomes are excellent: most patients have complete or major relief of symptoms and a normal or near-normal life expectancy. Valve repair has better long-term outcomes than replacement where feasible, and tissue valves can be replaced when they wear out. Long-term success depends on regular follow-up, control of cardiovascular risk, and management of anticoagulation where required.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend regular check-ups and follow recommended surveillance schedules
    \item Prevent rheumatic fever by prompt treatment of strep throat and good living conditions
    \item Maintain excellent dental hygiene and treat infections promptly
    \item Control blood pressure and cholesterol, and quit smoking
    \item Report new or worsening symptoms promptly
    \item Discuss exercise and pregnancy plans with your specialist
    \item Carry a card or letter describing your valve condition and any anticoagulation
\end{itemize}
