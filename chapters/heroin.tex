\chapter{Heroin}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Heroin is a highly addictive opioid drug derived from morphine, which comes from the opium poppy. It is illegal in Australia and is usually injected, smoked, or snorted. Heroin produces intense euphoria followed by sedation, but it carries serious risks, including overdose with respiratory depression, dependence, withdrawal, and blood-borne infections from sharing needles. Dependence on heroin is a chronic relapsing condition, but it is treatable: opioid substitution therapy, such as methadone or buprenorphine, combined with support, substantially improves health and reduces harm.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Heroin use affects a small but significant proportion of Australians, with an estimated 40,000--60,000 people dependent on opioids including heroin. Overdose is the leading cause of drug-related death in Australia, and heroin is responsible for a large share of opioid overdoses. People who inject drugs are at high risk of hepatitis C, hepatitis B, and HIV, though needle and syringe programs have greatly reduced transmission. Treatment programs reach many people who use heroin, and deaths have fallen with increased access to naloxone and substitution therapy.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} heroin binds to opioid receptors in the brain, producing pain relief, euphoria, and sedation
    \item \textbf{Tolerance:} repeated use requires higher doses to achieve the same effect
    \item \textbf{Dependence:} the brain adapts to the drug, and stopping causes withdrawal
    \item \textbf{Withdrawal:} anxiety, sweating, muscle aches, abdominal cramps, vomiting, and diarrhoea
    \item \textbf{Overdose:} high doses suppress the respiratory centre, causing slow or absent breathing and death
    \item \textbf{Risk factors for dependence:} genetic vulnerability, trauma, mental illness, and social disadvantage
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} long-term use alters brain reward, stress, and pain systems, driving compulsive use
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Intoxication: euphoria, drowsiness, slowed breathing, pinpoint pupils, and slurred speech
    \item Overdose: unresponsiveness, slow or shallow breathing, blue lips and fingers, and limpness
    \item Withdrawal: begins within hours of the last dose, with flu-like symptoms, cravings, and agitation
    \item Chronic use: weight loss, poor health, constipation, and neglect of personal care
    \item Injection sites: track marks, abscesses, and vein damage
    \item Complications: infections, blood-borne viruses, and overdose
    \item Social consequences: relationship breakdown, unemployment, and financial problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Overdose of other opioids, sedatives, or alcohol can look identical
    \item Head injury, stroke, and hypoglycaemia cause reduced consciousness
    \item Withdrawal mimics flu and anxiety disorders
    \item Mental health conditions may coexist with and complicate heroin dependence
    \item Naloxone response and toxicology clarify the cause of collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek help early for heroin dependence; GPs, drug and alcohol services, and helplines provide confidential treatment. Anyone who uses heroin should carry naloxone and know how to respond to an overdose.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unresponsiveness or difficulty waking someone
    \item Slow, shallow, or stopped breathing
    \item Blue lips or fingers with collapse
    \item Seizures or choking
    \item Signs of severe infection: high fever, shaking, or abscess with spreading redness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} a comprehensive history of drug use, overdose risk, and physical and mental health
    \item \textbf{Toxicology:} urine or blood tests confirm opioid use where needed
    \item \textbf{Blood-borne virus screening:} HIV, hepatitis B and C testing is recommended
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} injection sites, veins, and general health
    \item \textbf{Mental health assessment:} depression, anxiety, and trauma are common and need treatment
    \item \textbf{Assessment of dependence severity:} guides the treatment setting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Overdose:} naloxone (Narcan) reverses opioid overdose; call 000 and start resuscitation if unresponsive
    \item \textbf{Opioid substitution therapy:} methadone or buprenorphine prevents withdrawal, reduces cravings, and blocks heroin's effect; it is the most effective treatment for dependence
    \item \textbf{Withdrawal management:} medically supervised detoxification with support and medication
    \item \textbf{Counselling and psychology:} cognitive behavioural therapy and relapse prevention
    \item \textbf{Harm reduction:} needle and syringe programs, naloxone training, and safer injecting advice
    \item \textbf{Treatment of complications:} infections, blood-borne viruses, and mental health conditions
    \item \textbf{Peer support:} programs such as SMART Recovery and Narcotics Anonymous
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Overdose, the leading cause of heroin-related death
    \item Hepatitis C, hepatitis B, and HIV from sharing injecting equipment
    \item Bacterial infections: abscesses, cellulitis, endocarditis, and sepsis
    \item Respiratory depression and aspiration during overdose
    \item Dependence and severe withdrawal
    \item Mental health problems and suicide risk
    \item Social harms: homelessness, incarceration, and family breakdown
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With treatment, the outlook for heroin dependence is good: opioid substitution therapy reduces death rates by around half or more, and many people achieve long-term stability, employment, and healthy relationships. Relapse is common and part of the recovery process rather than a failure. Harm-reduction measures save lives by preventing overdose deaths and infections. The key predictors of good outcomes are engagement in treatment, social support, and treatment of coexisting mental health conditions.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Never use heroin alone, and carry naloxone if you use opioids
    \item Use a new needle and syringe for every injection
    \item Seek help early from a GP or drug and alcohol service
    \item Consider opioid substitution therapy; it is safe and effective long term
    \item Get tested and treated for hepatitis B and C
    \item Build a support network and address underlying mental health and social issues
    \item Learn overdose first aid and how to administer naloxone
    \item Call 000 immediately in any suspected overdose
\end{itemize}
