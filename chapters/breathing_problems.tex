\chapter{Breathing Problems}
\index{Breathing Problems}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The term "breathing problems" covers any difficulty in moving air in and out of the lungs, including shortness of breath (dyspnoea), wheezing, chest tightness, a persistent cough, and the feeling of being unable to take a deep breath. Breathing difficulty may come on suddenly or develop gradually over weeks or months, and it can originate in the airways, the lung tissue, the chest wall, the heart, the blood, or even in anxiety. Common causes include asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), chest infections, and stress, but breathlessness can also be the first warning of serious conditions such as a pulmonary embolism, heart failure, or lung cancer. Because the underlying cause dictates treatment, any new, persistent, or severe breathing problem requires proper medical assessment, and sudden or severe breathing difficulty is a medical emergency.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Breathing problems are among the most frequent reasons for seeking medical care. Asthma affects roughly one in nine people and is more common in children and in those with a personal or family history of allergy. COPD is strongly associated with smoking and is common in older adults, particularly long-term smokers. Acute respiratory infections such as bronchitis and pneumonia peak in winter and affect all ages, with the very young, the elderly, and people with chronic disease at highest risk. Exertional breathlessness becomes increasingly common with age and is often driven by cardiovascular disease. Anxiety and panic attacks are a frequent cause of breathlessness in younger adults and can coexist with, or mimic, physical lung disease.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Breathing problems arise from any process that narrows the airways, damages the lung tissue, impairs gas exchange, weakens the pump driving ventilation, or reduces the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asthma:} airway narrowing from inflammation, mucosal oedema, and bronchial muscle spasm, triggered by allergens, exercise, cold air, viruses, or irritants
    \item \textbf{COPD:} progressive destruction of the airways and air sacs, usually from long-term smoking or occupational dust exposure
    \item \textbf{Respiratory infections:} inflammation and mucus within the bronchi or alveoli from viruses or bacteria, causing colds, bronchitis, and pneumonia
    \item \textbf{Heart failure:} reduced cardiac output allows fluid to accumulate in the lungs, producing exertional and nocturnal breathlessness
    \item \textbf{Pulmonary embolism:} a blood clot obstructs pulmonary arteries, disrupting gas exchange and straining the right heart
    \item \textbf{Anaemia:} reduced haemoglobin limits the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity
    \item \textbf{Anxiety and hyperventilation:} stress-driven rapid breathing causes air hunger and tingling, without physical lung disease
    \item \textbf{Other causes:} obesity, rib fractures, pleural effusion, foreign-body aspiration, lung cancer, and neuromuscular conditions affecting the chest wall
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The presentation varies with the cause, but common features include the following.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Shortness of breath at rest or on exertion
    \item Wheezing or a whistling sound, most obvious when breathing out
    \item A cough that may be dry or productive of phlegm
    \item Chest tightness, heaviness, or pain
    \item Rapid or shallow breathing, or the sense of being unable to take a full breath
    \item Visible effort of breathing, including nasal flaring, use of accessory muscles, or grunting
    \item Blue discoloration of the lips or skin (cyanosis) in severe cases
    \item In asthma: symptoms that fluctuate, worsen at night or early morning, and improve with reliever medication
    \item Fatigue, disturbed sleep, and reduced capacity for exercise
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asthma versus COPD:} episodic, reversible airway narrowing versus persistent, progressive airflow obstruction
    \item \textbf{Cardiac breathlessness:} heart failure or angina, which can mimic lung disease and present with exertional dyspnoea or orthopnoea
    \item \textbf{Pulmonary embolism:} sudden breathlessness and chest pain that may mimic pneumonia, anxiety, or asthma
    \item \textbf{Respiratory infection:} bronchitis and pneumonia versus a simple viral cold
    \item \textbf{Anaemia and thyroid disease:} systemic causes of fatigue, breathlessness, and exercise intolerance
    \item \textbf{Anxiety and panic:} hyperventilation syndromes that can be mistaken for, or coexist with, physical disease
    \item \textbf{Structural lung disease:} lung cancer, pleural effusion, or a narrowed airway in persistent or progressive breathlessness
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if breathlessness is new, persistent, or worsening, if wheezing or coughing becomes frequent or disturbs sleep, if exercise capacity is falling, or if reliever inhaler use is increasing. People with existing lung or heart disease should seek early review when their symptoms change.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden or severe difficulty breathing, or the inability to speak in full sentences
    \item Chest pain, heaviness, or pressure, especially in association with breathlessness
    \item Lips or face turning blue, or a grey, clammy appearance
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or a faint feeling
    \item Coughing up blood
    \item An asthma attack that is not responding to the reliever inhaler
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} onset and progression, triggers and relievers, smoking history, occupational exposure, medications, and past history of asthma, heart disease, blood clots, or cancer
    \item \textbf{Examination:} auscultation of the chest, observation of work of breathing, and measurement of oxygen saturation with a pulse oximeter
    \item \textbf{Spirometry:} a blowing test that measures lung volumes and flow to confirm asthma or COPD, including reversibility testing
    \item \textbf{Peak flow monitoring:} serial home peak flow readings to assess asthma control and variability
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} to identify pneumonia, heart failure, effusions, or masses
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, inflammatory markers, and, where appropriate, troponin or markers of heart strain
    \item \textbf{Further tests:} electrocardiogram, arterial or venous blood gas analysis, and CT imaging or echocardiography in selected cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asthma:} a regular preventer inhaler (usually an inhaled corticosteroid) to reduce airway inflammation, a reliever inhaler (a short-acting bronchodilator) for sudden symptoms, and a written asthma action plan
    \item \textbf{COPD:} inhaled therapy, pulmonary rehabilitation, smoking cessation, vaccination, and prompt treatment of exacerbations
    \item \textbf{Respiratory infections:} rest, fluids, and simple analgesia; antibiotics only when a bacterial infection is likely
    \item \textbf{Heart failure:} medications that reduce fluid retention and cardiac strain, together with lifestyle measures
    \item \textbf{Pulmonary embolism:} urgent hospital care and anticoagulation
    \item \textbf{Anxiety-related breathlessness:} controlled breathing techniques, relaxation training, and psychological support
    \item \textbf{General measures:} smoking cessation, avoidance of triggers, graded physical activity, and up-to-date influenza and pneumococcal vaccination
    \item \textbf{Oxygen therapy:} supplementary oxygen in hospital when saturation is low
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe or life-threatening asthma attacks requiring hospital or intensive care
    \item Respiratory failure, with falling oxygen or rising carbon dioxide levels
    \item Recurrent infections, hospital admissions, and progressive lung damage in COPD
    \item Pulmonary hypertension and right-heart strain from chronic lung disease
    \item Pneumonia complicating influenza or other viral infections
    \item Deconditioning, muscle weakness, and weight loss from chronic breathlessness
    \item Anxiety, depression, and sleep disturbance linked to ongoing symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the underlying cause. Asthma is usually well controlled with regular preventer treatment, and most affected people live full, active lives; the principal risks arise from under-treated or unrecognised severe attacks. COPD is a progressive condition, but smoking cessation and good treatment slow the decline and improve quality of life. Most chest infections resolve within one to three weeks. Breathlessness from heart disease or pulmonary embolism improves with treatment but requires ongoing specialist review. Across all causes, early diagnosis, treatment of the underlying condition, and avoidance of smoking give the best chance of a good outcome.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Do not smoke, and avoid exposure to second-hand smoke and inhaled pollutants
    \item If you have asthma, take the preventer inhaler regularly and follow the written asthma action plan
    \item Keep influenza and pneumococcal vaccinations up to date
    \item Manage coexisting conditions such as heart disease, diabetes, and obesity
    \item Avoid known triggers, including pollen, dust, animal dander, and cold air where possible
    \item Stay as active as your condition allows, building exercise gradually with professional guidance where needed
    \item Practise slow, controlled breathing and relaxation for stress-related breathlessness
    \item See a doctor early for any new, persistent, or worsening breathing symptom
\end{itemize}
