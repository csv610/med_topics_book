\chapter{Gastric Sleeve Surgery}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Gastric sleeve surgery, also called sleeve gastrectomy, is an operation that removes most of the stomach to help people with severe obesity lose weight. After surgery, the stomach is a narrow tube, so it holds far less food, and the removal of the hunger-hormone-producing part of the stomach reduces appetite. The operation is performed by keyhole (laparoscopic) surgery, requires a short hospital stay, and produces substantial, lasting weight loss with major health benefits for people with obesity-related conditions such as type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, and sleep apnoea. This chapter explains who is eligible, how the surgery is performed, and what life is like afterwards.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Gastric sleeve surgery is the most commonly performed weight-loss operation in Australia. It is offered to people with a body mass index (BMI) of 40 or more, or 35 or more with serious obesity-related disease, after non-surgical approaches have failed. Thousands of Australians undergo the operation each year, and its popularity reflects its good results and simplicity relative to other bariatric procedures. Weight loss averages 50--70% of excess weight, and remission of type 2 diabetes occurs in a large proportion of people. Serious complications are uncommon, and the mortality rate is low.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The procedure:} about 75--80% of the stomach is removed along its outer curve, leaving a sleeve-shaped stomach roughly the size of a banana
    \item \textbf{Restriction:} the small sleeve limits meal size, producing early fullness and reduced intake
    \item \textbf{Hormonal effects:} removal of the fundus, where most ghrelin (the hunger hormone) is made, reduces hunger, and satiety hormones rise
    \item \textbf{Weight loss:} gradual and sustained, occurring mainly over the first 12--24 months
    \item \textbf{Metabolic improvement:} blood sugar control and diabetes improve substantially, partly through weight loss and partly through hormone changes
    \item \textbf{No intestinal bypass:} the small intestine is untouched, so nutrient absorption is largely normal and nutritional risk is lower than after gastric bypass
    \item \textbf{Irreversibility:} the removed stomach cannot be replaced, and the procedure is permanent
    \item \textbf{Anaesthesia and surgery:} performed under general anaesthesia with laparoscopic instruments
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A hospital stay of 1--3 nights, with most people discharged quickly
    \item Small incisions on the abdomen from the laparoscopic approach
    \item A liquid diet for the first weeks, progressing to soft food and then small meals
    \item Rapid weight loss, visible within the first month and continuing for 1--2 years
    \item Early fullness and reduced appetite, with the need to eat slowly and chew well
    \item Improvement in diabetes, blood pressure, sleep apnoea, and joint pain
    \item The need for lifelong vitamins, exercise, and follow-up
    \item Possible nausea, vomiting, or reflux if the eating rules are broken
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Sleeve surgery is one of several weight-management options.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Structured diet and exercise programs and medicines for obesity
    \item Gastric bypass, which offers greater weight loss and diabetes remission at the cost of more nutritional risk
    \item The adjustable gastric band, now less commonly performed
    \item The choice is made individually after comprehensive assessment
    \item Eligibility requires that non-surgical approaches have been tried and that the benefits outweigh the risks
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone considering the operation should be assessed by a multidisciplinary bariatric team, and the decision should be informed by a full discussion of risks, benefits, and the lifelong changes required.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain, fever, or vomiting in the days and weeks after surgery
    \item Chest pain or breathlessness
    \item Inability to keep fluids down or signs of dehydration
    \item Redness, discharge, or opening of the wounds
    \item Any emergency symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{BMI and comorbidity assessment:} weight, diabetes, blood pressure, and sleep apnoea
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative work-up:} blood tests, cardiac and respiratory assessment, and sleep studies
    \item \textbf{Nutritional review:} baseline vitamin levels and dietary assessment
    \item \textbf{Psychological review:} eating behaviour, mental health, and readiness for change
    \item \textbf{Gastroscopy:} examination of the stomach, including H.\ pylori testing
    \item \textbf{Post-operative imaging:} contrast studies if a leak is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative preparation:} weight loss, stopping smoking, and optimising diabetes and other conditions
    \item \textbf{The surgery:} laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy, with careful management of the staple line
    \item \textbf{The post-operative diet:} clear fluids, then full fluids, then soft food, then small regular meals over about 6 weeks
    \item \textbf{Vitamins and minerals:} lifelong daily supplements
    \item \textbf{Eating habits:} small meals, protein first, chew thoroughly, and stop eating at fullness
    \item \textbf{Hydration:} frequent small sips between meals
    \item \textbf{Activity:} early walking, then regular exercise
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} bariatric team reviews, blood tests, and GP care for life
    \item \textbf{Reflux and other issues:} medicines and review as needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Leak from the staple line, the most serious early complication
    \item Bleeding and infection
    \item Stricture of the sleeve with difficulty eating
    \item Acid reflux, which is common and may need treatment
    \item Nausea and vomiting from eating too fast or too much
    \item Nutritional deficiencies without supplements
    \item Weight regain in a minority
    \item Anaesthetic risks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Gastric sleeve surgery achieves substantial and durable weight loss for most people, with major improvements in obesity-related disease and quality of life. It is safer and less complex than gastric bypass, and most people recover quickly and adapt well. Success depends on permanent changes in eating, activity, and follow-up: those who commit to the program maintain their weight loss, while those who return to old habits may regain. For well-selected people, the benefits of the operation are life-changing.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend the full pre-operative assessment program
    \item Commit to the post-operative diet, eating rules, and supplements for life
    \item Eat small, slow meals and stop when full
    \item Keep well hydrated between meals
    \item Exercise regularly and build muscle
    \item Attend every follow-up appointment
    \item Report abdominal pain, fever, or vomiting early
    \item Seek support from the bariatric team and support groups
\end{itemize}
