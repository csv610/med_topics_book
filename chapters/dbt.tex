\chapter{Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) is a structured form of cognitive-behavioural therapy developed in the 1990s to treat people with intense emotional instability, particularly those with borderline personality disorder. It combines standard cognitive-behavioural techniques with concepts of mindfulness and acceptance, built around the "dialectic" of balancing change with validation. DBT teaches practical skills in four areas: mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness. It is delivered individually and in skills-training groups, and has a strong evidence base for reducing self-harm and suicidal behaviour.

\section*{Epidemiology}
DBT was originally developed for people with borderline personality disorder, which affects around 1--2\% of the general population and is diagnosed more often in women in clinical settings. Borderline personality disorder is strongly associated with repeated self-harm, suicidal crises, and substantial use of mental health services. Since its development, DBT has been adapted and shown to help people with other conditions characterised by emotional dysregulation, including eating disorders, substance use disorders, and treatment-resistant depression. Access to DBT in Australia has grown through public mental health services and private practitioners.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Emotional vulnerability:} biological sensitivity to emotional stimuli, with intense reactions and slow return to baseline
    \item \textbf{Invalidating environment:} a childhood context in which emotions are dismissed or punished, failing to teach emotion regulation
    \item \textbf{Transactional model:} the interaction of biological vulnerability and environmental invalidation produces the patterns of borderline personality disorder
    \item \textbf{Skill deficits:} people with emotional dysregulation often lack skills in tolerating distress, regulating emotions, and managing relationships
    \item \textbf{Behavioural reinforcement:} self-harm and impulsive behaviours can be maintained by relief from distress or attention from others, and DBT targets these functions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Intense and rapidly shifting emotions that feel overwhelming and hard to control
    \item Recurrent suicidal thoughts, self-harm, or impulsive behaviours
    \item Unstable and intense relationships, with idealisation and devaluation
    \item Chronic feelings of emptiness and fear of abandonment
    \item Difficulty tolerating distress, with crises in response to minor triggers
    \item Impulsivity in spending, substance use, eating, or driving
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bipolar disorder, in which mood swings are episodic and less reactive to interpersonal events
    \item Major depressive disorder and anxiety disorders
    \item Post-traumatic stress disorder, including complex PTSD
    \item Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, which can mimic impulsivity
    \item Substance use disorders, which may produce emotional instability
    \item Other personality disorders with overlapping features
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone experiencing intense emotional distress, repeated relationship crises, or thoughts of self-harm should seek help from a GP, psychologist, or mental health service. A GP can arrange referral to a psychologist trained in DBT. People diagnosed with borderline personality disorder or related difficulties should ask about DBT as an evidence-based treatment option.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Immediate risk of self-harm or suicide
    \item Any crisis in which the person is in danger or unable to keep themselves safe
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Support lines:} Lifeline (13 11 14) and Suicide Call Back Service (1300 659 467) are available 24 hours.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Structured clinical interview:} assessment of mood, behaviour, relationships, and history of self-harm
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic assessment:} confirmation of borderline personality disorder or another indication for DBT
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} careful evaluation of suicide risk and safety planning
    \item \textbf{Baseline measures:} questionnaires tracking emotional dysregulation, self-harm, and quality of life
    \item \textbf{Medical review:} to exclude physical causes of mood disturbance where relevant
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Individual therapy:} weekly sessions addressing motivation, skills application, and obstacles to progress
    \item \textbf{Skills group:} weekly group teaching of mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness
    \item \textbf{Phone coaching:} between-session telephone support to help apply skills in real-time crises
    \item \textbf{Therapist consultation team:} DBT therapists meet regularly to maintain treatment fidelity and prevent burnout
    \item \textbf{Commitment strategies:} explicit agreements to work toward reducing life-threatening behaviours
    \item \textbf{Adjunctive treatment:} medication for co-occurring depression, anxiety, or insomnia, and treatment for substance use where present
    \item \textbf{Adapted DBT:} shorter or modified programmes for adolescents and for specific conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ongoing risk of self-harm and suicide if treatment is not accessed
    \item Drop-out from therapy, which can undermine progress
    \item Deterioration of relationships, employment, and physical health from chronic instability
    \item Misuse of emergency services during crises
    \item Co-occurring substance use, eating disorders, or depression complicating recovery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
DBT is one of the best-evidenced psychological treatments for borderline personality disorder. Randomised trials show significant reductions in self-harm, suicidal behaviour, hospital admissions, and emergency presentations. With sustained participation, typically over a year or more, most people develop lasting skills in managing emotions and relationships. Long-term follow-up studies show gradual improvement in functioning over years even after treatment ends, and many people go on to live stable and fulfilling lives.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Learn and practise mindfulness to observe emotions without being overwhelmed by them
    \item Build a "crisis survival kit" of distress-tolerance skills to use when urges arise
    \item Establish a regular routine of sleep, meals, and activity to stabilise mood
    \item Maintain a safety plan with trusted contacts and crisis numbers
    \item Communicate needs clearly and practise assertiveness in relationships
    \item Attend appointments consistently and complete skills homework
    \item Seek help early when distress rises, rather than waiting for a crisis
\end{itemize}
