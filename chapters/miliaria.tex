\chapter{Miliaria}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Miliaria, colloquially known as heat rash, prickly heat, or sweat rash, is a common, benign, and transient inflammatory skin condition caused by the occlusion of eccrine sweat ducts. This blockage leads to sweat retention within different layers of the skin, resulting in the formation of characteristic microvesicles or papules. Miliaria is classified into three major subtypes based on the anatomical depth of the ductal obstruction: miliaria crystallina (superficial obstruction within the stratum corneum), miliaria rubra (epidermal obstruction triggering localized inflammation, which is the most common form), and miliaria profunda (deep obstruction at the dermo-epidermal junction). The condition is primarily triggered by hot, humid environments, high fever, or physical exertion that induces profuse sweating.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Miliaria occurs globally but is highly prevalent in tropical, hot, and humid climates. It can affect individuals of any age, gender, or race, but is most common in neonates and infants due to the anatomical immaturity of their sweat ducts. In infants, the peak incidence is around one week of age. In adults, miliaria occurs frequently in military personnel, outdoor labourers, athletes, and individuals who travel abruptly from temperate to tropical climates, affecting up to 30\% of those exposed to sustained hot conditions. It is also common in hospitalised or bedbound patients who suffer from persistent fevers or who are wrapped in occlusive bedding.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The pathogenesis of miliaria involves mechanical obstruction of the sweat pore followed by leakage of sweat into the surrounding cutaneous layers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ductal immaturity:} Neonates have immature eccrine ducts that easily rupture when sweat volume increases, leading to superficial sweat extravasation.
    \item \textbf{Excessive sweating (hyperhidrosis):} High ambient temperatures, physical exertion, or fevers prompt continuous sweat production, which can saturate the stratum corneum, causing the cells to swell and block the sweat pore.
    \item \textbf{Bacterial biofilm:} Strains of the commensal bacterium \textit{Staphylococcus epidermidis} produce a sticky extracellular polysaccharide substance (biofilm) that physically plugs the distal eccrine duct.
    \item \textbf{Miliaria crystallina mechanism:} Duct obstruction occurs at the superficial stratum corneum, trapping sweat just beneath the skin surface, creating clear, non-inflammatory vesicles.
    \item \textbf{Miliaria rubra mechanism:} The block occurs in the deeper epidermis, causing sweat to leak into the epidermal tissue, which triggers a localized lymphocytic inflammatory response, presenting as red, pruritic papules.
    \item \textbf{Miliaria profunda mechanism:} Obstruction occurs deep in the dermis at the dermo-epidermal junction. Sweat leaks into the papillary dermis, forming firm, non-inflammatory, flesh-coloured papules.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features of miliaria are highly characteristic and vary depending on the depth of the sweat gland obstruction:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Miliaria crystallina features:} Tiny (1--2 mm), clear, dewdrop-like, superficial vesicles that are thin-walled and rupture easily. They are entirely asymptomatic, non-pruritic, and resolve with fine scaling. They are commonly located on the trunk, neck, and head of neonates.
    \item \textbf{Miliaria rubra features:} Small, uniform, erythematous papules and vesicles that do not cluster around hair follicles. They are accompanied by intense, distressing itching, stinging, or ``prickling'' sensations that worsen with sweating. Typical sites include the neck, upper chest, back, and intertriginous folds.
    \item \textbf{Miliaria profunda features:} Firm, non-pruritic, flesh-coloured papules (resembling goosebumps) that appear rapidly on the trunk and limbs after physical exertion and disappear within hours of cooling down.
    \item \textbf{Anhidrosis and heat intolerance:} In areas affected by miliaria profunda, sweating is reduced, which can impair systemic thermoregulation.
    \item \textbf{Miliaria pustulosa:} A variant of miliaria rubra where the vesicles become pustular due to localized inflammation, in the absence of active bacterial infection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Miliaria must be differentiated from other common neonatal and adult papulovesicular rashes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Folliculitis:} A bacterial or fungal infection of the hair follicles, distinguished from miliaria by pustules and papules that are strictly centered around hair shafts and are non-symmetrical.
    \item \textbf{Viral exanthems:} Systemic viral infections (such as varicella, enterovirus, or measles) causing vesicular rashes, distinguished by systemic symptoms (fever, malaise, coryza) and mucosal involvement.
    \item \textbf{Eczema (Atopic dermatitis):} A chronic, relapsing inflammatory skin disease characterized by dry, scaly, itchy plaques in typical flexural distributions, lacking the acute onset associated with heat or sweat.
    \item \textbf{Erythema toxicum neonatorum:} A benign neonatal rash presenting with yellow-white pustules on a broad erythematous base, appearing within the first 48 hours of life, containing eosinophils rather than sweat.
    \item \textbf{Neonatal cephalic pustulosis (Neonatal acne):} Inflammatory papules and pustules confined to the face and scalp, typically triggered by Malassezia species, whereas neonatal miliaria is usually on the trunk and neck.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most cases of heat rash resolve spontaneously with cooling measures. Patients or parents should seek medical advice if the rash persists for more than a few days, worsens, or if there are signs of secondary infection.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of heat exhaustion or heat stroke: high body temperature, confusion, dizziness, rapid pulse, rapid breathing, headache, and a lack of sweating despite extreme heat (often precipitated by extensive miliaria profunda).
    \item Loss of consciousness, seizures, or sudden collapse.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of miliaria is based on a clinical history of heat exposure, fever, or occlusive clothing, combined with the visual appearance of the rash:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical inspection:} Evaluating the morphology and distribution of the vesicles and papules, noting the absence of follicular localization.
    \item \textbf{Dermoscopy:} High-magnification inspection showing white, circular scales in miliaria crystallina, or red halos surrounding the eccrine duct openings in miliaria rubra.
    \item \textbf{Skin biopsy:} Rarely indicated, but histopathology will confirm the level of ductal obstruction, sweat duct dilatation, and localized lymphocytic infiltration, ruling out immunobullous diseases.
    \item \textbf{Microbiological swab:} Performed only if secondary bacterial superinfection is suspected due to weeping, crusting, or increased warmth.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The primary objective of management is to reduce sweating, cool the skin, and relieve pruritus:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Environmental cooling:} Transferring the patient to a cool, air-conditioned, or well-ventilated space to stop active sweat production.
    \item \textbf{Skin cooling:} Applying cool, damp compresses to the affected areas, or taking cool showers and letting the skin air-dry.
    \item \textbf{Topical soothing agents:} Application of calamine lotion, or lotions containing menthol or camphor, to alleviate the itching and stinging sensation.
    \item \textbf{Mild topical corticosteroids:} A brief course of 1\% hydrocortisone cream for severe, highly inflamed cases of miliaria rubra to reduce epidermal inflammation.
    \item \textbf{Oral antihistamines:} Non-sedating or sedating antihistamines (e.g. cetirizine, diphenhydramine) to control severe nocturnal itching and improve sleep quality.
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} Topical (e.g. mupirocin) or oral antibiotics (e.g. cephalexin) if the rash becomes secondarily infected with bacteria (impetiginised).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While generally mild, miliaria can lead to several complications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Secondary bacterial infection (superinfection):} Scratching due to severe pruritus breaks the skin barrier, introducing pathogens (usually \textit{Staphylococcus aureus}), leading to impetigo, folliculitis, or cellulitis.
    \item \textbf{Impaired thermoregulation and heat stroke:} Extensive, recurrent miliaria profunda blocks sweat output across a large surface area, preventing efficient evaporative cooling and predisposing the patient to heat exhaustion.
    \item \textbf{Anhidrosis (localised):} Temporary inability to sweat in the affected skin zones, which can persist for several weeks after the rash has cleared.
    \item \textbf{Compensatory hyperhidrosis:} Increased sweating in unaffected areas of the skin to compensate for the blocked eccrine glands.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for miliaria is excellent. Miliaria crystallina typically resolves within 24--48 hours once the patient is moved to a cool environment. Miliaria rubra resolves within 3 to 7 days of ceasing sweat stimulation. Miliaria profunda may take several weeks to clear, as the deep ductal plugs must be slowly exfoliated or reabsorbed. The skin recovers completely with no permanent scarring, pigmentary changes, or long-term damage, provided secondary infections are avoided.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventative measures focus on keeping the skin cool and preventing ductal occlusion:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Wear breathable clothing:} Choose loose-fitting, light-coloured garments made of natural fibres like cotton, or specialized moisture-wicking athletic fabrics. Avoid tight-fitting synthetic clothing.
    \item \textbf{Optimise indoor environments:} Use fans and air conditioning to keep the home, workplace, and bedroom cool and dry.
    \item \textbf{Avoid occlusive skin products:} Avoid heavy ointments, oils, greases, or thick sunscreens that can physically clog pores. Opt for light, water-based, non-comedogenic lotions.
    \item \textbf{Bathing habits:} Bathe in cool or lukewarm water. Use gentle, soap-free body washes, and pat the skin dry with a clean towel instead of rubbing.
    \item \textbf{Manage fevers:} Use antipyretics (such as paracetamol) to manage high fevers in ill or bedbound patients to reduce excessive sweat production.
\end{itemize}
