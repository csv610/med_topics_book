\chapter{Graves Disease}
\index{Graves Disease}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Graves disease is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system mistakenly attacks the thyroid gland, causing it to produce excessive amounts of thyroid hormone (hyperthyroidism). It is the most common cause of hyperthyroidism and affects the whole gland, causing a diffusely enlarged thyroid (goitre). Graves disease can also affect the eyes, causing bulging, dryness, and double vision (thyroid eye disease), and in some cases affects the skin of the shins. Symptoms develop because the excess thyroid hormone speeds up the body's metabolism. Treatment options include antithyroid medicines, radioactive iodine, and surgery, and most people respond well.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Graves disease is the most common cause of hyperthyroidism, accounting for 60--80\% of cases. It affects around 1--2\% of the population and is much more common in women, who are affected five to ten times more often than men. It typically develops between the ages of 20 and 50, though it can occur at any age, including in children and older adults. There is a strong genetic component, and people with a family history of thyroid or other autoimmune disease are at higher risk. Smoking increases the risk and severity of thyroid eye disease.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Graves disease is caused by antibodies that stimulate the thyroid gland to produce excess hormone.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Autoantibodies:} the immune system produces thyroid-stimulating antibodies (TSH receptor antibodies) that bind to the thyroid and switch it on continuously
    \item \textbf{Hyperthyroidism:} the gland produces excess thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), accelerating the body's metabolism
    \item \textbf{Goitre:} continuous stimulation causes the gland to enlarge diffusely
    \item \textbf{Thyroid eye disease:} antibodies and inflammation affect tissues behind the eyes, causing swelling of the eye muscles and fatty tissue, which pushes the eyes forward
    \item \textbf{Genetic susceptibility:} certain HLA genes increase risk
    \item \textbf{Triggers:} stress, infection, and the postpartum period can trigger onset in susceptible people
    \item \textbf{Thyroid storm:} a rare, life-threatening extreme of hyperthyroidism, often triggered by infection, surgery, or stopping treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms reflect the effects of excess thyroid hormone on the body.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Weight loss despite an increased appetite
    \item Rapid or irregular heartbeat, palpitations, and sometimes breathlessness
    \item Heat intolerance and excessive sweating
    \item Tremor of the hands
    \item Anxiety, irritability, and difficulty sleeping
    \item Fatigue and muscle weakness
    \item Frequent bowel movements
    \item Irregular or light periods in women
    \item A diffuse goitre, sometimes with a bruit (whooshing sound) over the gland
    \item Eye symptoms: bulging eyes, dry or gritty eyes, redness, double vision, and puffiness
    \item Skin changes: warm, moist skin, and occasionally pretibial myxoedema (thickened skin on the shins)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of hyperthyroidism and similar symptoms should be considered.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Toxic multinodular goitre, which causes hyperthyroidism without eye disease
    \item Toxic adenoma, a single overactive nodule
    \item Subacute thyroiditis and silent thyroiditis, which cause temporary hyperthyroidism from inflammation
    \item Anxiety and panic disorder
    \item Caffeine excess and stimulant use
    \item Pheochromocytoma and other causes of palpitations
    \item Weight loss and tremor from other causes, including malignancy
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor promptly for symptoms of hyperthyroidism, including unexplained weight loss, palpitations, tremor, anxiety, or a swelling in the neck. Women planning pregnancy should have thyroid function checked and treated before conceiving.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Thyroid storm: high fever, severe agitation or confusion, rapid heart rate, vomiting, and sweating, which is a life-threatening emergency
    \item Chest pain, severe breathlessness, or fainting
    \item Sudden loss of vision or severe eye pain in thyroid eye disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Graves disease is diagnosed with blood tests and sometimes imaging.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Thyroid function tests:} low TSH with high free T4 and T3 confirms hyperthyroidism
    \item \textbf{TSH receptor antibodies:} their presence confirms Graves disease
    \item \textbf{Thyroid scan:} a nuclear medicine scan showing increased uptake throughout the gland distinguishes Graves from thyroiditis
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} assesses the gland and nodules
    \item \textbf{Eye assessment:} examination by an ophthalmologist for thyroid eye disease, including measurement of eye protrusion
    \item \textbf{Other tests:} blood count, liver function, and bone density where relevant
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment aims to control thyroid hormone levels and manage complications.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antithyroid medicines:} carbimazole (or methimazole) blocks thyroid hormone production and is the usual first treatment; propylthiouracil is used in pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Beta-blockers:} propranolol or similar medicines quickly relieve palpitations, tremor, and anxiety while other treatment takes effect
    \item \textbf{Radioactive iodine:} destroys overactive thyroid tissue, usually leading to hypothyroidism that is then treated with thyroid hormone replacement
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} thyroidectomy for large goitres, thyroid eye disease concerns, or when other treatments are unsuitable
    \item \textbf{Eye disease management:} artificial tears, avoiding smoke, and for severe disease, steroids, radiation, or surgery to the eye socket
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular blood tests during treatment, and lifelong follow-up because hypothyroidism commonly follows definitive treatment
    \item \textbf{Thyroid storm:} emergency treatment in hospital with intensive medication and supportive care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Thyroid storm, a life-threatening crisis
    \item Cardiac complications: atrial fibrillation, heart failure, and high blood pressure
    \item Osteoporosis from long-term excess thyroid hormone
    \item Thyroid eye disease, which can cause vision loss if severe
    \item Fertility problems and pregnancy complications if untreated
    \item Side effects of antithyroid medicines, including rash, joint pain, liver injury, and rarely a severe drop in white blood cells (agranulocytosis)
    \item Hypothyroidism after radioactive iodine or surgery
    \item Effects on mental health, including anxiety and depression
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people with Graves disease do well. Around half of those treated with antithyroid medicines go into remission and can stop treatment, though many relapse and need definitive treatment with radioactive iodine or surgery. Both approaches control the disease in over 90\% of people, after which thyroid hormone replacement restores normal health. Thyroid eye disease improves in most people, though it can persist or worsen, and severe cases need specialist care. With appropriate treatment and follow-up, people with Graves disease lead normal, healthy lives.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take antithyroid medicines exactly as prescribed and attend blood test monitoring
    \item Report a sudden fever and sore throat immediately while on antithyroid medicines, as this can indicate a dangerous drop in white cells
    \item Stop smoking, as it worsens thyroid eye disease
    \item Protect the eyes with artificial tears and sunglasses if the eyes are affected
    \item Manage stress, which can trigger flares
    \item Limit caffeine, which can worsen palpitations
    \item Maintain a healthy diet and adequate calcium and vitamin D for bone health
    \item Women planning pregnancy should have thyroid function controlled first
    \item Attend lifelong follow-up, as the condition and its treatments affect thyroid function permanently
\end{itemize}
