\section*{Definition and Overview}
Muscle relaxation and stress-relief techniques are strategies that reduce physical tension and help regulate stress responses. They can support wellbeing and symptom management but do not replace assessment of persistent pain or a mental-health condition.

\section*{Techniques}
Options include slow diaphragmatic breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, stretching, mindfulness, guided imagery, yoga, regular physical activity, and a consistent sleep routine. Choose a comfortable technique and practise regularly.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical review for new severe pain, weakness, numbness, fever, injury, chest pain, or symptoms that persist. Seek urgent help for difficulty breathing, collapse, or thoughts of self-harm.

\section*{Safe Practice}
Relaxation should not cause sharp pain, dizziness, breath-holding, or worsening anxiety. People with significant injury, pregnancy-related concerns, or chronic disease should ask a clinician about suitable activity.

\section*{Self-Care}
Start gently, use reliable instruction, pace activity, reduce excess caffeine and alcohol, and combine relaxation with practical support for the source of stress. A GP or mental-health professional can help when stress impairs daily functioning.

\section*{Expanded clinical context}
Muscle relaxation and stress relief is a mental-health topic. Interpretation should account for age, baseline health, medicines, comorbidities, goals, and access to care. This chapter supports discussion with a qualified clinician and does not establish a diagnosis.

\section*{Assessment and decision points}
Assessment should consider onset, duration, triggers, sleep, substance use, physical health, developmental context, social stressors, and immediate safety. Ask about self-harm or harm from others when appropriate. Document the main concern, time course, functional impact, relevant risks, and red flags. Use targeted investigations when their result is likely to change management.

\section*{Management and follow-up}
Management may include education, psychological therapy, practical support, treatment of coexisting conditions, medication when indicated, and an agreed follow-up or safety plan. Explain expected improvement, when reassessment is needed, and how follow-up can be accessed. Avoid starting, stopping, or sharing prescription medicines without professional advice.

\section*{Safety-netting}
Seek urgent help for immediate danger, suicidal intent, psychosis, severe confusion, inability to meet basic needs, or rapidly worsening symptoms.

\section*{Practical prevention}
Use plain-language information, supportive relationships, regular routines, and professional review when symptoms persist or impair daily life. Keep written instructions and seek review if the topic recurs, changes, or does not improve as expected.

\section*{A simple starting plan}
Choose one technique, practise for five minutes once or twice daily, and record whether it changes tension, sleep, or distress. Increase duration gradually rather than forcing a stretch or breath.
