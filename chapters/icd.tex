\chapter{Implantable Cardioverter Defibrillator (ICD)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) is a small, battery-powered device placed under the skin of the chest that continuously monitors the heart's rhythm and delivers an electric shock to restore a normal heartbeat if a dangerously fast or disorganised rhythm occurs. ICDs are used to treat life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias, such as ventricular tachycardia and ventricular fibrillation, which can cause sudden cardiac arrest. The device also acts as a pacemaker, pacing the heart if it beats too slowly, and can record information about episodes for review by the treating team. ICDs are inserted either for primary prevention, in people at high risk of future dangerous arrhythmias, or for secondary prevention, in people who have already survived a cardiac arrest or dangerous arrhythmia.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Sudden cardiac death accounts for a substantial proportion of deaths from heart disease worldwide, and ventricular arrhythmias are the underlying mechanism in most cases. Around 20,000--30,000 Australians experience a cardiac arrest each year, and the majority occur outside hospital. ICDs have been shown in large clinical trials to reduce the risk of sudden cardiac death by around 30--50\% in selected high-risk populations, including people with significantly reduced heart pumping function and those who have survived a prior cardiac arrest. Implantation rates in Australia have risen steadily over recent decades as evidence for the devices has grown and the population has aged, and thousands of devices are now implanted each year.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Dangerous ventricular arrhythmias arise from disturbances in the heart's electrical system, most commonly in the setting of structural heart disease. Coronary artery disease with prior heart attacks produces scar tissue that can disrupt the orderly spread of electrical impulses, creating circuits that sustain rapid rhythms. Cardiomyopathies, including dilated, hypertrophic, and arrhythmogenic forms, also create an arrhythmic substrate, as do inherited ion channel disorders such as long QT syndrome and Brugada syndrome. The ICD works by continuously sensing the rhythm and, when a life-threatening arrhythmia is detected, delivering either antitachycardia pacing (a rapid burst of pacing impulses that can terminate some tachycardias painlessly) or a high-energy shock that depolarises the entire heart and allows the normal pacemaker to resume control.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item People who have survived a cardiac arrest caused by ventricular fibrillation or tachycardia
    \item People with significantly reduced heart function (ejection fraction below around 35\%) after a heart attack or with heart failure
    \item People with inherited conditions that predispose to sudden cardiac death, such as long QT syndrome, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, or Brugada syndrome
    \item Recurrent fainting episodes caused by documented ventricular arrhythmias
    \item The device itself causes no symptoms when functioning normally, apart from a small surgical scar
    \item Patients may feel a thumping sensation during antitachycardia pacing or a sudden shock during therapy delivery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The decision to implant an ICD depends on careful risk assessment for the individual patient.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Medical therapy alone:} beta-blockers, amiodarone, and other antiarrhythmic medicines in people at lower risk
    \item \textbf{Cardiac resynchronisation therapy:} a special pacemaker that coordinates heart contractions in some heart failure patients, sometimes combined with ICD function (CRT-D)
    \item \textbf{Subcutaneous ICD (S-ICD):} an alternative device that avoids leads inside the heart in selected patients
    \item \textbf{Wearable defibrillator:} a temporary external vest used while a permanent decision is being made
    \item \textbf{Treating the underlying cause:} coronary revascularisation or treatment of heart failure may reduce arrhythmia risk in some people
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with unexplained fainting, palpitations with collapse, or a family history of sudden cardiac death should be assessed by a cardiologist. People with an ICD who experience a shock should contact their device clinic as arranged; a single shock that restores normal rhythm and makes the person feel well is often managed with a planned review, but multiple shocks or symptoms after a shock require urgent attention.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A person with an ICD who is unconscious or unresponsive, even if the device may have fired
    \item Multiple shocks in a short period
    \item Chest pain, severe breathlessness, or dizziness after a shock
    \item Any signs of cardiac arrest: no response and no normal breathing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment before implantation aims to establish the underlying diagnosis and quantify arrhythmia risk.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Electrocardiogram (ECG):} to document baseline rhythm, conduction disease, and features of inherited conditions
    \item \textbf{Echocardiography:} to measure heart function (ejection fraction) and detect structural abnormalities
    \item \textbf{Ambulatory ECG monitoring:} Holter monitors or event recorders to capture symptomatic arrhythmias
    \item \textbf{Exercise stress testing:} to provoke arrhythmias and assess ischaemia
    \item \textbf{Cardiac MRI:} for detailed assessment of scar and cardiomyopathy type
    \item \textbf{Electrophysiology study:} invasive testing of the heart's electrical pathways in selected cases
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} for family members when an inherited arrhythmia syndrome is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Implantation procedure:} performed under local anaesthesia with sedation, usually with the device placed beneath the skin below the collarbone and leads advanced into the heart
    \item \textbf{Device programming:} tailored to detect and treat arrhythmias while avoiding inappropriate shocks
    \item \textbf{Ongoing optimisation of heart failure therapy:} medicines such as beta-blockers and ACE inhibitors reduce arrhythmia risk
    \item \textbf{Regular device checks:} every 3--12 months, either in clinic or remotely, to review battery life, lead function, and recorded episodes
    \item \textbf{Battery replacement:} a minor procedure every 5--10 years depending on device use
    \item \textbf{Shock awareness training:} patients learn what to do if the device fires
    \item \textbf{Driving restrictions:} temporary licence restrictions apply after implantation or after a shock
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Surgical complications: bleeding, infection, pneumothorax, or damage to blood vessels at implantation
    \item Lead problems: fracture, displacement, or malfunction, which can cause false detection or failure to deliver therapy
    \item Inappropriate shocks: delivery of therapy for rhythms that did not require a shock, which can be distressing and painful
    \item Device-related infections, which may require removal of the entire system
    \item Psychological effects: anxiety, fear of shocks, and reduced quality of life in some patients
    \item Battery depletion requiring eventual replacement
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
ICDs are highly effective at terminating life-threatening arrhythmias and substantially reduce the risk of sudden cardiac death in appropriately selected patients. The device does not cure the underlying heart disease, which continues to require medical treatment and follow-up. Most people adapt well to living with an ICD and return to a full and active life, including work, sport (with some contact-sport restrictions), and travel. Ongoing device surveillance and psychological support are important components of long-term care.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend all scheduled device checks and keep the device clinic informed of any changes in symptoms
    \item Carry the ICD identification card at all times and wear a medical alert bracelet
    \item Keep mobile phones and strong magnets away from the device site, and avoid lying with the device directly against a charging phone
    \item Inform all health professionals, including dentists, about the device before procedures
    \item Avoid heavy contact sports and activities that risk a blow to the chest
    \item Follow driving restrictions advised by the treating team and state authorities
    \item Manage heart disease risk factors and take prescribed medicines as directed
    \item Seek psychological support if anxiety about the device is affecting daily life
\end{itemize}
