\chapter{Inserting an Intrauterine Contraceptive Device (IUD)}
\index{Inserting an Intrauterine Contraceptive Device (IUD)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An intrauterine contraceptive device (IUD) is a small, T-shaped device placed inside the uterus for long-acting contraception. There are two types: the copper IUD, which prevents pregnancy without hormones and can be left in place for up to 10 years, and the hormonal IUD (Mirena and similar), which releases a low dose of progestogen and is also an effective treatment for heavy periods, remaining effective for up to 5 to 8 years. Insertion is performed by a doctor or nurse with a speculum, takes only a few minutes, and is usually done in a clinic, sometimes with local anaesthetic. IUDs are among the most effective reversible contraceptives, with failure rates below one per cent.

\section*{Epidemiology}
IUD use has increased substantially in many countries over the past two decades, particularly among younger women, as long-acting reversible contraception has been recognised as the most effective and cost-effective reversible method. Around one in ten women using contraception currently uses an IUD. The hormonal IUD is used both for contraception and as a first-line treatment for heavy menstrual bleeding. IUDs are suitable for women of most ages and parities, and use in women who have not had children has become common, with modern devices designed for smaller uterine cavities.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The copper IUD prevents pregnancy mainly by releasing copper ions that are toxic to sperm, preventing fertilisation, and by altering the uterine environment so implantation is unlikely. The hormonal IUD thickens cervical mucus, blocking sperm, thins the uterine lining so implantation is less likely, and reduces menstrual bleeding. The device is placed in the uterine cavity with threads protruding through the cervix so that the woman and clinician can check it is in place. Insertion involves measuring the uterine cavity with a sound before passing the device through the cervical canal. The uterus adapts to the device, and in most women the risk of pelvic infection after insertion is low because the procedure is sterile.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Insertion takes about five minutes in a clinic, with cramping during and after
    \item Local anaesthetic may be offered, especially for women who have not had children
    \item Cramping and light bleeding are common for a few days after insertion
    \item The hormonal IUD causes lighter periods, and many women become amenorrhoeic
    \item The copper IUD may cause heavier or longer periods, especially in the first months
    \item The device provides immediate contraception, or from the time of fitting
    \item Threads can be felt in the upper vagina to check the device is in place
    \item Follow-up after three to six weeks confirms correct placement
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The choice of IUD and its suitability are assessed against alternatives and risks.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Other long-acting methods:} the contraceptive implant, another highly effective option
    \item \textbf{Combined hormonal methods:} pill, patch, and ring
    \item \textbf{Progestogen-only options:} pills and injections
    \item \textbf{Barrier methods:} condoms and diaphragms
    \item \textbf{Sterilisation:} permanent options for those who have completed their family
    \item \textbf{Treatment of heavy periods:} the hormonal IUD versus other medical and surgical treatments
    \item \textbf{Contraindications:} current pelvic infection, undiagnosed abnormal bleeding, and some uterine abnormalities
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Women considering an IUD should have a full discussion of the options, benefits, and risks with a doctor or nurse. Medical review is needed for pain, abnormal discharge, fever, a missed period after insertion, or threads that cannot be felt. A woman who suspects the device has moved or been expelled should use back-up contraception and seek review.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal or pelvic pain after insertion
    \item Heavy bleeding with faintness or dizziness
    \item High fever, which may indicate pelvic infection
    \item A positive pregnancy test with pain or bleeding, which may indicate ectopic pregnancy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Consultation:} contraceptive history, pregnancy test, and discussion of options
    \item \textbf{Examination:} checking the cervix and uterus, and screening for infection if needed
    \item \textbf{Insertion:} speculum examination, uterine measurement, and placement of the device
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} to confirm correct position if threads are missing or symptoms suggest displacement
    \item \textbf{Follow-up visit:} at three to six weeks to check threads and symptoms
    \item \textbf{Screening:} cervical screening and sexually transmissible infection testing as indicated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple analgesics and heat packs for cramping after insertion
    \item \textbf{Local anaesthetic:} offered to reduce discomfort during insertion
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} for women at risk of pelvic infection, or treatment of infection before insertion
    \item \textbf{Checking threads:} taught to the woman, and checked at follow-up
    \item \textbf{Managing side effects:} review of bleeding patterns and cramps, with reassurance or adjustment
    \item \textbf{Removal:} performed easily by a clinician at any time, with return of fertility immediately
    \item \textbf{Replacement:} scheduled according to the device type
    \item \textbf{Continuing care:} annual reviews and cervical screening as recommended
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cramping and pain, especially during and just after insertion
    \item Bleeding changes: spotting, heavier or lighter periods
    \item Expulsion of the device, most often in the first year
    \item Perforation of the uterus, rare, and usually recognised at insertion
    \item Pelvic infection, mainly in the weeks after insertion
    \item Pregnancy with the device in place, which is uncommon and requires careful management
    \item Missing threads, requiring assessment and ultrasound
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
IUDs are more than 99 per cent effective, matching sterilisation, and their effectiveness does not depend on remembering to take a pill. Hormonal IUDs also cure or greatly improve heavy periods in most women, often avoiding surgery. Side effects settle in most women within the first months, and continuation rates are high, with many women keeping the device for its full life. Fertility returns immediately on removal. For most women, the IUD is a safe, convenient, and highly effective choice.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discuss all contraceptive options to find the best fit
    \item Have insertion performed by an experienced clinician, with local anaesthetic if needed
    \item Take simple analgesics and rest after insertion if cramping
    \item Use back-up contraception as advised until the device is effective
    \item Check the threads monthly, especially after a period
    \item Report pain, fever, discharge, or a missed period promptly
    \item Attend the follow-up appointment
    \item Keep track of when the device needs replacing
    \item Know that you can have the device removed at any time
\end{itemize}
