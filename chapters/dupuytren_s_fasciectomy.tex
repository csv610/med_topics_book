\chapter{Dupuytren's Fasciectomy}
\index{Dupuytren's Fasciectomy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A fasciectomy is an operation to remove the thickened, cord-like tissue in the palm that causes Dupuytren's contracture. In this procedure, a hand surgeon makes an incision in the palm and finger and carefully excises the diseased palmar fascia, allowing the finger to straighten. Fasciectomy is the most definitive treatment for Dupuytren's contracture and is recommended when the contracture has become significant enough to interfere with hand function. This chapter explains what the operation involves, how to prepare, what to expect during recovery, and the possible risks.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Dupuytren's contracture affects a substantial minority of adults of northern European descent, with the condition becoming more common with age and affecting men more than women. A significant proportion of people with the condition develop contractures severe enough to consider surgery, and fasciectomy remains one of the most common hand operations for the condition, despite the availability of less invasive techniques. Recurrence after surgery is not uncommon, particularly in people with early-onset or severe disease.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Dupuytren's contracture results from the abnormal proliferation of fibrous tissue in the palmar fascia, the connective tissue layer beneath the skin of the palm. Contracting cords of this tissue pull the fingers into flexion. The underlying process involves genetic predisposition, ageing, and factors such as diabetes and smoking. Surgery addresses the mechanical problem by removing the offending tissue, restoring the finger to a straighter position.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features of Dupuytren's contracture that lead to consideration of fasciectomy include the following.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A firm cord running from the palm to the affected finger, most often the ring or little finger
    \item Progressive inability to straighten the finger, often measured as the angle of contracture
    \item Difficulty with daily tasks such as placing the hand flat, washing the face, or gripping large objects
    \item Discomfort or an unsightly appearance of the hand
    \item A positive table-top test, in which the hand cannot be placed flat on a table surface
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The decision to operate is based on the diagnosis of Dupuytren's contracture, which should be distinguished from other conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Trigger finger and other tendon problems
    \item Post-traumatic scarring or contracture
    \item Congenital contractures and camptodactyly
    \item Arthritis of the finger joints
    \item Ganglion cysts or other benign hand tumours
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Referral to a hand surgeon is appropriate when the contracture affects function, when the finger is bent significantly, or when the condition is progressing. Surgery is usually offered when the contracture at the knuckle joint exceeds about 30 degrees or when the finger begins to interfere with daily activities.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe pain, swelling, or redness in the hand after surgery, which may indicate infection or a nerve problem
    \item Sudden numbness, weakness, or coldness in the operated hand
    \item Bleeding that does not stop with pressure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Before surgery, the hand is assessed in detail.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} mapping the cords and measuring the contracture at each joint
    \item \textbf{Table-top test:} assessment of the functional impact
    \item \textbf{Preoperative assessment:} general health review, including blood tests where needed and discussion of anaesthesia options
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} usually not required, though ultrasound may be used in some centres
    \item \textbf{Informed consent:} detailed discussion of the procedure, alternatives, and risks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Fasciectomy is performed under local, regional, or general anaesthesia and usually takes one to two hours.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Incision:} the surgeon makes an incision in the palm and finger, often with Z-plasty or other skin-closing techniques to allow the finger to lengthen
    \item \textbf{Excision:} the diseased fascia and cords are carefully dissected and removed, taking care to preserve the nerves and blood vessels
    \item \textbf{Closure:} the wound is closed, often with a drain to prevent blood collecting
    \item \textbf{Dressing and splint:} a dressing and sometimes a splint are applied to protect the hand
    \item \textbf{Recovery:} the hand is elevated, pain is managed, and dressings are changed over the following days
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} hand therapy exercises begin early to maintain the gained movement
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding and haematoma formation, which can delay healing
    \item Infection of the wound
    \item Nerve injury, causing numbness or tingling in the fingers
    \item Stiffness and swelling of the hand, which usually improves with therapy
    \item Skin loss or wound healing problems, more common in smokers
    \item Complex regional pain syndrome, a rare but serious cause of persistent pain
    \item Recurrence of the contracture over the long term
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Fasciectomy is highly effective at straightening the finger, with the majority of patients achieving a good functional result that lasts for many years. The hand may take several weeks to recover, and scar tissue can cause some residual stiffness. Recurrence of Dupuytren's disease is common in the long term, but repeat treatment is possible. With hand therapy and time, most people return to normal activities.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Stop smoking before surgery, as smoking significantly impairs healing
    \item Follow the surgeon's instructions about elevation, dressings, and wound care
    \item Complete the prescribed physiotherapy programme to maximise movement
    \item Attend follow-up appointments to monitor healing and detect recurrence early
    \item Use good scar management, including massage and moisturiser once the wound has healed
\end{itemize}
