\chapter{Hypoglycemia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hypoglycemia, the American spelling of hypoglycaemia, is the condition of abnormally low blood glucose, usually below 4 mmol/L. It occurs most commonly in people being treated for diabetes, but it can also affect people without diabetes in certain circumstances. The brain depends on glucose for fuel, so low blood sugar quickly causes symptoms ranging from sweating and tremor to confusion, seizures, and unconsciousness. Prompt treatment with fast-acting carbohydrate reverses symptoms, and most episodes are preventable with education and careful management.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hypoglycemia is the most frequent acute complication of diabetes treatment. Most people with type 1 diabetes and many with type 2 diabetes taking insulin or sulfonylureas experience episodes, and severe episodes are reported by a significant proportion each year. Nocturnal and driving-related hypoglycemia are particular safety concerns. In people without diabetes, hypoglycemia is uncommon; when it occurs, it is usually related to medicines, alcohol, or rare medical conditions.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Excess insulin:} doses that are too high or mistimed
    \item \textbf{Diabetes medicines:} sulfonylureas and related drugs
    \item \textbf{Inadequate food:} skipped or small meals
    \item \textbf{Exercise:} unplanned or strenuous activity
    \item \textbf{Alcohol:} inhibits glucose production, especially without food
    \item \textbf{Illness and kidney or liver disease}
    \item \textbf{Without diabetes:} medication effects, insulin-producing tumours, and hormonal deficiencies
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} glucose supply falls below brain requirements, impairing neurological function
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Autonomic symptoms: sweating, tremor, palpitations, and hunger
    \item Neuroglycopenic symptoms: confusion, drowsiness, blurred vision, and difficulty speaking
    \item Headache, weakness, and irritability
    \item Behavioural changes that may be mistaken for intoxication
    \item Seizures and unconsciousness in severe cases
    \item Night sweats and morning headaches with overnight lows
    \item Symptoms are relieved quickly by glucose
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seizures, stroke, and other neurological events
    \item Intoxication and drug effects
    \item Panic attacks, which mimic the autonomic symptoms
    \item Sepsis and other acute illness
    \item Blood glucose measurement is diagnostic
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with diabetes should treat episodes immediately and seek review for frequent or severe hypoglycemia. Unexplained episodes in people without diabetes need assessment.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unconsciousness or seizures in a person with diabetes
    \item Inability to swallow or treat oneself
    \item Confusion that does not improve after glucose
    \item Any collapse where the cause is unknown
    \item Repeated severe episodes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood glucose test:} confirms the diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Glucose monitors:} identify patterns and lows
    \item \textbf{Treatment review:} insulin and medicines are reviewed
    \item \textbf{HbA1c and glucose records}
    \item \textbf{Without diabetes:} fasting glucose, insulin, and other tests for rare causes
    \item \textbf{Whipple's triad:} low glucose, symptoms, and relief with glucose
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immediate treatment:} 15 g fast-acting carbohydrate, recheck in 15 minutes, repeat as needed
    \item \textbf{Follow-up food:} a snack with complex carbohydrate after recovery
    \item \textbf{Severe episodes:} glucagon injection, or intravenous glucose by emergency services
    \item \textbf{Medicine adjustment:} dose reduction with medical guidance
    \item \textbf{Education:} recognition, prevention, and sick-day planning
    \item \textbf{Technology:} continuous glucose monitoring and insulin pumps reduce episodes
    \item \textbf{Driving advice:} check before driving and treat promptly
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Injury from falls and seizures
    \item Motor vehicle accidents
    \item Hypoglycemia unawareness after repeated episodes
    \item Cognitive effects in severe or recurrent cases
    \item Cardiac events in vulnerable people
    \item Fear and anxiety about episodes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Hypoglycemia is usually fully reversed with prompt treatment, and most people recover within minutes. Prevention through education, monitoring, and treatment adjustment markedly reduces episodes. Severe hypoglycemia is largely preventable, and modern technology has made it rarer. The outlook for people who manage their glucose levels carefully is excellent.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Carry fast-acting glucose at all times
    \item Monitor glucose regularly, including before driving
    \item Eat regular meals and coordinate food, activity, and medicine
    \item Limit alcohol and never drink on an empty stomach
    \item Learn your warning symptoms
    \item Educate family, friends, and colleagues
    \item Wear a medical alert if episodes are severe
    \item Review treatment regularly with your diabetes team
\end{itemize}
