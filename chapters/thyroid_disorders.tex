\medskip
\noindent\textit{Educational reference; always seek professional advice for individual care.}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Thyroid disorders are conditions that alter the structure or function of the thyroid gland, a butterfly-shaped endocrine organ at the front of the neck that produces thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3). These hormones regulate metabolic rate, heart function, temperature control, mood, and growth. Disorders fall broadly into underactivity (hypothyroidism), overactivity (hyperthyroidism and thyrotoxicosis), thyroiditis (inflammation), nodule and goitre disease, and thyroid cancer. Autoimmune disease---Hashimoto thyroiditis and Graves disease---dominates in iodine-replete countries such as some countries. Subclinical laboratory abnormalities are common and need careful interpretation. Pregnancy, medicines (including amiodarone and lithium), iodine exposure, and pituitary disease add complexity. This chapter offers an integrated overview of major thyroid disorders for patients and carers, with cross-cutting themes of testing, treatment, and when to seek help.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Thyroid disease is several times more common in women than men and increases with age. Hypothyroidism, often from Hashimoto thyroiditis, is one of the most frequent endocrine diagnoses in primary care. Graves disease is the leading cause of hyperthyroidism in younger women; toxic nodules become more important with age. Gestational thyroid test changes require trimester-specific interpretation. Thyroid nodules are extremely common on ultrasound; only a minority are malignant. Differentiated thyroid cancers have risen in incidence partly due to detection, with generally favourable outcomes when treated appropriately. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander health services manage thyroid disease within broader chronic care; iodine nutrition in many countries is generally adequate owing to fortification, though individual diets vary.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune hypothyroidism (Hashimoto):} lymphocytic destruction and antibody-mediated impairment of hormone synthesis
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune hyperthyroidism (Graves):} TSH-receptor stimulating antibodies drive hormone overproduction and can cause orbitopathy
    \item \textbf{Nodular autonomy:} mutations in thyroid follicular cells create TSH-independent toxic adenomas or multinodular goitres
    \item \textbf{Thyroiditis phases:} stored hormone leak causes transient thyrotoxicosis then hypothyroidism (for example post-viral, postpartum)
    \item \textbf{Iatrogenic and drug causes:} radioiodine, thyroidectomy, amiodarone, lithium, immune checkpoint inhibitors, excess iodine
    \item \textbf{Central hypothyroidism:} pituitary or hypothalamic disease with low TSH and low free T4
    \item \textbf{Malignancy pathways:} papillary, follicular, medullary (including MEN syndromes), and anaplastic cancers behave differently
    \item \textbf{Feedback axis:} pituitary TSH normally rises when T4 falls and falls when T4 is high---patterns guide diagnosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hypothyroid pattern:} fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin, hair loss, menorrhagia, low mood, bradycardia, myxoedema features when severe
    \item \textbf{Hyperthyroid pattern:} heat intolerance, weight loss, tremor, palpitations, anxiety, diarrhoea, proximal weakness, insomnia, atrial fibrillation risk
    \item \textbf{Goitre and neck pressure:} visible swelling, dysphagia, or cosmetic concern
    \item \textbf{Graves orbitopathy:} gritty eyes, stare, double vision, proptosis
    \item \textbf{Thyroid pain:} favours subacute thyroiditis over Graves
    \item \textbf{Nodule red flags:} hard fixed mass, rapid growth, cervical nodes, voice change
    \item \textbf{Myxoedema coma and thyroid storm:} rare life-threatening extremes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Depression, anaemia, and menopause versus hypothyroidism:} blood tests distinguish
    \item \textbf{Anxiety disorder versus hyperthyroidism:} examine for goitre, tremor, and order TFT
    \item \textbf{Graves versus toxic nodule versus thyroiditis:} antibody tests, uptake scanning, and ESR/clinical pain help
    \item \textbf{Central versus primary hypothyroidism:} TSH inappropriate for low free T4
    \item \textbf{Non-thyroidal illness:} sick euthyroid patterns in hospitalised patients
    \item \textbf{Pharyngeal and cervical masses:} thyroglossal cyst, lymphadenopathy, parathyroid disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a GP for persistent fatigue, unexplained weight change, palpitations, neck swelling, or menstrual change. Ask for thyroid tests when symptoms fit or when you have autoimmune disease, type 1 diabetes, postpartum symptoms, or relevant medicines.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe breathlessness, collapse, chest pain, or irregular fast heartbeat with thyrotoxic features
    \item Confusion, hypothermia, or extreme lethargy suggesting myxoedema crisis
    \item Fever, delirium, and severe thyrotoxic signs suggesting storm
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{TSH first-line:} with free T4 (and sometimes free T3) to classify overt and subclinical states
    \item \textbf{Thyroid antibodies:} TPO, thyroglobulin antibodies, TSH-receptor antibodies as indicated
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} nodule characterisation using structured systems (for example TIRADS-style approaches)
    \item \textbf{Fine-needle aspiration cytology:} for nodules meeting size and appearance criteria
    \item \textbf{Nuclear uptake scan:} distinguishes causes of thyrotoxicosis when needed
    \item \textbf{ECG:} atrial fibrillation screening in hyperthyroidism
    \item \textbf{Specialist bloods:} calcitonin, CEA, genetic testing in medullary pathways; pituitary panel if central disease suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Levothyroxine replacement:} cornerstone of primary hypothyroidism; dose titrated to TSH (or free T4 in central disease)
    \item \textbf{Antithyroid drugs:} carbimazole or propylthiouracil for Graves, with blood count and liver caution education
    \item \textbf{Beta-blockers:} symptom control of tremor and tachycardia
    \item \textbf{Radioiodine and surgery:} definitive options for Graves and toxic nodules after shared decision
    \item \textbf{Thyroiditis:} anti-inflammatory analgesia; temporary levothyroxine if hypothyroid phase is symptomatic; usually no antithyroid drugs for leak thyrotoxicosis
    \item \textbf{Nodule follow-up or surgery:} based on cytology and compressive symptoms
    \item \textbf{Thyroid cancer pathways:} surgery, radioiodine remnant ablation when indicated, TSH suppression strategies, and thyroglobulin surveillance
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy care:} early endocrine/obstetric collaboration; PTU versus carbimazole timing rules; dose rises in hypothyroid pregnancy often needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Atrial fibrillation, osteoporosis, and embolism risk:} untreated hyperthyroidism
    \item \textbf{Myxoedema coma and heart failure:} severe hypothyroidism
    \item \textbf{Agranulocytosis and liver injury:} rare antithyroid drug effects---sore throat with fever needs urgent FBC
    \item \textbf{Orbitopathy progression:} smoking worsens Graves eye disease
    \item \textbf{Recurrent laryngeal nerve and hypoparathyroidism risks:} after thyroidectomy
    \item \textbf{Cancer-specific risks:} recurrence, distant spread in higher-risk histologies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people with autoimmune hypo- or hyperthyroidism achieve good control and normal life expectancy with adherence and monitoring. Subclinical disease may be observed or treated based on TSH level, antibodies, symptoms, pregnancy plans, and heart risk. Differentiated thyroid cancer prognosis is excellent for many low-risk cases. Delayed treatment of extremes (storm, coma) still carries high mortality, underscoring emergency recognition.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take thyroid medicines consistently; separate levothyroxine from calcium, iron, and some foods as advised
    \item Do not stop carbimazole suddenly without advice
    \item Stop smoking, especially with Graves disease
    \item Attend scheduled blood tests after dose changes (usually six to eight weeks)
    \item Tell clinicians you have thyroid disease before contrast iodine, amiodarone, lithium, or pregnancy
    \item Seek urgent care for fever and sore throat on antithyroid drugs
    \item Use iodised salt as part of a normal diet unless your specialist gives different iodine advice (for example some nuclear medicine pathways)
\end{itemize}
