\section*{Definition and Overview}
The circulatory system (also called the cardiovascular system) is the body's transport network. It consists of the heart, the blood vessels (arteries, veins, and capillaries), and the blood itself. The heart acts as a pump that pushes blood around the body through two circuits: the pulmonary circuit, which carries blood to the lungs to collect oxygen, and the systemic circuit, which delivers oxygen and nutrients to the tissues and removes carbon dioxide and other waste products.

The system also distributes hormones, helps regulate body temperature, and works with the immune system to fight infection. When any part of this network is disrupted, blood flow to organs can be reduced, leading to damage of the heart, brain, kidneys, or limbs.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Diseases of the circulatory system are the leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for roughly one in three deaths, mainly through heart attacks and stroke. Coronary heart disease and cerebrovascular disease are the two most common forms. The risk rises with age, and men tend to develop circulatory disease at an earlier age than women, although risk in women rises sharply after the menopause.

Major risk factors are very common in the community: around one in three adults has high blood pressure, and elevated cholesterol, diabetes, smoking, physical inactivity, and obesity each affect large numbers of people. The burden is falling in some high-income countries thanks to better prevention and treatment, but rising in many low- and middle-income countries.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Most circulatory disease develops through a process called \textbf{atherosclerosis}, in which fatty deposits, cholesterol, and inflammatory cells build up as plaques inside artery walls.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Atherosclerosis:} plaques narrow the arteries and reduce blood flow; a plaque can rupture, triggering a blood clot that blocks the artery and causes a heart attack or stroke
    \item \textbf{Hypertension:} chronically raised pressure damages artery walls and the heart muscle, and accelerates atherosclerosis
    \item \textbf{Valve disease:} heart valves stiffen (e.g.\ age-related degeneration) or leak (e.g.\ past rheumatic fever), impairing the heart's pumping efficiency
    \item \textbf{Arrhythmias:} disturbances of the heart's electrical system, such as atrial fibrillation, causing an irregular heart rhythm
    \item \textbf{Heart failure:} the heart's ability to pump blood weakens, so fluid backs up in the lungs, legs, and abdomen
    \item \textbf{Venous disease:} damaged leg vein valves cause blood pooling (varicose veins, chronic venous insufficiency), and blood clots can form (deep vein thrombosis)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on which part of the circulation is affected, and many people with early circulatory disease have no symptoms at all.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, tightness, or pressure on exertion (angina)
    \item Shortness of breath, especially with activity or when lying flat
    \item Palpitations -- awareness of an irregular or racing heartbeat
    \item Pain or cramping in the calf on walking (claudication), relieved by rest
    \item Leg swelling (oedema), heaviness, or varicose veins
    \item Coldness, pallor, or colour change in the hands or feet
    \item Fainting or near-fainting, dizziness, or fatigue
    \item A silent elevation of blood pressure is usually symptomless until late
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Symptoms such as chest pain and breathlessness have many causes outside the circulation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain: reflux (heartburn), musculoskeletal pain, anxiety or panic attacks, shingles
    \item Breathlessness: lung disease (asthma, COPD, pneumonia), anaemia, anxiety, poor fitness
    \item Palpitations: anxiety, thyroid overactivity, caffeine or stimulant use, anaemia
    \item Leg swelling: kidney or liver disease, leg vein problems, medication side effects, lymphoedema
    \item Leg pain on walking: nerve compression (e.g.\ sciatica), arthritis, musculoskeletal strain
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor promptly if you have new, persistent, or worsening chest pain, breathlessness, palpitations, leg swelling, or leg pain on walking, or if you are diagnosed with risk factors such as high blood pressure or high cholesterol.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe chest pain, pressure, or discomfort lasting more than a few minutes
    \item Sudden difficulty breathing or breathlessness at rest
    \item Signs of stroke (FAST: Face, Arms, Speech, Time to contact local emergency services)
    \item Collapse, loss of consciousness, or an irregular rapid heartbeat with dizziness
    \item A sudden cold, pale, painful leg with no pulse (possible acute blockage)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of circulatory disease begins with a careful history and examination, including blood pressure measurement and feeling the pulses in the limbs.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} cholesterol and glucose levels, kidney function, and troponin (a marker of heart muscle damage) in suspected heart attack
    \item \textbf{Electrocardiogram (ECG):} records the heart's electrical activity and detects arrhythmias, strain, or damage
    \item \textbf{Echocardiogram:} ultrasound of the heart to assess pumping strength and valve function
    \item \textbf{Exercise stress testing:} ECG or imaging while exercising to detect reduced blood supply to the heart
    \item \textbf{CT coronary angiography or coronary angiography:} X-ray imaging to visualise narrowing of the coronary arteries
    \item \textbf{Ankle-brachial index and leg ultrasound:} to assess blood flow and detect deep vein thrombosis or blockages in the legs
    \item \textbf{Ambulatory monitoring:} a portable ECG worn for 24--72 hours to capture intermittent palpitations
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment aims to relieve symptoms, slow the progression of disease, and prevent heart attacks and stroke. It almost always includes lifestyle change and often involves medicines and, in some cases, procedures.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle change:} healthy diet, regular exercise, weight loss, stopping smoking, and limiting alcohol
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} cholesterol-lowering drugs (statins), blood pressure tablets, antiplatelet agents (such as low-dose aspirin) and anticoagulants to reduce clot risk, and medicines to control heart rate or rhythm
    \item \textbf{Procedures:} angioplasty and stenting to open narrowed arteries, and pacemakers or defibrillators for some arrhythmias
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} coronary artery bypass grafting for severe coronary disease, and valve repair or replacement for valve problems
    \item \textbf{Cardiac rehabilitation:} a supervised exercise and education programme after heart attacks and heart surgery
    \item \textbf{Long-term follow-up:} regular review of blood pressure, cholesterol, and symptoms to adjust treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated or poorly controlled circulatory disease can lead to serious complications, often over many years.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Heart attack (myocardial infarction) and angina
    \item Stroke or transient ischaemic attack (mini-stroke)
    \item Heart failure, with breathlessness and fluid retention
    \item Atrial fibrillation and other arrhythmias
    \item Aortic aneurysm and dissection
    \item Peripheral artery disease, with non-healing ulcers and risk of amputation
    \item Chronic kidney disease from damage to kidney blood vessels
    \item Deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism
    \item Treatment-related complications, such as bleeding with anticoagulants or reactions to procedures
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for most circulatory diseases is good when they are detected early and managed consistently. Many conditions, such as high blood pressure and high cholesterol, are chronic and require lifelong treatment, but good control can substantially reduce the risk of heart attack and stroke. Prognosis depends on the specific condition, its severity, and how well the person adheres to lifestyle changes and medicines. Early treatment of a heart attack or stroke limits permanent damage, and cardiac rehabilitation improves recovery and long-term outlook.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Much circulatory disease is preventable through the same habits that support overall health.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Eat a heart-healthy diet rich in vegetables, fruit, wholegrains, and fish, and low in salt and saturated fat
    \item Be physically active most days of the week -- aim for at least 30 minutes of moderate activity
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and avoid abdominal obesity
    \item Do not smoke, and limit alcohol intake
    \item Have blood pressure, cholesterol, and blood sugar checked regularly, especially from middle age
    \item Manage diabetes and other long-term conditions carefully
    \item Take prescribed medicines as directed and attend review appointments
    \item Learn to recognise the warning signs of a heart attack and stroke
\end{itemize}
