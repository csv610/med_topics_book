\chapter{Australia's Healthcare System}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Australia's healthcare system is the network of services, funding arrangements and professionals that provide health care to the community. It combines public and private care, funded and delivered through several schemes. Medicare, the national insurance scheme, subsidises visits to doctors, many tests and treatment in public hospitals. The Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) subsidises the cost of many prescription medicines. Private health insurance pays for treatment in private hospitals, covers some extras such as dental and physiotherapy, and can offer faster access to elective surgery. The wider system also includes the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS), aged care services, ambulance services, community health programmes and public health campaigns. Most Australians see a general practitioner (GP) as their first point of contact, and the system is designed to move people from general to specialist care as needed.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Australia's population is ageing, and most illness now comes from chronic conditions rather than infection. The most common causes of death and disability include heart disease, cancer, dementia, stroke and chronic lung disease. Almost half of all Australians live with a long-term health condition, and many have more than one. Rates of obesity, diabetes and mental health conditions have risen over recent decades. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people experience significantly poorer health and shorter life expectancy than other Australians, and closing this gap is a national priority. An ageing population, growing numbers of people with multiple chronic conditions and rising costs are placing increasing pressure on the system.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The system exists to prevent, diagnose and treat illness, so the drivers of healthcare need lie in the causes of disease in the community.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle risk factors:} smoking, unhealthy diet, physical inactivity and excess alcohol
    \item \textbf{Metabolic conditions:} obesity, diabetes, high blood pressure and high cholesterol
    \item \textbf{Infectious disease:} viruses and bacteria still cause illness, with immunisation reducing much of the burden
    \item \textbf{Injury:} road trauma, falls and workplace accidents generate demand for emergency and rehabilitation care
    \item \textbf{Mental health:} anxiety, depression and other conditions are among the most common reasons for GP visits
    \item \textbf{Genetic and developmental conditions:} present from birth or inherited
    \item \textbf{Social determinants:} income, education, housing and remoteness strongly influence health outcomes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
For an individual, the system appears as a series of services arranged from general care to highly specialised care.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary care:} the GP practice, the usual first stop, plus community pharmacies, dentists and allied health professionals
    \item \textbf{Secondary care:} specialists and hospital services, usually reached through a GP referral
    \item \textbf{Tertiary care:} highly specialised centres, for example major trauma, transplant and cancer services
    \item \textbf{Emergency care:} hospital emergency departments and ambulance services for urgent problems
    \item \textbf{Preventive services:} immunisation, screening programmes and health promotion campaigns
    \item \textbf{Community and home care:} nursing, allied health, mental health and aged care delivered outside hospital
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Patients may be treated in several settings or sectors that can overlap, and it helps to understand the difference.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Public vs.\ private hospital care:} public patients are treated under Medicare at no or low cost; private patients use health insurance and can choose their doctor
    \item \textbf{GP vs.\ specialist care:} GPs manage most problems; specialists are for complex or uncommon conditions
    \item \textbf{Primary vs.\ allied health:} doctors and nurses, versus physiotherapists, psychologists, podiatrists and others
    \item \textbf{Acute vs.\ community care:} hospital treatment versus care at home or in the community
    \item \textbf{Health vs.\ disability services:} Medicare and the health system, versus the NDIS for disability support
    \item \textbf{Health vs.\ aged care:} medical treatment, versus residential care and home support for older people
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek care early: see a GP for new, persistent or worrying symptoms, and ask for a review if things are not improving. GP visits and many tests are bulk-billed or subsidised, and GPs can refer to specialists and arrange hospital care.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, difficulty breathing, or sudden weakness
    \item Signs of stroke (FAST: Face, Arms, Speech, Time)
    \item Heavy bleeding, major injury, or loss of consciousness
    \item Any sudden, severe symptom that worries you
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis happens through a pathway of consultations, tests and specialist referrals, mostly coordinated by the GP.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{GP consultation:} history and examination, the foundation of most diagnoses, with Medicare covering or subsidising the visit
    \item \textbf{Pathology and imaging:} blood tests, X-rays, ultrasounds, CT and MRI scans, largely subsidised by Medicare
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} the GP refers to a specialist, which may involve a waiting period and out-of-pocket costs
    \item \textbf{Public hospital assessment:} emergency departments or outpatient clinics for urgent or complex problems
    \item \textbf{Health screening:} national programmes such as cervical, breast and bowel cancer screening
    \item \textbf{Health records:} a shared digital record (My Health Record) can help care move safely between services
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management within the system is a partnership between the individual, their GP and specialist services.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Self-care:} most minor illness is managed at home with rest, fluids and over-the-counter medicines
    \item \textbf{GP-led care:} the GP manages chronic conditions and coordinates other providers
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} the PBS makes many prescription medicines affordable
    \item \textbf{Allied health:} physiotherapy, psychology, dietetics and other services, with some subsidised care plans for chronic conditions
    \item \textbf{Hospital treatment:} emergency care and elective surgery, with public waiting lists or private options
    \item \textbf{Chronic disease programmes:} care plans that fund coordinated care and regular review
    \item \textbf{Community supports:} home care, palliative care, and support for disability and mental health
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
No health system is perfect, and the gaps can affect the quality and equity of care.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Waiting lists:} for elective surgery, specialist appointments and some scans
    \item \textbf{Out-of-pocket costs:} gaps in private specialist care, dental care and some medicines can discourage people from seeking care
    \item \textbf{Workforce shortages:} rural and remote areas struggle to attract doctors, nurses and allied health professionals
    \item \textbf{Overcrowded emergency departments:} pressure from high demand and difficulty getting GP appointments
    \item \textbf{Unequal outcomes:} Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people and those in remote or disadvantaged areas have worse health
    \item \textbf{Fragmentation:} poor communication between services can leave patients to coordinate their own care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Australia's health system performs well by international standards: life expectancy is among the highest in the world, survival after heart attack and many cancers has improved steadily, and childhood vaccination rates are high. Most Australians receive good, timely care for the majority of problems. However, outcomes are not equal across the population, and chronic disease and an ageing population will keep demanding more from the system. The outlook for the individual depends greatly on where they live, their income and how well their conditions are managed in the community.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Much of the healthcare system's work is preventive, and individuals play a central role in their own health.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Register with a GP and attend regular check-ups, even when well
    \item Keep vaccinations up to date, including flu, COVID-19 and childhood vaccines
    \item Take part in national screening programmes for bowel, breast and cervical cancer
    \item Eat well, stay active, avoid smoking and limit alcohol
    \item Manage chronic conditions with the GP and take medicines as prescribed
    \item Use the health system wisely: GP first for non-emergencies, and keep 000 for emergencies
    \item Check eligibility for subsidised services and support, including those for chronic conditions and disability
\end{itemize}
