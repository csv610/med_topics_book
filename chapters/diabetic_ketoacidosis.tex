\chapter{Diabetic Ketoacidosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is a serious, life-threatening complication of diabetes in which a severe lack of insulin causes the body to break down fat for energy, producing acidic substances called ketones that accumulate in the blood. It most commonly affects people with type 1 diabetes, but it can also occur in type 2 diabetes under severe stress. DKA develops over hours to days, often triggered by infection, missed insulin, or other illness, and it requires urgent hospital treatment with fluids, insulin, and careful monitoring. With prompt care, the vast majority of people recover fully.

\section*{Epidemiology}
DKA is the most common cause of death in people with type 1 diabetes under age 50 and is a frequent reason for hospital admission. It is the initial presentation of type 1 diabetes in around 15--30\% of children and adults newly diagnosed. Rates are higher in adolescents and in people who miss insulin doses, have limited access to care, or use insulin pumps. Although uncommon in type 2 diabetes, DKA can occur during severe illness or surgery. In Australia, DKA accounts for a significant number of emergency presentations and hospital admissions each year.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Insulin deficiency:} without insulin, glucose cannot enter cells and blood glucose rises steeply
    \item \textbf{Ketone production:} the body switches to burning fat, producing ketones that make the blood acidic
    \item \textbf{Triggers:} infection, missed or inadequate insulin, new-onset diabetes, heart attack, stroke, and other severe illness
    \item \textbf{Dehydration:} high blood glucose causes glucose to spill into the urine, dragging water and electrolytes out with it
    \item \textbf{Acidosis:} ketones accumulate, lowering blood pH and disrupting every body system
    \item \textbf{Electrolyte disturbance:} potassium and other electrolytes are lost, which can cause dangerous heart rhythm problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Excessive thirst, frequent urination, and dehydration
    \item Nausea, vomiting, and abdominal pain
    \item Deep, rapid breathing (Kussmaul breathing) as the body tries to blow off acid
    \item A fruity or acetone smell on the breath
    \item Fatigue, weakness, and confusion
    \item Dry mouth, sunken eyes, and reduced skin elasticity
    \item High blood glucose and ketones in the blood or urine
    \item In severe cases: drowsiness, coma, and shock
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hyperglycaemic hyperosmolar state, which occurs in type 2 diabetes with very high glucose but less ketosis
    \item Gastroenteritis, which can cause vomiting and dehydration in a person with diabetes
    \item Sepsis or other acute infections
    \item Abdominal emergencies, such as appendicitis or pancreatitis, which can mimic the abdominal pain
    \item Alcohol or drug intoxication
    \item Hypoglycaemia, which presents differently with sweating, tremor, and confusion
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with diabetes who feel unwell, have vomiting, or are unable to eat or drink should check blood glucose and ketones and contact their doctor or diabetes team urgently. DKA is a medical emergency and requires hospital treatment.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vomiting with high blood glucose and ketones
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or difficulty waking
    \item Deep or rapid breathing, or a fruity smell on the breath
    \item Severe abdominal pain with nausea
    \item Any person with diabetes who is seriously unwell
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood glucose:} is high, usually above 11 mmol/L
    \item \textbf{Ketones:} measured in blood or urine; elevated levels confirm ketosis
    \item \textbf{Blood gases:} arterial or venous blood gas shows acidosis
    \item \textbf{Electrolytes:} potassium, sodium, and other levels guide treatment
    \item \textbf{Kidney function and full blood count:} assess dehydration and infection
    \item \textbf{ECG:} monitors for potassium-related heart rhythm problems
    \item \textbf{Identifying the trigger:} cultures, chest X-ray, or other tests where infection is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hospital admission:} DKA is always treated in hospital, often in intensive care
    \item \textbf{Intravenous fluids:} replace lost fluid to restore circulation and kidney function
    \item \textbf{Insulin:} given intravenously to stop ketone production and lower blood glucose
    \item \textbf{Potassium replacement:} careful correction of potassium, which falls as insulin and fluids take effect
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} frequent blood tests of glucose, electrolytes, and blood gases guide treatment
    \item \textbf{Treating the trigger:} antibiotics for infection and treatment of other precipitating conditions
    \item \textbf{Transition to normal care:} once acidosis resolves, the person returns to their usual insulin regimen
    \item \textbf{Education:} review of sick-day rules and insulin use to prevent recurrence
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cerebral oedema, a dangerous swelling of the brain, mainly in children
    \item Low potassium causing heart rhythm problems
    \item Fluid overload or excessive fluid loss
    \item Hypoglycaemia during treatment
    \item Acute kidney injury and shock
    \item Blood clots
    \item Death in severe or untreated cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt hospital treatment, the mortality from DKA is under 1--2\% in experienced centres and most people recover fully within 1--3 days. The key to good outcomes is early recognition and urgent treatment, especially in children. After recovery, education about insulin use, monitoring, and sick-day rules greatly reduces the risk of recurrence. DKA is preventable with good diabetes self-management, and most people who have had one episode can avoid future episodes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take insulin exactly as prescribed, even when feeling unwell
    \item Learn and follow "sick-day rules": check glucose and ketones more often, and know when to seek help
    \item Never stop insulin without medical advice
    \item Have a plan for what to do when vomiting prevents eating
    \item Check ketones when glucose is high or when unwell
    \item Contact your diabetes team early when problems develop
    \item Wear medical identification stating you have diabetes
\end{itemize}
