\chapter{Ulcers and mouth sores}
\index{Ulcers and mouth sores}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Oral ulcers and mouth sores represent a diverse group of painful, inflammatory lesions that breach the mucosal membrane of the oral cavity. Anatomically, an ulcer is defined as a complete loss of the epithelial thickness, exposing the underlying lamina propria and superficial nerve endings. This exposure makes the lesion highly sensitive to mechanical, thermal, and chemical stimuli, leading to significant discomfort. Mouth sores can range from benign, self-limiting lesions, such as recurrent aphthous stomatitis (canker sores), to manifestations of systemic autoimmune diseases, severe infections, or malignant neoplasms like oral squamous cell carcinoma.

Understanding oral ulceration is of paramount clinical significance. Beyond the immediate physical pain and functional impairment, such as difficulty masticating, speaking, and swallowing, these lesions serve as important clinical indicators. They are frequently the first visible sign of underlying systemic conditions, including inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis), celiac disease, immunodeficiencies (such as HIV/AIDS), and connective tissue disorders. Consequently, a comprehensive clinical evaluation is essential to distinguish between primary localized oral diseases and secondary manifestations of systemic pathology. The impact on patient quality of life can be profound, especially in chronic or highly recurrent cases, leading to nutritional deficiencies, weight loss, and psychological distress. This chapter provides a detailed reference on the clinical presentation, pathophysiological mechanisms, diagnostic pathways, and therapeutic interventions for oral ulcers and mouth sores.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Oral ulcers and mouth sores are among the most common complaints in clinical dentistry and general medicine. Recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS) is the most prevalent form of non-traumatic oral ulceration, affecting approximately 10\% to 20\% of the general population worldwide. However, some epidemiological studies report prevalence rates up to 30\% in specific demographics, particularly in university students, individuals from high socioeconomic backgrounds, and professionals under high psychological stress.

Demographic analyses reveal that females are more frequently affected than males, and the condition demonstrates a higher incidence in children, adolescents, and young adults. The initial onset of RAS typically occurs during the second decade of life, with the severity and frequency of episodes peaking in young adulthood before gradually declining in later life. While RAS shows no strict geographical boundaries, certain ethnic and genetic populations exhibit higher susceptibility. For instance, individuals with a family history of oral ulcers have a significantly elevated risk; if both parents are affected, their offspring have a 90\% chance of developing the condition.

Other infectious causes of mouth sores, such as cold sores caused by Herpes Simplex Virus Type 1 (HSV-1), have a massive global footprint. The World Health Organization estimates that approximately 67\% of the global population under the age of 50 is infected with HSV-1, many of whom experience recurrent outbreaks of painful oral and perioral vesicles that subsequently ulcerate. Traumatic ulcers, resulting from accidental biting, dental appliances, or thermal burns, occur ubiquitously across all age groups and demographics, representing the most common cause of acute, solitary oral ulceration.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The aetiology of oral ulcers and mouth sores is multifactorial, involving a complex interplay of genetic predisposition, environmental triggers, local trauma, systemic diseases, and immunological dysregulation. The primary pathophysiological mechanism of non-infectious ulceration, particularly RAS, is a cell-mediated immune response. This process involves the activation of T-lymphocytes (specifically CD8+ cytotoxic T-cells) and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-alpha) and interleukin-2, which target and destroy the mucosal keratinocytes, leading to epithelial desquamation and ulcer formation.

Key factors contributing to the development of oral ulcers and mouth sores include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Local Trauma}: Mechanical trauma is the most frequent trigger for acute oral ulcers. This includes accidental biting of the buccal mucosa or tongue, irritation from sharp teeth, fractured restorations, orthodontic brackets, or ill-fitting dentures. Chemical trauma (such as from toothpastes containing sodium lauryl sulfate) and thermal burns from hot foods or beverages also disrupt the mucosal barrier.
    \item \textbf{Genetic Susceptibility}: Heredity plays a substantial role in the pathogenesis of recurrent ulcers. Certain human leukocyte antigen (HLA) types, including HLA-A2, HLA-B12, and HLA-DR2, are strongly associated with an increased susceptibility to RAS. Family studies indicate that genetic factors influence the frequency, severity, and age of onset of the disease.
    \item \textbf{Nutritional Deficiencies}: Deficiencies in essential micronutrients impair mucosal regeneration and compromise immune function. Crucial deficiencies associated with oral ulceration include iron, folate (vitamin B9), cobalamin (vitamin B12), zinc, and pyridoxine (vitamin B6). Correcting these deficiencies often leads to a resolution of recurrent mouth sores.
    \item \textbf{Systemic Inflammatory and Autoimmune Diseases}: Oral ulcers are common extra-intestinal manifestations of Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis. They are also central to the diagnosis of Behcet's disease (a systemic vasculitis), celiac disease (gluten-sensitive enteropathy), systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and reactive arthritis.
    \item \textbf{Microbial Pathogens}: Infectious mouth sores are caused by various viruses, bacteria, and fungi. Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV-1 and HSV-2), Varicella-Zoster Virus (VZV), and Coxsackievirus (the causative agent of hand-foot-and-mouth disease) are common viral causes. Fungal infections, primarily Candida albicans, lead to thrush and associated erosive lesions, while bacterial causes include Treponema pallidum (syphilis) and Mycobacterium tuberculosis.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological Agents}: Certain medications can induce oral ulceration either through direct mucosal toxicity or by systemic mechanisms. Common culprits include nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), beta-blockers, nicorandil (a potassium-channel activator used in angina), alendronate, and cytotoxic chemotherapeutic agents used in oncology.
    \item \textbf{Psychological Stress}: High levels of anxiety and stress trigger the release of salivary cortisol and catecholamines, which modulate the immune system and lower the threshold for mucosal inflammation, frequently precipitating outbreaks in susceptible individuals.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of oral ulcers and mouth sores varies depending on the underlying pathology, but they generally manifest with localized pain, erythema, and functional impairment. The most common form, recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS), exhibits three distinct clinical presentations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Minor Aphthous Ulcers}: Accounting for approximately 80\% of RAS cases, these lesions are small (typically less than 10 mm in diameter), shallow, and oval or round. They present with a grey-yellow pseudomembranous base surrounded by a well-demarcated, erythematous halo. They are painful but usually heal within 7 to 14 days without scarring. They primarily affect non-keratinized mobile mucosa, such as the labial mucosa, buccal mucosa, and the lateral borders of the tongue.
    \item \textbf{Major Aphthous Ulcers (Sutton's Disease)}: Representing about 10\% of cases, these are larger (often exceeding 10 mm and sometimes up to 30 mm in diameter), deeper, and more irregular in shape. They are extremely painful and can persist for several weeks or even months. Because the ulceration extends deep into the submucosa, they heal with significant scarring and can lead to tissue destruction. Major ulcers can affect both keratinized and non-keratinized tissues, including the soft palate, tonsils, and pharyngeal wall, often causing systemic symptoms such as difficulty swallowing (dysphagia) and low-grade fever.
    \item \textbf{Herpetiform Ulcers}: Making up the remaining 10\% of RAS cases, these present as multiple, tiny, pinhead-sized (1 to 2 mm) lesions that occur in clusters of 10 to 100. Despite their name, they are not caused by the herpes virus, although they can coalesce to form larger, irregular, painful erosions. They typically heal within 10 to 30 days without scarring.
    \item \textbf{Prodromal Phase}: In many patients, a prodromal sensation of burning, tingling, or itching precedes the visible appearance of the ulcer by 24 to 48 hours.
    \item \textbf{Associated Symptoms}: Severe pain is characteristically out of proportion to the size of the lesion. This pain is exacerbated by acidic, spicy, salty, or hot foods. Consequently, patients may experience significant difficulty with mastication, speech, and oral hygiene. Localized submandibular lymphadenopathy may also be present, particularly in cases of secondary bacterial infection or major aphthous ulcers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Distinguishing benign mouth sores from more serious systemic conditions or malignancies is a critical responsibility of the clinician. A broad differential diagnosis must be considered when evaluating patients presenting with oral ulcerations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Oral Squamous Cell Carcinoma (OSCC)}: This is the most critical differential diagnosis to rule out in any chronic, non-healing ulcer. Carcinomatous ulcers are typically solitary, persistent (lasting more than 2 to 3 weeks), have indurated (hardened) borders, raised margins, and a granular, easily bleeding base. They are often painless in their early stages and are strongly associated with tobacco and alcohol consumption.
    \item \textbf{Primary Herpes Simplex Stomatitis}: Unlike recurrent aphthous ulcers, which occur on non-keratinized mobile mucosa, primary herpes infections present with widespread vesicles that rupture to form small ulcers on keratinized tissues (such as the gingiva and hard palate). This is often accompanied by systemic symptoms, including high fever, malaise, and generalized lymphadenopathy.
    \item \textbf{Oral Candidiasis (Thrush)}: While not typically presenting as a classical ulcer, severe pseudomembranous or erythematous candidiasis can cause painful, raw, mucosal surfaces. The white plaques of thrush can be scraped off, leaving a red, tender, bleeding surface, which differentiates it from the necrotic base of an aphthous ulcer.
    \item \textbf{Lichen Planus (Erosive Type)}: This chronic autoimmune condition presents with painful, irregular mucosal erosions and ulcers that are typically surrounded by characteristic white, radiating, lace-like striae (known as Wickham's striae). The lesions are often bilateral and symmetric, commonly affecting the buccal mucosa.
    \item \textbf{Erythema Multiforme}: An acute, self-limiting inflammatory disease that presents with characteristic "target" or "bullseye" lesions on the skin, accompanied by severe, painful oral ulcerations. The lips are frequently covered with hemorrhagic, dark crusts, and the patient may experience fever and joint pain.
    \item \textbf{Pemphigus Vulgaris}: A potentially life-threatening autoimmune blistering disease that affects the skin and mucous membranes. Intraepithelial blisters form due to antibodies targeting desmoglein proteins. These blisters are thin-walled and rupture rapidly, leaving large, painful, slow-healing erosive surfaces throughout the mouth. A positive Nikolsky's sign (where gentle lateral pressure on normal-appearing mucosa causes desquamation) is highly characteristic.
    \item \textbf{Mucous Membrane Pemphigoid}: An autoimmune subepithelial blistering disease that primarily affects the mucous membranes, particularly the gingiva (presenting as desquamative gingivitis). The blisters are more tense and less prone to immediate rupture than those of pemphigus, but healing can result in mucosal scarring.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
While the majority of oral ulcers are benign and resolve spontaneously, certain clinical presentations warrant prompt medical or dental evaluation. Patients should be advised to consult a healthcare professional if they experience any of the following symptoms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item An oral ulcer or mouth sore that persists for more than two to three weeks without showing signs of healing or improvement.
    \item Severe, debilitating pain that is not relieved by over-the-counter analgesic medications and prevents adequate fluid or food intake.
    \item The sudden appearance of multiple, widespread mouth sores accompanied by a high fever, general malaise, or swollen lymph nodes.
    \item Ulcers that are larger than 10 mm in diameter, have irregular, hardened borders, or bleed easily upon contact.
    \item Frequent, recurrent outbreaks of mouth sores (e.g., several times a month) that significantly impair the patient's quality of life.
    \item Signs of systemic involvement, such as unexplained weight loss, joint pain, abdominal pain, diarrhea, or ulcers on other mucous membranes (e.g., genital ulcers).
    \item Signs of severe dehydration, including extreme thirst, dry mouth, reduced or dark urine output, dizziness, or confusion.
\end{itemize}
In rare cases, severe swelling associated with an infection or allergic reaction in the mouth or throat can cause airway compromise. If a patient experiences difficulty breathing, inability to swallow saliva (resulting in drooling), or severe swelling of the tongue, lips, or neck, they must seek emergency services immediately. Contact emergency dispatch by dialing \textbf{local emergency services} in many countries, \textbf{911} in the United States, or \textbf{999} in the United Kingdom.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnostic approach to oral ulcers and mouth sores begins with a thorough medical history and a meticulous physical examination. The clinician must document the duration of the lesions, their onset, frequency of recurrence, the presence of prodromal symptoms, any potential triggers, and a detailed review of systems to identify systemic symptoms. The physical examination must involve a complete inspection of all oral mucosal surfaces, noting the size, shape, number, distribution, and base characteristics of the ulcers, as well as checking for cervical lymphadenopathy.

In cases where the diagnosis is not straightforward, or if systemic disease or malignancy is suspected, the following investigations are indicated:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Complete Blood Count (CBC) and Inflammatory Markers}: A CBC is essential to screen for anemia, leukopenia, cyclic neutropenia, or leukaemia. Elevated inflammatory markers, such as Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) or C-reactive protein (CRP), may suggest an underlying systemic inflammatory condition.
    \item \textbf{Nutritional Screen}: Serum levels of ferritin, iron, total iron-binding capacity, folate (vitamin B9), cobalamin (vitamin B12), and zinc should be measured, as deficiencies in these micronutrients are correctable causes of recurrent ulceration.
    \item \textbf{Biopsy (Tissue Histopathology)}: A tissue biopsy (typically an incisional or punch biopsy) is the gold standard investigation for any solitary oral ulcer persisting for more than three weeks. It is critical for ruling out oral squamous cell carcinoma, deep fungal infections, tuberculosis, or chronic granulomatous diseases. The biopsy should include both the ulcer margin and adjacent normal tissue.
    \item \textbf{Immunofluorescence Studies}: Direct and indirect immunofluorescence testing of perilesional mucosal biopsies is necessary if autoimmune blistering diseases, such as pemphigus vulgaris or mucous membrane pemphigoid, are suspected.
    \item \textbf{Serological Testing for Systemic Conditions}: If celiac disease is suspected, serological screening for tissue transglutaminase IgA (tTG-IgA) and total serum IgA is warranted. For suspected connective tissue diseases like systemic lupus erythematosus, an Antinuclear Antibody (ANA) test should be performed.
    \item \textbf{Microbiological Cultures and PCR}: Swabs of the ulcer bed or vesicular fluid should be sent for Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) testing if a viral infection (such as HSV or VZV) is suspected. Fungal cultures or smear preparations (KOH prep) are useful for confirming oral candidiasis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of oral ulcers and mouth sores is directed toward reducing pain, accelerating healing, preventing secondary infections, and minimizing the frequency and severity of recurrences. Treatment strategies are tailored to the severity of the lesions and their underlying cause, ranging from localized therapies to systemic pharmacotherapy:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Topical Pain Relief (Analgesics and Anesthetics)}: Over-the-counter and prescription topical agents provide rapid, temporary pain relief. Benzocaine gels or ointments can be applied directly to the ulcers. Lidocaine 2\% viscous solution or gel can be used to numb the oral cavity, especially before meals, to facilitate food intake. Benzydamine hydrochloride mouthwash acts as both a local anesthetic and an anti-inflammatory agent.
    \item \textbf{Topical Corticosteroids}: These are the mainstay of treatment for non-infectious inflammatory ulcers like RAS. They suppress the localized immune response and reduce pain and healing time. Options include triamcinolone acetonide 0.1\% in an adhesive dental paste (Orabase), clobetasol propionate 0.05\% gel, or dexamethasone elixir (0.5 mg/5 mL) used as a rinse-and-spit mouthwash. These should be applied at the earliest sign of an ulcer outbreak (during the prodromal phase).
    \item \textbf{Antiseptic and Antimicrobial Rinses}: Chlorhexidine gluconate 0.2\% or 0.12\% mouthwash helps maintain oral hygiene when tooth brushing is painful, reduces bacterial load, and prevents secondary infection of the ulcer bed. Hydrogen peroxide or warm saline rinses can also keep the area clean and promote healing.
    \item \textbf{Systemic Pharmacotherapy}: Reserved for severe, refractory, or major aphthous ulceration that does not respond to topical treatments. A short course of systemic corticosteroids (e.g., oral prednisolone) may be prescribed to control acute, severe outbreaks. Immunomodulatory agents, such as colchicine, dapsone, pentoxifylline, montelukast, or thalidomide, may be initiated under specialist supervision for patients with chronic, debilitating recurrences.
    \item \textbf{Nutritional Supplementation}: If laboratory investigations reveal a nutritional deficiency, targeted supplementation with iron, vitamin B12, folate, or zinc is indicated. This correction often resolves or dramatically reduces the frequency of recurrent ulcers.
    \item \textbf{Low-Level Laser Therapy (LLLT)}: This clinical procedure, performed in a dental or medical setting, involves the application of a low-energy laser to the ulcer. LLLT provides immediate pain relief and accelerates tissue regeneration and healing, often resolving lesions within a few days.
    \item \textbf{Elimination of Triggers and Dietary Changes}: Patients should avoid hard, crunchy, sharp, spicy, acidic, or very salty foods that can mechanically or chemically irritate the mucosa. Using a soft-bristled toothbrush and switching to a toothpaste that is free of sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) can significantly reduce the incidence of outbreaks.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Although minor mouth sores often heal without incident, severe, chronic, or untreated oral ulcerations can lead to several localized and systemic complications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Secondary Bacterial Infection}: The exposed submucosa of an oral ulcer is vulnerable to colonization by oral microflora. Secondary bacterial infection leads to increased localized pain, swelling, purulent discharge, delayed healing, and, in severe cases, the development of localized cellulitis or abscesses requiring antibiotic therapy.
    \item \textbf{Tissue Destruction and Scarring}: Major aphthous ulcers (Sutton's disease) destroy deeper connective tissues and heal by secondary intention. This process leads to the formation of dense fibrotic scar tissue, which can cause structural changes in the mouth, such as mucosal contractures, microstomia (narrowing of the oral opening), or distortion of the soft palate and uvula, potentially impacting speech and swallowing.
    \item \textbf{Nutritional Impairment and Dehydration}: Severe, persistent pain during chewing and swallowing (odynophagia) can lead to a significant reduction in oral intake. In vulnerable populations, such as young children, the elderly, or patients with major ulcers, this can rapidly result in clinical dehydration and progressive malnutrition.
    \item \textbf{Psychological and Social Impact}: Chronic, painful recurrences of mouth sores can interfere with daily activities, including speaking, smiling, and performing basic oral hygiene. The constant discomfort and aesthetic concerns can lead to significant psychological distress, social withdrawal, anxiety, and depression.
    \item \textbf{Delayed Detection of Malignancy}: A serious complication is the patient or provider misidentifying a chronic malignant ulcer (OSCC) as a benign mouth sore. Delaying a biopsy for a persistent, non-healing ulcer can allow the cancer to progress to advanced stages, increasing the risk of regional lymph node metastasis and significantly worsening the overall prognosis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for the vast majority of individuals suffering from oral ulcers and mouth sores is highly favorable. Minor aphthous ulcers, which constitute the bulk of clinical cases, have an excellent prognosis. They are self-limiting, resolve completely within 7 to 14 days, and leave no permanent scars or tissue damage. Herpetiform ulcers also carry a very good prognosis, typically healing within a few weeks without scarring, although they can cause substantial temporary discomfort due to their numerous lesions.

For patients with major aphthous ulcers, the prognosis is more guarded due to the chronic, painful nature of the disease, the potential for permanent scarring, and the long healing times (frequently 4 to 6 weeks or longer). In these cases, consistent medical management and close follow-up are required to minimize tissue loss and manage pain.

For recurrent aphthous stomatitis, the natural history of the disease is one of gradual improvement. While outbreaks may continue for several decades, the frequency and severity of episodes generally decrease with age, with many patients experiencing spontaneous and permanent remission by their fourth or fifth decade of life. When oral ulcers are secondary to a systemic condition (such as celiac disease or Crohn's disease), the long-term prognosis depends entirely on the successful diagnosis and management of the underlying systemic disease. Once the primary condition is controlled, the oral manifestations typically resolve.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventative strategies and self-care measures are vital components in managing mouth sores, reducing the frequency of outbreaks, and alleviating discomfort during active ulceration. Implementing the following habits can significantly protect the oral mucosa:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Meticulous and Gentle Oral Hygiene}: Patients should use a toothbrush with extra-soft bristles to minimize the risk of mechanical micro-trauma to the delicate mucosal surfaces. Careful brushing techniques and regular flossing help maintain a clean oral environment, reducing bacterial load and the risk of secondary infections. Regular dental check-ups are essential to identify and smooth sharp tooth edges, repair fractured restorations, and ensure that dentures or orthodontic appliances are fitted correctly.
    \item \textbf{Dietary Management}: Identifying and avoiding personal dietary triggers is crucial. Common triggers include highly acidic fruits (such as oranges, lemons, and grapefruits), tomatoes, chocolate, coffee, nuts, cheese, and spicy or heavily salted dishes. Consuming a balanced diet rich in essential vitamins (B-complex) and minerals (iron, zinc) helps support mucosal health.
    \item \textbf{Use of SLS-Free Dental Products}: Sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) is a foaming agent found in many commercial toothpastes. Because SLS can dry out and disrupt the protective mucosal barrier, switching to an SLS-free toothpaste has been shown to reduce the frequency and severity of aphthous ulcers in many individuals.
    \item \textbf{Stress Reduction and Management}: Since emotional stress is a well-established trigger for recurrent mouth sores, practicing stress-reduction techniques is highly beneficial. Patients should be encouraged to engage in regular physical exercise, ensure consistent and adequate sleep, and practice mindfulness, meditation, yoga, or deep-breathing exercises.
    \item \textbf{Adequate Hydration}: Maintaining optimal hydration levels supports the production of saliva, which contains natural antimicrobial enzymes and protective proteins that help lubricate and safeguard the oral mucosa.
\end{itemize}
