\chapter{Going Home from Hospital: Discharge Planning}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Discharge planning is the process of preparing a patient to leave hospital and return home safely. It begins as early as possible after admission and involves the patient, family and carers, doctors, nurses, pharmacists, and allied health professionals. Good discharge planning ensures that the patient leaves with a clear understanding of their medicines, follow-up appointments, and warning signs of complications, and that any home supports, equipment, or services are in place. It reduces the risk of readmission, helps patients recover well at home, and improves the experience of leaving hospital. Asking questions, understanding your discharge plan, and following up are the keys to a smooth transition home.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hospital discharge is a high-risk time for patients. Around one in five older Australians is readmitted within 28 days of discharge, and many of these readmissions are avoidable. Medication errors, missed follow-up, and inadequate home support are common contributors. Around half of older patients leave hospital with complex medicine regimens, and many experience functional decline that can persist after discharge. Good discharge planning is therefore a priority in Australian hospitals, and programs such as discharge summaries, medicine reviews, and community follow-up have been shown to reduce readmissions and improve outcomes.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Discharge planning addresses the medical and practical factors that determine whether a patient recovers at home.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Medicine changes:} new, changed, or stopped medicines at discharge are a major source of error and harm
    \item \textbf{Follow-up care:} arranging GP review, specialist appointments, and pathology or imaging tests
    \item \textbf{Wound and device care:} managing surgical wounds, drains, catheters, or dressings at home
    \item \textbf{Mobility and function:} ensuring the patient can manage stairs, cooking, bathing, and daily tasks
    \item \textbf{Home environment:} identifying hazards and arranging equipment such as rails, commodes, or hospital beds
    \item \textbf{Community services:} organising nursing, physiotherapy, home care, or residential support where needed
    \item \textbf{Education:} teaching patients and carers about their condition, medicines, and warning signs
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Situations and signs that suggest discharge may need extra planning include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Older age, especially over 75, or living alone
    \item Multiple chronic conditions or complex medicine regimens
    \item Reduced mobility, confusion, or previous falls
    \item Living in rural or remote areas, or with limited transport
    \item Carer strain or no available carer
    \item Recent surgery, stroke, or other event requiring ongoing care
    \item Communication or language barriers
    \item A history of frequent hospital admissions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Discharge planning considers the reasons a patient may not cope at home.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Medical instability requiring continued hospital care
    \item Unresolved symptoms, such as pain, fever, or poor mobility
    \item Unsafe home environment or lack of support
    \item Cognitive impairment affecting the ability to manage medicines or care independently
    \item Need for nursing care that cannot be provided at home
    \item Social issues, including homelessness or family conflict
    \item Readiness of the patient and family to manage at home
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should not be discharged until it is safe, and they should speak up if they feel unprepared. After discharge, seek medical care promptly for new or worsening symptoms, fever, uncontrolled pain, wound problems, or difficulty taking medicines.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, severe breathlessness, or collapse after discharge
    \item Sudden weakness, facial drooping, or difficulty speaking
    \item Heavy bleeding, a wound that has opened, or signs of severe infection
    \item Confusion, seizures, or loss of consciousness
    \item Any emergency condition related to the reason for admission
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Planning is based on a comprehensive assessment of the patient's needs.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Discharge planning assessment:} review of medical, functional, social, and environmental needs
    \item \textbf{Medicine reconciliation:} a pharmacist compares the medicines taken before admission with those prescribed at discharge
    \item \textbf{Functional assessment:} physiotherapy and occupational therapy assess mobility and the ability to manage daily tasks
    \item \textbf{Home assessment:} an occupational therapist may visit the home to assess safety and equipment needs
    \item \textbf{Discharge summary:} a written summary is provided to the patient and sent to the GP
    \item \textbf{Community care arrangements:} referrals are organised before leaving hospital
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
A well-managed discharge is planned, explained, and followed up.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Early planning:} discharge planning starts at or soon after admission
    \item \textbf{Medicines at discharge:} a clear, written list of all medicines, doses, and changes, explained by a pharmacist
    \item \textbf{Follow-up arrangements:} a booked GP review within days to weeks and any specialist appointments
    \item \textbf{Written instructions:} a discharge summary covering diagnosis, treatment, warning signs, and when to seek help
    \item \textbf{Community services:} district nursing, rehabilitation, home care, and equipment arranged before discharge
    \item \textbf{Carer involvement:} carers are informed and trained in any required care
    \item \textbf{Telehealth and phone follow-up:} a call or telehealth review shortly after discharge can catch problems early
    \item \textbf{Transport arrangements:} organising a safe way home
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Medication errors, including wrong doses, missed medicines, or interactions, when discharge is rushed
    \item Readmission to hospital because of complications or inadequate follow-up
    \item Falls and injuries at home
    \item Wound infections and other complications of surgery managed poorly at home
    \item Dehydration, poor nutrition, or missed doses in patients without support
    \item Carer burnout when the burden of care is underestimated
    \item Anxiety and reduced confidence in patients who feel unprepared
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Good discharge planning significantly improves outcomes. Patients who leave hospital with clear instructions, arranged follow-up, and adequate support recover faster, have fewer complications, and are less likely to be readmitted. While some problems after discharge are unavoidable, most readmissions within 28 days are considered preventable with good planning and early follow-up. Patients who understand their medicines, attend their GP review, and know their warning signs are the most likely to recover well at home.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ask questions before you leave: about your condition, medicines, and what to do if things go wrong
    \item Make sure you have a written list of your medicines and understand any changes
    \item Arrange a GP appointment for within a week or two of discharge
    \item Take home your discharge summary and all prescriptions and test results
    \item Organise support at home before you leave, including help with meals, transport, and care
    \item Keep a list of your warning signs and who to call if they occur
    \item Ask about telehealth or phone follow-up options
    \item Do not leave until you feel prepared; speak to the team if you have concerns
    \item Have a plan for getting your medicines from a pharmacy after discharge
\end{itemize}
