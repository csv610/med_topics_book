\chapter{Burns}
\index{Burns}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A burn is an injury to the skin and the tissue beneath it, caused by heat, chemicals, electricity, friction, or radiation. Most burns result from flames, hot liquids and steam (scalds), or contact with hot objects. Burns range from a mild reddening of the skin that heals within days to deep, life-threatening injuries that destroy all layers of the skin and require surgery.

Burns are classified by their depth and by the size of the body surface affected, and these two factors determine how serious the injury is and how it should be treated. In many countries, major burns are managed in specialised burns units, while minor burns can usually be treated at home or by a general practitioner. Early and correct first aid substantially reduces tissue damage and improves the final outcome.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Burns are among the most common household injuries. Scalds from hot drinks, cooking, and bathing account for a large share of cases, particularly in children under five and in older adults. Flame burns from fires are more common in adults and carry the highest risk of death. In many countries, thousands of people are admitted to hospital with burns each year, and worldwide burns are a major cause of injury-related death and disability, with the greatest burden in low- and middle-income countries. Young children, older people, and those with disabilities or chronic illness are at the highest risk of serious burns.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Burns are caused by:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scalds:} hot water, tea, coffee, soup, and steam -- the most common cause, especially in children
    \item \textbf{Flames and fires:} house fires, clothing catching alight, and explosions
    \item \textbf{Contact with hot objects:} irons, ovens, heaters, and hot metal
    \item \textbf{Chemicals:} strong acids and alkalis in cleaning products, batteries, and industrial chemicals
    \item \textbf{Electricity:} household current, power lines, and lightning, which can cause deep internal injury out of proportion to the visible skin wound
    \item \textbf{Friction and sunburn:} road rash and prolonged sun exposure
\end{itemize}

Heat denatures the proteins and damages the cells of the skin, killing tissue at the point of contact and injuring a wider zone around it. Blood vessels leak fluid into the damaged area, causing swelling and blisters. In large burns, so much fluid is lost from the damaged skin that the circulation becomes unstable, and the body's immune defences are weakened, making infection likely. The depth of injury evolves over the first 24 to 48 hours as blood flow to the injured zone changes.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Superficial burns:} red, painful, dry skin resembling sunburn, without blisters
    \item \textbf{Partial-thickness burns:} blisters, moist pink or red skin, and intense pain
    \item \textbf{Full-thickness burns:} white, waxy, leathery, or charred skin that may be relatively painless because the nerve endings have been destroyed
    \item \textbf{Swelling} of the burned area, which develops over the first hours
    \item \textbf{Inhalation injury:} facial burns, singed nasal hairs, soot in the mouth or throat, a hoarse voice, cough, or breathing difficulty after a fire
    \item \textbf{Severe burns:} thirst, reduced urine output, confusion, a rapid pulse, and low blood pressure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Superficial versus partial-thickness versus full-thickness burn; depth is frequently reassessed over the first two days as the wound evolves
    \item Chemical burn versus thermal burn, since chemical burns require prolonged washing and may continue to damage tissue
    \item Electrical burn with hidden deep injury versus a simple skin burn
    \item Allergic contact dermatitis or a severe blistering reaction that mimics a burn
    \item Infected burn blister or cellulitis versus normal burn inflammation
    \item Blistering skin diseases, such as toxic epidermal necrolysis, which may present with burn-like skin loss
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
First aid for any burn: stop the burning process, remove clothing and jewellery near the burn unless they are stuck to the skin, cool with running water for 20 minutes, and cover with a clean, non-stick dressing or cling film. Do not burst blisters, apply ice, or use creams, butter, or toothpaste.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Burns larger than the palm of the person's hand
    \item Deep burns that are white, waxy, or charred
    \item Burns of the face, hands, feet, genitals, or across a joint
    \item Chemical or electrical burns
    \item Burns with breathing difficulty, a hoarse voice, or soot in the mouth or nose
    \item Burns in babies, young children, older adults, or people with chronic illness
    \item Any burn where the person is confused, faint, or in severe pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} what caused the burn, when it happened, and whether there was exposure to smoke or fumes
    \item \textbf{Examination:} assessment of the burn's depth by appearance, blistering, and sensation, and of its size as a percentage of total body surface area
    \item \textbf{Airway and breathing assessment:} essential when the face is burned or smoke has been inhaled, because airway swelling can develop rapidly
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} for larger burns, to check fluid balance, kidney function, and markers of tissue damage
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, and urine output for clinically significant burns
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Minor burns:} clean gently, leave blisters intact, apply a non-stick dressing, and use simple pain relief
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} paracetamol or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medicines; stronger analgesia for larger burns
    \item \textbf{Cleaning and dressings:} removal of dead tissue and regular dressing changes, sometimes using silver-based creams or dressings
    \item \textbf{Fluids:} intravenous fluids for large burns to replace losses and protect the kidneys
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} debridement of dead tissue and skin grafting for deep burns
    \item \textbf{Infection control:} antibiotics are given only if a wound infection develops
    \item \textbf{Burns unit care:} referral for large, deep, or critical burns, and for burns with inhalation injury
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy to maintain joint movement, pressure garments for scarring, and psychological support
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection of the burn wound and, in severe cases, blood poisoning (sepsis)
    \item Fluid and electrolyte disturbances in large burns
    \item Scarring, including raised hypertrophic scars and keloids
    \item Contractures: tightening of scarred skin that restricts joint movement
    \item Loss of sensation, chronic itch, or altered skin colour in healed areas
    \item Psychological distress, including post-traumatic stress and depression
    \item Inhalation injury with lung damage and pneumonia
    \item Multi-organ failure and death in very large burns
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most minor burns heal within one to three weeks with little permanent effect. Partial-thickness burns may take two to three weeks and can leave scarring, particularly in children and people with darker skin. Full-thickness burns always leave scars and require grafting. With modern specialist care, even very extensive burns are often survivable, and function and appearance can continue to improve for a year or more. Good first aid, meticulous wound care, and active rehabilitation all improve the eventual outcome.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep hot drinks, kettles, and pot handles out of reach of children, and test bath water before use
    \item Set the hot water system to a safe temperature to prevent scalds
    \item Install and maintain smoke alarms, and plan and practise a fire escape route
    \item Keep matches, lighters, and chemicals locked away from children
    \item Use guard screens around fires and heaters
    \item Wear protective clothing and use safe practices when cooking, welding, or handling chemicals
    \item Protect skin from the sun to prevent radiation burns
    \item Keep burns clean, follow dressing advice, and seek care early if a burn becomes red, painful, or smelly
\end{itemize}
