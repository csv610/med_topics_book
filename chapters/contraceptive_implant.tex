\chapter{Contraceptive implant}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A contraceptive implant is a highly effective, long-acting reversible contraceptive (LARC) device consisting of a small, flexible, non-biodegradable plastic rod (typically 4 cm in length and 2 mm in diameter) that is placed subdermally in the inner aspect of a patient's non-dominant upper arm. The most widely prescribed system in Australia (Implanon NXT) contains 68 mg of etonogestrel, a synthetic progestogen, which is slowly and continuously released into the systemic circulation. It provides continuous, reliable contraception for up to three years. The contraceptive implant is the most effective reversible method of birth control currently available, with a failure rate lower than that of permanent surgical sterilisation.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Subdermal contraceptive implants are widely utilized globally and are highly popular in Australia. Approximately 10\% to 15\% of Australian women who use contraception choose the subdermal implant. Major clinical bodies, including the Royal Australian and New Zealand College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RANZCOG), recommend implants and other LARCs as first-line options for individuals seeking reliable contraception because they remove the risk of user-compliance failure. The implant is highly cost-effective and is subsidized under the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) in Australia, making it highly accessible across all demographics, including adolescent and nulliparous populations.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The etonogestrel implant prevents pregnancy by releasing a steady, low dose of synthetic hormone that acts on multiple reproductive organs and feedback loops.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ovulation suppression:} Exogenous etonogestrel suppresses the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis, preventing the mid-cycle luteinising hormone (LH) surge and thereby inhibiting follicular maturation and ovulation.
    \item \textbf{Cervical mucus thickening:} The progestogen increases the viscosity and thickness of cervical mucus, creating a hostile environment that prevents sperm from migrating from the vagina into the uterine cavity.
    \item \textbf{Endometrial atrophy:} Chronic exposure to progestogen prevents normal endometrial proliferation, resulting in a thin, atrophic lining that is unsuitable for blastocyst implantation.
    \item \textbf{Steady-state hormone release:} The ethylene vinyl acetate copolymer core of the rod regulates the diffusion of etonogestrel, ensuring circulating levels remain above the threshold required to suppress ovulation for three years.
    \item \textbf{Oestrogen-free formulation:} By excluding oestrogen, the implant avoids the increased physiological risks of venous thromboembolism and cardiovascular events associated with combined hormonal contraceptives.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical profile of the contraceptive implant is characterised by high efficacy, cycle changes, and a minor insertion and removal procedure.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Exceptional typical-use efficacy:} A failure rate of less than 0.05\% under typical-use conditions, which is comparable to vasectomy, because it does not require daily compliance or coital preparation.
    \item \textbf{Unpredictable bleeding patterns:} The most common side effect is a change in the menstrual cycle, which can manifest as amenorrhoea (in approximately 20\% of users), infrequent spotting, or prolonged or frequent bleeding episodes.
    \item \textbf{Subdermal palpability:} Under normal conditions, the rod can be easily felt as a small, firm object under the skin of the inner arm.
    \item \textbf{Procedural insertion and removal:} Minor, in-clinic procedures performed under local anaesthetic; insertion uses a specialized pre-loaded applicator, and removal is performed via a small 2 mm incision.
    \item \textbf{Systemic progestogenic effects:} Potential side effects including acne, breast tenderness, headache, mood swings, and minor weight fluctuations, which often settle within the first few months of use.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
In clinical practice, the "differential diagnosis" for a patient considering a contraceptive implant involves distinguishing it from other LARC and hormonal methods to find the optimal match.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hormonal intrauterine device (IUD):} A LARC method that acts locally within the uterus, associated with lower systemic hormone levels and a higher rate of amenorrhoea, but requiring an intrauterine procedure.
    \item \textbf{Depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA) injection:} A progestogen-only method requiring injections every 12 weeks, which has a higher typical-use failure rate and can cause a delayed return to baseline fertility.
    \item \textbf{Combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP):} A daily oral contraceptive containing oestrogen, which provides regular withdrawal bleeding and acne control but carries higher thromboembolic risk and requires daily compliance.
    \item \textbf{MEC Category 4 Contraindications:} Absolute contraindications where the implant must not be used, including active breast cancer, severe liver tumors, or decompensated cirrhosis.
    \item \textbf{MEC Category 3 Conditions:} Conditions where risks generally outweigh advantages, such as unexplained abnormal vaginal bleeding (prior to diagnosis), acute deep vein thrombosis, or active ischaemic heart disease.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should contact their general practitioner or sexual health clinic if they can no longer feel the implant in their arm, if the implant appears bent or broken, or if they develop signs of local infection or severe bleeding.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) immediately following the insertion procedure, such as swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or severe difficulty breathing.
    \item Sudden, severe chest pain or shortness of breath (a rare thromboembolic complication).
    \item Severe, acute lower abdominal or pelvic pain accompanied by a positive pregnancy test (to rule out ectopic pregnancy, though extremely rare).
    \item Severe, spreading redness, swelling, warmth, and purulent discharge at the insertion site accompanied by fever and chills (suggestive of deep cellulitis or abscess).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Clinical evaluation before insertion ensures patient safety, while post-insertion investigations focus on verifying the presence and location of the device if it becomes impalpable.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy screening:} A urine beta-hCG test performed prior to insertion if the patient has had unprotected intercourse or irregular menstrual cycles, to avoid inserting the rod during pregnancy.
    \item \textbf{Physical palpation check:} Immediate manual examination by the inserting clinician to confirm the rod is correctly positioned subcutaneously in the upper arm.
    \item \textbf{High-frequency soft-tissue ultrasound:} The primary imaging modality used to locate an impalpable or deeply placed implant within the arm.
    \item \textbf{Plain X-ray (radiography):} Utilising the implant's barium sulfate content to locate a lost or migrated device that cannot be identified on ultrasound.
    \item \textbf{Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):} An alternative imaging technique used to locate deeply embedded rods prior to surgical removal.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management involves the precise execution of insertion and removal procedures, managing side effects, and coordinating timely replacements.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Aseptic insertion technique:} Administering local anaesthetic (e.g., 2 mL of 1\% lidocaine), followed by the insertion of the rod using the sterile disposable applicator subdermally on the inner side of the non-dominant arm, 8-10 cm above the medial epicondyle.
    \item \textbf{Standard removal procedure:} Administering local anaesthetic, making a 2 mm longitudinal incision at the distal end of the rod, and gently extracting the implant using hemostatic forceps.
    \item \textbf{Management of unscheduled bleeding:} Treating prolonged bleeding with a short course of combined oral contraceptive pills (COCP) or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) after excluding pelvic pathology or STIs.
    \item \textbf{Timely device replacement:} Removing the old implant and immediately inserting a new rod through the same incision site at the end of the three-year efficacy period.
    \item \textbf{Post-removal fertility care:} Advising the patient that the contraceptive effect reverses rapidly, with etonogestrel clearing from the blood and ovulation returning within weeks of removal.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Complications related to the contraceptive implant are rare, with most occurring during insertion or removal.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Insertion site reactions:} Minor complications including bruising, localized pain, swelling, erythema, hematoma, or superficial wound infection.
    \item \textbf{Deep insertion and migration:} Placement of the rod deep to the subcutaneous tissue (subfascial or intramuscular), which can lead to neurovascular damage during removal or, in extremely rare cases, migration into the pulmonary vasculature.
    \item \textbf{Difficult removal and localization failure:} Structural encapsulation of the rod by fibrous tissue or inability to locate the device, requiring specialized ultrasound-guided surgical removal.
    \item \textbf{Unscheduled bleeding discontinuation:} Premature removal of the device due to user intolerance of unpredictable spotting or prolonged bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Device failure and ectopic pregnancy:} Although the risk of pregnancy is extremely low, if fertilisation does occur, there is an increased relative risk of the pregnancy being ectopic.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for preventing pregnancy with the contraceptive implant is exceptional, with a success rate exceeding 99.95\% over its three-year lifespan. Bleeding changes are expected, but they tend to stabilize after the first three to six months. Studies show high user satisfaction, with continuation rates of approximately 75\% to 80\% at the end of the first year. Future fertility is fully preserved; the return to baseline ovulatory function is rapid, occurring within 7 to 30 days following removal, allowing for immediate conception planning.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Patients can support the safety and efficacy of their contraceptive implant by following basic wound care and monitoring guidelines.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Regular self-palpation:} Gently checking the upper arm with fingers once a month to confirm the implant is still in place.
    \item \textbf{Post-procedure wound care:} Leaving the pressure bandage on for 24 hours and the small adhesive strip (Steri-Strip) intact for 3 to 5 days to prevent wound dehiscence or infection.
    \item \textbf{Symptom tracking:} Recording any episodes of spotting or bleeding in a calendar or mobile app to assist the doctor in managing cycle changes.
    \item \textbf{Preserving the user card:} Keeping the patient card containing the insertion date, batch number, and the exact date when the implant is due to be replaced.
    \item \textbf{Informing other clinicians:} Telling all healthcare providers about the presence of the implant, particularly when starting new medications, to check for potential drug interactions (e.g., with hepatic enzyme-inducing anticonvulsants).
\end{itemize}
