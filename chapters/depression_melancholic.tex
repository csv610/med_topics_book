\chapter{Melancholic Depression}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Melancholic depression, also called melancholia, is a specific and severe form of major depressive disorder characterised by profound loss of pleasure in almost all activities, deep sadness, and marked slowing of movement and thinking. People with melancholic depression often have features such as early-morning waking, weight loss, and symptoms that are worse in the morning. It is distinguished from other forms of depression by its severity, its biological features, and its good response to physical treatments such as antidepressant medication and electroconvulsive therapy (ECT). This chapter explains the features, diagnosis, and treatment of melancholic depression.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Melancholic depression accounts for roughly 15--30\% of people with major depressive disorder, and it is more common in older adults and in people with more severe illness. It occurs across all ages and in both sexes, though some studies find it slightly more common in men than other subtypes. It is associated with high rates of hospitalisation, functional impairment, and suicide risk. Melancholia is frequently under-recognised, especially when it presents as physical symptoms or appears with ageing, and it may be more common in people with a family history of severe depression.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Brain changes:} melancholia is associated with overactivity of the stress hormone axis (the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis) and elevated cortisol
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} a family history of severe or psychotic depression increases risk
    \item \textbf{Neurotransmitters:} disturbances in serotonin, noradrenaline, and dopamine systems contribute
    \item \textbf{Distinct biology:} melancholic depression has a more pronounced biological signature than other subtypes, which is why physical treatments are effective
    \item \textbf{Stress:} significant life events can trigger episodes in vulnerable people
    \item \textbf{Neuroimaging:} structural and functional changes in brain regions involved in emotion and reward
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Profound anhedonia: loss of pleasure in almost all activities, including those previously enjoyed
    \item Depressed mood that does not lift even when good things happen
    \item Psychomotor changes: slowing of movement and thinking, or agitation
    \item Early-morning waking, with mood at its worst in the morning
    \item Significant weight loss and loss of appetite
    \item Excessive guilt and self-blame
    \item Difficulty concentrating and memory problems
    \item Symptoms are severe and persistent, with substantial impairment in function
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Non-melancholic depression, which is more responsive to environment and has less biological disturbance
    \item Psychotic depression, in which delusions or hallucinations occur
    \item Bipolar depression, in which there is a history of mania or hypomania
    \item Dementia and other cognitive disorders in older adults
    \item Medical conditions causing depression, such as thyroid disease, anaemia, or neurological disorders
    \item Medication-induced depression or substance use
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with severe, persistent low mood, loss of pleasure, changes in sleep and appetite, or thoughts of death should see a doctor urgently. Melancholic depression is a serious illness that responds well to treatment, and early treatment reduces the risk of complications.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Thoughts of suicide, self-harm, or concerns that the person is in danger
    \item Inability to eat, drink, or care for oneself
    \item Severe agitation, confusion, or psychosis
    \item Sudden worsening with signs of physical illness
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Support lines:} Lifeline (13 11 14) and Suicide Call Back Service (1300 659 467) are available 24 hours.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical interview:} detailed assessment of mood, pleasure, sleep, appetite, movement, and thoughts
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic criteria:} melancholic features are specified in diagnostic classifications
    \item \textbf{Physical examination and blood tests:} exclude medical causes such as thyroid disease, vitamin deficiency, and anaemia
    \item \textbf{Assessment of severity and risk:} including suicide risk assessment
    \item \textbf{Family history:} enquire about severe depression and bipolar disorder in relatives
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} to a psychiatrist for severe or treatment-resistant cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antidepressant medication:} the first-line treatment, with tricyclic antidepressants and newer agents both effective; response may take several weeks
    \item \textbf{Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT):} the most rapidly effective treatment for severe melancholic depression, especially with psychosis, catatonia, or high suicide risk
    \item \textbf{Combination treatment:} medication combined with psychological therapy for the best long-term outcomes
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} for severe illness, risk of self-harm, or inability to function
    \item \textbf{Maintenance treatment:} continued medication to prevent relapse after recovery
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} structured therapy can help, though melancholia is less responsive to psychotherapy alone
    \item \textbf{Managing physical health:} nutrition, activity, and treatment of co-existing medical conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Suicide, the most serious complication of severe depression
    \item Malnutrition, dehydration, and physical decline from poor intake
    \item Psychotic features in severe cases
    \item Cognitive impairment that can be mistaken for dementia in older adults
    \item Chronic or recurrent depression and treatment resistance
    \item Impaired work, relationships, and quality of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Melancholic depression responds well to physical treatments: the majority of people improve significantly with adequate doses of antidepressant medication, and ECT produces remission in a high proportion of severe cases, often within weeks. Without treatment, episodes can last many months and recurrence is common, so maintenance treatment is important. With appropriate and sustained treatment, most people recover and regain their previous level of function, although some experience recurrent episodes requiring long-term management.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek help early when symptoms of depression develop
    \item Take medication exactly as prescribed, even when feeling better
    \item Maintain a regular routine of sleep, meals, and activity
    \item Engage in gentle exercise, which improves mood
    \item Avoid alcohol and other substances
    \item Stay connected with supportive people
    \item Have a relapse plan and attend follow-up appointments
    \item Family members should encourage treatment and provide support without blame
\end{itemize}
