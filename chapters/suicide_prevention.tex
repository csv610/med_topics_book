\section*{Definition and Overview}
Suicide prevention is the collective effort, by individuals, families, communities, health services, and governments, to reduce the number of deaths by suicide and the harm caused by suicidal behaviour. It rests on a simple but powerful idea: most suicides are preventable. People who are suicidal are almost always experiencing a temporary crisis or an underlying illness that can be treated, and the moment of highest danger usually passes if the person is connected to help, support, and safety. Preventing suicide is therefore not one action but many, from a friend who asks a difficult question, to a GP who treats depression, to national policy that improves access to crisis services.

Suicide is a leading cause of death in many countries, and its impact spreads far beyond the person who dies: every death by suicide is associated with the profound grief of family, friends, and communities, and with an increased risk of further suicides. This chapter explains what suicide prevention involves at each level, from what an individual can do when they are worried about someone, to the treatments that work, and the broader public health measures that save lives. It also addresses common fears, such as the belief that asking about suicide puts the idea in someone's head, which is not true; asking with care is one of the most protective things anyone can do. The message of this chapter is hopeful and practical: suicide prevention works, everyone has a role, and help is available.

\section*{Epidemiology}
In many countries, around 3,000 people die by suicide each year, and suicide remains the leading cause of death among young people in some age groups, with especially high rates among young adults. Men account for roughly three-quarters of deaths by suicide, while women make more attempts. Rates vary across the community: Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people, rural and remote communities, people living with mental illness, those in contact with the criminal justice system, and people who have experienced violence or abuse all carry elevated risk. Around a quarter of people who die by suicide were not known to be at risk, which is why community-level awareness matters alongside clinical care.

For every death by suicide there are many more attempts, and for every attempt many more people with suicidal thoughts. Suicide is rare relative to the amount of suicidal thinking in the community, which is reassuring and also instructive: it means the steps between thinking, attempting, and dying are opportunities for intervention. Over recent decades, some countries have developed a national suicide prevention strategy, established crisis services such as a local crisis service and a local youth crisis service, and funded targeted programs. While rates have not fallen as much as anyone would wish, the infrastructure of support, research, and community awareness that exists today is far stronger than it was, and this infrastructure saves lives.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Suicide is never the result of a single cause. It arises at the intersection of individual vulnerability, mental illness, and life circumstances, against a background of social and environmental factors. The best-supported way to think about it is through the "biopsychosocial" model: biological factors such as inherited mood disorders, a family history of suicide, and brain differences in how stress and impulse are processed; psychological factors such as hopelessness, impulsivity, and the feeling of being trapped or a burden; and social factors such as isolation, bereavement, relationship breakdown, financial strain, and exposure to the suicide of others.

The path to suicide is often not linear. Many people experience fluctuating risk: periods of distress punctuated by calmer times, with acute crises triggered by events such as a relationship ending, a legal problem, intoxication, or the anniversary of a loss. This has two practical implications for prevention. First, limiting access to lethal means during high-risk periods genuinely saves lives, because it converts the period of maximum danger into time for help to arrive. Second, because risk fluctuates, prevention cannot be a single event; it is an ongoing connection, maintained through follow-up appointments, safety planning, and the presence of people who care.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mental illness:} depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder are the strongest individual risk factors
    \item \textbf{Substance misuse:} alcohol and drugs increase impulsivity and are involved in a large share of suicides
    \item \textbf{Previous attempts:} a prior attempt or significant self-harm is one of the strongest predictors of later suicide
    \item \textbf{Life crises:} relationship breakdown, bereavement, job loss, financial difficulty, and legal problems
    \item \textbf{Isolation:} social disconnection and living alone amplify risk
    \item \textbf{Trauma and violence:} childhood abuse, intimate partner violence, and discrimination
    \item \textbf{Exposure to suicide:} knowing someone who died by suicide increases vulnerability, and media reporting can also influence rates
    \item \textbf{Chronic physical illness:} pain, disability, and serious or terminal illness are associated with higher risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{What Suicide Prevention Looks Like}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Community awareness and education:} teaching people to recognise warning signs and to ask about suicide directly; programs such as gatekeeper training equip ordinary people to respond
    \item \textbf{Crisis services:} telephone and online counselling through a a local crisis service, Suicide Call Back Service 1300 659 467, a local youth crisis service 1800 55 1800, and a local culturally appropriate crisis service for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people
    \item \textbf{Accessible mental health care:} timely treatment of depression and other conditions through GPs, psychologists, psychiatrists, and public services
    \item \textbf{Safety planning:} a written plan that lists warning signs, coping strategies, people to contact, and ways to make the environment safer
    \item \textbf{Means restriction:} reducing access to firearms, large quantities of medicines, and other lethal means, which is one of the most effective interventions known
    \item \textbf{Follow-up after attempts:} prompt, compassionate contact after an attempt or after discharge from hospital dramatically reduces repeat risk
    \item \textbf{Support for bereaved families:} care for people who have lost someone to suicide, because they are themselves at higher risk
    \item \textbf{Responsible media reporting:} guidance that avoids sensational detail and instead shares support information and stories of recovery
    \item \textbf{Population-level programs:} policies that address housing, employment, financial stress, and substance misuse, and targeted programs for high-risk groups
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Do not wait for a crisis to be obvious before seeking help. If you are struggling, or if someone you care about is struggling, speak to a GP, a counsellor, or a crisis service early. Anyone who has made an attempt, harmed themselves, or expressed suicidal thoughts should be assessed, and anyone who has had a significant change in mood, withdrawal, or talk of hopelessness deserves a professional conversation.

If you need support right now: a a a local crisis service, a local mental-health support service 1300 22 4636, a local youth crisis service 1800 55 1800, and a local culturally appropriate crisis service (13 92 76) for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item An attempt that has occurred, such as an overdose or self-harm requiring medical care
    \item A clear threat to end life, with a plan and the means to carry it out
    \item Someone who has expressed suicidal intent and is now out of contact
    \item Behaviour indicating imminent danger, including giving away possessions or saying goodbye
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Direct questioning:} asking someone directly and calmly whether they have thoughts of harming themselves or dying; contrary to common fear, this reduces rather than increases risk
    \item \textbf{Suicide risk assessment:} an assessment of the nature of any thoughts, plans, access to means, intent, and protective factors, usually by a GP or mental health professional
    \item \textbf{Mental health assessment:} screening for depression, bipolar disorder, psychosis, and substance misuse
    \item \textbf{Physical assessment:} review of medications, alcohol, and physical illness that may contribute
    \item \textbf{Follow-up planning:} arranging review within days, not weeks, after a crisis, because the period after discharge is one of highest risk
    \item \textbf{Involvement of family and supports:} a safe discharge plan that involves the people around the person, with their consent
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} public and private mental health services for comprehensive assessment and treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Treat the underlying illness:} effective treatment of depression, anxiety, and other conditions is the foundation of prevention
    \item \textbf{Psychological therapy:} cognitive behaviour therapy and specialised approaches such as dialectical behaviour therapy reduce suicidal behaviour and are effective in the long term
    \item \textbf{Medication:} antidepressants and other medicines help, especially combined with therapy, with careful monitoring early in treatment
    \item \textbf{Alcohol and drug treatment:} addressing substance misuse removes a major amplifier of risk
    \item \textbf{Safety planning:} a personalised written plan, agreed with a professional, that the person can follow during a crisis
    \item \textbf{Limiting access to means:} safe storage or removal of medicines, weapons, and other lethal means during high-risk periods
    \item \textbf{Intensive support:} regular contact through follow-up calls, community teams, or case management, particularly in the weeks after a crisis or discharge
    \item \textbf{Peer support:} connection with others who have lived experience of suicidal thoughts can provide understanding and hope
    \item \textbf{Care of the carers:} family members and friends need support themselves, so they can keep helping without being overwhelmed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Death by suicide:} the outcome that prevention aims to avoid, and one that can occur even with care in place, which is why every preventive layer matters
    \item \textbf{Attempts with injury:} surviving with serious physical injury, sometimes permanent disability
    \item \textbf{Repeated crises:} without sustained treatment, suicidal behaviour can recur
    \item \textbf{Burden on family and community:} the distress and grief of those close to the person, and elevated risk among them
    \item \textbf{Complicated grief:} bereavement after suicide is associated with prolonged, intense grief and increased health problems
    \item \textbf{Service gaps:} gaps in follow-up and access to care are themselves a risk factor, which is why planning for continuity is essential
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The evidence that suicide prevention works is strong and growing. Measures such as means restriction, follow-up after attempts, crisis services, and treatment of depression are all associated with reduced rates of death by suicide. At the individual level, people who survive a suicide attempt and receive comprehensive follow-up have a greatly reduced risk of a further attempt, and the large majority never die by suicide. Recovery from suicidal thinking is not only possible but common; with treatment, support, and time, people move beyond the crisis and go on to live full lives. The outlook depends heavily on continuity of care, which is why the follow-up period after a crisis is treated as critically important. For communities, the consistent message of prevention research is that the combined effect of many small, caring, evidence-based actions is measurable: awareness saves lives, services save lives, and every person who reaches out and is met with kindness shifts the odds in favour of recovery.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Learn the warning signs of suicide, and if you see them in someone, ask directly and listen without judgement
    \item Keep crisis numbers saved and share them with the people around you: a a local crisis service, a local mental-health support service 1300 22 4636, a local youth crisis service 1800 55 1800, and a local culturally appropriate crisis service 13 92 76
    \item If you are worried about someone, involve others: a GP, a trusted family member, or a crisis service; you do not have to manage it alone
    \item Do not leave someone who is in immediate danger alone, and remove or secure access to means
    \item Look after yourself if you are supporting someone in crisis; carers need rest, support, and their own check-ins
    \item Support responsible media and online content about suicide, which influences community risk
    \item If you have been affected by suicide, seek support for yourself; bereavement after suicide is a genuine risk period
    \item Talk about mental health openly in your family, school, workplace, and community; connection and conversation are prevention
\end{itemize}
