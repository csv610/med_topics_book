\chapter{Arthroscopy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Arthroscopy is a minimally invasive, keyhole surgical technique used to inspect, diagnose, and treat problems inside a joint. A narrow telescope called an arthroscope, fitted with a light and camera, is passed into the joint through a small skin incision, and the images are displayed on a screen. Fine instruments are inserted through one or more additional small incisions so that damaged tissue can be repaired without the large exposure of open surgery. Arthroscopy is used most often for the knee and shoulder, but it can also be performed on the hip, ankle, elbow, and wrist. In most cases the procedure is performed as day surgery.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Arthroscopy is one of the most common orthopaedic procedures performed worldwide, with many hundreds of thousands of operations each year. It is undertaken across all ages, from young athletes with ligament and cartilage injuries to older adults with degenerative joint damage. The knee and shoulder account for the large majority of procedures. Advances in optics, instruments, and anaesthesia have made routine arthroscopy a safe, short-stay procedure, and its use continues to expand as indications and techniques develop.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Arthroscopy is used to diagnose and treat a range of injuries and conditions affecting a joint. The problems addressed include both traumatic and degenerative pathology.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Torn cartilage:} meniscal tears in the knee and labral tears in the shoulder and hip
    \item \textbf{Ligament injuries:} such as tears of the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) in the knee
    \item \textbf{Articular cartilage damage:} chondral flaps, loose fragments, or early osteoarthritic change
    \item \textbf{Loose bodies:} free fragments of bone or cartilage floating within the joint and causing locking
    \item \textbf{Synovial problems:} chronic inflammation, scarring, or overgrowth of the joint lining
    \item \textbf{Infection:} arthroscopic washout and drainage of a septic joint
    \item \textbf{Unexplained symptoms:} pain, swelling, or locking when clinical examination and imaging have not reached a diagnosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms before arthroscopy reflect the underlying joint problem.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain, swelling, or stiffness in the affected joint
    \item Clicking, catching, or locking during movement
    \item Instability or a sensation of the joint giving way under load
    \item Reduced range of movement or difficulty with weight-bearing
    \item Restricted function in sport, work, or daily activities
\end{itemize}

After arthroscopy, mild pain, swelling, and bruising around the small incisions are normal for the first few days and settle with rest, ice, and elevation.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Conditions that can produce similar joint symptoms and may need to be distinguished before arthroscopy include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Osteoarthritis and inflammatory arthritis
    \item Ligament sprains and strains that settle without surgery
    \item Tendinopathy and bursitis around the joint
    \item Referred pain from another area, such as hip pathology causing knee pain
    \item Stress fractures or other bone pathology
    \item Septic arthritis or crystal arthropathy such as gout and pseudogout
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the surgical team after arthroscopy if you develop severe or worsening pain not controlled by prescribed analgesia, increasing redness, warmth, swelling, or discharge from an incision, a persistent fever above 38 degrees Celsius, calf pain or swelling, chest pain, or breathlessness, a joint that feels unstable, gives way, or locks, or numbness or weakness in the limb.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden chest pain or severe breathlessness, which may indicate a pulmonary embolism
    \item Collapse or loss of consciousness
    \item Signs of a severe allergic reaction or anaphylaxis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the mechanism of injury, the nature of symptoms, and the effect on function
    \item \textbf{Examination:} joint stability, range of movement, swelling, effusion, and point tenderness
    \item \textbf{X-ray:} to exclude fractures, assess alignment, and look for arthritis
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} useful for tendon and bursal assessment in some joints
    \item \textbf{MRI:} the best imaging modality for cartilage, ligaments, and menisci or labra
    \item \textbf{Arthroscopy itself:} direct visual inspection remains the most accurate diagnostic test for intra-articular pathology
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anaesthesia:} general anaesthesia, or regional techniques such as spinal anaesthesia or a nerve block, depending on the joint and procedure
    \item \textbf{Operative techniques:} trimming or repair of torn cartilage, ligament reconstruction, labral repair, removal of loose bodies, debridement of damaged tissue, or washout of infection
    \item \textbf{Same-day discharge:} most arthroscopy is performed as day surgery with planned analgesia
    \item \textbf{Pain management:} simple analgesia, ice, elevation, and a short period of activity restriction
    \item \textbf{Weight-bearing:} crutches may be advised for a short time, depending on the procedure performed
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} a graded rehabilitation programme to restore strength, range, and function
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} clinic review to assess healing and plan a graded return to work and sport
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection in the joint or at the incisions, which is uncommon but serious when it occurs
    \item Bleeding into the joint causing marked swelling
    \item Deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism, especially after procedures on the leg
    \item Stiffness, persistent pain, or failure to improve
    \item Damage to nerves, blood vessels, or joint structures during the procedure
    \item Rarely, the need for a further operation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people recover well from arthroscopy, with small scars, a short recovery, and a quicker return to activity than after open surgery. The outcome depends on the underlying problem: ligament and meniscal repairs in younger patients usually do very well, whereas procedures for established arthritis may give only partial or temporary benefit. Full recovery of strength and endurance takes weeks to months and depends on consistent, structured rehabilitation.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Strengthen the muscles around joints to protect them from injury
    \item Use correct technique and appropriate equipment in sport
    \item Warm up properly before activity and cool down afterwards
    \item Maintain a healthy weight to reduce stress on the joints
    \item Follow the post-operative rehabilitation plan exactly, including prescribed exercises
    \item Attend follow-up appointments and report any concerning symptoms early
\end{itemize}
