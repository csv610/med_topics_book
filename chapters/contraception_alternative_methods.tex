\section*{Definition and Overview}
"Alternative" contraceptive methods is the term commonly used for the non-hormonal, user-dependent options that couples can use without a device being fitted or an injection or implant being given by a clinician. They include fertility awareness-based methods (also called natural family planning), which involve tracking signs of ovulation to time intercourse away from the fertile window, the withdrawal method, lactational amenorrhoea during breastfeeding, and spermicides. These methods need no prescription, but they rely heavily on correct and consistent use, so their effectiveness is generally lower than that of hormonal or long-acting methods.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Fertility awareness and other alternative methods are used by a minority of couples in most high-income countries, while the withdrawal method is among the most widely reported methods worldwide. These options appeal to couples who want to avoid hormones, spend nothing, or follow religious or cultural preferences. Typical-use failure rates are considerably higher than with implants, injections, or IUDs: roughly 1 in 5 women using a user-dependent method may fall pregnant within the first year, although perfect-use rates are much lower.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Each alternative method prevents pregnancy by a different mechanism:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fertility awareness methods:} identify the fertile window by tracking menstrual dates (calendar method), basal body temperature, cervical mucus changes, or a combination of signs
    \item \textbf{Withdrawal (coitus interruptus):} the penis is removed from the vagina before ejaculation so that sperm does not enter the vagina
    \item \textbf{Lactational amenorrhoea method:} exclusive, frequent breastfeeding suppresses ovulation for up to about 6 months after birth, provided periods have not returned
    \item \textbf{Spermicides:} chemicals placed in the vagina immobilise sperm before they can reach the cervix
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
As these are not diseases, the "clinical features" are the observations a user relies on:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Regular, predictable menstrual cycles are needed for calendar-based methods to be reliable
    \item Cervical mucus becomes clear, slippery, and stretchy around ovulation
    \item Basal body temperature rises slightly (about 0.2--0.5 degrees) after ovulation
    \item Withdrawal and spermicides cause no visible changes, which makes correct use difficult to confirm
    \item Absence of periods is expected while exclusively breastfeeding in the first 6 months after birth
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When relying on these methods, certain symptoms need checking because they can signal problems:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Missed or delayed period --- pregnancy must be excluded if the method may have failed
    \item Irregular cycles --- reduce the reliability of fertility awareness and may indicate hormonal or other gynaecological problems
    \item Vaginal discharge --- normal mucus changes around ovulation can be confused with infection such as candidiasis, bacterial vaginosis, or an STI
    \item Bleeding patterns after childbirth --- can be mistaken for the return of fertility or the opposite
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a GP or family planning clinic for help choosing or using a method correctly, for a pregnancy test if a period is late, and for emergency contraception if unprotected sex has occurred during the fertile window.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services} for:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heavy vaginal bleeding with severe abdominal or pelvic pain
    \item Shoulder-tip pain with bleeding and a positive pregnancy test (possible ectopic pregnancy)
    \item Fever with pelvic pain or abnormal discharge
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test:} urine or blood beta-hCG if a period is missed or the method may have failed
    \item \textbf{Menstrual and cycle diary:} to confirm cycles are regular enough for fertility awareness
    \item \textbf{Pelvic examination and swabs:} to assess discharge or pain and screen for STIs if a barrier has not been used
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} if cycles are irregular, to look for causes of ovulatory problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Education and counselling:} detailed instruction in the specific method substantially improves effectiveness
    \item \textbf{Combined use:} fertility awareness plus a barrier method (condoms) during the fertile window
    \item \textbf{Backup contraception:} emergency contraception (such as the levonorgestrel or ulipristal acetate pill, or a copper IUD) if unprotected sex occurs near ovulation
    \item \textbf{Spermicides:} placed high in the vagina shortly before intercourse and reapplied with repeat acts
    \item \textbf{Condoms:} recommended as well, since none of these alternative methods protects against sexually transmitted infections
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unplanned pregnancy if the method fails or is used incorrectly
    \item Increased risk of sexually transmitted infections when no barrier is used
    \item Irritation or allergy with spermicide use
    \item Difficulty tracking fertility reliably after childbirth, with irregular cycles, or approaching menopause
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With perfect, consistent use, fertility awareness methods can be effective in preventing pregnancy, and lactational amenorrhoea is reliable within its limited window. In everyday use, however, many couples find these methods hard to sustain, and failure rates are noticeably higher than with hormonal or long-acting methods. Couples who find a method easy to use consistently often continue with it for many years; for others, switching to a more reliable method is the better choice.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Receive formal instruction before relying on fertility awareness
    \item Track cycle dates, temperature, and mucus every day without skipping
    \item Use a barrier method or abstain during the fertile window
    \item Keep emergency contraception available as a backup
    \item Use condoms to prevent sexually transmitted infections
    \item Seek advice after childbirth or if cycles become irregular
\end{itemize}
