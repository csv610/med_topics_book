\section*{Definition and Overview}
A tapeworm is a type of parasitic flatworm that can live in the human intestine. Tapeworms belong to a group of parasites called cestodes, and their bodies are made up of a small head, called a scolex, followed by a chain of flat segments known as proglottids, which grow continuously behind the head. In humans, the adult worm attaches to the lining of the small intestine, where it absorbs nutrients from the food being digested. The most important tapeworms that affect people are the pork tapeworm (Taenia solium), the beef tapeworm (Taenia saginata), the fish tapeworm (Diphyllobothrium latum), and the dwarf tapeworm (Hymenolepis nana). People become infected by eating raw or undercooked meat or fish that contains tapeworm larvae, or, in the case of the dwarf tapeworm, by swallowing the eggs from food, water, or hands contaminated with faeces. Most people who carry an adult worm have no symptoms, or only mild ones. However, if a person swallows the eggs of the pork tapeworm, the eggs can form cysts in the tissues, including the brain, a disease called cysticercosis that can cause seizures and is a major cause of epilepsy in parts of the world. This chapter explains how tapeworm infections are caught, recognised, and safely treated and prevented, with an emphasis on the local situation.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Tapeworm infections are found worldwide, but their distribution varies greatly with diet, sanitation, and farming practices. Intestinal infection with the adult worm is most common in regions where people eat raw or undercooked pork or beef, particularly in parts of Asia, Africa, Eastern Europe, and Latin America. The pork tapeworm, and the cysticercosis that results from its eggs, is a major public-health problem in many low-income countries, where it is one of the leading preventable causes of epilepsy. In many countries, intestinal tapeworm infection is uncommon, and most cases are diagnosed in people who have travelled to endemic regions, in recent migrants, or in people who have eaten raw or lightly cooked imported meat or fish. The fish tapeworm is caught by eating raw or undercooked freshwater fish, and cases occasionally follow the consumption of imported raw fish products. The dwarf tapeworm is the most common tapeworm worldwide and is spread directly from person to person through contaminated hands and food, with children and people in crowded or poorly sanitised conditions most affected. Overall, improved meat inspection, freezing of meat, and better sanitation have made tapeworm infection rare in countries like some countries, but it remains an important diagnosis to consider in the right circumstances, because the treatment is simple and the consequences of a missed pork tapeworm infection can be serious.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Tapeworms have complex life cycles that alternate between humans and other animals, and understanding these cycles explains both how people are infected and how infection is prevented.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Beef and pork tapeworms:} humans catch the adult worm by eating undercooked meat containing larval cysts. The larva attaches in the intestine and grows into an adult worm that releases eggs into the faeces; when cattle or pigs eat contaminated pasture or feed, the cycle continues
    \item \textbf{Fish tapeworm:} caught by eating raw or undercooked freshwater fish containing larvae; the adult worm grows very long and can absorb vitamin B12, causing anaemia in some people
    \item \textbf{Dwarf tapeworm:} the only tapeworm that can complete its whole life cycle inside a single human host, so it spreads easily from person to person through contaminated hands, food, or water, and can also be caught from fleas or beetles in grain
    \item \textbf{Cysticercosis:} if a person swallows pork tapeworm eggs, from contaminated food or hands, the eggs hatch into larvae that travel through the blood and form cysts in muscle, skin, eyes, or the brain, where they cause the disease neurocysticercosis
    \item \textbf{Adult worm in the gut:} the worm's head is armed or sucker-like and anchors to the intestinal wall, while the growing chain of segments absorbs nutrients, and mature segments break off and pass out of the body in the stool
\end{itemize}

The presence of an adult worm in the intestine rarely causes serious harm, because it competes only mildly for nutrients. The potentially serious disease is cysticercosis, which occurs when eggs are swallowed, not when meat is eaten, and this is why hygiene after contact with an infected person is so important.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Most adults with an intestinal tapeworm have no symptoms, and many are unaware they are infected until they notice worm segments in their stool. When symptoms do occur, they are usually mild:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Passing segments in stool:} the most distinctive feature; small, flat, whitish, motile segments appear in the faeces or around the anus, and may be mistaken for grains of rice
    \item \textbf{Abdominal discomfort:} vague pain, bloating, or cramping in the upper or mid abdomen
    \item \textbf{Nausea and poor appetite:} some people feel nauseated, especially in the morning, or lose interest in food
    \item \textbf{Diarrhoea or altered bowel habit:} loose stools or constipation can occur, though many notice no change
    \item \textbf{Weight loss:} occasionally present despite a normal diet, because the worm absorbs nutrients
    \item \textbf{Anal itching:} segments crawling near the anus can cause itching or irritation
    \item \textbf{Anaemia:} with fish tapeworm infection, vitamin B12 deficiency can develop, causing tiredness, weakness, and sometimes nerve tingling
    \item \textbf{Seizures and headaches:} the key features of neurocysticercosis, when pork tapeworm larvae form cysts in the brain; seizures are the commonest presentation
    \item \textbf{Lumps under the skin:} painless nodules can form where larvae encyst in muscle or skin
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Intestinal tapeworm infection produces symptoms that overlap with many common gut conditions, and the diagnosis is usually confirmed by the characteristic segments or by laboratory testing.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Threadworm (pinworm):} the commonest worm in children, causes anal itching and occasionally visible worms, but the worms are thin, thread-like, and much smaller than tapeworm segments
    \item \textbf{Giardia and other protozoa:} cause diarrhoea, bloating, and cramping after overseas travel, and are separated from worms by stool testing
    \item \textbf{Irritable bowel syndrome:} bloating, cramping, and altered bowel habit are common in IBS and can be wrongly attributed to worms; a negative stool test and no segments help separate the two
    \item \textbf{Coeliac disease:} chronic bloating, diarrhoea, and weight loss can resemble tapeworm infection, but coeliac antibody blood tests are specific
    \item \textbf{Food poisoning or gastroenteritis:} acute diarrhoea and vomiting after a meal can be mistaken for a worm infection, though gastroenteritis settles within days
    \item \textbf{Epilepsy from other causes:} seizures from neurocysticercosis must be separated from other causes of epilepsy using brain imaging and blood tests
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a general practitioner if you pass worm segments in your stool, if you have persistent bloating, diarrhoea, or abdominal pain after travelling or eating raw meat or fish, or if you or someone in your household has unexplained anal itching. If you were told you carry a tapeworm, or you know you have been exposed to pork tapeworm eggs, seek advice promptly even if you feel well, because treatment can prevent cysticercosis. Household members of someone with a pork tapeworm should also be checked, because they may have swallowed eggs from contaminated hands.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A first-ever seizure, or repeated or prolonged seizures
    \item Severe headache with vomiting, confusion, or a stiff neck
    \item Sudden weakness, vision loss, or difficulty speaking, which can occur with brain involvement
    \item Signs of severe dehydration from vomiting or diarrhoea
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of tapeworm infection is straightforward for the intestinal worm and more involved when cysticercosis is suspected.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stool examination:} a sample of faeces is examined under the microscope for eggs and worm segments; several samples on different days may be needed because egg shedding is intermittent
    \item \textbf{Identification of segments:} the species of tapeworm can be confirmed from the appearance of the segments and eggs, which determines the treatment and the need to look for cysticercosis
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} serology (antibody or antigen tests) for pork tapeworm is used when cysticercosis is suspected, and a full blood count and vitamin B12 level can reveal anaemia from fish tapeworm
    \item \textbf{Brain imaging:} CT or MRI scans look for cysts and inflammation when neurocysticercosis is suspected, such as in someone with seizures and relevant exposure
    \item \textbf{Family screening:} household contacts may be tested for eggs and treated if needed, to break the cycle of spread
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} people with neurocysticercosis, eye cysts, or infection in pregnancy are managed in consultation with specialists in infectious diseases, neurology, or ophthalmology
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Intestinal tapeworms are treated with simple, well-tolerated medicines, and the outcome is excellent.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Praziquantel:} a single dose of this antihelmintic medicine is highly effective for most adult tapeworm infections; it is taken by mouth and causes the worm to disintegrate and pass out of the body
    \item \textbf{Niclosamide:} an alternative medicine for some tapeworms, which kills the worm's head; it is not absorbed from the gut and is sometimes chosen in pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Treatment of household contacts:} close contacts are often tested and treated to prevent re-infection and to stop the spread of pork tapeworm eggs
    \item \textbf{Treatment of cysticercosis:} neurocysticercosis requires a longer course of an antihelmintic medicine such as albendazole, combined with anti-inflammatory steroids to reduce brain swelling around the dying cysts, and anti-seizure medicines for epilepsy
    \item \textbf{Vitamin B12 replacement:} people with fish tapeworm anaemia are treated with B12 to correct the deficiency
    \item \textbf{Follow-up stool checks:} a stool sample is rechecked a few weeks after treatment to confirm the worm has gone
    \item \textbf{Referral:} eye or brain involvement, and infection during pregnancy, are managed with specialist teams
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Most tapeworm infections in the gut resolve without any complication, but a few situations need attention.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurocysticercosis:} pork tapeworm larvae in the brain cause seizures, headaches, and raised pressure inside the skull, and are a leading preventable cause of epilepsy worldwide
    \item \textbf{Ocular cysticercosis:} larvae in the eye can cause blurred vision, floaters, or permanent vision loss
    \item \textbf{Vitamin B12 deficiency:} the fish tapeworm can compete for B12, causing megaloblastic anaemia with tiredness, tingling, and balance problems
    \item \textbf{Biliary or intestinal blockage:} very rarely, a long worm can obstruct the bile duct or the intestine
    \item \textbf{Gallbladder and appendix problems:} worm segments occasionally migrate into the bile ducts or appendix, causing pain or inflammation
    \item \textbf{Re-infection:} without attention to food hygiene and handwashing, the same person or family can become infected again
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Intestinal tapeworm infection is easily cured. A single dose of the right medicine clears the worm in the great majority of people, symptoms settle quickly, and follow-up testing confirms the infection is gone. The prognosis is also good for cysticercosis in most cases: antihelmintic treatment combined with steroids and seizure medicines controls the infection, and many people become seizure-free, although some need long-term epilepsy treatment if brain scarring has occurred. The fish tapeworm's vitamin B12 deficiency corrects with treatment. The most important message is that tapeworm infection is preventable and treatable, and that the serious complications, particularly neurocysticercosis, are largely avoidable with good hygiene and proper cooking. In many countries, where the infection is uncommon, the key to a good outcome is recognising the possibility and seeking medical review before any harm is done.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cook beef, pork, and other meat thoroughly so that there is no pink flesh in the centre, which kills any tapeworm larvae
    \item Avoid eating raw or lightly cooked freshwater fish, and check whether imported raw fish products have been treated to kill parasites
    \item Freeze meat destined for raw or lightly cooked dishes for several days, as freezing at the correct temperature kills tapeworm larvae
    \item Wash hands thoroughly with soap and water after using the toilet and before preparing or eating food, especially if a household member has a tapeworm
    \item Wash and peel raw fruit and vegetables, and drink safe, treated water when travelling
    \item Ensure meat is purchased from inspected, regulated suppliers rather than from unregulated sources
    \item Treat infected household members and close contacts promptly so that eggs are not passed on
    \item After treatment, follow up with the stool check your doctor recommends, to confirm the infection has cleared
\end{itemize}
