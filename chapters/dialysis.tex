\chapter{Dialysis}
\index{Dialysis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dialysis is a treatment that performs the work of the kidneys when they can no longer filter waste products and excess fluid from the blood. It is used for people with end-stage kidney disease and for some cases of acute kidney injury. There are two main types: haemodialysis, in which blood is filtered through a machine, and peritoneal dialysis, in which the lining of the abdomen is used as a filter. Dialysis is a life-sustaining treatment, not a cure for kidney failure, but it allows people to live active lives while awaiting a transplant or as long-term therapy. This chapter explains how dialysis works, the options available, and what to expect.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Kidney disease is a growing health problem in many countries. Diabetes and high blood pressure are leading causes of kidney failure requiring dialysis, although inherited, inflammatory, infectious, and other conditions can also be responsible. Peritoneal dialysis and haemodialysis may be provided at home or in a centre, depending on health needs, resources, and preference. Kidney transplantation is preferred for some people, but dialysis remains essential for those awaiting or not suitable for transplant.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Kidney failure:} the kidneys lose the ability to filter waste products, balance fluids and electrolytes, and produce hormones
    \item \textbf{Causes:} diabetes, high blood pressure, glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease, and other conditions
    \item \textbf{Haemodialysis:} blood is pumped through a filter (dialyser) where waste and excess fluid pass into a dialysis solution
    \item \textbf{Peritoneal dialysis:} a sterile fluid is run into the abdomen through a catheter, and the peritoneal membrane filters the blood
    \item \textbf{Vascular access:} haemodialysis requires a fistula, graft, or catheter to connect to the bloodstream
    \item \textbf{Effects of kidney failure:} accumulation of urea and other toxins causes nausea, fatigue, and confusion; fluid overload causes swelling and breathlessness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Symptoms of advanced kidney failure: fatigue, nausea, poor appetite, swelling, shortness of breath, and itching
    \item Dialysis sessions remove waste and fluid, improving these symptoms
    \item Haemodialysis is typically three sessions of 4--5 hours per week in a centre, or overnight or shorter sessions at home
    \item Peritoneal dialysis is done at home daily, using exchanges or a machine overnight
    \item The need for dialysis is decided by kidney function tests, symptoms, and overall health
    \item Dialysis is life-long unless a kidney transplant is performed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Haemodialysis versus peritoneal dialysis, chosen based on lifestyle, medical factors, and preference
    \item Dialysis versus conservative (non-dialysis) management for frail or older people
    \item Dialysis versus kidney transplantation as the longer-term goal
    \item Acute versus chronic kidney failure, which affects whether dialysis is temporary or ongoing
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with chronic kidney disease should be under regular specialist care, with dialysis planned well before it is urgently needed. Seek medical attention promptly for symptoms such as breathlessness, swelling, confusion, or significantly reduced urine output.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, chest pain, or severe swelling (possible fluid overload or heart problems)
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or seizures
    \item Severe weakness or collapse
    \item Signs of infection around a dialysis catheter or access site with fever
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Kidney function tests:} blood tests measuring creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)
    \item \textbf{Urine tests:} protein and blood in the urine
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} assessment of kidney size and structure
    \item \textbf{Assessment for dialysis:} discussion of modality, access planning, and education
    \item \textbf{Monitoring on dialysis:} regular blood tests of dialysis adequacy, anaemia, bone health, and electrolytes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Haemodialysis:} three sessions weekly in a dialysis centre, or self-care dialysis at home with training
    \item \textbf{Peritoneal dialysis:} daily exchanges at home, with continuous ambulatory or automated (night-time) options
    \item \textbf{Vascular access:} creation and care of an arteriovenous fistula, the preferred access for haemodialysis
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} phosphate binders, erythropoietin for anaemia, blood pressure medicines, and vitamin D analogues
    \item \textbf{Diet:} a kidney-friendly diet with limits on salt, potassium, phosphate, and fluid
    \item \textbf{Transplant referral:} assessment for kidney transplantation, the best long-term treatment for eligible people
    \item \textbf{Support services:} social work, dietitian, and psychological support
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection of the access site, bloodstream, or peritoneum (peritonitis)
    \item Low blood pressure and cramping during haemodialysis
    \item Anaemia, bone disease, and mineral imbalance from kidney failure
    \item Fluid overload or inadequate dialysis
    \item Cardiovascular disease, which is the leading cause of death in dialysis patients
    \item Depression and reduced quality of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Dialysis is life-sustaining and allows people with kidney failure to live for many years, although survival is lower than the general population, mainly due to cardiovascular disease. Outcomes depend on age, general health, and adherence to treatment. Home dialysis is associated with good outcomes and greater flexibility. Kidney transplantation offers the best long-term survival and quality of life, and many people on dialysis are successfully transplanted.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Manage diabetes and blood pressure to prevent kidney disease progression
    \item Attend dialysis sessions as scheduled or complete exchanges correctly
    \item Care for the access site and report signs of infection promptly
    \item Follow the kidney-friendly diet advised by your dietitian
    \item Take medicines as prescribed
    \item Stay active and maintain social connections
    \item Discuss transplant assessment with your kidney team
    \item Seek support for the emotional impact of dialysis
\end{itemize}
