\chapter{Halitosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Halitosis, commonly called bad breath, is an unpleasant odour from the mouth that is persistent or offensive. It is a very common problem that affects a large proportion of the population at some stage. In around 85--90% of cases, the cause lies in the mouth itself: bacteria on the tongue, between the teeth, and around the gums break down food and dead cells, releasing foul-smelling sulphur compounds. Poor oral hygiene, gum disease, and a dry mouth are major contributors. Less commonly, halitosis comes from conditions of the nose, throat, lungs, or digestive system. Most cases are improved dramatically by good oral hygiene and dental care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Halitosis is common: studies suggest around 25--50% of people have noticeable bad breath, and it is a common reason for dental and medical visits. It becomes more common with age and is associated with smoking, gum disease, dry mouth, and certain foods and medicines. Around a quarter of people report that bad breath affects their social life and confidence. Many people who believe they have bad breath do not actually have it (pseudohalitosis), while others are unaware of their own bad breath.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Most halitosis originates in the mouth from bacterial activity.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Oral bacteria:} anaerobic bacteria on the tongue and in the gum pockets break down proteins, releasing volatile sulphur compounds such as hydrogen sulphide
    \item \textbf{Poor oral hygiene:} plaque and food debris provide a substrate for bacteria
    \item \textbf{Tongue coating:} the back of the tongue is the main site of odour production
    \item \textbf{Gum disease:} periodontal pockets harbour odour-producing bacteria
    \item \textbf{Dry mouth:} reduced saliva allows bacteria to flourish; caused by medicines, mouth breathing, dehydration, and some conditions
    \item \textbf{Food:} garlic, onions, alcohol, and coffee contribute
    \item \textbf{Smoking:} causes odour and dry mouth
    \item \textbf{Non-oral causes:} sinusitis, post-nasal drip, tonsil stones, acid reflux, and rare lung or systemic conditions
    \item \textbf{Systemic causes:} diabetes, kidney failure, and liver disease can cause characteristic odours
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Bad breath is often noticed by others rather than the person themselves.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A persistent unpleasant odour from the mouth
    \item A bad taste in the mouth
    \item Dry mouth and thirst
    \item Coated tongue
    \item Bleeding gums or gum disease
    \item Post-nasal drip and throat clearing
    \item Tonsil stones (small white deposits in the tonsils)
    \item Acid reflux or heartburn
    \item Odour that is worse on waking ("morning breath")
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The source of bad breath should be identified before treatment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Oral causes: poor hygiene, tongue coating, gum disease, dental decay, and dry mouth
    \item Nasal and sinus causes: sinusitis, post-nasal drip, and foreign bodies (especially in children)
    \item Tonsil stones and tonsillitis
    \item Gastro-oesophageal reflux and stomach conditions
    \item Respiratory conditions, including lung infection
    \item Systemic conditions: uncontrolled diabetes (fruity odour), kidney failure (fishy odour), and liver failure (musty odour)
    \item Foods, alcohol, tobacco, and medicines
    \item Pseudohalitosis: a perception of bad breath that others cannot detect
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a dentist if bad breath persists despite good oral hygiene, is associated with gum problems, or is accompanied by a bad taste. See a doctor if it is associated with other symptoms, such as post-nasal drip, reflux, or signs of systemic illness.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item This condition is not an emergency; however, seek urgent care for sudden difficulty breathing or swallowing associated with a throat infection
    \item Bad breath with high fever, confusion, or signs of serious infection
    \item Any sign of a dental abscess spreading into the face or neck
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is based on history, examination, and identification of the source.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dental examination:} assessment of oral hygiene, gum disease, and decay
    \item \textbf{Tongue assessment:} coating and odour
    \item \textbf{Organoleptic testing:} sniffing the breath, which remains the standard method
    \item \textbf{Sulphide monitoring:} devices measure sulphur compounds
    \item \textbf{Review of contributing factors:} foods, medicines, smoking, and dry mouth
    \item \textbf{ENT review:} for sinus, tonsil, and nasal causes
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} for suspected systemic causes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Most halitosis resolves with attention to oral hygiene.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Brushing:} brush twice daily, including the gums and the surface of the tongue
    \item \textbf{Tongue cleaning:} using a tongue scraper or brush removes the main source of odour
    \item \textbf{Flossing:} daily interdental cleaning removes food and plaque
    \item \textbf{Professional cleaning:} a dentist or hygienist removes plaque and tartar and treats gum disease
    \item \textbf{Antiseptic mouthwashes:} those containing chlorhexidine or cetylpyridinium reduce bacteria
    \item \textbf{Hydration:} drinking water and managing dry mouth
    \item \textbf{Dental treatment:} treating decay and gum disease
    \item \textbf{Managing non-oral causes:} treating sinusitis, tonsil stones, and reflux
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} reducing alcohol, coffee, and tobacco
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Social embarrassment, reduced confidence, and avoidance of social situations
    \item Relationship and work-related effects
    \item Anxiety and, in some cases, pseudohalitosis with obsessive checking
    \item Missed diagnosis of underlying conditions, including gum disease and systemic illness
    \item Costs of repeated products that do not address the cause
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for halitosis is excellent. Most cases are resolved or greatly improved with consistent oral hygiene, tongue cleaning, and dental care, usually within weeks. Gum disease, the most common underlying cause, is treatable. Where the cause is non-oral, treating it resolves the odour. People who maintain good oral habits keep their breath fresh, and those concerned about perceived bad breath can be reassured by a professional assessment.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Brush teeth twice daily with fluoride toothpaste
    \item Clean the tongue daily with a tongue scraper
    \item Floss or use interdental brushes daily
    \item Visit the dentist regularly, at least yearly
    \item Drink plenty of water and chew sugar-free gum to stimulate saliva
    \item Limit alcohol, coffee, and tobacco
    \item Eat a healthy diet and limit strongly flavoured foods
    \item Manage dry mouth with fluids and, if persistent, discuss with a doctor
    \item Seek professional advice if bad breath persists despite good hygiene
\end{itemize}
