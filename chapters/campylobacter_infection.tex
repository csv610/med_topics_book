\chapter{Campylobacter Infection}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Campylobacter infection is a common bacterial infection of the bowel that causes gastroenteritis, usually with diarrhoea, abdominal cramps, and fever. It is caused by the bacterium \textit{Campylobacter}, most often \textit{Campylobacter jejuni}, and is one of the most frequent causes of food poisoning worldwide.

The infection is usually acquired by eating undercooked poultry or consuming contaminated food, water, or milk. Most people recover within a week without specific treatment, but the infection can be serious in young children, older adults, and people with weakened immune systems, and it can occasionally trigger long-term complications such as reactive arthritis or Guillain--Barré syndrome.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Campylobacter is the most commonly reported cause of bacterial gastroenteritis in Australia and in many developed countries, with tens of thousands of notified cases each year. It can occur at any age but is most common in children and young adults, and it peaks in the warmer months. Most cases occur singly, but outbreaks can follow shared meals, contaminated milk, or contaminated water supplies. Campylobacter is also an important cause of traveller's diarrhoea in regions with poor sanitation.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Campylobacter infection is caused by the \textit{Campylobacter} bacterium, which is spread by the faecal--oral route:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Source:} undercooked or contaminated poultry is the main source; unpasteurised milk, contaminated water, raw or undercooked meat, and contact with infected animals also transmit the infection
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} the bacteria are swallowed and multiply in the small and large bowel, damaging the gut lining
    \item \textbf{Incubation:} symptoms usually begin two to five days after exposure
    \item \textbf{Infection dose:} only a small number of bacteria are needed to cause illness
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} eating out, handling raw poultry, drinking untreated water, and travel to areas with poor sanitation increase the risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms develop suddenly, usually within a few days of exposure:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Watery diarrhoea, which may become bloody
    \item Abdominal cramps and pain, which can be severe
    \item Fever, chills, and a general feeling of being unwell
    \item Nausea and sometimes vomiting
    \item Headache and muscle aches
    \item Dehydration from fluid loss, especially in young children and older adults
    \item Symptoms usually last several days and can continue for up to a week or more
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The illness resembles other causes of gastroenteritis and diarrhoea:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Other bacterial bowel infections, such as \textit{Salmonella}, \textit{Shigella}, and \textit{E.\ coli}
    \item Viral gastroenteritis, such as norovirus or rotavirus
    \item Parasitic infections, such as giardia or cryptosporidium
    \item Inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn disease, ulcerative colitis) presenting with diarrhoea and cramps
    \item Appendicitis or other abdominal emergencies when pain is the predominant feature
    \item Food poisoning from ingested toxins rather than infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most people with campylobacter recover at home with fluids and rest, but medical assessment is needed for severe or persistent illness.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Blood in the stool, or very frequent and heavy diarrhoea
    \item Signs of severe dehydration, such as little or no urine, extreme thirst, sunken eyes, or fainting
    \item High fever, severe abdominal pain, or confusion
    \item Diarrhoea in a very young baby, a frail older adult, or someone with a weakened immune system
    \item An inability to keep fluids down because of vomiting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Campylobacter is diagnosed by testing a stool sample, and further tests may be needed in severe cases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stool culture:} a stool sample is tested in the laboratory to grow and identify the bacterium, which also allows antibiotic sensitivity testing
    \item \textbf{Molecular testing:} PCR testing of stool can detect campylobacter quickly and accurately
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} may be used to check for dehydration, kidney function, or signs of severe infection
    \item \textbf{Other tests:} in complicated cases, imaging or endoscopy may be used, although these are rarely needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Most people recover with supportive care at home, and antibiotics are reserved for specific circumstances:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fluids:} the main treatment is replacing lost fluids; oral rehydration solutions are recommended, especially for children and older adults
    \item \textbf{Rest and simple pain relief:} paracetamol for fever and aches
    \item \textbf{Food:} eat bland, easily digested food as the appetite returns, and avoid dairy and heavy meals early on
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} used for severe illness, prolonged symptoms, high-risk patients such as pregnant women, older people, or those with weakened immunity, and during outbreaks; treatment is effective only if started early
    \item \textbf{Anti-diarrhoea medicines:} not usually recommended for a bacterial infection such as campylobacter
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} intravenous fluids may be needed for severe dehydration
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dehydration and electrolyte imbalance, especially in children and older adults
    \item Prolonged or relapsing diarrhoea, and a carrier state in which bacteria are shed for weeks
    \item Reactive arthritis, with joint pain and swelling that can develop weeks after the infection
    \item Guillain--Barré syndrome, a rare but serious condition in which the immune system damages nerves, causing weakness, usually developing one to three weeks after the illness
    \item Bacteraemia (bacteria in the bloodstream) in people with weakened immune systems, which can be severe
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people with campylobacter infection recover fully within a week, although some feel tired for longer. The illness usually settles with fluids and rest, and severe complications are uncommon in healthy people. Guillain--Barré syndrome and reactive arthritis are rare but real risks, so any new weakness, numbness, or joint pain after a bowel infection warrants medical review. With good hygiene and proper cooking, campylobacter infection is largely preventable.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cook poultry thoroughly so that it is steaming hot all the way through with no pink meat
    \item Wash hands with soap and water after handling raw poultry, meat, and eggs
    \item Keep raw meat separate from cooked food, and use separate chopping boards and utensils
    \item Drink pasteurised milk and treated water, especially when travelling
    \item Wash fruit and vegetables before eating
    \item Avoid preparing food for others while you have diarrhoea, and for 48 hours after it settles
    \item Keep children with diarrhoea home from childcare and school until symptoms resolve
\end{itemize}
