\chapter{Ankle Arthrodesis}
\index{Ankle Arthrodesis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Ankle arthrodesis is an operation that fuses the ankle joint, permanently joining the tibia and the talus so that no movement occurs at the joint. Its purpose is to relieve severe pain from end-stage ankle arthritis that has not responded to non-surgical treatment. The damaged cartilage is removed and the two bones are held together with screws, plates, or an intramedullary rod until they grow into a single solid mass of bone.

A fused ankle is painless because the worn joint surfaces no longer rub against each other, and most people walk comfortably and confidently once healing is complete. The ankle no longer flexes up and down, and that loss of motion is permanent, but the surrounding foot and hip joints compensate for most walking needs. Ankle arthrodesis has been the standard operation for end-stage ankle arthritis for many decades and remains a reliable and effective option.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Severe ankle arthritis is less common than arthritis of the knee or hip, but it frequently follows an earlier injury such as an ankle fracture, a severe sprain, or repeated ligament damage. Post-traumatic arthritis is the leading cause of end-stage disease, accounting for a large proportion of cases in people of working age.

Ankle arthrodesis is performed across a wide age range, from younger patients with post-traumatic arthritis to older adults with osteoarthritis, with a slight male preponderance. In recent decades total ankle replacement has emerged as an alternative that preserves motion, and it is increasingly used in older, less active people, while arthrodesis remains preferred where bone quality is poor, deformity is severe, or infection has been present.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Post-traumatic arthritis:} the most common cause, developing years after an ankle fracture or severe ligament injury that damages the articular cartilage
    \item \textbf{Osteoarthritis:} age-related wear-and-tear damage to the joint cartilage
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory arthritis:} rheumatoid arthritis and other inflammatory joint diseases
    \item \textbf{Avascular necrosis:} death of the talus from a loss of blood supply, sometimes after injury
    \item \textbf{Infection:} septic arthritis can permanently destroy the joint surface
    \item \textbf{Neuropathic arthropathy:} loss of sensation and protective joint support, such as Charcot arthropathy in diabetes
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} as cartilage thins and is lost, exposed bone ends grind against each other with weight-bearing, causing pain, stiffness, and deformity; fusion removes the moving, painful surfaces and re-establishes a stable, load-bearing column
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms that lead to ankle fusion reflect the underlying arthritis:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain in the ankle that is worse with weight-bearing, walking, or standing
    \item Swelling and stiffness around the joint
    \item Difficulty walking, a limp, and reduced walking endurance
    \item Pain at rest and at night in advanced cases
    \item A sensation that the joint is unstable or gives way
    \item A history of previous ankle injury, surgery, or inflammatory joint disease
    \item Radiographic findings of a narrowed joint space, bone spurs, subchondral cysts, or deformity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Pain around the ankle may arise from structures other than the ankle joint itself:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Ankle arthritis versus arthritis of the subtalar joint or other hindfoot joints
    \item Osteochondral lesions of the talus (damaged cartilage and underlying bone)
    \item Achilles tendinopathy or rupture
    \item Ligament injury or chronic ankle instability
    \item Peroneal tendon disorders or sinus tarsi syndrome
    \item Nerve entrapment, such as tarsal tunnel syndrome
    \item Stress fractures of the tibia, fibula, or talus
    \item Pain referred from the knee, hip, or lumbar spine
\end{itemize}

Clinical examination and targeted imaging usually identify the true source of pain.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if ankle pain is persistent, limits walking, or has not improved with rest, footwear changes, physiotherapy, or simple pain relief. Seek assessment sooner if the ankle is swollen, hot, and painful after an injury, if there is a visible deformity, or if you are losing function despite treatment.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe ankle pain with obvious deformity or an inability to bear weight (possible fracture)
    \item A hot, swollen, red, painful joint with a fever (possible infection)
    \item Numbness, tingling, coldness, or a colour change in the foot
    \item An open wound or a bone protruding through the skin
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the mechanism of pain, previous injuries, functional limitations, and assessment of alignment, swelling, and stability
    \item \textbf{Weight-bearing X-rays:} standing views to confirm arthritis, joint-space narrowing, deformity, and bone quality
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} detailed three-dimensional views of the joint and the bone stock when planning surgery
    \item \textbf{MRI:} to assess cartilage, ligaments, tendons, and the blood supply of the talus when indicated
    \item \textbf{Isotope bone scan or SPECT:} helpful when it is unclear whether the pain comes from the ankle or from a nearby joint
    \item \textbf{Assessment of adjacent joints:} because subtalar and other hindfoot arthritis often coexist and influence the choice of procedure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Non-surgical options are always tried first, and fusion is reserved for pain that persists despite them:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Self-care:} rest, ice, elevation, and modification of aggravating activities
    \item \textbf{Footwear:} supportive, cushioned shoes with a stiff sole and a rocker bottom
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} paracetamol and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medicines
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} strengthening, range-of-motion, and balance work
    \item \textbf{Bracing:} an ankle-foot orthosis or a walking boot to support and unload the joint
    \item \textbf{Injections:} corticosteroid injection into the joint for temporary relief, which can also help confirm the source of pain
    \item \textbf{Surgery -- arthrodesis:} damaged cartilage is removed and the bones are compressed and fixed with screws or plates so that they fuse
    \item \textbf{Alternative surgery:} total ankle replacement is offered to some people who wish to retain motion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Non-union, failure of the bones to heal together, which is the most common significant problem and may need further surgery
    \item Infection of the wound or of the bone
    \item Nerve injury causing numbness over the foot or toes
    \item Vascular injury or poor wound healing
    \item Deep vein thrombosis in the leg
    \item Malalignment requiring corrective surgery
    \item Increased stress on adjacent joints (subtalar, midfoot, and knee), which may later develop arthritis
    \item A permanent limp and restriction of activities such as running and descending stairs because the ankle no longer moves
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Ankle arthrodesis relieves pain in the great majority of patients, and most achieve a solid, stable, painless fusion. The ankle will not move up and down, so running, hopping, and negotiating slopes become difficult, and a slight limp is common. Bone healing takes several months: patients typically spend eight to twelve weeks non-weight-bearing in a cast or boot before gradually returning to full walking.

Because the surrounding joints bear additional load, arthritis of the subtalar and midfoot joints often develops over the following years, and a minority of patients eventually require further surgery, including conversion to an ankle replacement. Overall, satisfaction rates are high, and the fused ankle usually remains stable and functional for life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item After a fracture or severe sprain, complete the full rehabilitation programme to reduce the risk of post-traumatic arthritis
    \item Maintain a healthy weight to reduce the load on the ankle
    \item Wear well-fitting, supportive footwear for walking and sport
    \item Stop smoking well before surgery, since smoking markedly impairs bone healing
    \item Control blood sugar if you have diabetes, and manage inflammatory arthritis with your specialist
    \item After fusion, follow the weight-bearing instructions precisely and attend physiotherapy
    \item Report wound problems, fever, calf pain, or sudden swelling after surgery promptly
\end{itemize}
