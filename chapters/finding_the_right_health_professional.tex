\chapter{Finding the Right Health Professional}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Finding the right health professional is an important step in getting good care, and it is about more than picking a name from a list. The right professional is one who is qualified and registered, experienced in the condition being treated, available and affordable, and someone the patient can communicate with and trust. In Australia, the starting point is usually a general practitioner (GP), who provides continuing care, coordinates referrals, and acts as the gateway to specialists, allied health providers, and hospital services. This chapter explains the types of health professionals, how to choose among them, and the questions to ask before committing to treatment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Australians see health professionals frequently: most people visit a GP several times a year, and large numbers see dentists, physiotherapists, psychologists, specialists, and other providers. The health workforce includes more than 40,000 GPs, thousands of specialists, and a large allied health sector, with national registration ensuring minimum standards. Choice of provider is influenced by location, cost, availability, and reputation, and most people choose their GP based on proximity and recommendation. Around a third of people report difficulty seeing a professional promptly, and out-of-pocket costs are a significant barrier, particularly for specialists and allied health.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Choosing well matters because the relationship between patient and professional shapes outcomes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Qualification and registration:} all doctors, nurses, and allied health professionals must be registered with the Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency (AHPRA), which enforces training and conduct standards
    \item \textbf{The GP's role:} the GP provides first-contact and continuing care, knows the whole person, and coordinates referrals and follow-up
    \item \textbf{Referral pathways:} most specialists require a GP referral, which also affects Medicare rebates
    \item \textbf{Specialists:} doctors with advanced training in a field, such as cardiology, surgery, or psychiatry, who provide expert assessment and treatment
    \item \textbf{Allied health:} physiotherapists, psychologists, dietitians, and others provide specific therapies, often with Medicare or private health support
    \item \textbf{Communication:} a professional who listens, explains, and involves the patient in decisions improves adherence and outcomes
    \item \textbf{Continuity:} seeing the same professional over time improves detection of problems and the quality of chronic disease care
    \item \textbf{Safety:} choosing unregistered or unregulated providers carries risk of ineffective or harmful treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Good care has recognisable features.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A professional who listens carefully, takes time, and addresses concerns
    \item Clear explanations of the diagnosis, options, and next steps in plain language
    \item Respect for preferences and involvement in decisions
    \item Availability for appointments within a reasonable time, and a clear system for urgent needs
    \item Transparency about costs, including any gap between the fee and the Medicare rebate
    \item Appropriate referral when the problem is outside their expertise
    \item Good communication between providers, such as the GP and specialist
    \item A practice or clinic that is accessible, including for people with disability
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The choice depends on the problem and the circumstances.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute illness: a GP, or urgent care and emergency departments for serious problems
    \item Chronic disease: a regular GP with practice nurses, supported by specialists where needed
    \item Mental health: a GP for assessment, then psychologists, psychiatrists, and digital services
    \item Pregnancy: shared care between a GP, midwife, and obstetric services
    \item Children: GPs with paediatric training, and paediatricians for complex conditions
    \item Allied health needs: physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and others with GP or self-referral
    \item The distinction between registered professionals and unregulated providers, who should be checked carefully
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with a new, persistent, or worrying symptom should see a GP, and people with chronic conditions should have a regular doctor they see for reviews. Finding a good fit may take a change or two; it is reasonable to switch professionals if communication or trust is poor.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, difficulty breathing, or severe bleeding
    \item Sudden weakness, numbness, confusion, or difficulty speaking
    \item Severe pain, collapse, or any symptom that is sudden and severe
    \item A medical crisis in which waiting for a scheduled appointment is unsafe
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Check registration:} verify a professional's registration and any conditions on it through the AHPRA public register
    \item \textbf{Consider credentials:} look for specialist qualifications (such as FRACGP for GPs and Fellowship for specialists) and membership of professional colleges
    \item \textbf{Seek recommendations:} ask family, friends, the local health service, and professional colleges
    \item \textbf{Review practice details:} location, bulk billing, appointment availability, and after-hours arrangements
    \item \textbf{First visit:} use the first appointment to assess communication, thoroughness, and whether the professional seems right
    \item \textbf{Research the condition:} understand the condition so the visit is productive, and prepare questions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Start with a GP:} register with a local practice and build a continuing relationship; the GP coordinates all other care
    \item \textbf{Use referral wisely:} ask the GP to refer to a specialist suited to the condition, and take records and questions to the visit
    \item \textbf{Ask about costs upfront:} request a written quote for specialist procedures and ask about gap payments before agreeing
    \item \textbf{Check Medicare and private health cover:} know what rebates and cover apply, and whether the provider participates in no-gap arrangements
    \item \textbf{Use allied health with care:} see practitioners registered with AHPRA, and ask about accreditation for unregulated providers
    \item \textbf{Seek a second opinion:} it is a normal and reasonable step for major diagnoses, surgery, or uncertainty
    \item \textbf{Change if needed:} if trust or communication fails, move on without guilt; finding the right professional is part of good care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis when the professional is not well matched to the condition
    \item Poor outcomes and harm from unregistered or unregulated providers
    \item High out-of-pocket costs when fees and gaps are not clarified
    \item Fragmented care when there is no coordinating GP
    \item Avoidance of care after a bad experience, leading to deterioration
    \item Time and expense lost to appointments that do not address the problem
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
People who find the right health professional receive better care and have better outcomes across virtually every condition. A trusting relationship improves the accuracy of the history, adherence to treatment, and satisfaction, and continuity of care reduces hospital admissions and complications. The system works best when patients are active participants: asking questions, seeking second opinions where needed, and expecting to be heard. With the right GP as the hub, most people navigate specialists and allied health successfully and receive effective, coordinated care.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Choose a GP you can see regularly and who knows your history
    \item Prepare for appointments: write down symptoms, medicines, and questions
    \item Bring a support person for major or complex appointments
    \item Ask about costs before procedures, and use the informed financial consent process
    \item Verify registration through the AHPRA register
    \item Keep your own health records, including test results and medicine lists
    \item Follow up on referrals and test results, and do not assume no news means good news
    \item Change professionals if the relationship is not working
\end{itemize}
