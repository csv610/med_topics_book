\chapter{Adrenaline}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Adrenaline, also known as epinephrine, is both a naturally occurring hormone and one of the most important emergency medicines in clinical use. In the body it is produced by the inner part of the adrenal glands (the medulla) and by sympathetic nerve endings, and it is the principal driver of the "fight or flight" response that prepares the body to deal with danger, stress, injury, or sudden physical demands.

As a medicine, adrenaline is used in life-threatening emergencies, most importantly as the first-line treatment for severe allergic reactions (anaphylaxis). It is also given during cardiac arrest as part of resuscitation, and it may be used in severe asthma or croup to open the airways. Because it is potent and acts within seconds, it is administered by injection or inhalation under carefully controlled conditions, and in the community it is given through an autoinjector by people at risk of anaphylaxis and their carers.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The natural hormone acts in everyone, and its release increases with stress, fear, low blood sugar, and physical exertion. As a medicine, adrenaline is used most often for anaphylaxis, which affects roughly 1 to 2 in 100 people over a lifetime in some countries and is becoming more common as food allergies in children increase. Cardiac arrest, in which adrenaline forms part of advanced resuscitation, remains a leading cause of death worldwide, and community access to defibrillators and trained responders is improving survival.

People at risk of anaphylaxis -- for example from food allergy, insect venom, or certain medicines -- are commonly prescribed an adrenaline autoinjector to carry at all times. Because adrenaline is inexpensive, widely available, and highly effective, it is the standard first-line treatment for anaphylaxis in virtually every health system.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Adrenaline works by activating the sympathetic nervous system and switching on specific receptors (alpha- and beta-adrenergic receptors) on cells throughout the body.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Release:} the body releases adrenaline in response to stress, fear, injury, low blood sugar, and exertion, preparing it for immediate physical action
    \item \textbf{Heart:} adrenaline makes the heart beat faster and pump more forcefully, raising blood pressure and increasing blood flow to vital organs
    \item \textbf{Airways:} it relaxes the smooth muscle of the air passages, widening them and easing breathing
    \item \textbf{Blood vessels:} it constricts vessels in the skin, mucosa, and peripheral tissues, which raises blood pressure and reduces swelling -- a key effect in reversing anaphylaxis
    \item \textbf{Energy:} it raises blood sugar and mobilises stored energy so that muscles have fuel for immediate use
    \item \textbf{Medical use:} in anaphylaxis, injected adrenaline rapidly reverses airway swelling, bronchoconstriction, and the dangerous drop in blood pressure that characterise the reaction, buying time until emergency care is available
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The effects of adrenaline are essentially the same whether the hormone comes from the body or from an injection.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal stress response:} rapid heartbeat, sweating, tremor or shaking, widened pupils, dry mouth, and a feeling of alertness or anxiety
    \item \textbf{After an adrenaline injection:} the effects above, plus facial pallor or flushing, headache, and palpitations -- these are expected, short-lived, and not dangerous in themselves
    \item \textbf{Signs of anaphylaxis (the emergency that adrenaline treats):} hives and itching, swelling of the lips, tongue, or throat, difficulty breathing or wheezing, vomiting or abdominal cramps, dizziness, and collapse
    \item \textbf{Excessive effect or overdose:} dangerously high blood pressure, chest pain, very fast or irregular heartbeat, and in severe cases stroke or heart attack
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When someone appears to have excessive adrenaline activity, the cause may be medical, psychological, or substance-related.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Panic attacks and severe anxiety, which produce similar surges of symptoms
    \item Phaeochromocytoma -- a rare adrenal tumour that releases surges of adrenaline and noradrenaline
    \item Overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism), which can cause a racing heart, tremor, and sweating
    \item Low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia), which causes shaking, sweating, and a rapid heartbeat
    \item Excessive caffeine or the use of stimulant drugs
    \item Conditions that can be confused with anaphylaxis, such as a fainting spell, severe asthma, or a panic attack
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Call emergency services immediately for signs of anaphylaxis: difficulty breathing, swelling of the face or throat, widespread hives with dizziness, vomiting, or collapse. If an adrenaline autoinjector is available, use it straight away and still call for help -- adrenaline is first aid, not a substitute for emergency medical care.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, wheezing, or a sensation of the throat closing
    \item Swelling of the lips, tongue, face, or throat
    \item Widespread hives together with dizziness, faintness, or collapse
    \item Persistent vomiting and a feeling of severe illness after allergen exposure
    \item Chest pain, palpitations, or a severe headache after adrenaline has been given
    \item Accidental injection of an autoinjector into a finger or hand
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
For emergency use, no tests are needed: anaphylaxis is diagnosed clinically, and adrenaline is given immediately, because any delay increases the risk of a poor outcome. Testing belongs to the period after the emergency.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical diagnosis:} recognition of the signs of anaphylaxis is the only "test" required before giving adrenaline
    \item \textbf{Allergy assessment:} referral to an allergy specialist for skin-prick testing or specific IgE blood tests to identify the trigger once the patient has recovered
    \item \textbf{Metanephrines:} blood or urine measurement of adrenaline breakdown products if a phaeochromocytoma is suspected
    \item \textbf{Observation:} hospital monitoring after anaphylaxis, because a biphasic reaction with a second wave of symptoms can occur in a minority of patients
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Adrenaline as a medicine is used by trained health professionals and, through autoinjectors, by people at risk and their carers.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anaphylaxis:} inject adrenaline into the outer thigh, call for emergency help, and lie the person flat with legs raised; a second dose may be given after five minutes if there is no improvement
    \item \textbf{Cardiac arrest:} adrenaline is administered intravenously or intraosseously by paramedics and hospital staff during resuscitation
    \item \textbf{Severe asthma and croup:} inhaled or nebulised adrenaline may be used under medical supervision to widen the airways
    \item \textbf{After the emergency:} everyone who receives adrenaline for anaphylaxis is observed in hospital, and a follow-up allergy assessment is arranged
    \item \textbf{Ongoing management:} a written anaphylaxis action plan, education of family and school or workplace contacts, and a supply of autoinjectors for those at risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Common but short-lived side effects of treatment: palpitations, tremor, headache, anxiety, and pallor
    \item Overdose or accidental intravenous injection causing dangerously high blood pressure, abnormal heart rhythms, and, rarely, myocardial infarction or stroke
    \item Accidental injection into a finger or hand, which can cause intense local pain and impaired blood flow and requires urgent medical review
    \item The far greater risk of not giving adrenaline promptly when it is needed in anaphylaxis, which can be fatal
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Adrenaline is a safe and life-saving medicine when used correctly and without delay. In anaphylaxis, early injection into the outer thigh dramatically improves the chance of survival, and most people who receive it and then reach hospital make a full recovery. Its side effects wear off within minutes as the drug is metabolised. The greatest risk is delay: giving adrenaline early is far safer than withholding it while waiting for help.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Carry your device:} if you are at risk of anaphylaxis, carry your autoinjector at all times and know how to use it
    \item \textbf{Action plan:} have a written anaphylaxis action plan shared with family, school, or workplace
    \item \textbf{Recognition:} learn the signs of anaphylaxis so that treatment is never delayed
    \item \textbf{Medical alert:} wear a medical alert bracelet describing your allergy
    \item \textbf{Avoid triggers:} avoid known triggers such as specific foods, insect stings, or medicines
    \item \textbf{Storage:} keep autoinjectors away from heat and light, check expiry dates regularly, and replace them before they expire
    \item \textbf{Practice:} practise using a trainer device so that the real injector can be used without hesitation in an emergency
\end{itemize}
