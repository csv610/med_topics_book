\chapter{High Blood Pressure (Hypertension)}
\index{High Blood Pressure (Hypertension)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
High blood pressure, or hypertension, is a chronic condition in which the force of blood against the walls of the arteries is persistently too high. It is diagnosed when blood pressure readings are consistently 140/90 mmHg or higher. Hypertension is often called the "silent killer" because it usually causes no symptoms while quietly damaging the heart, brain, kidneys, and eyes. It is one of the most important preventable causes of heart attack, stroke, kidney failure, and premature death. Treatment combines lifestyle change with medication and is highly effective at reducing risk.

\section*{Epidemiology}
High blood pressure is common worldwide and becomes more frequent with age. Many people are unaware of it. Prevalence, diagnosis, treatment, and control vary between countries and communities. Hypertension is a leading modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease and contributes substantially to stroke, heart disease, kidney disease, and premature death.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary (essential) hypertension:} no single cause; results from a combination of genetic, dietary, and lifestyle factors in over 90\% of cases
    \item \textbf{Secondary hypertension:} caused by an underlying condition such as kidney disease, adrenal tumours, thyroid disease, sleep apnoea, or medications
    \item \textbf{Contributing factors:} age, family history, obesity, excess salt, low potassium, alcohol, smoking, physical inactivity, and stress
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} increased cardiac output, peripheral resistance, or both raise arterial pressure; sustained high pressure damages the arterial lining, promotes atherosclerosis, and strains the heart, brain, and kidneys
    \item \textbf{Target organ damage:} left ventricular hypertrophy, stroke, kidney damage, and retinopathy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hypertension is almost always asymptomatic
    \item It is usually detected during routine blood pressure measurement
    \item Severe elevation can cause headache, dizziness, nosebleeds, or blurred vision
    \item Malignant hypertension causes severe headache, visual disturbance, confusion, and breathlessness, and is an emergency
    \item Symptoms may arise from complications: chest pain, stroke symptoms, breathlessness, or kidney problems
    \item Most people feel completely well despite significant risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item White-coat hypertension: elevated readings in the clinic but normal outside it
    \item Masked hypertension: normal clinic readings but elevated home readings
    \item A single high reading does not diagnose hypertension; confirmation over time is required
    \item Secondary causes must be considered in young people, sudden-onset hypertension, and resistant cases
    \item Ambulatory and home blood pressure monitoring distinguish these patterns
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Adults should have blood pressure checked as part of routine health care, ideally every 1--2 years, and more often if elevated or at high risk. Anyone with readings above normal should be assessed and followed up.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe headache with blurred vision, confusion, or seizures
    \item Chest pain, breathlessness, or signs of stroke
    \item Sudden weakness, facial drooping, or difficulty speaking
    \item Very high readings (above 180/120) with symptoms
    \item Fainting or collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure measurement:} multiple readings, using correct technique, ideally including home or 24-hour ambulatory monitoring
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular risk assessment:} age, smoking, cholesterol, diabetes, and family history combined into a risk score
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} kidney function, electrolytes, blood sugar, and cholesterol
    \item \textbf{Urine tests:} for protein or blood, indicating kidney damage
    \item \textbf{Electrocardiogram:} detects heart strain
    \item \textbf{Secondary cause work-up:} in selected patients, including hormone and imaging tests
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle first:} weight loss, reduced salt, healthy diet, regular exercise, limited alcohol, and smoking cessation
    \item \textbf{DASH-style diet:} rich in vegetables, fruit, wholegrains, and low-fat dairy
    \item \textbf{Medication:} ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, calcium channel blockers, diuretics, and beta-blockers, usually started one at a time
    \item \textbf{Combination therapy:} most people eventually need two or more medicines
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure targets:} usually below 140/90, and often lower in high-risk groups
    \item \textbf{Treatment of secondary causes:} for identified underlying conditions
    \item \textbf{Self-monitoring:} home readings improve control and engagement
    \item \textbf{Long-term review:} regular checks to adjust treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heart attack, angina, and heart failure
    \item Stroke and transient ischaemic attacks
    \item Chronic kidney disease and kidney failure
    \item Peripheral artery disease
    \item Vision loss from hypertensive retinopathy
    \item Aortic aneurysm and dissection
    \item Cognitive decline and dementia
    \item Premature death from cardiovascular disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Treatment of hypertension is among the most effective interventions in medicine: lowering blood pressure by 10/5 mmHg reduces stroke risk by about a third and heart attack risk by about a fifth. Most people can achieve control with lifestyle change and one to three medicines, and controlled blood pressure restores a near-normal risk profile. The benefits accrue over a lifetime, which is why early detection and sustained treatment matter. With adherence to treatment, most people with hypertension live long, healthy lives.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Know your blood pressure numbers and have them checked regularly
    \item Reduce salt intake and eat a balanced, plant-rich diet
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and waist circumference
    \item Be physically active most days
    \item Limit alcohol to within national guidelines
    \item Quit smoking
    \item Take medicines exactly as prescribed and attend reviews
    \item Measure blood pressure at home as advised and keep a record
\end{itemize}
