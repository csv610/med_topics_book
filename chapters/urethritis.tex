\chapter{Urethritis}
\index{Urethritis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Urethritis is defined as the inflammation of the urethra, the fibromuscular tube through which urine is discharged from the urinary bladder to the outside of the body. In biologically male individuals, the urethra serves a dual physiological function, acting as the conduit for both micturition and the ejaculation of semen. Urethritis is a significant clinical entity in contemporary public health, representing one of the most common syndromes managed within sexual health clinics, primary care settings, and urology practices worldwide. The inflammation is typically triggered by infectious pathogens, primarily sexually transmitted infections (STIs), although non-infectious mechanical, chemical, or systemic inflammatory etiologies are also well-documented.

Historically and clinically, urethritis is classified into two broad, distinct categories based on the presence or absence of the Gram-negative diplococcus \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae}: Gonococcal Urethritis (GU) and Non-Gonococcal Urethritis (NGU). Gonococcal urethritis is caused exclusively by infection with \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae}. In contrast, Non-Gonococcal urethritis is a heterogeneous syndrome caused by a variety of other pathogens, most notably \textit{Chlamydia trachomatis}, \textit{Mycoplasma genitalium}, \textit{Trichomonas vaginalis}, and certain viral agents.

The clinical significance of urethritis extends beyond the immediate morbidity of its presenting symptoms, which commonly include painful urination (dysuria), urethral pruritus, and purulent or mucoid urethral discharge. If left undiagnosed or inadequately treated, urethritis can lead to severe reproductive tract sequelae, including epididymitis, infertility, and urethral strictures in males, and pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), chronic pelvic pain, ectopic pregnancy, and tubal factor infertility in females. Furthermore, the local mucosal inflammation associated with urethritis recruits immune cells, such as CD4+ T-lymphocytes, to the urethral mucosa, significantly increasing the risk of acquiring or transmitting Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) and other viral STIs. Consequently, the rapid identification, diagnosis, pathogen-specific treatment, and concurrent management of sexual partners are vital public health strategies for controlling the transmission of STIs.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The global epidemiology of urethritis reflects its status as a major public health concern, with hundreds of millions of cases estimated to occur annually. In the United States alone, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimates that sexually transmitted infections, of which urethritis is a major clinical manifestation, account for nearly 20 million new infections each year. The exact global incidence of urethritis is difficult to quantify due to variable reporting systems, asymptomatic cases, and the widespread use of syndromic management in resource-limited settings where laboratory testing is unavailable.

Non-gonococcal urethritis is consistently reported to be more prevalent than gonococcal urethritis in most developed nations. \textit{Chlamydia trachomatis} remains the single most common identifiable pathogen in NGU, responsible for approximately 15\% to 40\% of cases. Over the last two decades, \textit{Mycoplasma genitalium} has emerged as the second most common bacterial cause of NGU, accounting for 15\% to 25\% of non-chlamydial NGU cases and displaying a strong association with persistent or recurrent urethritis due to emerging antimicrobial resistance. Other pathogens, such as \textit{Trichomonas vaginalis}, herpes simplex virus (HSV), and adenovirus, account for smaller percentages of NGU cases, though their prevalence varies widely depending on the demographic and geographical characteristics of the population.

The incidence of both GU and NGU varies significantly across different demographic groups. The primary risk factors are age and sexual behavior. The highest rates of infection are consistently observed among adolescents and young adults aged 15 to 24 years. This peak incidence is attributed to a combination of behavioral factors, such as higher rates of sexual partner change, lower rates of consistent condom use, and biological factors like increased cervical ectopy in young females, which increases susceptibility to infections. In addition, men who have sex with men (MSM) experience disproportionately high rates of urethritis, particularly cases caused by multi-drug resistant strains of \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae}. Socioeconomic determinants of health, including poverty, lack of access to healthcare services, and systemic barriers to sexual health education, contribute to significant disparities in urethritis rates among racial and ethnic minority populations.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The etiology of urethritis is multi-factorial, encompassing a wide range of bacterial, viral, protozoan, and non-infectious causes. The pathological mechanisms involve complex interactions between the invading pathogen and the host mucosal immune response.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neisseria gonorrhoeae}: This Gram-negative, bean-shaped diplococcus is the exclusive causative agent of gonococcal urethritis. The pathogen is highly adapted to human mucosal surfaces. It initiates infection by adhering to the non-ciliated columnar epithelial cells of the urethra using specialized hair-like appendages called pili and outer membrane proteins known as Opa (opacity-associated) proteins. Following adhesion, the bacteria induce their own engulfment by the host cells, translocating to the subepithelial space. Here, the release of lipooligosaccharide (LOS) and peptidoglycan fragments triggers a robust host inflammatory response. This leads to the recruitment of polymorphonuclear leukocytes (neutrophils), resulting in the characteristic thick, purulent discharge and severe dysuria.

    \item \textbf{Chlamydia trachomatis}: An obligate intracellular bacterium, specifically serovars D through K, which represents the primary bacterial cause of NGU. The life cycle of \textit{Chlamydia} alternates between two distinct forms: the elementary body (EB), which is the infectious, extracellular form, and the reticulate body (RB), which is the replicative, intracellular form. EBs infect the columnar epithelial cells of the urethra. Once inside, they differentiate into RBs, multiply within a membrane-bound vacuole (inclusion), and eventually redifferentiate into EBs before lysing the host cell. The cellular destruction and subsequent release of inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-8 (IL-8), trigger a moderate influx of neutrophils. Because the inflammatory response is less intense than that of \textit{N. gonorrhoeae}, symptoms of chlamydial urethritis are often milder or subclinical.

    \item \textbf{Mycoplasma genitalium}: A small, flask-shaped bacterium that lacks a peptidoglycan cell wall. It adheres to urethral epithelial cells using a specialized terminal organelle known as the tip structures, which contains the MgPa adhesin protein. The lack of a cell wall makes it naturally resistant to beta-lactam antibiotics like penicillin and cephalosporins. \textit{M. genitalium} is highly associated with persistent, recurrent urethritis and is notorious for its ability to rapidly develop resistance to macrolides (e.g., azithromycin) and fluoroquinolones (e.g., moxifloxacin).

    \item \textbf{Trichomonas vaginalis}: A flagellated protozoan parasite that can colonize the male and female genitourinary tracts. In males, it often resides in the urethra and prostate gland, causing mild, transient, or asymptomatic urethritis, but it can sometimes cause significant inflammation and discharge.

    \item \textbf{Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV-1 and HSV-2)}: Urethritis caused by HSV is usually associated with primary genital herpes infection. The virus infects the epithelial cells and sensory nerve endings of the urethra, leading to mucosal ulceration. HSV urethritis is characterized by severe, sharp dysuria, watery discharge, and often systemic symptoms such as fever and myalgia.

    \item \textbf{Adenovirus}: A viral pathogen that is increasingly recognized as a cause of NGU, particularly in men. Adenoviral urethritis is often transmitted via insertive oral sex and is frequently accompanied by conjunctivitis, a presentation referred to as the urethritis-conjunctivitis syndrome.

    \item \textbf{Non-Infectious Etiologies}: Urethral inflammation can also occur in the absence of infection. Causes include mechanical trauma (e.g., from urinary catheterization, insertion of foreign objects, or vigorous sexual activity), chemical irritation (e.g., from spermicides, lubricants, soaps, bubble baths, or douches), and systemic inflammatory conditions such as reactive arthritis (formerly Reiter's syndrome), which is characterized by the clinical triad of urethritis, conjunctivitis, and arthritis.
\end{itemize}

The common pathophysiology of infectious urethritis involves the disruption of the urethral mucosal barrier, cellular invasion or attachment, and the activation of the host's innate immune system. Pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) are recognized by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) on host epithelial cells and resident macrophages. This recognition stimulates the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, leading to local vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and the migration of white blood cells into the urethral lumen, manifesting clinically as discharge and pain.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of urethritis ranges from acute, highly symptomatic disease to entirely asymptomatic states. The severity and nature of the symptoms often depend on the causative pathogen, the sex of the patient, and the duration of the infection.

In male patients, the classic clinical features of urethritis include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urethral Discharge}: This is the most common sign of urethritis. The characteristics of the discharge can provide diagnostic clues, though they are not definitive. Gonococcal urethritis typically presents with a copious, thick, purulent, yellowish-green discharge. Non-gonococcal urethritis, on the other hand, more frequently presents with a scanty, thin, mucoid, or clear-to-white discharge that may only be visible upon milking the urethra or in the morning before the first void (``morning drip'').

    \item \textbf{Dysuria}: Patients commonly describe a burning, stinging, or sharp pain localized to the urethra during urination. This pain is often most severe during the initiation of micturition.

    \item \textbf{Urethral Pruritus}: A sensation of itching, tingling, or irritation inside the urethra, which can occur independently of or alongside dysuria.

    \item \textbf{Meatal Erythema and Edema}: Physical examination may reveal redness and swelling of the urethral meatus (the external opening of the urethra).

    \item \textbf{Dyspareunia and Painful Ejaculation}: Some men experience deep discomfort or pain during or after sexual intercourse, or during ejaculation, reflecting deeper urethral or prostatic inflammation.
\end{itemize}

In female patients, urethritis is frequently asymptomatic or clinically silent, which contributes to the delayed seeking of healthcare and increased risk of transmission and upper reproductive tract complications. When symptoms do occur in females, they include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dysuria}: Often described as an external burning sensation as urine passes over inflamed vulvar and urethral tissues. This is frequently misdiagnosed as an uncomplicated urinary tract infection (cystitis).

    \item \textbf{Increased Vaginal Discharge}: This is usually secondary to co-existing mucopurulent cervicitis, which is commonly caused by the same pathogens (e.g., \textit{C. trachomatis} or \textit{N. gonorrhoeae}).

    \item \textbf{Pelvic Discomfort}: Mild lower abdominal or pelvic pain, which may indicate the onset of ascending infection.
\end{itemize}

The temporal course of urethritis varies by pathogen. The incubation period for gonococcal urethritis is typically short, ranging from 2 to 5 days after exposure. In contrast, chlamydial urethritis has a longer and more variable incubation period, typically 7 to 14 days, and the onset of symptoms is often more gradual and insidious. Asymptomatic infections are particularly common in chlamydia, with up to 50\% of infected males and up to 80\% of infected females showing no clinical signs of disease, highlighting the importance of regular screening.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When evaluating a patient with symptoms suggestive of urethritis, clinicians must consider several alternative diagnoses that can mimic its presentation. An accurate differential diagnosis is essential to ensure appropriate therapy and prevent unnecessary antibiotic use.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urinary Tract Infection (UTI) / Cystitis}: While both conditions present with dysuria, classic cystitis is characterized by bladder mucosal inflammation, leading to urinary frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain, and sometimes hematuria. Urinalysis in cystitis typically reveals bacteriuria and nitrites, and urine cultures show significant growth of common uropathogens such as \textit{Escherichia coli}, which are absent in uncomplicated urethritis.

    \item \textbf{Prostatitis}: Inflammation of the prostate gland can cause dysuria and urethral discharge, mimicking urethritis. Acute bacterial prostatitis presents with systemic symptoms such as high fever, chills, perineal pain, and obstructive voiding symptoms (e.g., hesitancy, weak stream). Chronic prostatitis / chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS) presents with chronic pelvic pain lasting at least three months, often without documentable bacterial infection.

    \item \textbf{Epididymitis and Epididymo-orchitis}: Although often a direct complication of urethritis, epididymitis must be differentiated as it requires immediate, intensive therapy. It presents with unilateral testicular pain, swelling, tenderness, and warmth, with the tail of the epididymis being palpable and exquisitely tender.

    \item \textbf{Balanitis and Balanoposthitis}: Inflammation of the glans penis (balanitis) or the glans and foreskin (balanoposthitis) can cause dysuria due to urine contacting irritated external skin. It is characterized by visible erythema, scaling, and tenderness of the glans, often due to \textit{Candida} infection or poor hygiene, rather than internal urethral inflammation.

    \item \textbf{Urethral Stricture}: A physical narrowing of the urethral lumen due to scarring from previous trauma, catheterization, or infection. While it can cause chronic dysuria and a predisposition to urethritis, it primarily presents with mechanical symptoms such as a weak urinary stream, splitting of the stream, straining to urinate, and incomplete bladder emptying.

    \item \textbf{Chemical or Mechanical Irritation}: Non-infectious urethritis can be caused by exposure to local irritants. A detailed history regarding the use of new soaps, bubble baths, spermicides, lubricants, or recent mechanical trauma (such as catheter insertion or rough sexual activity) is crucial to differentiate this from infectious causes.

    \item \textbf{Genital Herpes (HSV Infection)}: External herpetic lesions on the penis or vulva can cause severe dysuria when urine contacts the open ulcers. Primary HSV infection can also cause direct urethral inflammation with watery discharge, which must be distinguished from bacterial urethritis through viral swabs and PCR testing.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients experiencing any symptoms of urethritis, such as painful urination, abnormal penile or vaginal discharge, or genital itching, should seek prompt medical evaluation from a qualified healthcare professional, such as a general practitioner, a sexual health clinic specialist, or a urologist. Prompt medical evaluation is critical to obtain an accurate diagnosis, start appropriate targeted antibiotic therapy, and prevent the development of serious complications. Self-treatment with leftover antibiotics or over-the-counter remedies is strongly discouraged, as it can lead to treatment failure, mask symptoms, and contribute to the development of antibiotic-resistant bacterial strains.

While urethritis is rarely a medical emergency in its early stages, certain symptoms indicate a severe, ascending, or systemic infection that requires immediate emergency medical care. Patients should go to the nearest hospital emergency department or call emergency services (such as \textbf{local emergency services} in many countries, \textbf{911} in the United States, or \textbf{999} in the United Kingdom) if they experience any of the following red-flag symptoms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item High fever, chills, rapid heart rate, or confusion, which may indicate systemic bacteremia or sepsis.

    \item Inability to urinate (acute urinary retention), causing severe lower abdominal distention and pain.

    \item Severe, sudden, or rapidly worsening pain and swelling in one or both testicles or the scrotum.

    \item Severe, acute lower abdominal or pelvic pain in females, especially if accompanied by nausea, vomiting, and fever, which could indicate acute pelvic inflammatory disease or a ruptured ectopic pregnancy.

    \item A spreading skin rash, joint pain, joint swelling, or tenderness in the tendons of the wrists or ankles, which are classic signs of disseminated gonococcal infection (DGI).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of urethritis is established through a combination of clinical history, physical examination, and laboratory investigations. The primary goals of diagnostic testing are to confirm urethral inflammation and to identify the specific causative pathogen to guide targeted antimicrobial therapy.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical History and Physical Examination}: The clinician will obtain a detailed sexual history, including the number of recent partners, condom use, history of previous STIs, and symptoms in partners. The physical examination involves inspecting the external genitalia for signs of discharge, erythema, edema, ulcerations, or lesions. The clinician will also palpate the inguinal lymph nodes, scrotum, epididymis, and testes to rule out complications like epididymitis. In female patients, a speculum examination is performed to inspect the cervix for mucopurulent discharge and contact bleeding (cervicitis).

    \item \textbf{Urethral Swab and Gram Stain}: In symptomatic male patients, a urethral swab can be collected by gently inserting a thin swab into the distal urethra. The specimen is smeared onto a slide and stained. The presence of five or more polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNs) per high-power field (HPF) under oil immersion microscopy confirms urethral inflammation. Furthermore, the visualization of Gram-negative, intracellular, kidney-bean-shaped diplococci within the PMNs is highly sensitive and specific (over 95\%) for the diagnosis of gonococcal urethritis. The absence of these diplococci in the presence of inflammation suggests non-gonococcal urethritis.

    \item \textbf{First-Void Urine Specimen}: This is the preferred non-invasive method for detecting urethritis in both symptomatic and asymptomatic patients. The patient must collect the first 10 to 20 milliliters of the urine stream (not a mid-stream clean catch) and must not have urinated for at least one to two hours prior to collection.

    \item \textbf{Urinalysis and Microscopy}: A first-void urine specimen can be tested for leukocyte esterase (a chemical marker of white blood cells) using a dipstick. Microscopic examination of the sediment showing ten or more white blood cells (WBCs) per high-power field (HPF) under high power confirms urethritis.

    \item \textbf{Nucleic Acid Amplification Tests (NAATs)}: NAATs are the gold standard for the modern diagnosis of urethritis due to their exceptional sensitivity and specificity. These molecular tests detect the DNA or RNA of specific pathogens. NAATs are routinely used to screen for \textit{Chlamydia trachomatis} and \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae} using first-void urine in males and vaginal or cervical swabs in females. Specialized NAATs are also available to detect \textit{Mycoplasma genitalium} and \textit{Trichomonas vaginalis}.

    \item \textbf{Bacterial Culture}: While NAATs have largely replaced culture for routine diagnosis, culturing \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae} remains clinically important. Culture allows for antibiotic susceptibility testing (AST), which is critical for monitoring and managing antibiotic resistance, particularly when treatment failure is suspected.

    \item \textbf{Screening for Co-infections}: Because individuals with one STI are at increased risk for others, all patients diagnosed with urethritis should undergo comprehensive screening for other pathogens, including syphilis (via serology), HIV (via serology), and Hepatitis B and C, as well as counselling on safe sex practices.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of urethritis involves prompt initiation of antimicrobial therapy, management of sexual partners, patient education, and follow-up to ensure cure. Treatment is often initiated empirically at the first clinical presentation to relieve symptoms, prevent complications, and reduce ongoing transmission.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Empiric Antimicrobial Therapy}: Before laboratory results are returned, patients presenting with signs of urethritis are treated with an empiric regimen designed to cover the most common pathogens, \textit{N. gonorrhoeae} and \textit{C. trachomatis}. Current international guidelines (such as those from the CDC or WHO) recommend a dual approach:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item For \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae} cover: A single intramuscular dose of ceftriaxone (typically 500 mg, or 1 g for patients weighing 150 kg or more).
        \item For \textit{Chlamydia trachomatis} cover: Oral doxycycline, 100 mg twice daily for a duration of 7 days. Azithromycin (1 g orally in a single dose) is no longer preferred as first-line therapy due to high rates of resistance in \textit{Mycoplasma genitalium} and reduced efficacy against \textit{C. trachomatis} in some studies, but it may be used if doxycycline is contraindicated (e.g., during pregnancy or allergy).
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Pathogen-Specific Targeted Therapy}: Once diagnostic test results are available, the antibiotic regimen should be adjusted if necessary:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Chlamydia Urethritis}: Doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for 7 days.
        \item \textbf{Gonococcal Urethritis}: Ceftriaxone 500 mg intramuscularly as a single dose. If ceftriaxone is unavailable or contraindicated, oral cefixime (800 mg single dose) may be used, though it has lower efficacy for pharyngeal infections.
        \item \textbf{Mycoplasma genitalium Urethritis}: Treatment is complex due to widespread macrolide resistance. Current guidelines recommend a two-stage approach: doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days to reduce bacterial load, followed immediately by oral moxifloxacin 400 mg once daily for 7 days. If the infection is confirmed to be macrolide-susceptible, azithromycin (1 g orally on day 1, followed by 500 mg daily on days 2 and 3) can be used instead of moxifloxacin.
        \item \textbf{Trichomonas vaginalis Urethritis}: Treated with oral metronidazole (500 mg twice daily for 7 days) or tinidazole (2 g orally in a single dose).
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Management of Sexual Partners}: All sexual partners of a patient diagnosed with urethritis who had sexual contact within the 60 days preceding the onset of symptoms or diagnosis should be contacted, evaluated, and treated presumptively. This is essential to prevent reinfection of the index patient (the ``ping-pong'' effect) and to control transmission within the community. In some jurisdictions, Expedited Partner Therapy (EPT) allows clinicians to provide antibiotic prescriptions or medications directly to the patient to deliver to their partner(s) without the partner needing to be examined.

    \item \textbf{Behavioral Modifications}: Patients must be instructed to abstain from all sexual activity (including vaginal, anal, and oral sex) until both they and their partners have completed their full antibiotic course, and until all physical symptoms have completely resolved. This typically means waiting at least 7 days after a single-dose regimen or until the last day of a multi-day regimen.

    \item \textbf{Follow-up and Test of Cure (TOC)}: A routine test of cure is not required for uncomplicated gonorrhea or chlamydia if symptoms resolve and the patient completed therapy. However, a TOC is strongly recommended for all cases of \textit{Mycoplasma genitalium} urethritis, performed 3 to 4 weeks after completion of therapy using NAAT, because of high treatment failure rates. Patients with persistent or recurrent symptoms after treatment (non-gonococcal urethritis recurrence) require re-evaluation for compliance, re-exposure, or resistant pathogens like \textit{M. genitalium} or \textit{T. vaginalis}.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
If left untreated, misdiagnosed, or inadequately treated, urethritis can progress to cause significant short-term and long-term complications. These complications can affect the genitourinary tract locally or manifest systemically.

In male patients, complications include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Epididymitis and Epididymo-orchitis}: Pathogens can ascend from the urethra through the vas deferens to infect the epididymis and testicles. This causes intense, acute testicular pain, unilateral scrotal swelling, and fever. Severe or bilateral epididymitis can lead to testicular atrophy and permanent infertility due to scarring and obstruction of sperm ducts.

    \item \textbf{Urethral Stricture}: Chronic or severe inflammation, particularly from untreated gonococcal urethritis, can lead to the formation of fibrous scar tissue in the urethral wall. Over time, this scar tissue contracts, narrowing the urethral lumen. Urethral strictures cause chronic urinary obstruction, a weakened urinary stream, urinary retention, and recurrent urinary tract infections, often requiring surgical interventions such as dilation or urethroplasty.

    \item \textbf{Prostatitis}: The infection can ascend into the prostatic ducts, causing acute or chronic bacterial prostatitis. This presents with pelvic pain, dysuria, urinary frequency, and painful ejaculation, and can be challenging to treat due to poor penetration of many antibiotics into prostatic tissue.
\end{itemize}

In female patients, the ascending spread of infection is particularly dangerous and includes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID)}: This is a major complication in women, occurring when pathogens like \textit{C. trachomatis} or \textit{N. gonorrhoeae} ascend from the cervix and vagina into the endometrium, fallopian tubes, and pelvic peritoneum. PID can cause severe pelvic pain, fever, and internal abscess formation.

    \item \textbf{Ectopic Pregnancy and Infertility}: PID-induced inflammation causes delicate mucosal damage and scarring within the fallopian tubes. This scarring can partially or completely obstruct the tubes. A partially blocked tube can prevent a fertilized egg from reaching the uterus, leading to a life-threatening ectopic pregnancy. Complete bilateral obstruction results in tubal factor infertility.

    \item \textbf{Fitz-Hugh-Curtis Syndrome}: A localized form of peritonitis that involves the capsule of the liver. It develops in a small percentage of women with PID, causing severe right upper quadrant abdominal pain and resulting in characteristic ``violin-string'' adhesions between the liver capsule and the anterior abdominal wall.
\end{itemize}

Systemic and general complications include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Disseminated Gonococcal Infection (DGI)}: In rare cases, \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae} enters the bloodstream. This leads to DGI, characterized by a triad of arthritis, tenosynovitis (inflammation of tendon sheaths, typically in the wrists and ankles), and dermatitis (pustular or necrotic skin lesions). DGI can also progress to life-threatening gonococcal meningitis or endocarditis.

    \item \textbf{Reactive Arthritis (Reiter's Syndrome)}: An autoimmune reaction triggered by a genitourinary infection, most commonly \textit{Chlamydia trachomatis}. It presents as an asymmetric polyarthritis affecting the lower extremities, accompanied by conjunctivitis (eye inflammation) and urethritis.

    \item \textbf{Enhanced HIV Transmission}: Urethritis significantly increases the concentration of HIV target cells (CD4+ T-cells and macrophages) in the urethral mucosa. This increases the likelihood that an HIV-negative individual will contract HIV during sex. For individuals who are HIV-positive, urethral inflammation increases the rate of viral shedding in semen, making them significantly more infectious to others.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals diagnosed with uncomplicated urethritis is generally excellent, provided they receive timely, appropriate, and pathogen-directed antimicrobial therapy. In the vast majority of cases, symptoms like dysuria and discharge begin to subside within 24 to 48 hours of starting antibiotics and resolve completely by the end of the treatment course. Long-term health outcomes are unaffected in these patients.

However, several clinical and behavioral factors can negatively influence the prognosis:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antimicrobial Resistance}: The rising prevalence of drug-resistant pathogens is a major threat. \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae} has developed resistance to almost every class of antibiotics historically used to treat it, including penicillins, tetracyclines, and fluoroquinolones. The current reliance on ceftriaxone is threatened by emerging ceftriaxone-resistant strains. Similarly, \textit{Mycoplasma genitalium} shows extremely high rates of resistance to macrolides (exceeding 50\% in many urban areas) and rising resistance to fluoroquinolones. Infection with a resistant strain leads to treatment failure, persistent symptoms, prolonged infectiousness, and a higher risk of complications.

    \item \textbf{Reinfection}: Urethritis does not confer immunity. Reinfection rates are high, particularly if the patient's sexual partners are not treated concurrently, or if the patient resumes sexual activity before both partners have completed treatment. Recurring infections increase the cumulative risk of long-term scarring and reproductive complications.

    \item \textbf{Delay in Treatment}: If diagnosis and treatment are delayed, the risk of permanent anatomical damage increases. Once complications like urethral strictures, fallopian tube scarring, or chronic pelvic pain have developed, the prognosis for restoring normal physiological function and resolving chronic pain is guarded, often requiring long-term specialist care and surgical procedures.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventing urethritis and managing the condition at home requires a combination of safe sexual practices, regular medical screening, self-monitoring, and healthy personal hygiene habits.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Consistent and Correct Condom Use}: The most effective method for preventing sexually transmitted urethritis is the consistent and correct use of latex or polyurethane male and female condoms during every sexual encounter, including vaginal, anal, and oral sex. Condoms act as a physical barrier that prevents contact with infected mucosal secretions.

    \item \textbf{Reducing the Number of Sexual Partners}: Limiting the number of concurrent and lifetime sexual partners reduces the statistical probability of exposure to sexually transmitted pathogens. Engaging in a mutually monogamous relationship with an uninfected partner is a highly effective preventive strategy.

    \item \textbf{Regular Screening and Testing}: Sexually active individuals, particularly those under the age of 25, men who have sex with men (MSM), and individuals with new or multiple sexual partners, should undergo routine annual screening for chlamydia and gonorrhea. Because many infections are asymptomatic, screening is the only way to detect and treat these infections early, preventing complications and onward transmission.

    \item \textbf{Avoidance of Chemical and Mechanical Irritants}: To prevent non-infectious urethritis, individuals should avoid exposing the urethral opening to potential irritants. This includes avoiding harsh soaps, bubble baths, heavily fragranced body washes, spermicides (such as nonoxynol-9), and vaginal douches. Opt for mild, unscented cleansers and rinse the genital area thoroughly with clean water.

    \item \textbf{Healthy Hygiene and Voiding Habits}: Practicing good personal hygiene can help reduce the risk of transient bacterial colonization. Urinating shortly after sexual intercourse is a simple and effective practice that helps flush out any bacteria that may have entered the urethra during sex. Staying well-hydrated by drinking plenty of water throughout the day ensures regular, dilute urine flow, which helps maintain a healthy urinary tract.

    \item \textbf{Partner Notification and Management}: If diagnosed with urethritis, it is a critical self-care and public health responsibility to inform all recent sexual partners so they can seek testing and treatment. This prevents the cycle of reinfection and protects partners from serious complications.

    \item \textbf{Vaccination}: While there is currently no vaccine specifically targeting the primary causes of urethritis (\textit{N. gonorrhoeae} or \textit{C. trachomatis}), receiving other recommended vaccinations is an important part of comprehensive sexual health. This includes the Human Papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine and the Hepatitis B vaccine. Additionally, recent clinical data suggests that the meningococcal B vaccine (4CMenB) provides a moderate degree of cross-protection against \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae} due to shared outer membrane antigens, and it may be recommended for individuals at high risk for gonococcal infection.
\end{itemize}
