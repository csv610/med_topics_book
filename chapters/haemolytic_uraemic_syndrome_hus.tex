\chapter{Haemolytic Uraemic Syndrome (HUS)}
\index{Haemolytic Uraemic Syndrome (HUS)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) is a rare but serious condition in which abnormal blood clotting damages small blood vessels, leading to destruction of red blood cells (haemolytic anaemia), low platelet counts, and acute kidney injury. It most commonly affects young children and is usually caused by infection with a toxin-producing strain of \textit{E. coli} (typically O157), which is spread through contaminated food and water. The classic picture follows a bout of bloody diarrhoea. HUS is a medical emergency requiring hospital care, and treatment is supportive, including dialysis for kidney failure. Most children recover, but some have long-term kidney problems.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Typical (STEC-associated) HUS is rare, affecting around 1--2 children per 100,000 per year in developed countries. It most often affects children under five, and outbreaks are associated with contaminated beef, unpasteurised milk, leafy greens, and water. Around 5--10\% of people infected with \textit{E. coli} O157 develop HUS. Atypical HUS, caused by genetic or immune abnormalities of the complement system, is much rarer and can occur at any age. In many countries, HUS is a notifiable condition, and surveillance tracks outbreaks and trends.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
HUS is caused by damage to the lining of small blood vessels, triggering abnormal clotting.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{STEC infection:} Shiga toxin-producing \textit{E. coli} (such as O157) releases Shiga toxin, which enters the bloodstream and damages the endothelium (the lining of blood vessels), especially in the kidneys
    \item \textbf{Microangiopathy:} damaged vessels cause platelets to clump, forming microthrombi (tiny clots)
    \item \textbf{Haemolytic anaemia:} red blood cells are destroyed as they pass through the narrowed, clot-lined vessels
    \item \textbf{Thrombocytopenia:} platelets are consumed in the clots, causing low platelet counts
    \item \textbf{Acute kidney injury:} the kidneys' filtering units are blocked and damaged by the microthrombi
    \item \textbf{Atypical HUS:} in a minority, complement system dysregulation causes the same process without an infection
    \item \textbf{Other triggers:} some medicines, infections, and conditions can trigger HUS
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
HUS typically develops about a week after the onset of bloody diarrhoea.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Bloody diarrhoea, which is the usual first symptom
    \item Pallor and fatigue from anaemia
    \item Easy bruising and bleeding from low platelets
    \item Reduced urine output, which is a key sign of kidney injury
    \item Swelling of the face, hands, and feet
    \item High blood pressure
    \item Lethargy, irritability, and reduced responsiveness
    \item Seizures and other neurological symptoms in severe cases
    \item Fever and abdominal pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of bloody diarrhoea, anaemia, and kidney injury should be considered.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Bacterial gastroenteritis from other organisms, including campylobacter, salmonella, and shigella
    \item Other causes of thrombotic microangiopathy, including thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)
    \item Immune thrombocytopenia and other causes of low platelets
    \item Haemolytic anaemia from other causes, including autoimmune disease
    \item Acute kidney injury from dehydration, sepsis, or other causes
    \item Henoch-Schönlein purpura and other vasculitides
    \item Intussusception and other surgical causes of bloody stools
    \item Inflammatory bowel disease presenting with bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor urgently for any child with bloody diarrhoea, especially with reduced urine output, pallor, lethargy, or bruising. Children with a suspected STEC infection need monitoring for the development of HUS.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A child with bloody diarrhoea who is lethargic, drowsy, or confused
    \item Reduced urine output or no urine for more than 12 hours
    \item Seizures or fits
    \item Signs of severe dehydration or shock, including pale, cold skin and rapid breathing
    \item Uncontrolled bleeding or bruising
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
HUS is diagnosed by blood and urine tests, with stool tests for the cause.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full blood count:} low haemoglobin (anaemia), low platelets, and characteristic red cell fragments (schistocytes) on the blood film
    \item \textbf{Kidney function tests:} elevated creatinine confirms kidney injury
    \item \textbf{Urine tests:} blood and protein in the urine
    \item \textbf{Stool tests:} culture and PCR identify \textit{E. coli} O157 and its toxin
    \item \textbf{Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH):} elevated in haemolysis
    \item \textbf{Complement testing:} for suspected atypical HUS
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} ultrasound of the kidneys may be performed
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} children are admitted for monitoring and supportive treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
HUS is managed in hospital, often in intensive care or a paediatric unit.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} fluids and electrolyte management are the cornerstone of treatment
    \item \textbf{Dialysis:} temporary dialysis for children whose kidneys fail, which is needed in around half of cases
    \item \textbf{Blood transfusions:} for severe anaemia
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure control:} medicines for hypertension
    \item \textbf{Avoiding antibiotics:} antibiotics for STEC infection are generally avoided, as they may increase the risk of HUS
    \item \textbf{Atypical HUS treatment:} a medicine called eculizumab, which blocks complement, is used for atypical HUS
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} careful tracking of blood counts, kidney function, and urine output
    \item \textbf{Long-term follow-up:} kidney checks after recovery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute kidney failure requiring dialysis
    \item Chronic kidney disease and high blood pressure persisting after recovery
    \item End-stage kidney disease in a minority, requiring long-term dialysis or transplant
    \item Neurological complications, including seizures and stroke
    \item Severe anaemia requiring transfusion
    \item Complications of atypical HUS, including relapses
    \item Mortality, which is around 3--5\% in typical HUS with modern care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most children with typical HUS recover, but it is a serious illness. Around half need dialysis temporarily, and most regain kidney function. However, about 5--10\% die in the acute phase, and up to a third have long-term kidney problems, including chronic kidney disease and high blood pressure, which may appear years later. With supportive care, survival has improved greatly. Atypical HUS has a higher risk of relapse and kidney failure, but modern treatment with eculizumab has transformed its outlook.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cook minced meat thoroughly until no pink remains
    \item Wash hands, utensils, and surfaces after handling raw meat
    \item Wash fruit and vegetables thoroughly
    \item Avoid unpasteurised milk and products made from it
    \item Practise good hand hygiene, especially after using the toilet and before preparing food
    \item Keep raw meat separate from other foods in the fridge
    \item Follow public health advice during outbreaks of \textit{E. coli}
    \item Seek prompt medical care for children with bloody diarrhoea
    \item Attend long-term kidney follow-up after recovery from HUS
\end{itemize}
