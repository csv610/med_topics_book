\section*{Definition and Overview}
Chronic fatigue syndrome (CFS), also called myalgic encephalomyelitis (ME) or ME/CFS, is a complex, long-term illness characterised by profound fatigue that is not relieved by rest and that is made worse by physical or mental activity. It is not simply being tired: the fatigue is disabling, lasts for at least six months in adults, and cannot be explained by another medical condition.

People with ME/CFS experience an abnormally severe drop in function after exertion, a feature known as post-exertional malaise (PEM). The condition varies widely between individuals -- some remain reasonably functional with pacing, while others become severely affected and largely housebound. ME/CFS is a recognised medical diagnosis, not a form of laziness or a purely psychological problem.

\section*{Epidemiology}
ME/CFS affects people of all ages, but it is most commonly diagnosed between the early twenties and mid-forties. It is more common in women than in men, by roughly a two- to three-fold margin. Accurate figures are difficult to establish because the condition is under-recognised and definitions vary, but studies suggest it may affect between one and five in a thousand people.

The condition can follow a viral infection, and clusters have been reported, but most cases occur sporadically. It occurs across all socioeconomic and ethnic groups. Many people are significantly disabled by the illness, and a substantial proportion are unable to work or study for extended periods.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The cause of ME/CFS is not fully understood, and it is likely that several factors combine to trigger and maintain the illness:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Triggering infection:} many people report onset after a viral or bacterial illness, such as glandular fever (Epstein--Barr virus), influenza, or COVID-19
    \item \textbf{Immune system disturbance:} ongoing low-grade immune activation and altered inflammatory markers are seen in some people, though there is no single consistent abnormality
    \item \textbf{Nervous system changes:} altered brain signalling, difficulty with blood pressure and heart rate regulation (dysautonomia), and disturbed sleep control
    \item \textbf{Energy metabolism problems:} evidence of impaired cellular energy production, which may explain profound fatigue and post-exertional malaise
    \item \textbf{Genetic susceptibility:} some people may be genetically more vulnerable, though no single gene has been identified
    \item \textbf{Psychological and social factors:} stress, anxiety, and difficult life circumstances do not cause the illness but may influence how it develops and how well someone copes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms typically fluctuate from day to day and week to week. The core features are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Post-exertional malaise (PEM):} symptoms worsen sharply after physical, mental, or emotional activity, often delayed by 12--48 hours and lasting for days
    \item \textbf{Profound fatigue:} disabling, unrefreshed by rest, lasting at least six months in adults
    \item \textbf{Unrefreshing sleep:} poor, fragmented sleep that does not restore energy
    \item \textbf{Cognitive difficulties (brain fog):} trouble concentrating, finding words, poor short-term memory, and slowed thinking
    \item \textbf{Orthostatic intolerance:} feeling dizzy, lightheaded, or faint when standing upright
    \item \textbf{Pain:} muscle aches, joint pain without swelling, and headaches
    \item \textbf{Other symptoms:} sore throat, tender lymph glands, new sensitivities to light, sound, or smells, and digestive complaints
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the symptoms are non-specific, other conditions must be excluded before ME/CFS is diagnosed. These include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Anaemia, iron deficiency, or vitamin B12 deficiency
    \item Thyroid disorders (underactive thyroid) and other hormonal problems
    \item Obstructive sleep apnoea and other sleep disorders
    \item Depression, anxiety, and chronic stress
    \item Chronic infections where these persist, such as glandular fever
    \item Autoimmune or inflammatory conditions, such as lupus or rheumatoid arthritis
    \item Diabetes, kidney disease, or liver disease
    \item Side effects of medications (including some antidepressants and blood pressure tablets)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with persistent, disabling fatigue lasting several months should see a doctor for assessment, especially if they also have post-exertional malaise, unrefreshing sleep, or difficulty with concentration. It is important to have symptoms checked because many other conditions can cause similar problems.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe fatigue with chest pain, breathlessness, or fainting
    \item New weakness, numbness, or difficulty speaking
    \item Confusion or reduced consciousness
    \item Thoughts of self-harm or suicide
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
There is no single test for ME/CFS. Diagnosis is clinical and made on the basis of the characteristic symptom pattern after other causes have been ruled out. In many countries, diagnosis is often supported by the internationally recognised Canadian Consensus or Institute of Medicine criteria.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical history:} the key diagnostic tool -- duration of fatigue, post-exertional malaise, sleep, and cognitive symptoms
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} to look for signs of other illness (e.g.\ thyroid enlargement, joint swelling, lymphadenopathy)
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, iron studies, thyroid function, B12, folate, liver and kidney function, glucose, inflammatory markers, and coeliac antibodies
    \item \textbf{Other tests as indicated:} sleep study, ECG or heart rate monitoring, and vitamin D where clinically appropriate
    \item \textbf{Exclusion of other diagnoses:} if features are atypical, referral to a specialist may be arranged
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
There is no cure for ME/CFS, and management focuses on relieving symptoms and helping people live as well as possible within their energy limits.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pacing:} balancing activity and rest within a consistent, manageable envelope of energy to reduce post-exertional malaise -- the cornerstone of self-management
    \item \textbf{Sleep support:} good sleep hygiene and, where needed, medical help for persistent sleep disturbance
    \item \textbf{Pain management:} simple analgesia, heat, and gentle techniques under medical guidance
    \item \textbf{Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT):} adapted to help people cope with the illness and its impact -- not as a cure
    \item \textbf{Graduated exercise therapy:} used cautiously and individually, and only in those who can tolerate it; it is not recommended as a standard treatment for everyone
    \item \textbf{Managing orthostatic intolerance:} increased salt and fluid intake, and leg exercises under medical advice
    \item \textbf{Allied health support:} occupational therapy, physiotherapy, and psychological support tailored to the individual
    \item \textbf{Regular follow-up:} monitoring for new symptoms and review of the treatment plan
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe disability, with some people becoming housebound or bedbound
    \item Inability to work, study, or maintain social relationships
    \item Depression and anxiety, which are common and can compound the illness
    \item Deconditioning and muscle weakness from prolonged inactivity
    \item Social isolation and financial strain
    \item Over-activity or over-exercising, which can cause prolonged relapses
    \item Misdiagnosis of an unrecognised alternative illness when investigations are not thorough
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The course of ME/CFS varies considerably. Some people improve substantially over months to years, and a minority recover fully, particularly younger people and those with milder symptoms. Many others improve partially but continue to live with a fluctuating illness, and a smaller group remains severely affected long term.

In children and adolescents, outcomes tend to be better than in adults. Because the illness fluctuates, periods of relapse and improvement are common. Early recognition, pacing, and good support may improve the outlook, although there is no guarantee of recovery.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Because the cause is not well understood, there is no reliable way to prevent ME/CFS. Self-care is nonetheless central to living with the illness:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Pace yourself: plan activity around your available energy and take planned rest breaks
    \item Avoid the boom-and-bust cycle of over-exertion followed by collapse
    \item Protect sleep with a regular routine, avoiding long daytime naps where possible
    \item Eat a balanced diet and stay hydrated
    \item Manage stress with relaxation, mindfulness, or counselling
    \item Keep a symptom and activity diary to identify your own limits and triggers
    \item Seek early help for new or worsening symptoms
    \item Connect with support organisations and, where useful, others living with the condition
\end{itemize}
