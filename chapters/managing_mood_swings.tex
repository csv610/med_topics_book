\chapter{Managing mood swings}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Mood swings refer to rapid, significant, or unpredictable fluctuations in a person's emotional state, characterized by transitions between periods of high energy, happiness, or irritability, and periods of low mood, sadness, or anxiety. While minor emotional fluctuations are a natural response to daily life events, severe, frequent, or prolonged mood swings can impair social, occupational, and personal functioning. Mood swings may be a primary symptom of underlying psychiatric conditions (such as bipolar disorder, cyclothymia, or borderline personality disorder), hormonal fluctuations (such as premenstrual dysphoric disorder, perimenopause, or thyroid disorders), or external lifestyle factors such as chronic stress, substance use, and sleep deprivation. Understanding the underlying mechanism is essential to establishing an effective, individualised management strategy.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Mood swings are a highly prevalent complaint in both primary care and psychiatric settings. The epidemiology is closely linked to the underlying cause: hormonally driven mood swings are extremely common in women, with premenstrual syndrome (PMS) affecting up to 75\% of menstruating women, and the more severe premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD) affecting 3--8\%. Psychiatric causes typically manifest in late adolescence or early adulthood; bipolar disorder has a lifetime prevalence of approximately 1--2\% in the Australian population, while borderline personality disorder (BPD) affects around 1\%. Mood swings are also highly prevalent in individuals experiencing high occupational stress, chronic sleep disruption, or substance use disorders, with no significant differences in prevalence across socioeconomic backgrounds.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The aetiology of mood swings is multifactorial, involving neurochemical imbalances, endocrine variations, and psychosocial triggers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychiatric disorders:} Episodic mood shifts are central to bipolar I and II disorders (cycling between mania/hypomania and depression) and major depressive disorder, while rapid, reactive emotional instability is characteristic of borderline personality disorder.
    \item \textbf{Endocrine and hormonal fluctuations:} Fluctuations in oestrogen and progesterone during the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, the postpartum period, and perimenopause alter central serotonergic and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) pathways.
    \item \textbf{Thyroid and adrenal dysfunction:} Hyperthyroidism can cause anxiety and irritability, while hypothyroidism leads to lethargy and depression. Similarly, dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis affects cortisol secretion and emotional stability.
    \item \textbf{Neurological injury:} Structural changes in the prefrontal cortex, amygdala, and limbic system due to traumatic brain injury, stroke, dementia, or multiple sclerosis impair emotional regulation.
    \item \textbf{Substance use and withdrawal:} Active use of or withdrawal from central nervous system stimulants (e.g. amphetamines, cocaine), depressants (e.g. alcohol, benzodiazepines), or common substances like caffeine and nicotine.
    \item \textbf{Chronic stress and sleep deprivation:** Sustained high levels of stress and poor sleep compromise prefrontal cortical executive control over the amygdala, leading to exaggerated emotional reactivity.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The presentation of mood swings varies based on the underlying driver, but typically involves shifts in affect, behaviour, and cognition:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Affective instability:} Shifting rapidly from a state of relative calm or happiness to intense anger, irritability, anxiety, or tearfulness.
    \item \textbf{Irritability and low frustration tolerance:} Outbursts of temper or feeling easily overwhelmed by minor stressors or daily challenges.
    \item \textbf{Fluctuations in energy and sleep:} Alternating periods of high energy, racing thoughts, and a decreased need for sleep, with periods of profound fatigue, lethargy, and hypersomnia.
    \item \textbf{Impulsive behaviours:} Engaging in risky behaviours (e.g. impulsive spending, reckless driving, or substance misuse) during periods of elevated or highly irritable mood.
    \item \textbf{Cognitive changes:} Difficulty concentrating, impaired decision-making, and negative thought loops during low-mood states.
    \item \textbf{Interpersonal conflict:} Strained relationships with partners, family members, or colleagues due to unpredictable or reactive emotional outbursts.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When evaluating a patient with mood swings, clinical assessment must distinguish between different psychiatric, endocrine, and lifestyle etiologies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bipolar disorder vs. Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD):} Bipolar mood states typically last for days to weeks and occur independently of external events, whereas BPD mood swings are highly reactive to interpersonal triggers (such as perceived rejection) and last for hours or minutes.
    \item \textbf{Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD) vs. Major Depressive Disorder (MDD):} PMDD symptoms are strictly confined to the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle and resolve within days of the onset of menses, whereas MDD symptoms are persistent throughout the month.
    \item \textbf{Hyperthyroidism vs. Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD):} Hyperthyroidism presents with anxiety, tremor, and weight loss, distinguished by abnormal thyroid function tests, whereas GAD is a primary psychiatric condition with normal thyroid levels.
    \item \textbf{Substance-induced mood disorder vs. Primary mood disorder:} Mood changes that occur solely during active substance use or withdrawal, confirmed by clinical history and urine toxicology.
    \item \textbf{Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) vs. Mood disorders:} ADHD is characterized by lifelong emotional dysregulation, hyperactivity, and inattention dating from childhood, rather than adult-onset episodic mood shifts.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
If mood swings are frequent, distressing, or interfere with work, studies, or relationships, medical advice should be sought from a general practitioner or mental health professional.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Active thoughts of suicide, self-harm, or planning to end one's life.
    \item Thoughts of harming others or planning violence.
    \item Severe agitation, panic, or loss of contact with reality (hallucinations or delusions).
    \item Accidental or intentional overdose of medications or substances.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A thorough evaluation involves a comprehensive psychiatric history, structured questionnaires, and laboratory tests to rule out organic causes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical psychiatric interview:} Detailed assessment of the timing, frequency, duration, triggers, and family history of mood disorders.
    \item \textbf{Mood charting:} Keeping a daily mood diary (recording mood, sleep, stressors, and menstrual cycles) to track patterns over several weeks.
    \item \textbf{Thyroid function tests (TFTs):} Assaying thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), free T3, and free T4 to rule out thyroid hyper- or hypo-activity.
    \item \textbf{Screening questionnaires:} Standardized diagnostic tools such as the Mood Disorder Questionnaire (MDQ) for bipolar screening, or the Borderline Symptom List (BSL).
    \item \textbf{Basic metabolic panel:} Full blood count (FBC), serum electrolytes, fasting blood glucose, and vitamin B12/D levels to rule out systemic causes of fatigue and mood lability.
    \item \textbf{Urine toxicology screen:} Used when substance-induced mood changes are suspected to identify drug metabolites.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management requires a multimodal approach encompassing psychotherapy, pharmacological intervention, and structured lifestyle changes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychotherapy:} Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) to identify and alter negative thought patterns, Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT) for developing distress tolerance and emotional regulation skills, or family-focused therapy.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacotherapy:} Mood stabilisers (e.g. lithium, sodium valproate) or atypical antipsychotics (e.g. quetiapine, aripiprazole) for bipolar disorder. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) may be used for depression or PMDD, but require caution in bipolar patients to avoid triggering manic episodes.
    \item \textbf{Hormonal therapies:} Combined oral contraceptive pills or hormone replacement therapy (HRT) under specialist guidance for PMDD or menopause-associated mood swings.
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle modifications:} Strict sleep hygiene (maintaining a regular sleep-wake schedule), regular aerobic exercise (which stimulates endorphin release), and limiting alcohol, caffeine, and nicotine.
    \item \textbf{Stress reduction techniques:} Regular practice of mindfulness, meditation, progressive muscle relaxation, or yoga to regulate autonomic nervous system activity.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Unmanaged chronic mood swings can lead to significant psychological and social complications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Relationship breakdown:} Frequent arguments and emotional volatility leading to marital conflict, divorce, or social isolation.
    \item \textbf{Occupational impairment:} Poor work performance, absenteeism, conflict with coworkers, or job termination.
    \item \textbf{Substance use disorders:} Self-medicating with alcohol, prescription medications, or illicit drugs to manage emotional instability.
    \item \textbf{Financial problems:} Impulsive financial decisions, excessive spending, or high debt accrued during hypomanic or impulsive phases.
    \item \textbf{Self-harm and suicide:} An increased risk of suicidal ideation and self-directed violence during depressive or mixed affective states.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals experiencing mood swings is generally highly positive when the underlying cause is correctly diagnosed and managed. Most patients achieve significant stability through a combination of tailored psychotherapy and medical treatment. Conditions like bipolar disorder and borderline personality disorder require long-term, ongoing management but can be controlled effectively, allowing individuals to lead highly productive lives. Mood swings related to transient hormonal periods (such as pregnancy or perimenopause) typically resolve once the hormone levels stabilise or are medically addressed.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Practical steps that individuals can take to prevent severe mood fluctuations and manage symptoms at home include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Consistent daily routines:} Set fixed times for waking up, meals, exercise, and winding down at night.
    \item \textbf{Utilise mood tracking apps:} Use digital tools to log emotions, sleep duration, and menstrual cycle phases to identify specific triggers.
    \item \textbf{Prioritise sleep hygiene:} Ensure 7--9 hours of quality sleep by keeping the bedroom dark, quiet, and free of electronic screens before bed.
    \item \textbf{Avoid mood-altering substances:} Minimise alcohol, recreational drugs, and excessive caffeine, which can trigger or worsen mood instability.
    \item \textbf{Develop a relapse plan:} Work with a therapist or trusted family members to create a written plan listing early warning signs of mood changes and step-by-step coping strategies.
\end{itemize}
