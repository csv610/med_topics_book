\chapter{Lyme Heart}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Lyme Heart, clinically referred to as Lyme carditis, is a serious but highly reversible manifestation of early disseminated Lyme disease. Lyme disease is a multi-system infectious illness caused by the spirochete bacterium \textit{Borrelia burgdorferi} (and occasionally related species) and transmitted to humans through the bite of infected \textit{Ixodes} ticks. Lyme carditis occurs when the bacteria disseminate hematogenously to the tissues of the heart, triggering a localized inflammatory response. It can affect the myocardium, endocardium, and pericardium, but its most characteristic and clinically significant feature is atrioventricular (AV) conduction block. This conduction block can fluctuate rapidly, progressing from mild PR-interval prolongation to complete, life-threatening heart block.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Lyme carditis develops in approximately 1\% to 10\% of patients with untreated Lyme disease. It is highly prevalent in endemic regions, including the northeastern and mid-western United States, and parts of Europe and Asia. While Lyme-carrying ticks are not endemic to Australia, cases of Lyme carditis are diagnosed in Australian travellers returning from endemic international destinations. The condition exhibits a strong demographic bias, primarily affecting young males, with a male-to-female ratio of approximately 3:1. The reasons for this male predominance are not fully understood but may relate to outdoor exposure and immunological differences. The peak incidence occurs between June and September, matching the seasonal activity of nymphal and adult \textit{Ixodes} ticks.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The condition is caused by the invasion of cardiac tissues by \textit{Borrelia} spirochetes, which induces a cellular immune response that disrupts the heart's normal electrical pathways.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Causative pathogen:} \textit{Borrelia burgdorferi} sensu stricto is the primary cause in North America, while \textit{Borrelia garinii} and \textit{Borrelia afzelii} are more common in Europe and Asia.
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} The bite of infected blacklegged ticks (\textit{Ixodes scapularis} or \textit{Ixodes pacificus} in North America; \textit{Ixodes ricinus} in Europe). The tick must typically remain attached for 36 to 48 hours to transmit the bacteria.
    \item \textbf{Tissue tropism and infiltration:} Spirochetes migrate from the initial bite site into the bloodstream and lodge in the cardiac interstitium, showing a preference for the AV node and the bundle of His.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory cascade:} The presence of spirochetes triggers a dense interstitial infiltrate composed of lymphocytes, macrophages, and plasma cells. This cellular infiltrate causes local tissue oedema and mechanical disruption of the conduction fibres.
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune response:} It is hypothesized that the immune system may produce antibodies against bacterial antigens that cross-react with host cardiac proteins (molecular mimicry), compounding the tissue damage.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Lyme carditis typically presents 1 to 2 months (range 1 week to several months) after the initial tick bite and rash. The symptoms are predominantly related to conduction delay or myocardial inflammation.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conduction symptoms:} Lightheadedness, dizziness, presyncope, palpitations, and syncope (fainting), which result from sudden drops in heart rate due to fluctuating AV block.
    \item \textbf{Myopericarditis symptoms:} Dyspnoea (shortness of breath), orthopnoea, chest pain (which may be pleuritic and ease when sitting forward), and peripheral fluid retention.
    \item \textbf{Systemic features:} Flu-like symptoms including fever, chills, severe fatigue, generalised myalgia, and arthralgia.
    \item \textbf{Erythema migrans:} The classic "bullseye" skin rash, which is present in 50\% to 60\% of patients at the time of carditis presentation or may have resolved weeks prior.
    \item \textbf{Clinical signs:} Severe bradycardia, irregular pulse, pericardial friction rub, and signs of congestive heart failure.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because it presents with conduction block or chest pain in young, active individuals, Lyme carditis must be differentiated from several other cardiac conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Other infectious myocarditis:} Viral infections (e.g., Coxsackievirus, adenovirus, or COVID-19) or other bacterial infections (e.g., diphtheria) causing myocardial inflammation and conduction block.
    \item \textbf{Rheumatic heart disease:} An autoimmune carditis following group A streptococcal pharyngitis, presenting with joint pain, murmurs, and conduction changes in young cohorts.
    \item \textbf{Degenerative AV block:} Age-related calcification and fibrosis of the conduction system (Lev's or Lenegre's disease), which typically occurs in much older patients who lack infectious histories.
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune and infiltrative diseases:} Sarcoidosis, giant cell myocarditis, or systemic lupus erythematosus, which can cause heart block and myocardial inflammation.
    \item \textbf{Drug-induced bradycardia:} Conduction delay caused by medications such as beta-blockers, non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, or digoxin.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Travellers returning from Lyme-endemic areas who develop unexplained dizziness, fainting, shortness of breath, or palpitations must seek immediate medical evaluation.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fainting (syncope) or episodes of severe lightheadedness and near-collapse.
    \item Severe, sudden shortness of breath or difficulty breathing.
    \item Chest pain, chest tightness, or pain that radiates to the neck, back, or jaw.
    \item A pulse rate that is abnormally slow (below 50 beats per minute) associated with weakness or confusion.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A high index of clinical suspicion is required, especially in returning travellers. Diagnosis is established through a combination of electrocardiography and serology.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Electrocardiography (ECG):} The primary tool. It demonstrates first-degree AV block (PR interval greater than 200 ms), Mobitz type I or II second-degree block, or third-degree (complete) AV block. The blocks can change rapidly, necessitating continuous monitoring.
    \item \textbf{Serological testing:} Two-tier serology. An initial Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) followed by a confirmatory Western blot test to detect IgM and IgG antibodies to \textit{Borrelia burgdorferi}.
    \item \textbf{Echocardiography:} Performed to evaluate left ventricular systolic function, detect regional wall motion abnormalities, and screen for pericardial effusions.
    \item \textbf{Cardiac biomarkers:} Measurement of serum troponin and creatine kinase (CK-MB) levels, which may be elevated, reflecting active myocardial damage.
    \item \textbf{Endomyocardial biopsy:} Rarely required, but histopathology shows lymphocytic infiltration and can identify spirochetes using Warthin-Starry silver staining or PCR on tissue samples.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Laparotomy-like vigilance is required, as complete heart block can lead to sudden cardiac arrest. Patients must be hospitalised for treatment and continuous cardiac monitoring.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intravenous antibiotic therapy:} Indicated for patients with high-degree AV block (second- or third-degree) or a PR interval exceeding 300 ms. Intravenous ceftriaxone (2 g daily) is first-line, administered for 14 to 21 days.
    \item \textbf{Oral antibiotic transition:} Once the AV block has resolved and the patient is stable, they can transition to oral antibiotics (typically doxycycline 100 mg twice daily or amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily) to complete the course.
    \item \textbf{Continuous telemetry:} Admitting the patient to a cardiac care or intensive care unit with continuous telemetry monitoring because of the risk of rapid conduction degradation.
    \item \textbf{Temporary cardiac pacing:} A temporary transvenous or transcutaneous pacemaker is required in up to one-third of patients with complete heart block who are haemodynamically unstable.
    \item \textbf{Avoidance of permanent pacing:} Permanent pacemakers are almost never indicated because the conduction system recovers fully once the bacterial infection is eradicated by antibiotics.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Failure to recognise and treat Lyme carditis promptly can lead to severe and potentially fatal cardiac complications.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asystole and sudden death:} Complete failure of the heart's electrical conduction system leading to cardiac arrest.
    \item \textbf{Cardiogenic shock:} Severe impairment of myocardial contractility, resulting in inadequate systemic organ perfusion.
    \item \textbf{Chronic dilated cardiomyopathy:} Permanent structural damage and enlargement of the heart ventricles, causing chronic heart failure.
    \item \textbf{Pericardial effusion and tamponade:} Fluid accumulation in the pericardial sac compressing the heart and preventing normal filling.
    \item \textbf{Inappropriate device implantation:} Permanent pacemaker insertion due to a failure to recognise Lyme carditis as the underlying, reversible cause of heart block.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for Lyme carditis is exceptionally good when diagnosed and treated early with appropriate antibiotics. Over 90\% of patients experience complete resolution of their AV conduction blocks, often within the first 7 to 10 days of starting antibiotic therapy. Left ventricular dysfunction and pericardial symptoms also typically resolve without leaving long-term cardiac deficits. The risk of recurrence is negligible once the infection is fully eradicated, and patients are able to return to their normal baseline activity levels.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Prevention focuses on avoiding tick bites and managing tick exposure when visiting endemic regions overseas.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dress defensively:} Wear light-coloured clothing (making ticks easier to spot), long-sleeved shirts, and long pants tucked into socks when walking in wooded or grassy areas.
    \item \textbf{Use repellents:} Apply insect repellent containing DEET, picaridin, or IR3535 to exposed skin, and treat outdoor gear and clothing with permethrin.
    \item \textbf{Perform tick checks:} Conduct thorough full-body checks on yourself, children, and pets immediately after returning from outdoor activities in endemic zones.
    \item \textbf{Safe tick removal:} Remove ticks immediately using fine-tipped tweezers by grasping the tick close to the skin surface and pulling upward with steady, even pressure. Do not squeeze, twist, or burn the tick.
    \item \textbf{Seek early advice:} Consult a doctor if you develop a rash or flu-like symptoms within 30 days of a tick bite in an endemic region.
\end{itemize}
