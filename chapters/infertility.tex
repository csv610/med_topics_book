\chapter{Infertility}
\index{Infertility}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Infertility is the inability to conceive after twelve months of regular unprotected intercourse, or after six months in women over 35. It affects around one in six couples and can be caused by problems in the woman, the man, or both, or may be unexplained. The causes include ovulation disorders, blocked fallopian tubes, uterine problems, low sperm count or function, and age-related decline in fertility. Investigation and treatment are highly effective, and options range from lifestyle change and ovulation induction to intrauterine insemination and in vitro fertilisation (IVF). Infertility is also emotionally demanding, and support for the couple is an important part of care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Around one in six couples in many countries experience infertility, and the proportion of people seeking fertility treatment has risen steadily. Female age is the single most important factor: fertility declines gradually from the early 30s and more sharply after 35, as both egg quantity and quality fall. Around a third of infertility is due to female factors, a third to male factors, and the remainder to combined or unexplained causes. Ovulation disorders such as polycystic ovary syndrome are the most common female cause, while sperm problems account for most male infertility. Lifestyle factors, including smoking, alcohol, obesity, and being underweight, contribute significantly.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Conception requires ovulation, healthy sperm, open fallopian tubes, and a uterus capable of supporting implantation. Female causes of infertility include anovulation and irregular ovulation, as in polycystic ovary syndrome, premature ovarian insufficiency, and thyroid or hormonal disorders; tubal blockage from infection, endometriosis, or previous surgery; and uterine problems such as fibroids, polyps, or adhesions. Endometriosis can impair fertility through inflammation, adhesions, and tubal damage. Male causes include low sperm count, poor sperm motility or shape, obstruction of the sperm ducts, hormonal disorders, and genetic conditions. Unexplained infertility reflects subtle problems in fertilisation or implantation that current tests cannot detect.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Twelve months or more of regular unprotected intercourse without conception
    \item Six months without conception in a woman over 35
    \item Irregular or absent periods, suggesting ovulation problems
    \item Painful periods or pelvic pain, which may indicate endometriosis
    \item Previous pelvic infection, surgery, or ectopic pregnancy
    \item Symptoms of hormone disorders: acne, excess hair, or galactorrhoea
    \item No symptoms in many cases of male-factor or unexplained infertility
    \item Psychological features: anxiety, stress, and strain on the relationship
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The diagnosis of infertility is a description, not a single disease, and the underlying cause must be found.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ovulation disorders:} polycystic ovary syndrome, hypothalamic or thyroid causes, premature ovarian insufficiency
    \item \textbf{Tubal disease:} blockage from infection, endometriosis, or surgery
    \item \textbf{Uterine factors:} fibroids, polyps, adhesions, or congenital anomalies
    \item \textbf{Endometriosis:} affecting the tubes, ovaries, and pelvis
    \item \textbf{Male factors:} low count, poor motility, obstruction, or ejaculatory dysfunction
    \item \textbf{Unexplained infertility:} no abnormality found on standard testing
    \item \textbf{Age-related decline:} reduced egg quantity and quality
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Couples should seek assessment after twelve months of trying, or after six months if the woman is over 35. Earlier review is appropriate for women with irregular periods, known endometriosis or pelvic disease, and for couples with known risk factors or a strong desire to act early. Men with symptoms of hormonal, ejaculatory, or testicular problems should also be assessed. Fertility treatment should be planned with medical advice, and its emotional impact acknowledged.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe abdominal or pelvic pain, which may indicate a ruptured ovarian cyst or ectopic pregnancy
    \item Heavy vaginal bleeding with dizziness or collapse
    \item Shoulder-tip pain with a positive pregnancy test, which may indicate ectopic pregnancy
    \item Sudden painful swelling of the scrotum, which may indicate testicular torsion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History from both partners:} duration of trying, cycles, previous pregnancies, surgery, infections, medicines, and lifestyle
    \item \textbf{Ovulation assessment:} cycle tracking and mid-cycle blood tests of progesterone
    \item \textbf{Hormone tests:} for the woman, including FSH, oestradiol, anti-Müllerian hormone, thyroid function, and prolactin
    \item \textbf{Pelvic ultrasound:} assessment of the uterus, ovaries, and follicles
    \item \textbf{Tubal assessment:} hysterosalpingogram or ultrasound with contrast to check the fallopian tubes
    \item \textbf{Semen analysis:} the first test for the man, repeated if abnormal
    \item \textbf{Male hormone and genetic tests:} where sperm problems are found
    \item \textbf{Laparoscopy:} in selected cases to diagnose endometriosis or pelvic disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle optimisation:} healthy weight, stopping smoking, limiting alcohol, and reducing stress
    \item \textbf{Timed intercourse:} using ovulation prediction to time intercourse
    \item \textbf{Ovulation induction:} medicines such as clomiphene or letrozole, sometimes with ultrasound monitoring
    \item \textbf{Intrauterine insemination (IUI):} prepared sperm placed in the uterus around ovulation
    \item \textbf{In vitro fertilisation (IVF):} eggs collected after stimulation, fertilised in the laboratory, and a resulting embryo transferred
    \item \textbf{Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI):} a single sperm injected into an egg for severe male-factor infertility
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} treatment of endometriosis, tubal disease, fibroids, or varicoceles
    \item \textbf{Egg and sperm donation, and surrogacy:} for specific circumstances
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} counselling for the emotional and relationship impacts
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Multiple pregnancy and its risks with ovarian stimulation and embryo transfer
    \item Ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome, a potentially serious complication of stimulation
    \item Ectopic pregnancy
    \item Complications of egg collection and surgery
    \item Emotional distress, depression, and anxiety
    \item Strain on relationships and financial costs of treatment
    \item Rare risks of treatment, including a small increased risk of some ovarian conditions
    \item Unresolved infertility when treatment is unsuccessful
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Outcomes depend on the cause and, above all, on the woman's age. Live birth rates per IVF cycle fall with age: around 30 to 40 per cent for women under 35, declining steeply after 40. Ovulation induction is very effective for anovulatory women, and tubal surgery and endometriosis treatment help selected couples. Even without treatment, many couples with unexplained infertility conceive within a few years. Realistic expectations, early referral, and psychological support improve the experience of treatment, and adoption and donor options provide alternative paths to family.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek assessment early, especially after age 35
    \item Maintain a healthy body weight and stay physically active
    \item Stop smoking, limit alcohol, and avoid recreational drugs
    \item Limit caffeine and avoid excessive exercise
    \item Take folic acid if planning pregnancy
    \item Manage stress and support each other through the process
    \item Protect fertility: treat pelvic infections promptly and avoid unnecessary surgery
    \item Consider freezing eggs or sperm before treatments that harm fertility
    \item Seek counselling when the emotional load becomes heavy
\end{itemize}
