\chapter{Atherosclerosis (Peripheral)}
\index{Atherosclerosis (Peripheral)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Peripheral atherosclerosis is the build-up of fatty plaques inside the walls of arteries outside the heart and brain, most commonly in the arteries supplying the legs. The clinical term for the resulting disease is peripheral arterial disease (PAD). Over time, plaque narrows and hardens the arteries, reducing blood flow so that muscles and skin do not receive enough oxygen, particularly during exercise. The same disease process that causes PAD also causes heart attack and stroke, so a diagnosis of peripheral atherosclerosis is an important warning sign of widespread artery disease affecting the whole circulation. Early recognition allows risk factors to be treated and the risks of heart attack, stroke and limb loss to be substantially reduced.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Peripheral arterial disease becomes increasingly common with age and is rare before middle age. Roughly one in ten people over the age of 70 has PAD, and many of those affected have no symptoms at all. It is more common in men, in smokers, and in people with diabetes, high blood pressure, high cholesterol or a family history of heart disease. Smoking is the strongest risk factor. Many people with PAD also have coronary heart disease or have had a stroke, reflecting the systemic nature of atherosclerosis. Because symptoms often go unreported or are dismissed as part of ageing, the true number of affected people is higher than the number formally diagnosed.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Atherosclerosis begins when the inner lining (endothelium) of an artery is damaged by factors such as smoking, high blood pressure, diabetes or high cholesterol. Fatty material, including cholesterol, then accumulates in the artery wall and triggers a chronic inflammatory response.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Plaque formation:} cholesterol and inflammatory cells build up, forming a hardened, thickened area of artery wall
    \item \textbf{Narrowing:} plaque reduces the artery lumen and limits blood flow to the leg muscles and skin
    \item \textbf{Claudication:} during exercise the muscles demand more oxygen, so pain develops until rest restores supply
    \item \textbf{Plaque rupture:} a plaque can fissure and a clot form on it, suddenly blocking the artery
    \item \textbf{Critical ischaemia:} very severe narrowing reduces flow even at rest, threatening the limb
    \item \textbf{Risk factor synergy:} smoking, diabetes, hypertension and high cholesterol combine to accelerate damage
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The classic symptom is cramping calf pain brought on by walking and relieved by rest, called intermittent claudication. Many people, however, have no symptoms at all, and disease may be found only on examination.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intermittent claudication:} aching, cramping or tiredness in the calf, thigh or buttock that comes on with walking and settles within minutes of rest
    \item Pain in the foot or toes at night that is eased by hanging the leg out of bed
    \item Cold, pale or blue skin on the foot or toes
    \item Numbness, tingling or reduced sensation in the foot
    \item Poor wound healing, or non-healing sores and ulcers on the toes or feet
    \item Hair loss on the legs, shiny skin and weak or absent pulses in the foot
    \item In severe cases, pain at rest and signs of tissue death (gangrene)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Leg pain with walking has many causes besides blocked leg arteries.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spinal stenosis and nerve root compression:} leg symptoms arising from the spine, often with back pain, numbness or tingling
    \item \textbf{Osteoarthritis:} joint pain, typically in the hips or knees, made worse by movement of the joint itself
    \item \textbf{Muscle and tendon disorders:} strains, cramps or exertional compartment problems
    \item \textbf{Deep vein thrombosis:} acute swelling and pain from a clot in the veins
    \item \textbf{Venous disease:} calf discomfort from varicose veins or chronic venous insufficiency
    \item \textbf{Acute limb ischaemia:} sudden blockage from a clot or embolus, a surgical emergency
    \item \textbf{Diabetic neuropathy:} burning or shooting foot pain that is not from poor blood flow
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a GP if walking brings on calf or leg pain that settles with rest, if there are non-healing sores on the feet, or if there is a family history of vascular disease or other risk factors.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A suddenly cold, pale, blue or intensely painful leg or foot -- this is an emergency
    \item Sudden loss of a pulse or movement in a leg
    \item Chest pain, shortness of breath, or sudden weakness or speech problems, as PAD commonly accompanies heart attack and stroke
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis is made from the history and confirmed with simple, non-invasive tests that measure blood flow.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} walking distance before pain, and checking pulses, skin colour and temperature in the feet
    \item \textbf{Ankle-brachial index (ABI):} comparing blood pressure at the ankle with the arm; a low ratio suggests PAD
    \item \textbf{Post-exercise ABI:} measuring the index again after walking, which can reveal disease that is otherwise hidden at rest
    \item \textbf{Duplex ultrasound:} imaging that shows the arteries and measures blood flow at different points
    \item \textbf{CT or MR angiography:} detailed pictures of the leg arteries if an intervention is being considered
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} cholesterol, blood sugar (for diabetes), kidney function and blood count
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular risk assessment:} because the heart and brain arteries are usually affected too
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment aims to relieve symptoms, stop the leg arteries from worsening, and reduce the risk of heart attack and stroke.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Smoking cessation:} the single most important step; greatly improves symptoms and reduces the risk of amputation
    \item \textbf{Supervised exercise:} regular walking programmes improve walking distance in many people
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} statins to lower cholesterol, antiplatelet drugs such as aspirin or clopidogrel, and medicines to control blood pressure and diabetes
    \item \textbf{Symptom relief:} certain medicines can help with claudication pain when exercise alone is not enough
    \item \textbf{Foot care:} careful daily care of the feet and early treatment of any wound
    \item \textbf{Angioplasty and stenting:} opening a blocked artery with a balloon, sometimes leaving a small stent
    \item \textbf{Bypass surgery:} using a vein or synthetic tube to route blood around a long blockage
    \item \textbf{Amputation:} in advanced, irreversible disease, part of the limb may need to be removed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Peripheral atherosclerosis is a serious condition with risks extending beyond the legs.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Critical limb ischaemia:} severe pain at rest and tissue loss; without treatment the limb may be lost
    \item \textbf{Amputation:} risk is higher, especially in smokers and people with diabetes
    \item \textbf{Non-healing ulcers and gangrene:} foot infections can spread to bone and the bloodstream
    \item \textbf{Heart attack and stroke:} people with PAD are at high risk of events in other arteries
    \item \textbf{Acute limb ischaemia:} sudden blockage creating an emergency that threatens the limb
    \item \textbf{Complications of procedures:} bleeding, infection or re-narrowing after angioplasty or bypass
    \item Reduced mobility, independence and quality of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends heavily on risk factors. Many people with intermittent claudication remain stable or improve, especially if they stop smoking and take up exercise; fewer than one in four worsen over several years. However, PAD signals widespread atherosclerosis, so the risk of heart attack and stroke is considerably higher than in the general population, and this is the main threat to health. With vigorous risk-factor control and, when needed, revascularisation, most legs can be saved and symptoms improved. Regular vascular and cardiovascular follow-up is important.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Peripheral atherosclerosis is largely preventable because its main causes are lifestyle-related.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Do not smoke, and avoid exposure to tobacco smoke
    \item Keep blood pressure and cholesterol within a healthy range, taking prescribed medicines
    \item Manage diabetes carefully with good blood sugar control
    \item Walk regularly, aiming for daily activity that increases gradually
    \item Eat a heart-healthy diet low in saturated fat and salt, and keep a healthy weight
    \item Examine the feet daily, keep them clean and dry, and see a podiatrist regularly if diabetic
    \item Attend vascular and cardiovascular reviews as recommended, even when symptoms are mild
\end{itemize}
