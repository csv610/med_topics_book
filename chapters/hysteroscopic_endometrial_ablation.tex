\chapter{Hysteroscopic Endometrial Ablation}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hysteroscopic endometrial ablation is a procedure that removes or destroys the lining of the uterus (the endometrium) to reduce or stop heavy menstrual bleeding. It is performed through a hysteroscope, a thin telescope passed through the vagina and cervix into the uterus, which allows the surgeon to view the cavity while removing the lining using techniques such as electrosurgery, resection, or radiofrequency energy. The procedure is a day-surgery alternative to hysterectomy for women with heavy periods who have completed their family, and it is typically chosen when medical treatments have been ineffective or are not tolerated.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Heavy menstrual bleeding is one of the most common reasons women seek gynaecological care, affecting around one in four women of reproductive age. Endometrial ablation is used in a significant proportion of these women, particularly those aged over 40 whose symptoms have not responded to hormonal or non-hormonal medical treatment. As it is a fertility-ending procedure, its use has fallen in recent decades in favour of intrauterine hormonal devices and other conservative options, but it remains a valuable, less invasive alternative to hysterectomy for suitable women. The availability of trained surgeons and hospital day-surgery services influences how commonly the procedure is offered.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The endometrium is the hormone-sensitive inner lining of the uterus that thickens each month and is shed during menstruation. Heavy bleeding can result from hormonal imbalance, structural problems such as fibroids or polyps, or disorders of the blood clotting system, although in many women no cause is identified. Endometrial ablation works by removing the full thickness of the lining so that it cannot rebuild significantly, thereby eliminating or markedly reducing menstrual flow. Because the lining has limited ability to regenerate after the procedure, pregnancy is not safely possible afterwards, which is why ablation is reserved for women who have completed their family.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heavy or prolonged menstrual bleeding that interferes with daily life
    \item Periods requiring changing pads or tampons every one to two hours
    \item Flooding, passing clots, or anaemia with tiredness and breathlessness
    \item Symptoms present despite medical treatments such as hormonal therapy
    \item After the procedure: crampy pain and a watery or blood-stained discharge for a few weeks
    \item Successful outcomes include lighter periods, shorter periods, or no periods at all
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Heavy menstrual bleeding may be caused by conditions other than a dysfunctional lining, which change the management approach.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Uterine fibroids:} benign muscle tumours that often distort the cavity
    \item \textbf{Endometrial polyps:} benign growths of the lining that cause bleeding
    \item \textbf{Adenomyosis:} endometrial tissue growing into the uterine muscle
    \item \textbf{Endometrial hyperplasia or cancer:} abnormal proliferation of the lining, more likely in older women
    \item \textbf{Thyroid disease and bleeding disorders:} systemic causes of heavy periods
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy-related bleeding:} miscarriage or ectopic pregnancy in women of reproductive age
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Women with heavy or irregular bleeding should be assessed to identify the cause and to confirm that the lining is normal before ablation is considered. Review is needed if symptoms do not improve after the procedure, if pain is severe, or if there is a foul-smelling discharge or fever, which may indicate infection.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heavy bleeding that soaks pads or tampons within an hour, especially with faintness or dizziness
    \item Severe abdominal pain, high fever, or shaking after the procedure
    \item Signs of perforation or internal bleeding, including sudden severe pain and collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} menstrual pattern, contraceptive plans, and bimanual pelvic examination
    \item \textbf{Pelvic ultrasound:} to assess uterine size, the cavity, and any fibroids or polyps
    \item \textbf{Endometrial biopsy or hysteroscopy:} to confirm the lining is benign and exclude cancer or hyperplasia
    \item \textbf{Full blood count and iron studies:} to assess anaemia from chronic blood loss
    \item \textbf{Cervical screening and swabs:} to exclude infection before the procedure
    \item \textbf{Pre-procedure counselling:} to confirm the woman has completed her family and understands the risks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-procedure preparation:} the lining may be thinned beforehand with hormones or the procedure timed just after menstruation
    \item \textbf{Technique:} the endometrium is destroyed or resected under direct vision using the hysteroscope, usually under general anaesthesia
    \item \textbf{Aftercare:} most women go home the same day with analgesia and advice on expected discharge
    \item \textbf{Contraception:} reliable contraception is required because pregnancy after ablation is dangerous
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review at six to eight weeks to assess symptom response
    \item \textbf{Alternative treatments:} hormonal intrauterine devices, tranexamic acid, or hysterectomy for women who do not improve or who have structural problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection of the uterus or pelvis after the procedure
    \item Bleeding, uterine perforation, or damage to nearby organs, which is rare but serious
    \item Fluid overload from the fluid used to distend the uterus
    \item Post-ablation syndrome: cramping pain and fluid collections in women whose lining regrows behind scar tissue
    \item Failure to control bleeding, with need for further treatment or hysterectomy
    \item Danger of unrecognised pregnancy, which carries high risks of miscarriage and placenta problems
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Around three-quarters of women have significantly lighter periods after endometrial ablation, and many become amenorrhoeic. Between one in ten and one in five women later require further surgery, most often hysterectomy, usually for ongoing pain or bleeding. Complications are uncommon when the procedure is performed by experienced surgeons on carefully selected women. Overall satisfaction is high, and for suitable women the procedure offers a rapid recovery and avoids the greater risks and longer recovery of hysterectomy.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discuss all medical options for heavy bleeding, including the hormonal intrauterine device, before choosing ablation
    \item Confirm with a doctor that family is complete, as the procedure ends fertility
    \item Have the lining checked to exclude cancer or hyperplasia before the procedure
    \item Plan reliable contraception for afterwards
    \item Expect crampy pain and discharge for a few weeks and use simple analgesia as advised
    \item Report fever, severe pain, or foul-smelling discharge promptly
    \item Take iron supplements if anaemic and eat an iron-rich diet while recovering
\end{itemize}
