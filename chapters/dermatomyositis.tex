\chapter{Dermatomyositis}
\index{Dermatomyositis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Dermatomyositis is a rare autoimmune disease that causes inflammation of the muscles and characteristic skin changes. It belongs to a group of conditions called inflammatory myopathies. The muscle inflammation causes weakness, typically in the muscles closest to the trunk, such as the shoulders, hips, and neck, while the skin shows a distinctive rash, including a violet-coloured rash on the eyelids and red patches on the knuckles, elbows, and knees. Dermatomyositis can affect people of any age, including children, and requires specialist treatment. In adults it is associated with an increased risk of some cancers.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Dermatomyositis is rare, affecting around 5--10 people per million. It occurs in adults, most commonly in the 40--60 year age range, and in children, typically between ages 5 and 15, where it is called juvenile dermatomyositis. It is about twice as common in women as in men. The association with cancer is important in adults: around 10--25\% of adults with dermatomyositis develop an associated cancer, most often around the time of diagnosis. With treatment, most people improve, but it can cause significant disability if untreated.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune inflammation:} the immune system attacks the small blood vessels and muscle fibres, causing inflammation and weakness
    \item \textbf{Genetic susceptibility:} certain HLA genes increase risk
    \item \textbf{Environmental triggers:} infections, medications, and other exposures may trigger the disease in susceptible people
    \item \textbf{Skin and muscle involvement:} immune deposits in the skin cause the characteristic rash, while muscle inflammation causes weakness
    \item \textbf{Associated cancer:} in adults, the disease can be a paraneoplastic condition related to underlying malignancy
    \item \textbf{Interstitial lung disease:} the lungs can be involved in some people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Progressive muscle weakness, most marked in the shoulders, hips, and neck
    \item Difficulty raising the arms, climbing stairs, standing from a chair, or getting up from lying down
    \item The characteristic rash: a violet (heliotrope) discolouration of the eyelids, often with swelling
    \item Red or violet scaly patches over the knuckles (Gottron's papules), elbows, knees, and other joints
    \item The rash may appear before, with, or after the muscle weakness
    \item Muscle pain, fatigue, and difficulty swallowing in some people
    \item Weight loss, fever, and Raynaud's phenomenon
    \item In children: the same features, sometimes with calcium deposits under the skin (calcinosis)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Polymyositis, which has muscle inflammation without the skin rash
    \item Inclusion body myositis, which affects different muscles and older people
    \item Lupus, scleroderma, and other autoimmune connective tissue diseases
    \item Drug-induced myopathy, including from statins
    \item Thyroid disease and other metabolic causes of muscle weakness
    \item Muscular dystrophies and neurological conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with progressive muscle weakness and a skin rash should see a doctor urgently, as early treatment of dermatomyositis prevents permanent muscle damage. People diagnosed with the disease need specialist care from a rheumatologist and may need evaluation for associated cancer.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden difficulty breathing, which may indicate lung involvement
    \item Difficulty swallowing with choking or inability to manage secretions
    \item Sudden severe weakness or inability to move
    \item Chest pain or other signs of a serious complication
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} muscle enzymes such as creatine kinase are elevated in active disease
    \item \textbf{Autoantibodies:} myositis-specific antibodies help confirm the diagnosis and predict organ involvement
    \item \textbf{Electromyography (EMG):} shows the characteristic pattern of muscle inflammation
    \item \textbf{Muscle biopsy:} the definitive test, showing inflammation and immune deposits
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} MRI of muscles shows inflammation and guides biopsy
    \item \textbf{Cancer screening:} age-appropriate screening for associated malignancy in adults
    \item \textbf{Lung assessment:} lung function tests and imaging where interstitial lung disease is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Corticosteroids:} high-dose prednisolone is the first-line treatment, tapered slowly once control is achieved
    \item \textbf{Immunosuppressants:} drugs such as methotrexate, azathioprine, or mycophenolate reduce steroid requirements
    \item \textbf{Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG):} effective for resistant disease and for some complications
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} graded exercise and stretching maintain muscle strength and prevent contractures
    \item \textbf{Skin care:} sun protection, as the rash is worsened by ultraviolet light
    \item \textbf{Treatment of associated cancer:} treating the underlying malignancy can improve the myositis
    \item \textbf{Management of complications:} treatment of lung disease, swallowing problems, and calcinosis
    \item \textbf{Long-term monitoring:} regular review of muscle strength, enzymes, and organs
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Permanent muscle weakness and disability if treatment is delayed
    \item Interstitial lung disease, which can be serious
    \item Difficulty swallowing, leading to malnutrition and aspiration
    \item Associated malignancy in adults
    \item Calcinosis and contractures in juvenile disease
    \item Side effects of long-term steroids and immunosuppressants
    \item Osteoporosis from steroid treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With early and appropriate treatment, most people with dermatomyositis improve significantly, and many achieve remission and regain normal muscle strength. The response to treatment is generally good, although some people need long-term immunosuppression, and relapses can occur. The outlook is influenced by lung involvement and, in adults, by the presence of associated cancer. Juvenile dermatomyositis responds well to treatment, though calcinosis can be a long-term problem. Overall, with specialist care, most people live active lives.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek medical assessment promptly for muscle weakness with a rash
    \item Take immunosuppressant medication exactly as prescribed
    \item Attend regular follow-up, including blood tests and organ checks
    \item Protect the skin from the sun with sunscreen and clothing
    \item Maintain gentle, regular exercise guided by a physiotherapist
    \item Eat well and maintain weight, with speech pathology support if swallowing is affected
    \item Report new symptoms, including breathlessness, promptly
\end{itemize}
