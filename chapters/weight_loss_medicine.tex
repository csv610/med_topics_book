\chapter{Weight loss medicine}
\index{Weight loss medicine}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Obesity medicine, or weight loss medicine, is a specialized field of clinical practice focused on the pathophysiological mechanisms, diagnosis, and multi-modal treatment of overweight and obesity. For decades, clinical medicine and society at large viewed excess adiposity as a lifestyle choice or a failure of personal discipline. However, a major paradigm shift has occurred, led by major medical organizations such as the American Medical Association (AMA) in 2013, which formally recognized obesity as a chronic, progressive, relapsing disease. Weight loss medicine is indicated for individuals who meet specific diagnostic criteria, typically defined as having a Body Mass Index (BMI) of 30~kg/m$^2$ or greater, or a BMI of 27~kg/m$^2$ or greater when accompanied by one or more weight-related comorbidities, such as type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, or obstructive sleep apnoea. The therapeutic goal of weight loss medicine is not cosmetic; rather, it aims to reduce systemic inflammation, improve insulin sensitivity, mitigate cardiovascular risk, and reverse or control weight-related comorbidities.

The history of anti-obesity medications is characterized by safety challenges and subsequent regulatory withdrawals. Early treatments in the 20th century included thyroid hormone extracts, 2,4-dinitrophenol (which uncoupled mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation but caused fatal hyperthermia), amphetamines (which caused addiction and severe cardiovascular complications), and fenfluramine-phentermine (``fen-phen'', which was withdrawn in 1997 due to cardiac valvulopathy). Later, agents like sibutramine (withdrawn due to myocardial infarction and stroke risks) and rimonabant (withdrawn due to psychiatric adverse events, including depression and suicidal ideation) further highlighted the need for rigorous safety standards. Today, weight loss medicine is undergoing a revolution driven by a deeper understanding of the gut-brain axis and the development of highly effective, safe incretin-based therapies. These contemporary pharmacotherapies have transformed the treatment landscape, offering weight reduction efficacy that approaches that of bariatric surgery while maintaining favorable safety profiles.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of obesity indicates a global pandemic that threatens healthcare systems worldwide. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the global prevalence of obesity has nearly tripled since 1975. Currently, more than 1 billion people worldwide are living with obesity, including approximately 650 million adults, 340 million adolescents, and 39 million children. The economic burden of this pandemic is staggering, with direct and indirect healthcare costs estimated to exceed hundreds of billions of dollars annually. In the United States, the prevalence of obesity among adults is approximately 41.9\%, reflecting a steady upward trend over the past two decades. In many countries, the situation is similarly critical, with 31.3\% of adults classified as obese and another 35.6\% classified as overweight, meaning that approximately two-thirds of the adult population carries excess weight. In the United Kingdom, obesity affects approximately 28\% of the adult population.

Epidemiological data show significant disparities across demographics. In high-income countries, obesity rates are often inversely correlated with socioeconomic status and education level, reflecting systemic issues such as food insecurity, lack of access to fresh and affordable produce, and limited safe environments for physical recreation. Conversely, in low- and middle-income nations, the rapid transition to westernized diets and sedentary occupations has led to a dual burden of disease, where undernutrition and obesity co-exist within the same communities or households. Furthermore, ethnic differences influence metabolic susceptibility to adiposity. East Asian and South Asian populations, for example, exhibit a higher proportion of visceral fat and greater metabolic risk at lower BMIs than Caucasian populations. Consequently, the World Health Organization recommends lower BMI thresholds for these populations, defining overweight as 23~kg/m$^2$ or greater and obesity as 25~kg/m$^2$ or greater. Despite the high prevalence of individuals who qualify for weight loss medicine under clinical guidelines, a stark treatment gap exists. It is estimated that less than 5\% of eligible adults are prescribed anti-obesity medications. This underutilization is attributed to a combination of clinical inertia, deeply ingrained weight stigma among healthcare providers, patient reluctance, and systemic financial barriers, as many public health systems and private insurers do not cover anti-obesity pharmacotherapy.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The etiology of obesity is multifactorial, involving an interaction between genetic susceptibility, neuroendocrine pathways, environmental influences, and behavioral patterns. At its core, obesity is a disease of energy dysregulation where energy intake chronically exceeds energy expenditure, resulting in the storage of excess energy as triglycerides within adipose tissue. However, this simple thermodynamic equation is governed by a highly complex, homeostatically regulated neuroendocrine network. The primary site for the regulation of energy balance is the hypothalamus, specifically the arcuate nucleus (ARC). The ARC contains two distinct populations of neurons that receive peripheral signals regarding energy status. The first population consists of pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC) and cocaine- and amphetamine-regulated transcript (CART) neurons, which are anorexigenic (appetite-suppressing). When stimulated, POMC neurons release alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone ($\alpha$-MSH), which binds to melanocortin 4 receptors (MC4R) in the paraventricular nucleus, signaling satiety. The second population consists of neuropeptide Y (NPY) and agouti-related peptide (AgRP) neurons, which are orexigenic (appetite-stimulating) and act to inhibit MC4R, signaling hunger.

Peripheral feedback to these neuronal circuits is mediated by hormones from the adipose tissue and gastrointestinal tract. Leptin, a peptide hormone secreted by adipocytes in proportion to fat mass, acts as a long-term signal of energy stores, binding to receptors in the hypothalamus to stimulate POMC/CART neurons and inhibit NPY/AgRP neurons. In individuals with obesity, circulating leptin levels are elevated, but the satiety signal is ineffective due to leptin resistance, which is characterized by impaired transport across the blood-brain barrier and defective intracellular signaling. Conversely, ghrelin, secreted by the stomach preprandially, acts as an acute hunger signal, stimulating NPY/AgRP neurons. Postprandial satiety signals include peptide YY (PYY), cholecystokinin (CCK), and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), which are released by enteroendocrine cells in response to nutrient ingestion and signal satiety to the brain via the vagus nerve and direct circulation.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Genetic Predisposition}: Twin and familial studies indicate that the heritability of body weight is high, ranging between 40\% and 70\%. While monogenic obesity is rare and typically caused by mutations in the leptin gene, leptin receptor gene, or MC4R, common obesity is polygenic. It is driven by the cumulative effect of hundreds of genetic variants, such as those in the fat mass and obesity-associated (FTO) gene, which influence appetite, satiety, energy expenditure, and fat distribution.
\item \textbf{Neuroendocrine Dysregulation}: This is characterized by central resistance to satiety hormones (leptin and insulin) and a blunted postprandial release of gut-derived satiety peptides (GLP-1, PYY). Additionally, the hedonic (pleasure-seeking) pathways of the mesolimbic dopamine system can override homeostatic satiety, leading to hyperphagia in response to hyper-palatable food cues.
\item \textbf{Environmental and Dietary Factors}: The modern obesogenic environment is characterized by an abundance of cheap, calorie-dense, ultra-processed foods that are high in refined sugars and saturated fats but low in nutritional value and fiber. This is compounded by structural barriers to physical activity, sedentary occupations, and urban designs that favor vehicular transport over active commuting.
\item \textbf{Psychosocial and Behavioral Drivers}: Chronic psychological stress activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, elevating circulating cortisol levels, which promotes visceral fat accumulation and stimulates appetite. Sleep deprivation and circadian disruption disrupt the balance of ghrelin and leptin, promoting food cravings, while emotional eating acts as a maladaptive coping mechanism for trauma, depression, and anxiety.
\item \textbf{Medication-Induced Weight Gain}: The prescription of certain medications can inadvertently cause weight gain by increasing appetite, altering metabolic rate, or promoting fluid retention. Key offending agents include atypical antipsychotics (e.g., olanzapine, clozapine), systemic glucocorticoids (e.g., prednisone), tricyclic antidepressants, certain beta-blockers, and anti-diabetic medications such as sulfonylureas and insulin.
\item \textbf{Secondary Endocrine Pathologies}: Endocrine disorders can directly alter metabolic rate and energy homeostasis, leading to secondary weight gain. These include primary hypothyroidism (reduced metabolic rate due to low thyroid hormone levels), Cushing's syndrome (excessive cortisol production driving central adiposity), and polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS, where insulin resistance and hyperandrogenism promote weight retention).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of obesity is progressive and affects multiple organ systems. Patients seeking weight loss medicine present with a variety of symptoms and signs related to mechanical, metabolic, and psychological burdens. Mechanically, the physical strain of carrying excess weight leads to chronic joint pain, particularly in the weight-bearing joints such as the knees, hips, and lumbar spine, accelerating the onset of osteoarthritis. Patients frequently report reduced physical mobility, persistent fatigue, and exertional dyspnea (shortness of breath) due to the increased workload on the respiratory muscles and chest wall restriction. Sleep disturbances are common, with patients exhibiting classic symptoms of obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA), including loud snoring, witnessed nocturnal gasping or choking episodes, and severe daytime somnolence. Metabolically, patients often present with signs of insulin resistance, such as acanthosis nigricans, which is characterized by hyperpigmented, velvety plaques in intertriginous areas like the posterior neck and axillae. Other physical findings include abdominal striae (stretch marks), peripheral pitting oedema, and intertrigo (fungal infections in skin folds). From a cardiovascular standpoint, hypertension is common, manifested by elevated resting blood pressure measurements. Psychologically, patients frequently experience body image dissatisfaction, low self-esteem, clinical depression, and social anxiety, which can lead to social withdrawal and further impede their ability to engage in health-promoting behaviors.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Physical and Anthropometric Signs}: Body Mass Index (BMI) of 30~kg/m$^2$ or greater, abdominal adiposity indicated by a waist circumference exceeding 102 cm in men and 88 cm in women, and skin manifestations such as acanthosis nigricans and skin tag accumulation.
\item \textbf{Cardiorespiratory Symptoms}: Exertional dyspnea, orthopnea, nocturnal snoring, witnessed apnoea, and daytime sleepiness that impairs daily performance.
\item \textbf{Metabolic and Vascular Features}: Elevated blood pressure, signs of dyslipidaemia (such as xanthomas in rare cases), and systemic microvascular changes associated with chronic insulin resistance.
\item \textbf{Psychosocial and Functional Signs}: Reduced joint range of motion, antalgic gait, signs of depressive disorder, and social isolation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When evaluating a patient for weight loss medicine, it is essential to distinguish primary obesity (driven by lifestyle, genetic, and environmental factors) from secondary causes of weight gain or conditions that mimic fat accumulation. A thorough differential diagnosis ensures that underlying medical conditions are identified and treated appropriately.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Hypothyroidism}: An underactive thyroid gland leads to a deficiency of thyroid hormones (thyroxine, T4; and triiodothyronine, T3), which are key regulators of the basal metabolic rate. Hypothyroidism is characterized by weight gain (primarily due to fluid and mucopolysaccharide accumulation), lethargy, cold intolerance, bradycardia, dry skin, and constipation, and is confirmed by elevated TSH and low free T4 levels.
\item \textbf{Cushing's Syndrome}: This disorder results from chronic exposure to excess glucocorticoids, either due to endogenous overproduction (ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma, adrenal tumor) or exogenous administration. It presents with a distinct clinical phenotype, including central obesity with proximal muscle wasting, moon facies, a dorsocervical fat pad (``buffalo hump''), wide purple abdominal striae, easy bruising, and hypertension.
\item \textbf{Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS)}: A common endocrine disorder in premenopausal women characterized by ovulatory dysfunction, hyperandrogenism (hirsutism, acne), and polycystic ovaries on ultrasound. PCOS is strongly associated with insulin resistance and visceral adiposity, which makes weight management particularly challenging.
\item \textbf{Hypothalamic Obesity}: Damage to the satiety centers in the hypothalamus, particularly the ventromedial or paraventricular nuclei, can lead to severe, rapid-onset weight gain and hyperphagia. This damage is typically caused by brain tumors (such as craniopharyngiomas), cranial radiation, neurosurgery, or head trauma, and is characterized by a complete absence of satiety cues.
\item \textbf{Iatrogenic Weight Gain}: A critical differential is weight gain caused by prescription medications. A systematic audit of the patient's medication list must be performed to identify offending agents, such as atypical antipsychotics, glucocorticoids, gabapentinoids, tricyclic antidepressants, and insulin-secretagogues, and alternatives should be considered.
\item \textbf{Pathological Fluid Retention (Oedema)}: Conditions that cause significant fluid retention, such as congestive heart failure, chronic kidney disease (nephrotic syndrome), or liver cirrhosis, can mimic weight gain and increase abdominal girth. These are distinguished from obesity by the presence of pitting peripheral oedema, ascites, elevated jugular venous pressure, and abnormal renal, hepatic, or cardiac biomarkers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients struggling with weight management should seek a routine medical consultation to evaluate their eligibility for weight loss medicine, establish safe goals, and screen for metabolic complications. However, certain clinical situations and symptoms require urgent or emergency medical attention, particularly for patients undergoing pharmacological or surgical interventions for weight loss.

Signs and symptoms that require immediate clinical evaluation include acute, severe abdominal pain radiating to the back (indicative of acute pancreatitis, a rare but serious adverse effect of incretin therapies), high fever accompanied by right upper quadrant abdominal pain and nausea (suggesting acute cholecystitis or gallstones), persistent vomiting leading to the inability to tolerate fluids, or symptoms of severe clinical depression, sudden behavioral changes, and suicidal ideation (especially associated with centrally acting medications like naltrexone/bupropion). Furthermore, cardiovascular symptoms such as crushing chest pain, pressure, palpitations, or sudden shortness of breath demand immediate emergency assessment.

For any life-threatening symptoms or emergencies, contact emergency services immediately:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Local crisis support}: Contact local emergency services
\item \textbf{United States and Canada}: Call 911
\item \textbf{United Kingdom}: Call 999
\item \textbf{European Union}: Call 112
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of obesity and the evaluation of candidates for weight loss medicine involve a comprehensive workup. The process begins with a detailed medical history to assess weight history, prior weight loss efforts, eating patterns (e.g., binge eating, night eating), physical activity levels, and a thorough review of concurrent medications. The physical examination includes accurate measurement of height, weight, calculation of BMI, and measurement of waist circumference to assess visceral adiposity. Cardiovascular parameters, including resting blood pressure and heart rate, must be recorded.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Anthropometric Assessment}: Calculation of Body Mass Index (BMI = weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared) to classify obesity (Class I: 30.0--34.9; Class II: 35.0--39.9; Class III: $\ge$ 40 kg/m$^2$). Waist circumference is measured at the midpoint between the lower rib margin and the iliac crest, with values $>102$ cm in men and $>88$ cm in women indicating increased cardiometabolic risk.
\item \textbf{Laboratory Investigations}: A comprehensive fasting metabolic panel is performed to screen for complications. This includes a fasting lipid profile (total cholesterol, HDL, LDL, triglycerides), glycemic markers (fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c), renal function tests (serum creatinine, eGFR), liver function tests (alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase) to screen for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, and a thyroid panel (TSH) to rule out hypothyroidism.
\item \textbf{Cardiovascular Evaluation}: Routine measurement of blood pressure and a baseline electrocardiogram (ECG), which is especially important before initiating sympathomimetic weight loss medications (such as phentermine) that can cause tachycardia or arrhythmias.
\item \textbf{Specialized Screening and Imaging}: Overnight polysomnography (sleep study) for patients with suspected obstructive sleep apnoea. An abdominal ultrasound may be ordered to evaluate for gallbladder disease or quantify hepatic steatosis in patients with abnormal liver enzymes. If Cushing's syndrome is clinically suspected, screening tests such as a 24-hour urinary free cortisol or a low-dose dexamethasone suppression test are indicated.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of obesity requires a structured, multi-disciplinary approach. It is built on a foundation of intensive lifestyle modifications, which can be augmented by weight loss pharmacotherapy or metabolic surgery depending on the severity of the disease and the presence of comorbidities.

Pharmacotherapy is indicated when lifestyle modifications alone fail to achieve therapeutic targets. Modern pharmacotherapies target appetite regulation pathways in the brain and gut. Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, such as once-weekly subcutaneous semaglutide (2.4 mg) and once-daily liraglutide (3.0 mg), mimic the endogenous GLP-1 hormone. They stimulate insulin secretion, inhibit glucagon release, delay gastric emptying, and act on hypothalamic POMC/CART neurons to reduce appetite. Clinical trials (the STEP trials) demonstrated that semaglutide 2.4 mg achieves an average weight loss of 15\% to 18\%. Tirzepatide, a dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and GLP-1 receptor co-agonist, represents a newer class. The GIP component synergistically enhances the appetite-suppressing effects of GLP-1 while improving tolerability. The SURMOUNT trials showed that tirzepatide can achieve weight loss of up to 22.5\% (approximately 24 kg) at the highest dose (15 mg). Centrally acting agents include phentermine/topiramate (sympathomimetic and GABA modulator) and naltrexone/bupropion (opioid antagonist and antidepressant targeting the reward system). Orlistat, a gastric and pancreatic lipase inhibitor, acts locally in the gut to prevent the absorption of approximately 30\% of dietary fats. Bariatric and metabolic surgery (sleeve gastrectomy or Roux-en-Y gastric bypass) remains the most effective long-term intervention for severe obesity.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Dietary and Nutritional Restructuring}: Implementation of an individualized, nutritionally balanced, hypocaloric diet targeting a deficit of approximately 500--750~kcal/day below maintenance requirements, prioritizing whole foods, lean proteins (1.2--1.5~g/kg of ideal body weight), high-fiber carbohydrates, and adequate hydration.
\item \textbf{Physical Activity and Exercise Programs}: A minimum of 150--300~minutes/week of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise (e.g., brisk walking, cycling) combined with progressive resistance training 2 to 3 days per week to preserve skeletal muscle mass and support elevated energy expenditure.
\item \textbf{Glucagon-Like Peptide-1 (GLP-1) Receptor Agonists}: Prescribing of once-weekly subcutaneous semaglutide (escalated to 2.4 mg) or once-daily liraglutide (escalated to 3.0 mg) to induce satiety, delay gastric transit, and modify food preferences via hypothalamic GLP-1 receptor activation.
\item \textbf{Dual GIP and GLP-1 Receptor Co-Agonists}: Weekly subcutaneous administration of tirzepatide (escalated to 10 mg or 15 mg), which leverages GIP receptor activation to enhance the tolerability and metabolic efficacy of GLP-1 receptor agonism, facilitating profound weight reduction and glycemic control.
\item \textbf{Centrally Acting Combination Therapies}: Utilization of combination phentermine/topiramate extended-release capsules to suppress appetite and enhance satiety, or naltrexone/bupropion sustained-release tablets to target the mesolimbic reward system and reduce hedonic cravings.
\item \textbf{Gastrointestinal Lipase Inhibitors}: Administration of orlistat (120 mg three times daily with meals) to inhibit pancreatic and gastric lipases, preventing the digestion and absorption of approximately 30\% of dietary fats, which are excreted in the stool.
\item \textbf{Bariatric and Metabolic Surgery}: Consideration of laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy or Roux-en-Y gastric bypass for eligible patients with a BMI $\ge 40$~kg/m$^2$, or $\ge 35$~kg/m$^2$ with weight-related comorbidities, providing durable weight reduction and resolution of type 2 diabetes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The complications associated with obesity and its treatments are extensive and require careful management. Untreated obesity is a major risk factor for several chronic diseases, including cardiovascular disease (coronary artery disease, stroke, heart failure), type 2 diabetes mellitus, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH, which can progress to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma), obstructive sleep apnoea, chronic kidney disease, osteoarthritis, and at least 13 types of cancer (such as breast, colorectal, and endometrial cancers). Weight loss interventions also carry specific risks and side effects that must be balanced against the benefits of weight reduction.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Gastrointestinal Adverse Effects}: Incretin-based therapies (GLP-1 and GIP/GLP-1 agonists) frequently cause transient gastrointestinal side effects, including nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, constipation, and dyspepsia. More serious but rare complications include acute pancreatitis, gallbladder disease (cholelithiasis, cholecystitis) due to rapid weight loss, and gastroparesis (stomach paralysis).
\item \textbf{Cardiovascular and Neurological Stimulatory Effects}: Sympathomimetic agents (such as phentermine) can cause palpitations, tachycardia, and elevations in blood pressure. Topiramate can cause paresthesias (tingling in the fingers and toes), cognitive impairment (``brain fog''), and insomnia, and is associated with a risk of congenital malformations (cleft lip/palate) if taken during pregnancy.
\item \textbf{Neuropsychiatric Risks}: Centrally acting therapies, particularly the bupropion/naltrexone combination, carry warnings for neuropsychiatric adverse events. These include anxiety, sleep disturbances, irritability, and, rarely, suicidal ideation, necessitating close clinical monitoring, especially during treatment initiation or dose escalation.
\item \textbf{Malabsorptive and Nutritional Complications}: Orlistat frequently causes gastrointestinal side effects due to fat malabsorption, such as oily stools, fecal urgency, flatulence with discharge, and fecal incontinence. It can also impair the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), requiring daily supplementation. Bariatric surgery carries a high risk of chronic micronutrient deficiencies (vitamin B12, iron, folate, calcium, vitamin D) and protein-calorie malnutrition.
\item \textbf{Systemic and Surgical Complications}: Surgical interventions carry immediate risks such as anastomotic leaks, bleeding, deep vein thrombosis, and infection, as well as long-term risks like dumping syndrome, strictures, and marginal ulcers. For pharmacotherapy, a key complication is the weight rebound effect: upon discontinuation of weight loss medicine, most patients regain a significant portion of their lost weight, highlighting the chronic nature of the disease.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of individuals undergoing treatment with weight loss medicine has improved dramatically with the introduction of second-generation anti-obesity medications. Historically, achieving a 5\% to 10\% reduction in body weight was considered the clinical benchmark, as this level of weight loss is sufficient to improve glycemic control, lower blood pressure, improve lipid profiles, and reduce markers of systemic inflammation. With modern GLP-1 and GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists, patients frequently achieve weight loss of 15\% to 20\% or more, which approaches the efficacy of metabolic surgery.

The resolution of obesity-associated comorbidities is a key prognostic outcome. Studies have demonstrated that weight loss of 10\% to 15\% can lead to the remission of type 2 diabetes, especially in patients with a short duration of disease. Furthermore, weight loss significantly improves symptoms and severity of obstructive sleep apnoea, reduces liver fat content, and relieves joint pain, thereby improving physical function and health-related quality of life. In terms of hard cardiovascular outcomes, the SELECT (Semaglutide Effects on Cardiovascular Outcomes in People with Overweight or Obesity) clinical trial demonstrated a 20\% reduction in the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including cardiovascular death, non-fatal myocardial infarction, and non-fatal stroke, in patients with established cardiovascular disease and overweight or obesity who received once-weekly semaglutide 2.4 mg over a mean follow-up of 40 months.

However, the long-term prognosis depends heavily on treatment adherence and the continuation of therapy. Because obesity is a chronic disease governed by powerful neuroendocrine feedback loops that defend a set-point body weight, discontinuation of anti-obesity medications typically results in a slow but steady regain of weight. In clinical trials, patients who discontinued semaglutide or tirzepatide regained approximately two-thirds of their lost weight within one year of stopping the medication, along with a reversal of the improvements in metabolic biomarkers. Therefore, long-term or indefinite pharmacological treatment, combined with continuous lifestyle support, is necessary for most patients to maintain their weight loss and metabolic benefits.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
The prevention of obesity and the self-care required during weight loss treatment are critical components of long-term success. While public health initiatives focus on modifying the obesogenic environment through policies like sugar taxes, labeling regulations, and active transport infrastructure, individual self-care is essential for patients undergoing medical weight management. Self-care practices help maximize weight loss, preserve lean muscle tissue, prevent nutritional deficiencies, and ensure the safety of pharmacological or surgical interventions.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Nutritional Self-Monitoring and Dietary Quality}: Patients should engage in structured self-monitoring, such as keeping a food journal or using digital applications to track calorie and nutrient intake. Prioritizing dietary protein (e.g., 1.2--1.5~g/kg of ideal body weight daily) is critical to prevent sarcopenia (loss of skeletal muscle mass) during active weight loss. Consuming high-fiber foods and maintaining adequate hydration are essential to manage common side effects like constipation.
\item \textbf{Structured Exercise Integration}: A combination of aerobic and resistance exercise is necessary. While aerobic exercise (e.g., swimming, cycling, walking) supports energy expenditure and cardiovascular fitness, resistance training (e.g., weight lifting, resistance bands) is vital to preserve lean muscle mass and maintain the basal metabolic rate, which naturally slows during weight loss.
\item \textbf{Sleep Hygiene and Stress Management}: Chronic stress and inadequate sleep (less than 7 hours per night) raise cortisol levels, increase ghrelin (hunger), and decrease leptin (satiety), which can undermine the efficacy of weight loss medications. Practicing sleep hygiene and stress-reduction techniques (such as mindfulness, meditation, or cognitive behavioral therapy) helps stabilize appetite-regulating hormones.
\item \textbf{Routine Self-Weighing and Anthropometric Tracking}: Regular self-weighing (e.g., once or twice weekly under standardized conditions, such as in the morning while fasting) is a proven strategy for weight maintenance. It helps patients detect early weight regain and adjust their behavioral strategies before significant weight is regained.
\item \textbf{Behavioral Stimulus Control and Support Systems}: Modifying the home and work environment to reduce exposure to hyper-palatable food cues (e.g., not storing high-calorie snacks at home) is an effective behavioral strategy. Additionally, engaging with support systems, such as family, peer support groups, or professional multidisciplinary teams (dietitians, psychologists), provides accountability and emotional support.
\end{itemize}
