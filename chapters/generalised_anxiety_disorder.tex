\chapter{Generalised Anxiety Disorder}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD) is a common mental health condition characterised by persistent, excessive, and difficult-to-control worry about everyday matters such as work, health, family, and finances. Unlike the occasional worry that everyone experiences, the anxiety in GAD is disproportionate to the situation, occurs on more days than not, and lasts for at least six months. It is frequently accompanied by physical symptoms including muscle tension, restlessness, fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, and disturbed sleep. GAD is one of several anxiety disorders and often coexists with depression and other mental health conditions. With appropriate treatment, most people with GAD improve substantially, and it is a highly treatable condition.

\section*{Epidemiology}
GAD is one of the most common anxiety disorders worldwide. In Australia, around 3--4\% of the population experiences GAD in any twelve-month period, and the lifetime prevalence is estimated at approximately 5--6\%. It is about twice as common in women as in men. GAD can begin at any age but most often develops in early adulthood, with the peak age of onset in the 20s to 30s. Many people with GAD have symptoms for years before seeking help, and the condition tends to run a chronic, fluctuating course if untreated. It is associated with significant disability, reduced work productivity, and increased use of health services.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The exact cause of GAD is not fully understood, but it is thought to arise from a combination of biological, psychological, and environmental factors.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} anxiety disorders run in families, and twin studies suggest a moderate heritability
    \item \textbf{Brain chemistry and function:} altered activity of neurotransmitters such as serotonin, noradrenaline, and GABA, and overactivity of brain circuits involved in threat detection and emotional regulation
    \item \textbf{Personality traits:} people who are highly sensitive to uncertainty or who have a tendency toward negative thinking are more vulnerable
    \item \textbf{Life experiences:} chronic stress, childhood adversity, trauma, and major life events can trigger or worsen symptoms
    \item \textbf{Health conditions:} chronic illness, thyroid disorders, and substance use can contribute to or mimic anxiety
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The hallmark of GAD is worry that is excessive, persistent, and out of proportion to the actual situation, accompanied by a range of physical and psychological symptoms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Excessive worry about a range of everyday topics, present on most days for at least six months
    \item Difficulty controlling the worry, with a feeling of being on edge or keyed up
    \item Restlessness, feeling wound up, or an inability to relax
    \item Easy fatiguability and poor stamina
    \item Difficulty concentrating, or the mind going blank
    \item Irritability and short temper
    \item Muscle tension, aches, and pains
    \item Sleep disturbance, including difficulty falling or staying asleep and unsatisfying sleep
    \item Physical symptoms such as palpitations, sweating, trembling, dry mouth, and gastrointestinal discomfort
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Many conditions produce symptoms that overlap with GAD, and distinguishing between them is important for appropriate treatment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Other anxiety disorders:} panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, specific phobias, and health anxiety
    \item \textbf{Depression:} low mood and loss of interest may accompany or underlie anxiety
    \item \textbf{Bipolar disorder:} during mixed or manic states, anxiety can be prominent
    \item \textbf{Post-traumatic stress disorder:} follows a specific traumatic event and features intrusive memories
    \item \textbf{Substance use or withdrawal:} alcohol, caffeine, stimulants, and some medications can cause anxiety
    \item \textbf{Medical conditions:} hyperthyroidism, cardiac arrhythmias, hypoglycaemia, and chronic pain
    \item \textbf{Medication side effects:} some asthma, steroid, and thyroid preparations
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone whose worry or anxiety is persistent, distressing, or interfering with work, study, relationships, or daily life should speak with their GP. Early treatment improves outcomes. It is also important to seek help if anxiety is accompanied by symptoms of depression, or if you are using alcohol or other drugs to cope.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Thoughts of harming yourself or ending your life, or plans to do so
    \item Severe panic that is accompanied by chest pain, breathlessness, or feeling you cannot cope
    \item Any emergency mental health crisis where you feel unsafe
\end{itemize}
If you need immediate support but are not in an emergency, Lifeline (13 11 14) and Beyond Blue (1300 22 4636) are available around the clock in Australia.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
GAD is diagnosed clinically, based on a detailed history. There is no laboratory test for the condition.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical interview:} exploring the nature, duration, and impact of worries, and screening for other mental health conditions
    \item \textbf{Structured questionnaires:} tools such as the GAD-7 help assess severity and monitor response to treatment
    \item \textbf{Physical examination and blood tests:} thyroid function and other tests may be arranged to exclude medical causes of anxiety
    \item \textbf{Review of medications and substance use:} to identify contributing factors
    \item \textbf{Functional assessment:} evaluating the effect of symptoms on work, study, and relationships
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
GAD is best managed with a combination of psychological therapy, lifestyle measures, and, where appropriate, medication. Treatment is often stepped, starting with the least intensive option that is likely to help.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychological therapy:} cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is the first-line treatment and helps people identify and challenge unhelpful worry patterns; other effective approaches include mindfulness-based therapy and applied relaxation
    \item \textbf{Online and guided self-help:} structured programs based on CBT are effective for many people and may be accessed through a GP mental health care plan
    \item \textbf{Antidepressant medication:} selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) such as sertraline and escitalopram are commonly used and reduce anxiety over several weeks
    \item \textbf{Other medications:} pregabalin and certain tricyclic antidepressants are options when SSRIs are ineffective or not tolerated
    \item \textbf{Benzodiazepines:} used only for short-term relief of severe symptoms because of the risk of dependence
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} regular exercise, adequate sleep, reduced caffeine and alcohol, and stress-management techniques
    \item \textbf{Peer support and education:} understanding the condition and connecting with others who have similar experiences
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Depression, which coexists with GAD in up to half of those affected
    \item Other anxiety disorders, such as panic disorder and social anxiety
    \item Substance misuse, including alcohol and sedatives used to cope with anxiety
    \item Chronic insomnia and fatigue
    \item Impaired work performance, financial strain, and relationship difficulties
    \item Increased risk of physical health problems, including cardiovascular disease, over the long term
    \item Reduced quality of life and social withdrawal
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
GAD is a chronic condition for many people, but it is highly treatable. With appropriate therapy and, when needed, medication, the majority of people experience significant improvement in symptoms and quality of life. CBT produces lasting benefits by teaching skills that continue to help after treatment ends. Relapses can occur, particularly during stressful periods, but they respond well to early intervention. Long-term outcomes are best when treatment is commenced early, adherence is good, and lifestyle factors such as sleep and exercise are addressed. Many people with GAD go on to lead full and productive lives.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Learn about anxiety and recognise early warning signs so you can act early
    \item Practise regular physical activity, which reduces anxiety and improves mood
    \item Maintain a regular sleep routine and practise good sleep hygiene
    \item Limit caffeine and alcohol, which can trigger or amplify anxiety
    \item Build a daily routine with time for relaxation and enjoyable activities
    \item Use stress-management techniques such as slow breathing, mindfulness, and progressive muscle relaxation
    \item Stay connected with supportive family and friends
    \item See your GP early if worry is becoming hard to control, and discuss a mental health care plan
    \item If you have been prescribed medication, take it as directed and attend follow-up appointments
\end{itemize}
