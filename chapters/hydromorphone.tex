\chapter{Hydromorphone}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hydromorphone is a strong opioid pain-relieving medicine, similar to morphine but more potent, used to treat moderate to severe pain. It is available in Australia as Dilaudid, usually in tablet or liquid form, and by injection in hospital settings. Hydromorphone is used for severe acute pain, cancer pain, and other severe chronic pain under specialist supervision. Like all opioids, it can cause dependence, and overdose can be fatal. It is a controlled medicine, prescribed with caution, and treatment is reviewed regularly.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hydromorphone is one of several strong opioids prescribed in Australia, and its use has increased as an alternative to morphine and oxycodone. Opioid medicines are responsible for a significant proportion of medication-related harms in Australia, including dependence, overdose, and death, and strong opioids are now prescribed more cautiously with tighter regulation. Hydromorphone is more commonly used for cancer pain and in palliative care, where its benefits are well established.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} hydromorphone binds to opioid receptors in the brain and spinal cord, reducing the perception of pain
    \item \textbf{Potency:} approximately 5 times more potent than morphine by weight
    \item \textbf{Effects:} pain relief, sedation, and euphoria
    \item \textbf{Tolerance:} repeated use requires higher doses for the same effect
    \item \textbf{Dependence:} the brain adapts, and stopping causes withdrawal
    \item \textbf{Overdose:} respiratory depression, which can be fatal
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} opioid receptors modulate pain pathways; chronic use alters brain function
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Prescribed for severe pain, including cancer pain and pain after major surgery
    \item Immediate-release forms act quickly and last a few hours
    \item Controlled-release forms provide longer-lasting pain relief
    \item Side effects: constipation, nausea, drowsiness, and dizziness
    \item Itching and sweating in some people
    \item Signs of overdose: severe drowsiness, slow breathing, and pinpoint pupils
    \item Withdrawal on stopping: anxiety, sweating, cramps, and agitation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Other strong opioids, including morphine, oxycodone, and fentanyl, are alternatives
    \item The choice of opioid depends on the type of pain, kidney and liver function, and response
    \item Non-opioid options are preferred where possible
    \item Side effects may be confused with worsening illness
    \item Prescribers weigh benefits against the risks of dependence and overdose
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone prescribed hydromorphone should take it exactly as directed and report uncontrolled pain or troublesome side effects. Medicine reviews ensure the lowest effective dose is used.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe drowsiness, confusion, or difficulty waking
    \item Slow or shallow breathing
    \item Pinpoint pupils with collapse
    \item Signs of overdose, especially if more than prescribed was taken
    \item Allergic reaction: swelling, rash, or difficulty breathing
    \item Severe constipation or vomiting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pain assessment:} the type, severity, and cause of pain
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} history of substance use, breathing problems, and kidney or liver disease
    \item \textbf{Regular review:} effectiveness, side effects, and need for continuation
    \item \textbf{Opioid treatment plan:} agreed goals, doses, and review dates
    \item \textbf{Renal and liver tests:} dose adjustment in organ impairment
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} for misuse, dependence, and diversion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dosing:} the lowest effective dose, adjusted for kidney function
    \item \textbf{Regular vs as needed:} prescribed on a schedule or as required depending on the pain
    \item \textbf{Constipation prevention:} laxatives are usually prescribed alongside
    \item \textbf{Antiemetics:} for nausea in the early days
    \item \textbf{Tapering:} gradual dose reduction when stopping to avoid withdrawal
    \item \textbf{Alternatives:} non-opioid medicines, nerve blocks, and other strategies
    \item \textbf{Opioid dependence treatment:} specialist support and medicines where needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Constipation, nausea, and drowsiness
    \item Respiratory depression and overdose, which can be fatal
    \item Dependence and withdrawal
    \item Tolerance, requiring dose increases
    \item Impaired driving and accident risk
    \item Misuse and diversion of the medicine
    \item Falls, especially in older adults
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Hydromorphone provides effective pain relief when used appropriately, particularly in cancer and palliative care, where it can greatly improve quality of life. Its use for chronic non-cancer pain is limited by the risks of dependence and overdose, and outcomes are best with careful prescribing, regular review, and tapering when no longer needed. With responsible use, the benefits of hydromorphone outweigh the risks for most people who genuinely need a strong opioid.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take hydromorphone exactly as prescribed
    \item Do not increase doses or take extra doses
    \item Use laxatives and fluids to prevent constipation
    \item Do not drink alcohol while taking it
    \item Do not drive after starting or increasing a dose
    \item Store the medicine securely and dispose of leftovers at a pharmacy
    \item Never share opioids with others
    \item Report drowsiness, breathing problems, or difficulty stopping
\end{itemize}
