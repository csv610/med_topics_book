\chapter{Child Immunisation}
\index{Child Immunisation}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Child immunisation is the process of giving vaccines to infants, children, and adolescents to protect them against serious infectious diseases. Vaccines work by exposing the immune system to a weakened, inactivated, or partial form of a germ, so that the body produces protective antibodies and immune memory without the child developing the disease. If the child later encounters the real infection, the immune system responds quickly and usually prevents the disease or greatly reduces its severity. In many countries, childhood vaccines are delivered through the National Immunisation Program schedule, beginning soon after birth and continuing through the teenage years. Immunisation protects not only the individual child but also the wider community through herd immunity.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Vaccination is one of the most effective public health measures in history, and high childhood immunisation rates have dramatically reduced death and disability from diseases such as polio, measles, whooping cough, and meningitis. In many countries, roughly nine in ten children are fully immunised at each scheduled age, although coverage can be lower in some regional, remote, and disadvantaged communities. Outbreaks of measles and whooping cough still occur when immunisation rates fall, particularly in areas where vaccine hesitancy is common. Measles in particular is highly contagious, so high levels of community protection are needed to prevent spread to those who cannot be vaccinated for medical reasons.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\medskip
\textbf{How vaccines work.} Vaccines simulate an infection without causing the disease, teaching the immune system to recognise a germ:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Live attenuated vaccines:} contain a weakened form of the virus or bacterium that multiplies briefly but rarely causes illness (for example, MMR and chickenpox vaccine)
    \item \textbf{Inactivated vaccines:} contain killed germs or parts of germs and cannot cause infection (for example, whooping cough and hepatitis A vaccines)
    \item \textbf{Subunit and toxoid vaccines:} contain specific pieces of the germ or an inactivated toxin (for example, tetanus and diphtheria vaccines)
    \item \textbf{mRNA and viral-vector vaccines:} instruct cells to make a harmless piece of the germ, triggering immunity without using the live germ
    \item \textbf{Combined vaccines:} protect against several diseases in one injection, reducing the number of needles a child needs
\end{itemize}

\medskip
\textbf{Immune response.} The immune response takes around two weeks to build after a dose, which is why some vaccines require multiple doses or boosters to achieve long-lasting protection. Breastfeeding, minor illness, and the use of simple fever medicines do not reduce the effectiveness of routine childhood vaccines.

\section*{Clinical Features}
A child who is unwell after a vaccine usually develops only mild, short-lived symptoms while the immune system responds:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Soreness, redness, or swelling at the injection site
    \item Low-grade fever lasting a day or two
    \item Mild irritability, drowsiness, or poor appetite
    \item Headache or muscle aches in older children
    \item A mild rash or swollen glands after some live vaccines, such as the MMR vaccine
\end{itemize}

\medskip
More serious reactions, such as a severe allergic reaction or high fever with a convulsion, are rare. They usually occur within minutes to hours after vaccination, which is why a short period of observation may be recommended after some vaccines.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A fever or rash after a vaccine can be mistaken for an intercurrent viral illness, since many children pick up everyday infections around the time they are vaccinated
    \item A swollen limb or injection-site reaction can occasionally look like cellulitis or an abscess
    \item In a genuinely unwell child, vaccine reactions should not mask the diagnosis of conditions such as influenza, meningitis, or measles
    \item Persistent or unusual symptoms should be assessed for causes other than vaccination
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most vaccine reactions are mild and settle within a day or two. See a doctor if a child has a high fever lasting more than a couple of days, a severe or spreading rash, marked swelling, or symptoms that are getting worse rather than improving.

\medskip
\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, wheezing, or hoarseness after vaccination
    \item Swelling of the face, lips, or tongue
    \item Collapse, floppiness, or a convulsion
    \item Any sudden severe reaction within minutes to hours of a vaccine
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immunisation history check:} review of the child's record to identify which vaccines are due or have been missed
    \item \textbf{Assessment of reactions:} careful description of symptoms and their timing relative to vaccination, together with examination
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} antibody levels may be checked in special situations, such as suspected poor response or exposure to a vaccine-preventable disease
    \item \textbf{Notifiable disease reporting:} suspected cases of vaccine-preventable infections are reported to health authorities so that control measures can be taken
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Catching up on missed vaccines:} a doctor or nurse can plan a schedule to bring an overdue child up to date safely
    \item \textbf{Symptom relief:} paracetamol or ibuprofen at a weight-appropriate dose for fever or injection-site discomfort, and cool compresses for swelling
    \item \textbf{Documented medical exemptions:} children with genuine medical contraindications can be formally exempted from particular vaccines
    \item \textbf{Management of severe reactions:} anaphylaxis is treated as an emergency with adrenaline and urgent medical care
    \item \textbf{Advice and reassurance:} GPs, nurses, and immunisation clinics can answer concerns about vaccine safety and side effects
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Most children experience no significant complications from vaccination
    \item Rare severe allergic reactions (anaphylaxis) occur in roughly one in a million doses
    \item High fever with a febrile convulsion is uncommon and usually has no long-term consequences
    \item The greater risk is not vaccinating: unimmunised children can contract measles, whooping cough, or meningitis, which may cause pneumonia, brain damage, or death
    \item In rare cases, live vaccines can cause disease in children with severely weakened immune systems, which is why these children need specialist advice
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Children who are immunised on schedule are protected against a wide range of serious diseases, and that protection usually lasts for years or decades. Most vaccine reactions are brief and fully resolve. Very rare side effects are monitored by ongoing vaccine safety systems. When immunisation rates are high across a community, diseases can be controlled or even eliminated, as has happened with polio in most of the world. Some infections, such as whooping cough, still circulate, so keeping boosters up to date throughout childhood and adolescence remains important.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Follow the National Immunisation Program schedule from birth through adolescence
    \item Keep an up-to-date record of your child's vaccinations and the reason for any missed doses
    \item If a vaccine is missed, book a catch-up appointment as soon as possible
    \item Tell the nurse or doctor about any previous vaccine reactions or allergies
    \item Give paracetamol or ibuprofen for mild fever or soreness only if needed
    \item Ask trusted sources, such as your GP or immunisation clinic, about any concerns rather than relying on social media
    \item Encourage good hygiene and stay home when sick, since vaccines do not cover every infection
\end{itemize}
