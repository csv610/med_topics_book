\chapter{Leukaemia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Leukaemia is a group of malignant haematological neoplasms characterised by the rapid, unregulated clonal proliferation of abnormal white blood cells (leukaemic blasts or mature-looking dysfunctional leucocytes) within the bone marrow and lymphoid tissues. This uncontrolled growth impairs the normal haematopoietic function of the bone marrow, leading to the suppression of healthy red blood cells, platelets, and mature white blood cells. Leukaemias are broadly categorised based on their clinical progression (acute versus chronic) and the predominant cell lineage involved (myeloid versus lymphoid), yielding four primary subtypes: acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL), chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML), and chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL). Understanding these distinctions is critical, as they dictate clinical presentation, disease progression, therapeutic strategies, and prognosis.

\section*{Epidemiology}
In Australia and globally, leukaemia represents a significant public health burden, accounting for approximately 3\% to 4\% of all new cancer diagnoses. In Australia, it is estimated that over 4,500 individuals are diagnosed with leukaemia annually. The incidence and distribution vary markedly by age and subtype. Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia is the most common paediatric malignancy, peaking between two and five years of age. Conversely, chronic lymphocytic leukaemia and acute myeloid leukaemia are predominantly diseases of older adults, with the median age at diagnosis exceeding 65 years. Chronic myeloid leukaemia occurs across all age groups but is most frequently diagnosed in middle-aged and older individuals. Males are generally affected at slightly higher rates than females across all subtypes. Global variation exists, though incidence is highest in developed nations, likely reflecting diagnostic access and environmental factors.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Leukaemia arises from the accumulation of genetic abnormalities in a single haematopoietic progenitor cell, which drives clonal expansion and disrupts normal differentiation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chromosomal translocations:} Reciprocal translocations frequently generate oncogenic fusion proteins, such as the BCR-ABL1 fusion gene resulting from the t(9;22) translocation (the Philadelphia chromosome) in CML, or the PML-RARA fusion in acute promyelocytic leukaemia (APL).
    \item \textbf{Somatic genetic mutations:} Key mutations alter signal transduction pathways, transcription factors, or epigenetic regulators, such as FLT3, NPM1, or DNMT3A mutations in AML, and JAK2 mutations in myeloid neoplasms.
    \item \textbf{Ionising radiation:} Exposure to high-dose radiation is a well-established risk factor for developing AML, CML, and ALL.
    \item \textbf{Chemical exposure:} Workplace or environmental exposure to benzene and other organic solvents increases the risk of acute leukaemias.
    \item \textbf{Prior cytotoxic therapy:} Treatment with alkylating agents or topoisomerase II inhibitors for other malignancies can lead to therapy-related leukaemia (t-AML or t-ALL).
    \item \textbf{Genetic syndromes:} Pre-existing conditions such as Down syndrome (trisomy 21), Fanconi anaemia, and ataxia-telangiectasia are associated with a significantly elevated risk of developing acute leukaemia.
    \item \textbf{Viral infections:} Human T-lymphotropic virus 1 (HTLV-1) is directly implicated in adult T-cell leukaemia/lymphoma, while Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) is linked to certain subtypes of ALL.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of leukaemia reflects the cytopenias resulting from bone marrow failure, as well as tissue infiltration by neoplastic cells.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anaemia-related symptoms:} Progressive fatigue, generalised weakness, dyspnea on exertion, dizziness, and pallor due to suppressed erythropoiesis.
    \item \textbf{Thrombocytopenia-related signs:} Easy bruising (ecchymoses), petechiae (particularly on the lower extremities), epistaxis, gingival bleeding, and prolonged bleeding from minor cuts.
    \item \textbf{Leucopenia or dysfunctional leucocytes:} Recurrent, severe, or opportunistic infections, prolonged recovery from minor illnesses, and persistent fever.
    \item \textbf{Systemic B-symptoms:} Night sweats, unexplained weight loss, and low-grade fevers, which are more common in chronic leukaemias and lymphoma-like presentations.
    \item \textbf{Infiltrative features:} Hepatomegaly, splenomegaly (causing early satiety or left upper quadrant abdominal discomfort), lymphadenopathy (painless, rubbery nodes in the neck, axilla, or groin), and gingival hypertrophy (particularly in monocytic AML).
    \item \textbf{Bone and joint pain:} Deep, aching pain resulting from the expansion of the marrow cavity by leukaemic cells, frequently observed in paediatric ALL.
    \item \textbf{Neurological symptoms:} Headache, nausea, vomiting, cranial nerve palsies, or visual changes indicating central nervous system (CNS) involvement, especially in ALL.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because leukaemia presents with non-specific haematological and systemic signs, several other conditions must be considered during diagnostic evaluation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infectious mononucleosis:} Caused by the Epstein-Barr virus, presenting with fever, pharyngitis, lymphadenopathy, and atypical lymphocytosis that can mimic ALL.
    \item \textbf{Aplastic anaemia:} Characterised by pancytopenia and a hypocellular bone marrow, but distinguished from leukaemia by the absence of clonal blast cells.
    \item \textbf{Myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS):} Clonal haematopoietic disorders characterised by cytopenias and dysplastic marrow changes; MDS may progress to AML but has distinct diagnostic criteria.
    \item \textbf{Leukaemoid reaction:} A marked leucocytosis (often exceeding $50 \times 10^9$/L) in response to severe infection or inflammation, resembling CML but without the Philadelphia chromosome or clonal markers.
    \item \textbf{Severe vitamin deficiencies:} Vitamin B12 or folate deficiency can cause profound cytopenias and macrocytosis, mimicking bone marrow failure syndromes.
    \item \textbf{Other metastatic malignancies:} Non-haematological cancers metastasising to the bone marrow (myelophthisis), leading to leucoerythroblastic blood pictures.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients experiencing unexplained, persistent fatigue, recurrent infections, easy bruising, or unexpected bleeding should seek a timely consultation with a general practitioner for evaluation, including a full blood count.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item High fever associated with suspected neutropenia (neutropenic sepsis).
    \item Uncontrolled haemorrhage, such as severe nosebleeds or gastrointestinal bleeding.
    \item Sudden, severe shortness of breath or chest pain.
    \item Signs of stroke or altered mental state, which can indicate hyperleucocytosis and leukostasis.
    \item Persistent priapism, a potential emergency complication of hyperleucocytosis in CML.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A definitive diagnosis of leukaemia requires a comprehensive assessment of peripheral blood and bone marrow specimens.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full blood count (FBC) and peripheral blood smear:} Demonstrates cytopenias (anaemia, thrombocytopenia) and leucocytosis or leucopenia. The blood smear allows direct visual assessment of leukaemic cells (blasts in acute leukaemia, mature dysfunctional lymphocytes in CLL, or granulocytic precursors in CML).
    \item \textbf{Bone marrow aspirate and trephine biopsy:} Essential for evaluating marrow cellularity, morphology, and obtaining samples for diagnostic typing.
    \item \textbf{Immunophenotyping (Flow cytometry):} Analyses cell surface and cytoplasmic markers (CD markers) to differentiate myeloid from lymphoid lineages and identify specific leukaemia subtypes.
    \item \textbf{Cytogenetic analysis (Karyotyping and FISH):} Identifies clonal chromosomal abnormalities such as t(9;22) in CML or t(15;17) in APL, which are crucial for classification and risk stratification.
    \item \textbf{Molecular testing (PCR and next-generation sequencing):} Detects specific gene mutations or fusion transcripts (e.g.\ FLT3, NPM1, BCR-ABL1) to guide targeted therapies and monitor minimal residual disease (MRD).
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture:} Performed to evaluate cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) for central nervous system involvement, particularly in ALL and AML.
    \item \textbf{Biochemical profile:} Assesses renal function, liver enzymes, electrolytes, and uric acid or lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels, which are markers of cell turnover.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Leukaemia management is highly complex and depends on the specific subtype, genetic markers, age, and overall fitness of the patient.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy:} The cornerstone of treatment for acute leukaemias. It typically involves induction therapy to achieve complete remission, followed by consolidation (intensification) and sometimes maintenance therapy.
    \item \textbf{Targeted molecular therapy:} Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) like imatinib, dasatinib, and nilotinib have transformed CML management by targeting the BCR-ABL1 protein. Other targeted agents include FLT3 inhibitors (e.g.\ midostaurin) and IDH1/IDH2 inhibitors in AML.
    \item \textbf{Monoclonal antibodies and immunotherapy:} Monoclonal antibodies (e.g.\ rituximab in CLL, blinatumomab or inotuzumab ozogamicin in ALL) and chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy are increasingly utilised, especially in relapsed or refractory lymphoid leukaemias.
    \item \textbf{Allogeneic stem cell transplantation (SCT):} Offers a potential cure for high-risk or relapsed acute leukaemia by replacing the diseased marrow with healthy donor stem cells and inducing a graft-versus-leukaemia effect.
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} Prophylactic and therapeutic antimicrobials for infection prevention, transfusion support with irradiated and filtered red blood cells and platelets, and the administration of granulocyte colony-stimulating factors (G-CSF).
    \item \textbf{Tumour lysis syndrome prophylaxis:} Aggressive intravenous hydration and uric acid-lowering agents (allopurinol or rasburicase) during the initiation of cytoreductive therapy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neutropenic sepsis:} A life-threatening systemic infection occurring in patients with severe neutropenia secondary to the disease or chemotherapy.
    \item \textbf{Severe haemorrhage:} Bleeding in the central nervous system, gastrointestinal tract, or lungs due to profound thrombocytopenia or disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), particularly in APL.
    \item \textbf{Leukostasis:} Sludging of microvascular circulation in the brain or lungs due to extremely high numbers of circulating blasts, constituting a medical emergency.
    \item \textbf{Tumour lysis syndrome (TLS):} Metabolic emergencies (hyperkalaemia, hyperuricaemia, hyperphosphatemia, and hypocalcaemia) caused by the rapid destruction of leukaemic cells.
    \item \textbf{Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD):} A major complication of allogeneic stem cell transplantation where donor immune cells attack host tissues.
    \item \textbf{Long-term treatment sequelae:} Secondary malignancies, cardiotoxicity (e.g.\ from anthracyclines), infertility, and cognitive changes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of leukaemia has improved dramatically over the past few decades due to advanced risk-stratification, targeted therapies, and improved supportive care. In acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, the paediatric cure rate now exceeds 90\%, whereas adult ALL carries a more modest 5-year survival rate of approximately 40\% to 50\%. Chronic myeloid leukaemia has a near-normal life expectancy for patients who respond well to tyrosine kinase inhibitors. Acute myeloid leukaemia prognosis varies widely based on molecular risk groups, with younger patients achieving long-term survival in 40\% to 50\% of cases, while prognosis remains poor in older adults. Chronic lymphocytic leukaemia is often characterised by an indolent course, with many patients not requiring immediate treatment and surviving for decades, though transformation to a high-grade lymphoma (Richter's transformation) can occur.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Avoidance of environmental toxins:} Minimise exposure to known carcinogens such as benzene and agricultural chemicals in occupational settings.
    \item \textbf{Radiation protection:} Adhere to occupational and medical safety standards regarding exposure to ionising radiation.
    \item \textbf{Infection prevention:} Maintain meticulous hand hygiene, avoid crowds during neutropenic phases, and adhere to recommended vaccination schedules (using non-live vaccines).
    \item \textbf{Dietary and hydration support:} Consume a clean, well-cooked diet during periods of immunosuppression (neutropenic diet) and maintain high oral fluid intake to support renal function.
    \item \textbf{Psychosocial engagement:} Access support networks, counselling services, and leukaemia patient organisations to manage the emotional and psychological impact of diagnosis.
\end{itemize}
