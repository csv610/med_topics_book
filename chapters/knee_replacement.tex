\chapter{Knee Replacement}
\index{Knee Replacement}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Knee replacement surgery, also called knee arthroplasty, is a procedure in which a damaged knee joint is removed and replaced with an artificial joint made of metal and plastic. It is one of the most successful and commonly performed operations worldwide and is used mainly for advanced osteoarthritis that has not responded to non-surgical treatment. The aim is to relieve pain, correct deformity, and restore function and mobility. This chapter explains when knee replacement is considered, what the operation involves, and what to expect during recovery.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Knee replacement is one of the most frequently performed elective operations in many countries, with tens of thousands of procedures carried out each year. The number of operations has risen steadily as the population ages, obesity rates have increased, and surgical outcomes have improved. Most people having a knee replacement are over 60, but the operation is increasingly performed in younger patients with severe arthritis. Osteoarthritis is the underlying reason for the great majority of procedures, with rheumatoid arthritis and post-traumatic arthritis accounting for most of the remainder.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Osteoarthritis:} the most common reason; progressive loss of cartilage leaves bone rubbing on bone, causing pain, stiffness, and deformity
    \item \textbf{Rheumatoid arthritis:} chronic inflammation destroys joint cartilage and supporting structures, sometimes warranting surgery
    \item \textbf{Post-traumatic arthritis:} arthritis that develops years after fractures, ligament injuries, or meniscal injuries
    \item \textbf{Osteonecrosis:} death of bone tissue, often around the knee, leading to joint collapse
    \item \textbf{Deformity:} bow-leg or knock-knee deformity alters loading and accelerates arthritis
    \item \textbf{The implant:} a total knee replacement resurfaces the end of the thigh bone (femur), the top of the shin bone (tibia), and usually the kneecap (patella) with metal and a plastic bearing surface
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Knee replacement is offered to people whose symptoms meet several criteria.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, persistent knee pain that limits daily activities such as walking, stairs, and shopping
    \item Pain that is worse at night or wakes the person from sleep
    \item Stiffness that limits bending or straightening the knee
    \item Significant functional limitation despite physiotherapy, weight management, and pain medication
    \item X-ray evidence of advanced arthritis with loss of joint space or deformity
    \item Deformity such as bowing or knock-knee that affects walking
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Other causes of knee pain:} meniscal tears, ligament injuries, bursitis, or tendinopathy, which do not respond to joint replacement
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory arthritis:} gout, pseudogout, or active rheumatoid arthritis may require different treatment first
    \item \textbf{Infection:} septic arthritis must be excluded before surgery
    \item \textbf{Referred pain:} pain from the hip or lumbar spine can mimic knee pain
    \item \textbf{Vascular or neurological causes:} peripheral vascular disease or neuropathy
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People considering knee replacement should see an orthopaedic surgeon for assessment if they have severe arthritis symptoms that limit their life despite conservative treatment. Surgery is planned electively, and the decision is made jointly between the patient and surgeon after weighing the benefits, risks, and alternatives.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of infection after surgery: a hot, red, very painful knee with fever and wound discharge
    \item Chest pain, sudden breathlessness, or calf pain and swelling after surgery, which may indicate a blood clot
    \item Sudden severe pain, deformity, or inability to move the knee after surgery, which may indicate dislocation or fracture
    \item Wound bleeding that is heavy or not controlled by pressure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the severity and duration of pain, functional limitations, response to prior treatments, and general health
    \item \textbf{Examination:} range of movement, alignment, stability, and neurovascular status of the leg
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} standing views show loss of joint space, bone spurs, and deformity, and are used to plan the surgery
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, kidney function, and markers of inflammation; MRSA screening may be performed before surgery
    \item \textbf{Cardiac and anaesthetic assessment:} ECG and other tests to ensure fitness for anaesthesia
    \item \textbf{Bone scans or MRI:} occasionally used when plain X-rays are inconclusive
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Non-surgical options first:} physiotherapy, weight loss, activity modification, analgesia, and intra-articular injections are tried before surgery is considered
    \item \textbf{Total knee replacement:} both surfaces of the joint are resurfaced; the most common procedure
    \item \textbf{Partial knee replacement:} only one compartment is replaced, suitable for a minority of patients with arthritis confined to that area
    \item \textbf{Patellofemoral replacement:} resurfacing only the kneecap joint in selected patients
    \item \textbf{Prehabilitation:} strengthening exercises and weight loss before surgery improve recovery
    \item \textbf{Hospital stay:} usually one to three days, with early mobilisation on the day of or day after surgery
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy begins immediately and continues for weeks to months, focusing on range of movement, strength, and walking
    \item \textbf{Revision surgery:} a second operation to replace worn or loose components, required eventually in a small proportion of patients
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection of the wound or the artificial joint, which is serious and may require further surgery
    \item Blood clots in the legs (deep vein thrombosis) or lungs (pulmonary embolism), prevented with medication, compression, and early mobilisation
    \item Stiffness, with difficulty regaining full range of movement
    \item Nerve or blood vessel damage, uncommon but potentially serious
    \item Loosening or wear of the implant over many years
    \item Persistent pain in a small proportion of patients
    \item Anaesthetic complications and bleeding requiring transfusion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Knee replacement is highly successful: the great majority of patients report substantial reduction in pain and improvement in function and quality of life. Most modern implants last 15 to 20 years or more, after which revision surgery may be needed, particularly in younger, more active patients. Full recovery takes several months, with ongoing strengthening for up to a year. Outcomes are best when patients are well prepared, maintain a healthy weight, and commit to rehabilitation.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Lose weight before surgery if overweight; this improves outcomes and reduces complications
    \item Strengthen the quadriceps and hamstrings in the weeks before surgery
    \item Stop smoking before surgery to reduce the risk of complications
    \item Follow the hospital's fasting and medication instructions before the operation
    \item Prepare the home: arrange for help, clear pathways, and set up a safe recovery space
    \item Attend all physiotherapy sessions and perform the prescribed exercises daily
    \item Protect the new joint: avoid high-impact activities and contact sport
\end{itemize}
