\chapter{COPD}
\index{COPD}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD) is a common, preventable, and treatable chronic lung disease characterized by progressive, non-fully reversible airflow limitation and persistent respiratory symptoms. The condition is primarily caused by significant exposure to noxious particles or gases, which induces chronic airway and alveolar inflammation. COPD encompasses two main clinical phenotypes that often coexist: chronic bronchitis, characterized by a chronic productive cough, and emphysema, marked by the destruction of alveoli and enlargement of air spaces. COPD represents a major global health burden, causing severe, long-term respiratory impairment, reduced quality of life, and significant healthcare utilization.

\section*{Epidemiology}
COPD is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, currently ranked as the third leading cause of death globally. In many countries, it is a major public health challenge, affecting approximately 1 in 13 people aged 45 and older. The prevalence of COPD increases progressively with age and is significantly higher in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas and Indigenous communities, reflecting disparities in smoking rates and occupational environmental exposures. The disease is underdiagnosed, with estimates suggesting that up to half of all individuals with clinically significant airflow limitation remain undiagnosed until the disease reaches an advanced stage.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The development of COPD involves a complex interaction between environmental exposures and host genetic factors that result in chronic pulmonary inflammation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tobacco smoke exposure:} Cigarette smoking is the primary aetiological factor, accounting for approximately 80\% to 90\% of cases in developed countries, including passive exposure to second-hand smoke.
    \item \textbf{Occupational dusts and chemicals:} Prolonged exposure to organic and inorganic dusts, chemical agents, fumes, and industrial air pollutants increases the risk of developing COPD independently of smoking status.
    \item \textbf{Biomass fuel emission:} Globally, exposure to indoor air pollution from burning wood, animal dung, and crop residues for cooking and heating in poorly ventilated dwellings is a dominant cause, particularly in developing nations.
    \item \textbf{Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (AATD):} An inherited genetic disorder where a lack of the alpha-1 antitrypsin protease inhibitor leads to unchecked neutrophil elastase activity, causing early-onset, severe panlobular emphysema.
    \item \textbf{Airway hyper-reactivity:} Pre-existing asthma or persistent airway hyper-responsiveness can act synergistically with environmental exposures to accelerate the decline of lung function.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory tissue destruction:} Pathophysiologically, chronic inhalation of irritants recruits neutrophils, macrophages, and CD8+ T-lymphocytes, triggering protease-antiprotease imbalance, oxidative stress, and inflammatory remodeling of the small airways, alongside alveolar wall destruction (emphysema).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of COPD develops insidiously and progresses slowly over several decades, with symptoms often becoming pronounced in middle-aged or older individuals.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Progressive dyspnoea:} The hallmark symptom of COPD, which is characteristically persistent, worse with exertion, and gradually worsens over time, limiting activities of daily living.
    \item \textbf{Chronic cough:} Often the first symptom to develop, frequently dismissed by patients as a "smoker's cough." It may be intermittent initially but eventually becomes daily and productive.
    \item \textbf{Sputum production:} Patients commonly expectorate small amounts of tenacious, mucoid sputum, typically worst in the mornings. An increase in sputum volume or purulence indicates an acute exacerbation.
    \item \textbf{Wheezing and chest tightness:} Intermittent wheezing and a feeling of thoracic constriction are common, particularly during physical exertion or exacerbations.
    \item \textbf{Physical examination signs:} In moderate to severe cases, examination may reveal a barrel chest (increased anteroposterior thoracic diameter), accessory muscle use, pursed-lip breathing, prolonged expiratory phase, hyper-resonance on percussion, and decreased breath sounds or coarse crackles on auscultation.
    \item \textbf{Systemic manifestations:} Severe COPD is associated with weight loss, muscle wasting (cachexia), fatigue, and osteoporosis, reflecting systemic inflammation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the clinical features of COPD overlap with other cardiopulmonary diseases, a rigorous differential diagnosis is essential.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asthma:} Characterised by early-onset, highly variable symptoms, diurnal variation, and significant, often complete, reversibility of airflow limitation on spirometry.
    \item \textbf{Congestive heart failure:} Presents with exertional dyspnoea, orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, and basal crackles, but is differentiated by cardiomegaly on chest X-ray and abnormal echocardiography.
    \item \textbf{Bronchiectasis:} Marked by profuse, purulent sputum production, recurrent chest infections, coarse crackles, and distinctive bronchial dilation on high-resolution chest CT.
    \item \textbf{Tuberculosis or lung cancer:} Chronic cough, haemoptysis, and weight loss require exclusion of malignancy or chronic infections via imaging and microbiological sputum testing.
    \item \textbf{Interstitial lung disease (ILD):} Presents with progressive exertional dyspnoea and dry cough, but features fine end-expiratory velcro-like crackles on examination and restrictive lung function patterns.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Individuals with a history of smoking or occupational exposures who develop a persistent cough, daily sputum production, or unexplained shortness of breath should seek a medical evaluation from a GP.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, acute shortness of breath making it impossible to speak in full sentences or walk across a room
    \item A sudden change in mental state, such as confusion, severe drowsiness, or agitation (suggesting severe hypercapnia or hypoxia)
    \item Cyanosis, seen as a bluish or grey colour around the lips, tongue, or fingernails
    \item Chest pain or pressure, which may indicate a co-existing myocardial infarction or pneumothorax
    \item Sudden, severe swelling in the ankles, feet, or legs accompanying acute respiratory distress
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A diagnosis of COPD must be suspected in any patient presenting with characteristic symptoms and risk exposures, and confirmed using objective lung function testing.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spirometry:} The gold standard diagnostic investigation. A post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 second to forced vital capacity ratio (FEV1/FVC) of less than 0.70 confirms the presence of persistent, non-reversible airflow limitation.
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} Performed to rule out alternative diagnoses such as lung cancer, heart failure, or tuberculosis. In advanced emphysema, it may show hyperinflation, flattened diaphragms, and increased retrosternal airspace.
    \item \textbf{High-resolution CT (HRCT) scan:} Used to assess the distribution of emphysema, check for co-existing bronchiectasis, or screen for lung cancer in high-risk patients.
    \item \textbf{Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis:} Indicated in patients with severe airflow limitation (FEV1 $<$ 50\% predicted) or signs of respiratory failure to assess for hypoxaemia and hypercapnia.
    \item \textbf{Full blood count:} Checked to detect secondary polycythaemia (raised haematocrit due to chronic hypoxia) or to assess blood eosinophil counts to guide corticosteroid therapy.
    \item \textbf{Alpha-1 antitrypsin levels:} Recommended for all patients diagnosed with COPD below the age of 45, or those with a strong family history of early-onset lung disease.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
While specific treatment protocols are detailed in dedicated resources, the primary goals of COPD management are symptom relief, prevention of exacerbations, and slowing disease progression.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Smoking cessation:} The single most effective intervention to slow the accelerated decline in FEV1, managed with nicotine replacement therapy (NRT), behavioural counselling, or oral medications.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological therapy:} Relies on inhaled bronchodilators, including short-acting (SABA, SAMA) and long-acting (LABA, LAMA) formulations, along with inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) for patients with frequent exacerbations or high eosinophil counts.
    \item \textbf{Pulmonary rehabilitation:} A structured, multidisciplinary program incorporating exercise training, education, and behavioural changes, proven to reduce dyspnoea and hospital admissions.
    \item \textbf{Immunisation:} Annually scheduled influenza vaccinations, pneumococcal vaccines, and COVID-19 immunisations are vital to prevent severe infectious exacerbations.
    \item \textbf{Long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT):} Indicated for patients with severe resting hypoxaemia to improve survival rates and reduce the risk of secondary pulmonary hypertension.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The progressive nature of COPD leads to several secondary complications affecting the respiratory and cardiovascular systems.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute exacerbations:} Sudden worsening of respiratory symptoms triggered by viral or bacterial infections, air pollution, or temperature changes, requiring prompt medical intervention or hospitalisation.
    \item \textbf{Respiratory failure:} Chronic hypoxaemia and hypercapnia can progress to acute-on-chronic respiratory failure, requiring non-invasive ventilation (NIV) or mechanical ventilation.
    \item \textbf{Cor pulmonale:} Chronic pulmonary vasoconstriction in response to hypoxia increases pulmonary vascular resistance, leading to pulmonary hypertension and subsequent right-sided heart failure.
    \item \textbf{Spontaneous pneumothorax:} Rupture of subpleural emphysematous bullae can cause air to leak into the pleural space, causing sudden lung collapse.
    \item \textbf{Skeletal muscle dysfunction and cachexia:} Chronic systemic inflammation and inactivity lead to muscle wasting, worsening exercise tolerance.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
COPD is a progressive, chronic disease with a highly variable prognosis. The course is characterised by a gradual decline in FEV1, punctuated by acute exacerbations that accelerate lung function loss and increase mortality. Prognosis is assessed using multi-dimensional scoring systems such as the BODE index (incorporating Body Mass Index, Obstruction severity, Dyspnoea score, and Exercise capacity). Early diagnosis, successful smoking cessation, participation in pulmonary rehabilitation, and adherence to maintenance medications significantly improve long-term outcomes, reduce hospitalization rates, and prolong survival.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Primary prevention focuses on reducing exposure to tobacco smoke and environmental irritants, while self-care focuses on daily symptom control and action planning.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Avoid tobacco exposure:} Quit smoking and avoid exposure to second-hand smoke to prevent the development or rapid progression of COPD.
    \item \textbf{Minimise occupational and environmental hazards:} Use appropriate personal protective equipment (respirators) in dusty or chemical-heavy workplaces, and avoid outdoor activity during periods of high air pollution.
    \item \textbf{Maintain physical activity:} Engage in regular, moderate exercise to preserve cardiovascular fitness and skeletal muscle strength.
    \item \textbf{Follow a COPD Action Plan:} Work with a GP to develop a written plan that outlines daily medication dosages and clear instructions on how to recognize and treat early signs of an acute exacerbation.
    \item \textbf{Optimise nutrition:} Consume a high-protein, nutrient-rich diet to maintain muscle mass, eating smaller, frequent meals if shortness of breath makes large meals difficult.
\end{itemize}
