\chapter{INR Test}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The INR (international normalised ratio) is a blood test that measures how long it takes the blood to clot, compared with a normal standard. It is used to monitor the effect of warfarin, a blood-thinning medicine, and to guide its dose so that the blood is thinned enough to prevent clots but not so much that bleeding becomes dangerous. The INR is calculated from the prothrombin time (PT) test and standardised across laboratories so that results are comparable. For most conditions treated with warfarin the target INR is between 2.0 and 3.0, while a higher range of 2.5 to 3.5 is used for mechanical heart valves. Regular INR testing keeps warfarin therapy safe and effective.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Warfarin is taken by many Australians to prevent blood clots in conditions such as atrial fibrillation, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and mechanical heart valves, and the INR test is one of the most frequently performed blood tests. Because warfarin has a narrow therapeutic range and its effect varies with diet, medicines, and illness, people taking it require regular testing, from weekly to monthly. Around one in ten tests falls outside the target range, requiring dose adjustment. The introduction of direct oral anticoagulants, which do not require monitoring, has reduced warfarin use, but many people still depend on INR testing.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Warfarin works by blocking the action of vitamin K, which is needed to make several clotting factors in the liver. As the effect of these factors falls, the blood takes longer to clot, and the INR rises. The target range balances protection against clots against the risk of bleeding. Many factors alter the INR: changes in vitamin K intake from foods such as green leafy vegetables; interactions with antibiotics, other medicines, and herbal remedies; alcohol; liver disease; and illness, especially fever and diarrhoea. The INR therefore fluctuates, and regular testing with dose adjustment keeps the level in range.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A blood test taken from the arm, or a finger-prick test at some clinics
    \item Results are reported as a number, with the target usually between 2.0 and 3.0
    \item A result below target means higher clot risk; above target means higher bleeding risk
    \item Dose adjustments are made by the doctor, pharmacist, or anticoagulant clinic
    \item Point-of-care testing gives results within minutes
    \item Testing may be weekly, fortnightly, or monthly depending on stability
    \item Some people self-test at home with training
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
An abnormal INR must be interpreted in the context of the person's medicines and health.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low INR:} underdosing, missed doses, increased vitamin K intake, or drug interactions reducing warfarin effect
    \item \textbf{High INR:} over-anticoagulation from drug interactions, illness, liver disease, or dietary changes
    \item \textbf{Non-adherence:} missed or extra doses of warfarin
    \item \textbf{Laboratory variation:} errors or unstable blood samples
    \item \textbf{Underlying clotting disorders:} liver disease or vitamin K deficiency raising the INR independently
    \item \textbf{Bleeding complications:} a high INR with bleeding symptoms needs urgent assessment
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People taking warfarin need regular INR testing as scheduled, and should seek medical advice promptly for any sign of bleeding, such as blood in the urine or stool, prolonged bleeding from cuts, bruising, nosebleeds, or black stools. New medicines, illness, and dietary changes should be discussed with the doctor, as they affect the INR. Bleeding that is severe or worrying requires urgent care.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe or uncontrolled bleeding
    \item Blood in the vomit, or black, tarry stools
    \item Sudden severe headache, weakness, or difficulty speaking, which may indicate bleeding in the brain
    \item Blood in the urine or heavy bleeding that does not stop
    \item Any serious injury or fall while taking warfarin
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{INR measurement:} venous blood testing in a laboratory, or point-of-care testing at clinics and at home
    \item \textbf{Frequency of testing:} determined by the stability of results and the condition being treated
    \item \textbf{Full blood count:} checking for anaemia if bleeding is suspected
    \item \textbf{Review of medicines and diet:} identifying interactions and changes that affect the INR
    \item \textbf{Assessment of illness:} infection, liver disease, and other factors affecting warfarin
    \item \textbf{Clinic review:} anticoagulant clinics provide structured monitoring and dose adjustment
    \item \textbf{Home testing programs:} for selected, trained people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dose adjustment:} the warfarin dose is adjusted according to the INR and the target range
    \item \textbf{Regular monitoring:} at a frequency matched to stability
    \item \textbf{Patient education:} understanding the target range, diet, and medicine interactions
    \item \textbf{Management of high INR:} dose reduction, and in some cases vitamin K or urgent treatment for bleeding
    \item \textbf{Management of low INR:} dose increase, and review of adherence and interactions
    \item \textbf{Consistent vitamin K intake:} keeping dietary habits stable
    \item \textbf{Coordination with other doctors:} informing all prescribers about warfarin use
    \item \textbf{Anticoagulation cards:} carrying details of the dose and target range
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding, the main risk of anticoagulation, ranging from minor bruising to life-threatening haemorrhage
    \item Blood clots from under-treatment
    \item Stroke and embolism when the INR is below target in atrial fibrillation
    \item Interactions with medicines, food, and illness causing unstable levels
    \item The inconvenience and cost of frequent testing
    \item Rarely, bleeding into the brain or other organs
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With regular INR monitoring, warfarin is safe and effective for most people, and the time spent in the therapeutic range is the strongest predictor of outcomes. People whose INR is in range for more than 60 to 70 per cent of the time have low rates of both clotting and bleeding. Structured anticoagulant clinics and self-monitoring improve the time in range. For people who struggle with monitoring, switching to a direct oral anticoagulant may be considered.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend all scheduled INR tests
    \item Take warfarin at the same time each day and never double a missed dose without advice
    \item Keep vitamin K intake consistent, especially from green leafy vegetables
    \item Tell your doctor and pharmacist about all medicines, including over-the-counter and herbal products
    \item Avoid starting new medicines without checking for interactions
    \item Limit alcohol
    \item Report any bleeding or unusual bruising promptly
    \item Use a soft toothbrush and take care with activities that risk injury
    \item Carry your anticoagulation card and tell all health professionals you take warfarin
    \item Seek urgent care for serious injuries, falls, or head strikes
\end{itemize}
