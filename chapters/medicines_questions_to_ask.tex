\chapter{Medicines (questions to ask)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Medicines (questions to ask) provides a clinical framework and practical guide to assist patients, carers, and healthcare consumers in communicating effectively with doctors, pharmacists, and nurses about their medications. Rational prescribing and patient adherence are cornerstones of pharmacotherapy. Active patient participation in clinical consultations has been shown to reduce medication errors, prevent adverse drug reactions, and improve therapeutic outcomes. By asking structured, key questions regarding the indications, administration, side effects, interactions, and long-term management of their medicines, patients can make informed decisions, safely manage their treatments at home, and contribute to their own safety.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Medication errors and adverse drug events are major public health issues globally. In Australia, medication-related hospital admissions represent a substantial healthcare burden, with an estimated 250,000 admissions annually due to medication-related problems, costing the healthcare system over 1.4 billion dollars. Approximately half of these admissions are considered preventable, often resulting from poor communication during transitions of care (such as hospital discharge), inadequate patient understanding of dosages, or unrecognised drug-drug interactions. Polypharmacy (defined as the concurrent use of five or more medications) is highly prevalent in older adults, affecting over 30--40\% of Australians aged 65 years and over. Older patients, those with multiple chronic comorbidities, and individuals with low health literacy are at the highest risk for medication mishaps, emphasising the critical need for active patient questioning and structured medication reviews.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The need for active patient engagement in medication management is driven by the complex pharmacological processes and human factor vulnerabilities inherent in prescribing, dispensing, and administering drugs.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics:} Medicines undergo absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (pharmacokinetics), and exert therapeutic or toxic effects at target receptors (pharmacodynamics). Patient-specific factors, such as age-related decline in renal or hepatic clearance, require dosage adjustments and patient awareness.
    \item \textbf{Transitions of care vulnerabilities:} Discrepancies in medication lists commonly occur when patients move between general practice, specialists, and hospitals, leading to unintended dose changes, omissions, or drug duplications.
    \item \textbf{Drug-drug and drug-food interactions:} Concurrent administration of multiple drugs can alter therapeutic efficacy or precipitate toxicity (such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs combined with ACE inhibitors and diuretics, known as the "triple whammy", which can cause acute kidney injury).
    \item \textbf{Patient non-adherence:} Often caused by a lack of understanding of the medicine's purpose, fear of side effects, complex dosing schedules, or financial barriers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
While this chapter focuses on a communication framework, the clinical presentation that prompts these questions is the receipt of a new prescription, a change in therapy, or the experience of potential drug side effects.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Receiving a new prescription or recommendation for an over-the-counter medicine during a medical consultation.
    \item Experiencing potential adverse effects, such as dizziness, nausea, skin rashes, dry mouth, or muscle aches, after starting a new medicine.
    \item Difficulty managing a complex medication regimen, presenting as missed doses, confusion about timing, or anxiety surrounding pill taking.
    \item The presence of multiple doctors prescribing different treatments, leading to confusion about which medicines are active.
    \item Hospital discharge, which typically involves a discharge summary listing new, ceased, or altered medications.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
In a clinical context, a patient experiencing new symptoms must distinguish between a new medical condition and a drug-induced side effect:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adverse drug reaction vs. new disease progression:} Differentiating whether a new symptom (such as a dry cough from an ACE inhibitor, or muscle pain from a statin) is a drug side effect or a new primary illness.
    \item \textbf{Drug-induced cognitive impairment vs. dementia:} Distinguishing between reversible confusion or sedation caused by anticholinergic or sedative medicines and progressive neurodegenerative dementia.
    \item \textbf{Drug interaction vs. treatment failure:} Differentiating whether a lack of therapeutic response is due to disease progression or an interaction that reduces drug efficacy (such as antacids impairing thyroid hormone absorption).
    \item \textbf{Allergic reaction vs. intolerance:} Distinguishing between a true immunologically mediated drug allergy (such as penicillin anaphylaxis) and a predictable pharmacological side effect (such as stomach upset from erythromycin).
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should feel empowered to contact their pharmacist or doctor whenever they have questions or concerns about their medicines. Certain symptoms related to medication changes require urgent medical attention.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of a severe allergic reaction, such as swelling of the face, lips, or tongue, difficulty breathing, or severe dizziness.
    \item Sudden collapse, loss of consciousness, or chest pain after taking a medicine.
    \item Severe confusion, extreme drowsiness, or seizures.
    \item Suspected medication overdose, whether accidental or intentional.
\end{itemize}

Other non-emergency reasons to seek medical or pharmaceutical review include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Experiencing bothersome or persistent side effects that make you want to stop taking your medicine.
    \item Realising you have missed multiple doses or made a dosing error.
    \item Noticing that your medicine does not seem to be working or that your symptoms are worsening.
    \item Wanting to start taking a new over-the-counter medicine, vitamin, or herbal supplement alongside your prescribed treatments.
    \item Experiencing confusion about how or when to take your medicines, especially after a hospital stay.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnostic processes for medication safety involve medication reconciliation, clinical review, and monitoring drug levels.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Medication reconciliation:} A systematic process of obtaining a complete and accurate list of the patient's current medicines (including prescription, over-the-counter, and complementary medicines) and comparing it against the doctor's records.
    \item \textbf{Home Medicines Review:} A clinical assessment conducted in the patient's home by an accredited pharmacist upon referral from a general practitioner, resulting in a collaborative plan to optimise medication safety.
    \item \textbf{Therapeutic drug monitoring:} Blood tests to measure the concentration of specific medicines with narrow therapeutic indexes (such as digoxin, lithium, phenytoin, or warfarin) to ensure they are in the safe, effective range.
    \item \textbf{Renal and hepatic function monitoring:} Blood tests (creatinine, eGFR, liver enzymes) performed before and during therapy to adjust dosages of renal- or hepatic-cleared medicines.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Optimising medication safety relies on patient-provider communication, structured questioning, and self-management strategies.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{What is the medicine's name and purpose?} Asking for both the brand name and active ingredient, and exactly what condition it is treating.
    \item \textbf{How and when should I take it?} Clarifying the dose, frequency, timing (morning versus night), and whether it should be taken with or without food.
    \item \textbf{How long do I need to take it?} Finding out if the medicine is a short course (like antibiotics) or a long-term treatment for a chronic condition.
    \item \textbf{What should I do if I miss a dose?} Clarifying if you should take it as soon as you remember, skip it, or contact your pharmacist, to avoid double-dosing.
    \item \textbf{What are the potential side effects?} Understanding common side effects, how to manage them, and which severe symptoms require immediate medical care.
    \item \textbf{Does it interact with other substances?} Asking about interactions with existing prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, vitamins, herbal supplements, alcohol, or specific foods.
    \item \textbf{How should I store this medicine?} Checking if the medicine requires refrigeration or protection from light and moisture.
    \item \textbf{Is a generic equivalent available?} Asking if there is a lower-cost version with the same active ingredient and efficacy.
    \item \textbf{Active self-management tools:} Utilising tools such as a current medication list, dose administration aids (such as Webster-paks), smartphone reminder apps, and scheduling regular MedsChecks with your community pharmacist.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Adverse drug events:} Physical harm resulting from medication use, including side effects, toxicities, and allergic reactions.
    \item \textbf{Therapeutic failure:} Suboptimal control of chronic diseases (such as hypertension, epilepsy, or diabetes) due to non-adherence or unrecognised drug interactions.
    \item \textbf{Accidental overdose:} Toxic drug accumulation, potentially leading to organ failure, respiratory depression, or death, often caused by duplicating active ingredients (such as taking multiple paracetamol-containing products).
    \item \textbf{Withdrawal syndromes:} Severe rebound symptoms or physical distress caused by abruptly stopping certain medicines (such as beta-blockers, antidepressants, or benzodiazepines) without medical supervision.
    \item \textbf{Increased healthcare costs:} Preventable hospital admissions, additional medical consultations, and wasted medications.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Asking active questions and understanding one's medicines is associated with excellent patient outcomes. Research shows that patients who are well-informed about their pharmacotherapy have significantly higher rates of treatment adherence, experience fewer adverse drug reactions, and are less likely to require emergency medical attention or readmission to hospital. A collaborative relationship between the patient, general practitioner, and community pharmacist forms the foundation of lifelong safe medication management.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep an up-to-date, written or digital list of all your medicines, including their doses, purposes, and active ingredients, and carry it with you to all medical appointments.
    \item Ask your pharmacist to package your medicines in a dose administration aid if you struggle to remember when to take them.
    \item Do not share your prescribed medicines with others, and do not take medicines prescribed for someone else.
    \item Store all medicines in their original packaging, in a cool, dry place out of reach of children and pets, and avoid storing them in damp bathroom cabinets.
    \item Return expired or unused medicines to your local community pharmacy for safe disposal through the Return Unwanted Medicines (RUM) scheme.
    \item Read the Consumer Medicine Information leaflet provided with your prescription for detailed, patient-focused safety guidance.
    \item Ask for a Home Medicines Review if you are taking multiple daily medicines, have recently been discharged from hospital, or are experiencing suspected side effects.
\end{itemize}
