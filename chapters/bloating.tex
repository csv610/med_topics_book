\chapter{Bloating}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bloating is the sensation of a full, tight, or swollen abdomen. It is frequently accompanied by abdominal distension, in which the abdomen actually protrudes or measures larger than usual. Bloating is extremely common and is usually linked to the digestive system, particularly to gas production and the movement of food through the bowel. In most cases it is harmless and passes quickly, but persistent or severe bloating can indicate an underlying bowel disorder or, rarely, more serious disease such as ovarian cancer, so it should not be dismissed when it is persistent or worsening.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bloating is one of the most frequently reported gut symptoms in the community, affecting roughly one in three adults at some time. It is more common in women than in men, is especially frequent in people with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), and many women experience bloating around menstruation. The prevalence rises with age, and bloating is also common during pregnancy. Most people who experience bloating never seek medical help, which suggests that the majority of cases are mild and self-limiting.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Bloating arises from a combination of gas, fluid, and stool within the abdomen, and the way the gut responds to them. Several mechanisms may act together:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Excess gas:} from swallowed air, or from bacteria in the colon fermenting undigested carbohydrates
    \item \textbf{Sluggish bowel:} constipation slows the passage of contents, allowing gas and stool to accumulate
    \item \textbf{Abnormal gut reflexes:} in IBS, the bowel wall does not relax normally, so even normal amounts of gas cause discomfort and distension
    \item \textbf{Food intolerance:} lactose, fructose, gluten (coeliac disease), and other poorly absorbed carbohydrates
    \item \textbf{Fluid retention:} premenstrual syndrome, and kidney or liver disease, can cause a tense, full abdomen
    \item \textbf{Gynaecological causes:} ovarian cysts, fibroids, and ovarian cancer can press on or distend the abdomen
    \item \textbf{Serious abdominal causes:} bowel obstruction, ascites from liver disease, and abdominal tumours
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Bloating may be described in a number of ways, and the pattern helps point towards the cause:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A feeling of pressure, fullness, or tightness in the abdomen
    \item Visible swelling of the belly that is often worse later in the day
    \item Wind, burping, or excessive passage of gas
    \item Cramping or discomfort that eases after passing wind or stool
    \item Symptoms that worsen after particular foods, such as beans, onions, wheat, or dairy
    \item Associated constipation or diarrhoea
    \item In some cases, a change in waist size or difficulty fastening trousers
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The clinician considers a range of possible causes, from the benign to the serious:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Irritable bowel syndrome: bloating with abdominal pain and altered bowel habit
    \item Constipation and functional gut disorders
    \item Coeliac disease and food intolerances (lactose, fructose, FODMAPs)
    \item Gynaecological conditions: premenstrual bloating, ovarian cysts, and fibroids
    \item Gastro-oesophageal reflux and poor digestion
    \item Ascites: fluid within the abdomen from liver disease or heart failure
    \item Bowel obstruction, especially in older adults with pain and vomiting
    \item Ovarian cancer: persistent bloating with pelvic pain, loss of appetite, and urinary urgency
    \item Pregnancy
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Occasional bloating is normal and does not need medical attention, but a doctor should be consulted when:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Bloating is persistent, occurs on most days, and has lasted more than two to three weeks
    \item It is associated with a change in bowel habit or blood in the stool
    \item It is accompanied by unexplained weight loss or loss of appetite
    \item There is pain, nausea, or vomiting
    \item The abdomen is visibly distended and tender
    \item A woman has persistent bloating with pelvic pain and urinary urgency
    \item Bloating is accompanied by fevers or night sweats
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, sudden abdominal pain or a swollen, hard abdomen
    \item Repeated vomiting, especially with an inability to pass wind or open the bowels (possible obstruction)
    \item Vomiting blood or passing black, tarry stools
    \item Fainting, collapse, or other signs of shock
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Investigation is guided by the history and by the presence of worrying (red-flag) features:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the pattern of bloating, diet, bowel habit, and abdominal examination
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, coeliac serology, liver and kidney function, and thyroid function
    \item \textbf{Stool tests:} for inflammation or infection when diarrhoea is present
    \item \textbf{Breath tests:} for lactose intolerance or small intestinal bacterial overgrowth
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} ultrasound of the abdomen and pelvis, with CT in selected cases
    \item \textbf{Endoscopy:} gastroscopy or colonoscopy when bleeding, weight loss, or other red flags are present
    \item \textbf{Gynaecological review:} for women with persistent or progressive symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on the underlying cause and usually begins with simple, reversible measures:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary changes:} eating smaller meals, eating slowly, and identifying and avoiding trigger foods
    \item \textbf{Low FODMAP diet:} a structured, short-term diet under dietitian guidance that reduces fermentable carbohydrates
    \item \textbf{Fibre management:} adjusting fibre intake, and increasing fluids, for constipation-related bloating
    \item \textbf{Medication:} antispasmodics, laxatives, and remedies for trapped wind, used judiciously
    \item \textbf{Treating the cause:} a gluten-free diet for coeliac disease, lactose-free products for intolerance, and antibiotics for bacterial overgrowth
    \item \textbf{Gut-directed therapies:} psychological and relaxation approaches that act on the gut--brain axis in IBS
    \item \textbf{Exercise:} regular activity improves bowel motility and the clearance of gas
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discomfort, pain, and interference with work, study, and social life
    \item Reduced enjoyment of food, and unintentional weight loss from overly restrictive diets
    \item Anxiety and distress, especially when the cause is unclear
    \item Delayed diagnosis of underlying disease if persistent bloating is dismissed
    \item Complications of the underlying condition, such as bowel obstruction or ovarian cancer, if it goes untreated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For most people, bloating is intermittent and improves with dietary and lifestyle measures. Bloating associated with IBS can be persistent, but is usually manageable with a combination of dietary change, medication, and gut-directed therapy. Coeliac disease responds well to a strict gluten-free diet, and constipation-related bloating improves with regular toileting, fibre, fluids, and activity. Bloating that does not respond to simple measures, or that is accompanied by red-flag symptoms, warrants full investigation, because the underlying cause is often treatable when found.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Eat slowly, chew well, and avoid gulping drinks to reduce swallowed air
    \item Keep a food diary to identify personal trigger foods
    \item Stay active and drink plenty of fluid to keep the bowels regular
    \item Avoid fizzy drinks, chewing gum, and drinking through straws
    \item Exercise regularly, which helps move gas through the bowel
    \item Do not rely on laxatives or wind remedies long term without medical advice
    \item See a doctor promptly for persistent or unexplained bloating
\end{itemize}
