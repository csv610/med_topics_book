\section*{Definition and Overview}
"The clap" is a common slang name for \textbf{gonorrhoea}, a bacterial sexually transmitted infection (STI) caused by \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae}. The infection most often affects the urethra in men and the cervix, urethra, and fallopian tubes in women, but it can also infect the throat, rectum, and eyes.

Gonorrhoea is easily transmitted during vaginal, anal, and oral sex, and can also be passed from mother to baby during birth. It is curable with antibiotics, but it often causes few or no symptoms, so many people do not know they are infected and can unknowingly pass it on. Untreated infection can lead to serious complications, including infertility.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Gonorrhoea is one of the most common bacterial STIs worldwide, second only to chlamydia. Reported rates have been rising in many countries over recent decades. The highest rates are in sexually active young people aged 15--29, and cases are more common in men than in women. Certain groups are disproportionately affected, including men who have sex with men and people who have multiple sexual partners or do not use condoms.

Because many infections cause no symptoms, the true number of cases is likely higher than those reported. The emergence of strains resistant to many antibiotics is a growing public health concern, and monitoring of antibiotic resistance is part of routine care.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Gonorrhoea is caused by the bacterium \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae}, which is transmitted almost exclusively through sexual contact or from mother to baby during childbirth.

The bacteria attach to the moist lining cells of the genital tract, throat, rectum, and conjunctiva of the eye, where they multiply and trigger an inflammatory response. This inflammation produces pus and discharge and can damage the delicate lining of the reproductive tract. In women, infection ascending into the upper genital tract can cause pelvic inflammatory disease, which can scar and block the fallopian tubes. In men, it can spread to the epididymis, the tube that carries sperm.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Many people with gonorrhoea -- particularly women -- have no symptoms. When symptoms occur, they usually appear within two to fourteen days of exposure.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{In men:} discharge from the penis (often thick, cloudy, or yellow-green), pain or burning when urinating, and occasionally painful or swollen testicles
    \item \textbf{In women:} increased or changed vaginal discharge, pain or burning when urinating, bleeding between periods or after sex, and lower abdominal or pelvic pain
    \item \textbf{Throat infection:} a sore throat (often with no symptoms at all)
    \item \textbf{Rectal infection:} discharge, itching, soreness, or bleeding from the anus
    \item \textbf{Eye infection:} redness, discharge, and pain (from contact of infected fluid with the eye)
    \item \textbf{In newborns:} a severe eye infection (conjunctivitis) acquired during birth
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Symptoms of gonorrhoea overlap with other infections and conditions, so testing is essential:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Chlamydia, which often coexists with gonorrhoea
    \item Non-specific urethritis and other causes of urethral discharge
    \item Trichomoniasis and other causes of vaginal discharge
    \item Urinary tract infection (cystitis) causing pain on urination
    \item Pelvic inflammatory disease from other causes
    \item Epididymitis or orchitis in men
    \item Conjunctivitis of the eye from other causes
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek testing if you have any symptoms of a sexually transmitted infection, if a partner has been diagnosed with an STI, or after unprotected sex with a new or casual partner -- even with no symptoms.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal or pelvic pain, fever, and feeling unwell (possible pelvic infection or spread of infection)
    \item In a pregnant woman, early labour or preterm symptoms with a known or suspected infection
    \item Sudden severe pain and swelling of a testicle
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Gonorrhoea is diagnosed by laboratory testing, usually with modern nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs), which are highly accurate.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urine test} to detect infection of the urethra in men
    \item \textbf{Swabs} of the cervix, vagina, urethra, throat, or rectum, depending on the sites of exposure
    \item \textbf{Culture} of a swab to test which antibiotics the bacteria are sensitive to (important because of drug resistance)
    \item \textbf{Full STI screen:} testing at the same time for chlamydia, HIV, syphilis, and hepatitis B, since these infections often occur together
    \item \textbf{Partner notification:} recent sexual partners should be told and offered testing and treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Gonorrhoea is treated with antibiotics. Because resistance to older antibiotics is widespread, treatment is usually given as a single injection, often followed by a course of tablets. The treating clinician will choose the medicine based on local resistance patterns.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antibiotic treatment} of the infected person and of all recent sexual partners at the same time
    \item \textbf{Avoiding sex} until treatment is complete, symptoms have settled, and cure has been confirmed, to prevent re-infection
    \item \textbf{Test of cure:} a follow-up test, often a few weeks later, to confirm the infection has cleared
    \item \textbf{Treatment of co-infections:} antibiotics for chlamydia if also found, plus treatment for other STIs
    \item \textbf{Treatment in pregnancy} with antibiotics that are safe for the baby, and treatment of the newborn's eyes at birth
    \item \textbf{Partner notification and contact tracing} through sexual health services
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} repeat testing of pregnant women after treatment and referral for complications if they occur
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated gonorrhoea can cause serious, sometimes permanent, problems:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Pelvic inflammatory disease in women, leading to chronic pelvic pain and blockage of the fallopian tubes
    \item Infertility in both men (from epididymitis) and women
    \item Ectopic pregnancy, which can be life-threatening
    \item Disseminated gonococcal infection, in which the bacteria spread through the bloodstream, causing fever, rash, and painful, swollen joints
    \item Increased risk of acquiring and transmitting HIV
    \item Eye infection in newborns, which can cause scarring and blindness if untreated
    \item Antibiotic-resistant infection that is harder to treat
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt antibiotic treatment, gonorrhoea is cured in the vast majority of cases, and most people recover completely with no lasting problems. The outlook is worse when treatment is delayed, because damage to the reproductive organs may already have occurred before symptoms develop. This is why routine testing after exposure is so important. Because the infection does not produce immunity, people can catch it again, and the risk of complications increases with repeat infection.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use condoms consistently for vaginal, anal, and oral sex to reduce the risk of transmission
    \item Get tested regularly if sexually active, especially with new or multiple partners
    \item If diagnosed, take the full course of antibiotics, avoid sex until cleared, and tell partners so they can be treated
    \item Do not share sex toys; wash them or use a new condom between partners
    \item Pregnant women should be tested early in pregnancy so infection can be treated before birth
    \item Consider free or low-cost sexual health clinics, which offer confidential testing and partner notification
\end{itemize}
