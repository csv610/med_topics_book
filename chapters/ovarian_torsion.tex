\chapter{Ovarian torsion}
\index{Ovarian torsion}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Ovarian torsion refers to the partial or complete rotation of the ovary, and often the fallopian tube (adnexal torsion), around its vascular pedicle (the infundibulopelvic and utero-ovarian ligaments). This rotation compromises lymphatic and venous outflow, leading to severe congestion, stromal oedema, and increased pressure within the ovary. If untreated, the condition rapidly progresses to arterial occlusion, ischaemia, and subsequent haemorrhagic infarction and necrosis of the ovarian tissue. Ovarian torsion is a gynaecological emergency requiring urgent surgical evaluation to restore perfusion and preserve ovarian function and future fertility.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Ovarian torsion is the fifth most common gynaecological emergency, accounting for approximately 2.7\% of all emergency gynaecological presentations. While it can occur in females of any age, including infants and postmenopausal women, the vast majority of cases (approximately 70--80\%) occur in reproductive-aged females, typically between 15 and 30 years of age. The risk is significantly increased in the presence of an ovarian mass or cyst, particularly those measuring between 5 and 10 cm in diameter, as smaller masses do not create sufficient leverage and larger masses are physically constrained within the pelvis. A right-sided predominance is observed in clinical practice, likely because the longer right utero-ovarian ligament allows greater mobility, and the presence of the sigmoid colon on the left anatomically limits ovarian movement. In many countries, the presentation of ovarian torsion is standard across emergency departments, requiring prompt access to pelvic ultrasound and gynaecological surgery.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The mechanical rotation of the ovary is facilitated by anatomical variations or pathological changes that alter the ovary's normal balance and axis of suspension.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ovarian masses and neoplasms:} The presence of benign ovarian cysts (such as dermoid cysts, mature cystic teratomas, follicular cysts, or corpus luteum cysts) is the most common predisposing factor, creating an asymmetric weight distribution that facilitates twisting.
    \item \textbf{Ligamentous hypermobility:} Congenital or acquired laxity of the infundibulopelvic or utero-ovarian ligaments, which is particularly common in children and adolescents, allows excessive movement of the adnexa.
    \item \textbf{Ovulation induction therapy:} Assistive reproductive technologies that stimulate the ovaries can cause significant ovarian enlargement and the formation of multiple lutein cysts, dramatically increasing the risk of torsion.
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} The risk is elevated during the first trimester of pregnancy, as the gravid uterus displaces pelvic organs, and the presence of a corpus luteum cyst increases ovarian size and mobility.
    \item \textbf{Venous congestion and arterial occlusion:} Initial rotation occludes the low-pressure venous and lymphatic vessels, leading to massive ovarian oedema. As tissue pressure increases, the high-pressure arterial inflow is compromised, culminating in ischaemic necrosis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of ovarian torsion is classic but can be variable, often mimicking other acute abdominal conditions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden onset of severe, unilateral lower abdominal or pelvic pain, which is typically sharp, stabbing, or colicky in nature.
    \item Intermittent pain, which may occur if the ovary undergoes periods of partial rotation and spontaneous detorsion (untwisting) prior to complete occlusion.
    \item Nausea and vomiting, occurring in up to 70\% of patients, closely mimicking acute appendicitis.
    \item Low-grade fever, which may develop later in the clinical course and indicates tissue necrosis or pelvic peritonitis.
    \item Unilateral adnexal tenderness and a palpable pelvic mass on bimanual pelvic examination, though examination may be limited by severe patient discomfort.
    \item Abdominal guarding or rebound tenderness in the lower abdomen, indicating peritoneal irritation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the signs and symptoms of ovarian torsion overlap significantly with other gynaecological, gastrointestinal, and urological disorders, a broad differential diagnosis must be considered.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ruptured ovarian cyst:} Presents with acute pelvic pain, but the pain typically peaks immediately and gradually improves, and ultrasound shows free pelvic fluid without a twisted pedicle.
    \item \textbf{Ectopic pregnancy:} Can cause acute unilateral pain and bleeding; ruled out by a positive beta-human chorionic gonadotropin ($\beta$-hCG) test.
    \item \textbf{Acute appendicitis:} Characterised by periumbilical pain migrating to the right lower quadrant, anorexia, and localised tenderness, particularly challenging to distinguish from right-sided ovarian torsion.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic inflammatory disease or tubo-ovarian abscess:} Typically presents with bilateral pelvic pain, purulent discharge, cervical motion tenderness, and systemic signs of infection, rather than sudden unilateral pain.
    \item \textbf{Ureteric colic:} Presents with acute flank pain radiating to the groin, haematuria, and costovertebral tenderness, without an adnexal mass on imaging.
    \item \textbf{Degenerating uterine leiomyoma:} Fibroids undergoing red degeneration can cause severe acute pelvic pain, differentiated by pelvic ultrasound or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Ovarian torsion is a surgical emergency. Anyone experiencing sudden, severe, and unexplained lower abdominal or pelvic pain should seek immediate medical assessment at an emergency department.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, intense, and worsening unilateral pelvic or lower abdominal pain.
    \item Pelvic pain accompanied by persistent vomiting and inability to keep down fluids.
    \item Pelvic pain combined with a high fever, sweating, dizziness, or fainting.
    \item Severe abdominal pain in a patient known to have an ovarian cyst or undergoing fertility treatment.
    \item Acute pelvic pain during pregnancy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is based on clinical suspicion, supplemented by urgent imaging. Laboratory tests are primarily useful for excluding other causes of acute abdomen.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transvaginal and transabdominal ultrasound (Doppler):} The initial diagnostic modality of choice. Key findings include a significantly enlarged, oedematous ovary (often $>4$ cm), peripheral displacement of follicles (the "string of pearls" sign), a twisted vascular pedicle (the "whirlpool" sign), and variable or absent Doppler flow. Normal Doppler flow does not rule out torsion due to dual blood supply and intermittent detorsion.
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test:} Serum or urine $\beta$-hCG is mandatory to exclude ectopic pregnancy.
    \item \textbf{Full blood count (FBC):} May show mild leukocytosis, which can rise significantly if tissue necrosis or secondary peritonitis has occurred.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory markers:} C-reactive protein (CRP) is often normal initially but may become elevated in late-presenting cases with tissue ischaemia.
    \item \textbf{Computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):} Utilised if ultrasound findings are equivocal, demonstrating ovarian enlargement, deviation of the uterus toward the affected side, and a thickened pedicle.
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic laparoscopy:} The definitive gold standard for both diagnosis and treatment, allowing direct visualisation of the rotated adnexa.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The definitive treatment for ovarian torsion is surgical. Conservative medical management is not appropriate, and delay increases the risk of permanent ovarian damage and loss of fertility.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urgent laparoscopy:} The preferred surgical approach. The primary intervention is detorsion (untwisting) of the affected ovary and fallopian tube to restore blood flow, rather than primary oophorectomy, especially in patients of reproductive age.
    \item \textbf{Assessment of viability:} Visual assessment of the ovary after detorsion. Most ovaries, even those that appear congested, dark, or haemorrhagic, show significant functional recovery and should be preserved.
    \item \textbf{Cystectomy or cyst aspiration:} If a benign cyst or mass is present, cystectomy may be performed (either concurrently or at a later date once inflammation has subsided) to prevent recurrence.
    \item \textbf{Oophoropexy:} Surgical fixation of the ovary to the pelvic sidewall or posterior uterus, which may be considered in cases of recurrent torsion or in children with hypermobile ligaments.
    \item \textbf{Salpingo-oophorectomy:} Indicated only if the ovary is necrotic, black, or completely friable, or in postmenopausal women where ovarian preservation is not clinically necessary.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ovarian necrosis and atrophy, leading to permanent loss of endocrine and reproductive function of the affected ovary.
    \item Secondary peritonitis due to pelvic inflammation from necrotic tissue.
    \item Pelvic adhesion formation, which can cause chronic pelvic pain or mechanical tubal infertility.
    \item Systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) or sepsis resulting from necrotic tissue absorption.
    \item Recurrent torsion of the same or contralateral ovary, particularly in patients with predisposing anatomical factors.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for ovarian torsion is excellent if surgical intervention is performed promptly, ideally within 36 hours of pain onset. When detorsion is executed early, ovarian function is preserved in over 90\% of cases, with normal follicular development and ovulation resuming in subsequent menstrual cycles. If surgery is delayed, the likelihood of irreversible ischaemic necrosis increases, necessitating oophorectomy. However, because females have two ovaries, the loss of one ovary does not typically result in menopause or a significant reduction in overall fertility, provided the contralateral ovary and fallopian tube remain healthy and functional.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Monitor known ovarian cysts through scheduled ultrasound scans, and discuss elective surgical removal (cystectomy) with a gynaecologist if the cyst is large (e.g., $>5$ cm) to prevent torsion.
    \item Restrict strenuous physical activity, vigorous exercise, or sudden twisting movements if diagnosed with a large ovarian cyst or while undergoing ovarian stimulation for IVF.
    \item Seek immediate medical attention if pelvic pain recurs, especially if there is a history of previous ovarian torsion.
    \item Complete all post-operative instructions, including wound care and avoiding heavy lifting, to optimise recovery and reduce adhesive scarring.
\end{itemize}
