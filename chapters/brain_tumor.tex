\chapter{Brain Tumor}
\index{Brain Tumor}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A brain tumour is an abnormal growth of cells within the brain or its surrounding structures, such as the meninges or the pituitary gland. Tumours are classified in two fundamental ways: by their cell of origin (primary tumours arise within the brain itself, whereas secondary or metastatic tumours have spread from a cancer elsewhere in the body, most commonly the lung, breast, or skin) and by their behaviour (benign tumours are slow-growing and non-cancerous, while malignant tumours are aggressive and capable of invading surrounding tissue). Because the skull is a rigid, closed container, even a benign and slowly growing tumour can cause serious harm simply by occupying space, blocking the circulation of cerebrospinal fluid, and raising the pressure inside the head. This explains why the symptoms and management of brain tumours are governed as much by anatomy and intracranial pressure as by the tumour's pathology.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Brain tumours are relatively uncommon compared with cancers of the breast, lung, or bowel: roughly 1 in 100 people develop a primary brain tumour in their lifetime, and they account for around 2\% of all cancers. They occur at any age, including in children, where they are the most common solid tumour, but most are diagnosed in adults over 60. The most common benign primary tumour is the meningioma, while the most common malignant primary tumour is glioblastoma. Metastatic brain tumours are actually more common overall than primary tumours, and their incidence has risen as systemic cancer treatments have improved and people survive longer with their primary disease. Malignant brain tumours are slightly more common in men than in women, whereas meningiomas are more common in women.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The cause of most brain tumours remains unknown, and they usually arise sporadically without any identifiable trigger. A small minority have a clear cause or predisposition:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ionising radiation:} previous therapeutic radiation to the head increases the risk of meningiomas, gliomas, and other tumours, typically years or decades later
    \item \textbf{Inherited genetic syndromes:} neurofibromatosis type 1 and 2, tuberous sclerosis, Li--Fraumeni syndrome, and others predispose to specific tumour types
    \item \textbf{Immunosuppression:} a weakened immune system is associated with certain primary central nervous system lymphomas
    \item \textbf{Age and sex:} particular tumour types are more common in older people or in one sex, reflecting developmental and hormonal influences
\end{itemize}

Extensive research has found no convincing link between mobile phone use and brain tumours. Whatever the trigger, a tumour develops when cells acquire successive genetic alterations that allow them to proliferate out of control. As it enlarges, the tumour invades or displaces brain tissue, obstructs cerebrospinal fluid flow, and provokes surrounding vasogenic oedema; together these effects raise intracranial pressure and disrupt normal neurological function.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on the tumour's size, site, and growth rate, and typically evolve progressively:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Headache, particularly when new, persistent, worse on waking, or accompanied by nausea
    \item Seizures -- a first-ever seizure in an adult is an important warning sign and is the presenting feature of many brain tumours
    \item Weakness, numbness, or clumsiness affecting one side of the body
    \item Difficulty speaking, understanding language, or finding words
    \item Visual problems, including blurred, double, or peripheral vision
    \item Balance and coordination problems, or unsteadiness
    \item Personality, behaviour, memory, or concentration change
    \item Nausea and vomiting, especially in the morning
    \item Drowsiness and reduced alertness in advanced cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Stroke or transient ischaemic attack, which can produce similar focal symptoms
    \item Brain abscess or other intracranial infection, which can appear remarkably similar on imaging
    \item Multiple sclerosis or other inflammatory or demyelinating conditions
    \item Chronic subdural haematoma, particularly in older adults
    \item Migraine and other primary headache disorders
    \item Idiopathic intracranial hypertension -- raised pressure within the skull without a tumour
    \item Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis or a vascular malformation
    \item Epilepsy from another cause presenting with seizures
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor promptly for any new, persistent, or worsening neurological symptom, particularly a new headache that does not respond to usual treatment, a first-ever seizure, or progressive weakness, speech, or vision problems. While most such symptoms have a benign cause, early imaging is essential whenever a brain tumour is a possibility.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A seizure or fitting episode
    \item A sudden, severe headache
    \item Sudden weakness, numbness, or difficulty speaking
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or loss of consciousness
    \item Sudden loss of vision
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} a detailed account of the onset and progression of symptoms, and a full neurological examination including fundoscopy to look for papilloedema, a sign of raised intracranial pressure
    \item \textbf{MRI of the brain:} the most sensitive imaging modality, routinely performed with and without contrast to characterise the lesion
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} used for rapid assessment in an emergency or where MRI is unavailable or contraindicated
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} histological sampling of the tumour, either during resection or by image-guided needle biopsy, to establish the exact diagnosis and grade
    \item \textbf{Molecular and genetic testing:} analysis of tumour tissue for markers such as IDH status, MGMT promoter methylation, and 1p/19q codeletion that guide treatment and predict behaviour
    \item \textbf{Staging:} when a metastasis is suspected, imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis and other tests to identify the primary cancer
    \item \textbf{Functional imaging:} techniques such as perfusion and spectroscopy MRI, or PET, to add diagnostic precision in selected cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is planned by a multidisciplinary team of neurosurgeons, clinical oncologists, neuropathologists, radiologists, and specialist nurses, and depends on the tumour type, grade, location, and the person's age and fitness:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} maximal safe resection where feasible, or a diagnostic biopsy where removal would risk unacceptable damage; awake surgery with mapping is used for tumours near eloquent areas
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} targeted radiation to treat residual or inaccessible disease, including highly focused techniques such as stereotactic radiosurgery for small lesions
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy:} cytotoxic drugs given by mouth, intravenously, or occasionally directly into the brain, either alone or with radiotherapy
    \item \textbf{Targeted and immunotherapy:} newer agents directed at specific molecular features of the tumour, used increasingly in selected tumour types
    \item \textbf{Corticosteroids:} dexamethasone and similar drugs reduce oedema around the tumour and can relieve symptoms rapidly
    \item \textbf{Antiepileptic medication:} used to control seizures; it is not routinely prescribed to people who have never had one
    \item \textbf{Supportive and palliative care:} symptom control, rehabilitation, and psychological and practical support, which are important at every stage and central when cure is not possible
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Raised intracranial pressure, which can be life-threatening if not relieved
    \item Seizures, which may continue or recur despite treatment
    \item Neurological deficits such as weakness, speech disturbance, or memory loss, from the tumour itself or from surgery and radiotherapy
    \item Hydrocephalus from obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid flow
    \item Treatment-related effects: infection and bleeding after surgery, radiation injury to healthy brain, and fatigue, nausea, or lowered immunity from chemotherapy
    \item Tumour recurrence after treatment
    \item Cognitive and psychological difficulties, and the substantial emotional impact of a serious diagnosis on the person and their family and carers
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook varies enormously according to tumour type, grade, and location. Many benign tumours, such as meningiomas, can be cured surgically and carry an excellent long-term prognosis. Low-grade gliomas may allow many years of good-quality life, whereas high-grade tumours such as glioblastoma are generally not curable, although multimodal treatment can meaningfully extend survival and control symptoms. Prognosis is better in younger, fitter people and where a complete resection is possible. Because individual outcomes are so variable, the prognosis should always be discussed in detail with the treating team, and many people benefit from a combination of active treatment and careful symptom management.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Avoid unnecessary radiation to the head, including repeated head CT scans that are not medically indicated
    \item Attend recommended screening for cancers that can spread to the brain, such as melanoma, breast, and lung cancer
    \item Seek prompt medical review of any new or persistent neurological symptom
    \item Follow the treatment plan and attend all scans and follow-up appointments
    \item Take prescribed medicines, including corticosteroids and antiepileptic drugs, exactly as directed
    \item Maintain general health with a balanced diet, gentle activity, and adequate rest, as advised by the team
    \item Seek psychological support and connect with support groups, since a brain tumour diagnosis affects the whole family
\end{itemize}
