\chapter{Milia}
\index{Milia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Milia (singular: milium) are small, benign, superficial, keratin-filled subepidermal cysts that appear as tiny, firm, white-to-yellow papules on the skin. They are most commonly located on the face, particularly around the eyes, cheeks, nose, and forehead, although they can develop on any part of the body, including the mucosal surfaces. Milia are common and occur across all age groups, but they are particularly prevalent in newborns, in whom they are known as neonatal milia. Milia are classified as primary (arising spontaneously from undeveloped sebaceous glands or vellus hair follicles) or secondary (developing after skin trauma, disease, or medication use). While milia are completely harmless and cosmetically benign, they are often a source of aesthetic concern for patients. Primary neonatal milia resolve spontaneously within a few weeks, whereas adult milia tend to be persistent and may require dermatological intervention for removal.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Neonatal milia are extremely common, affecting approximately 40--50\% of all newborn infants, with no predilection for sex or ethnicity. Primary milia in newborns are congenital and typically present at birth. In older children and adults, milia can occur at any age, but their exact prevalence is undocumented because they are benign and frequently go unreported. Secondary milia can develop in any individual following local skin injuries, such as burns, blistering skin diseases (for example, epidermolysis bullosa or porphyria cutanea tarda), dermabrasion, or long-term use of topical corticosteroids. There are no specific global or local statistics due to the benign nature of the condition, but milia represent a very common reason for dermatological and cosmetic consultations.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Milia develop when dead skin cells (keratin) become trapped in microscopic pockets just beneath the skin surface, rather than shedding normally.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Keratin entrapment:} The physical accumulation of lamellated keratin sheets within a small cyst wall lined by stratified squamous epithelium, located in the upper dermis.
    \item \textbf{Primary milia origin:} Arise spontaneously, believed to originate from the infundibulum of vellus hair follicles or undeveloped sebaceous glands.
    \item \textbf{Secondary milia origin:} Develop from epithelial structures (such as sweat ducts, hair follicles, or epidermis) that have been damaged by trauma, blistering, or scarring.
    \item \textbf{Skin trauma and blistering:} Injuries such as second-degree burns, chemical peels, dermabrasion, laser resurfacing, or blistering disorders (such as bullous pemphigoid) that disrupt the integrity of the adnexal ducts, leading to keratin retention.
    \item \textbf{Medication-associated milia:} Associated with the chronic use of topical corticosteroids (which cause skin thinning and follicular structural weakening) or certain systemic drugs like 5-fluorouracil.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of milia is highly characteristic, featuring distinct physical skin findings.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Small, firm, dome-shaped papules, typically measuring 1 to 2 millimetres in diameter.
    \item A distinct pearly white-to-yellow colour, reflecting the trapped keratin beneath the thin epidermal layer.
    \item Most commonly distributed on the face, specifically the cheeks, nose, forehead, and delicate skin around the eyelids.
    \item In newborns, milia are often found on the nose, upper lip, and mucosal surfaces (where they are called Epstein's pearls when located on the palate).
    \item The papules are completely asymptomatic, with no associated pain, itching, redness, or warmth, unless they become mechanically irritated.
    \item Unlike acne vulgaris, milia lack a central pore and are firm to the touch, resisting manual extrusion.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Milia must be distinguished from other benign and inflammatory facial papules:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acne vulgaris:} Closed comedones (whiteheads) are softer, have a central follicular opening, are often accompanied by inflammatory lesions (papules and pustules), and are extractable with gentle pressure.
    \item \textbf{Sebaceous hyperplasia:} Benign enlargement of sebaceous glands; presents as larger, yellowish papules with a characteristic central indentation (umbilication) and visible blood vessels, common in older adults.
    \item \textbf{Syringomas:} Benign tumours of the eccrine sweat glands; present as flesh-coloured or yellowish papules around the lower eyelids, typically flatter and less pearly than milia.
    \item \textbf{Xanthelasma:} Flat, yellowish plaques representing lipid deposits, typically located on the medial aspects of the eyelids, associated with hyperlipidaemia.
    \item \textbf{Miliaria:} Caused by blocked eccrine sweat ducts; presents as transient, itchy, red papules or tiny clear vesicles on the trunk and neck, related to heat and sweating.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Milia are benign, self-limiting in infants, and do not require medical intervention. However, patients may seek advice for diagnosis or cosmetic removal.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Any severe, acute skin reaction (such as widespread blistering, peeling, or high fever) unrelated to milia but representing a medical emergency (such as Stevens-Johnson syndrome).
\end{itemize}

Other non-emergency reasons to consult a general practitioner or dermatologist include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The lumps are growing rapidly, changing colour, or spreading extensively.
    \item The lumps become red, painful, warm, or drain pus (suggesting secondary bacterial infection).
    \item You are unsure of the diagnosis and want to rule out more serious skin conditions.
    \item You desire cosmetic removal of persistent milia, especially if they are located near the eyes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is almost entirely clinical, based on the physical appearance and location of the lesions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dermatological examination:} Direct visual inspection of the skin, checking the distribution, size, firmness, and colour of the papules.
    \item \textbf{Dermoscopy:} Use of a handheld dermatoscope to visualise the white, structured, round nests of keratin, helping differentiate milia from comedones or syringomas.
    \item \textbf{Skin biopsy:} Rarely required, but can confirm the diagnosis in atypical, eruptive, or plaque-like presentations, showing a small subepidermal cyst filled with lamellated keratin.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
In newborns, milia require no treatment and resolve spontaneously. In adults, treatment is purely cosmetic and should be performed by a trained professional to avoid scarring.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reassurance and observation:} The preferred approach, particularly in infants, as neonatal milia clear without intervention within 2--4 weeks.
    \item \textbf{Manual extraction:} The standard clinical removal method, where a practitioner uses a sterile needle or lancet to create a tiny nick in the epidermis over the cyst, followed by gentle pressure or a comedone extractor to lift out the intact keratin pearl.
    \item \textbf{Topical retinoids:} Prescription creams (such as tretinoin) that promote cell turnover and exfoliation, helping to thin the skin over the cysts and prevent new milia from forming.
    \item \textbf{Cryotherapy:} Application of liquid nitrogen to freeze and destroy the cysts, useful for multiple lesions but carries a small risk of temporary hypopigmentation.
    \item \textbf{Electrosurgery and laser ablation:} Micro-cautery or carbon dioxide laser therapy to vaporise the cysts, suitable for extensive or hard-to-reach lesions near the eyelids.
    \item \textbf{Chemical peels:} Superficial peels (such as glycolic or salicylic acid) to exfoliate the outer layers of the skin, helping clear superficial milia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scarring and hyperpigmentation:} Can occur if patients attempt to squeeze, pop, or cut milia at home using unsterile tools.
    \item \textbf{Secondary infection:} Bacterial entry leading to localised cellulitis or abscess formation due to self-manipulation.
    \item \textbf{Milia en plaque:} A rare, benign variant characterised by multiple milia arising on an erythematous plaque, typically located on the ears, eyelids, or cheeks, which can be cosmetically disfiguring and difficult to treat.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for milia is excellent. Neonatal milia resolve completely within the first month of life without leaving scars or pigment changes. In adults, primary and secondary milia can persist indefinitely if untreated, but they are completely harmless. Professional removal techniques (such as manual de-roofing or laser therapy) yield immediate, highly effective cosmetic results with minimal risk of scarring or recurrence when performed by a dermatologist.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cleanse your face daily with a mild, soap-free cleanser and warm water to remove excess oils and dead skin cells.
    \item Avoid squeezing, scratching, or attempting to pop milia at home; since they lack a pore, squeezing only damages the surrounding skin and increases infection risk.
    \item Incorporate a gentle, non-abrasive chemical exfoliant (such as a low-strength salicylic or glycolic acid toner) into your skincare routine 2--3 times a week to promote cell turnover.
    \item Use oil-free, non-comedogenic moisturisers and sunscreens to prevent clogging the skin pores and ducts.
    \item Protect your skin from sun damage by wearing a broad-spectrum SPF 30+ sunscreen daily, as sun damage can coarsen the skin and make milia more persistent.
    \item Limit the use of heavy, oil-based cosmetics or thick eye creams if you are prone to milia around the eyelids.
\end{itemize}
