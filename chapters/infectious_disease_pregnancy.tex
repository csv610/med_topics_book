\chapter{Infectious Diseases in Pregnancy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Some infections are more serious during pregnancy because they can affect not only the mother but also the developing baby, either across the placenta, during birth, or through breastfeeding. This chapter covers the prevention, recognition, and management of infectious diseases in pregnancy, including common infections such as urinary tract infections and influenza, and the important infections that can harm the fetus, such as rubella, cytomegalovirus, toxoplasmosis, chickenpox, and syphilis. Routine antenatal screening detects several of these infections early, and vaccination, hygiene measures, and prompt treatment protect both mother and baby. The principles are simple: prevent infection where possible, screen for it, and treat it early and safely.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Infections are among the most common complications of pregnancy. Urinary tract infections affect around one in ten pregnancies, and influenza and other respiratory viruses circulate each season. Serious congenital infections are less common: rubella has been virtually eliminated in Australia through vaccination, while cytomegalovirus affects around one in a hundred pregnancies and is the most common congenital infection. Rates of sexually transmissible infections, including chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and syphilis, have risen in recent years, and syphilis in pregnancy, which can cause stillbirth and severe fetal disease, requires particular vigilance. Group B streptococcus colonises the vagina in around one in five pregnant women and can infect the baby during birth.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Infections can affect the fetus in several ways. Some cross the placenta during maternal infection, potentially causing malformations, growth restriction, or fetal death, depending on the timing: rubella in early pregnancy, for example, is most damaging in the first trimester. Others infect the baby at the time of birth, such as group B streptococcus, herpes simplex, and HIV, by contact with the birth canal. Cytomegalovirus and toxoplasmosis can cross the placenta at any time and cause brain and eye damage. The immune changes of pregnancy make the mother more vulnerable to certain infections, and respiratory illness such as influenza can be more severe. Prevention, screening, and treatment are aimed at interrupting these pathways.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Many infections in pregnancy cause no symptoms in the mother and are found on screening
    \item Fever, aches, and respiratory symptoms with influenza and COVID-19
    \item Pain or burning with urination, or smelly urine, with urinary tract infection
    \item Genital ulceration, discharge, or rash with sexually transmissible infections
    \item A typical rash with chickenpox, rubella, or parvovirus infection
    \item Effects on the baby may include growth restriction, anaemia, hydrops, jaundice, hearing loss, and neurodevelopmental problems
    \item Flu-like illness with cytomegalovirus or toxoplasmosis, though often asymptomatic
    \item Stillbirth or miscarriage in the most serious cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Illness in pregnancy has many possible causes, and the specific infection must be identified.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urinary tract infection and pyelonephritis:} common, and screened for routinely
    \item \textbf{Influenza and COVID-19:} respiratory viruses with fever and body aches
    \item \textbf{Cytomegalovirus and toxoplasmosis:} often mild or silent in the mother
    \item \textbf{Parvovirus B19:} a mild childhood illness that can cause fetal anaemia
    \item \textbf{Listeria:} foodborne infection causing fever and flu-like illness
    \item \textbf{Chickenpox and shingles:} varicella-zoster virus infection
    \item \textbf{Rubella and syphilis:} screened for routinely because of fetal effects
    \item \textbf{Non-infectious causes:} pre-eclampsia, other pregnancy complications, and common illnesses
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Pregnant women should seek early antenatal care, attend all screening tests, and be vaccinated against influenza and whooping cough in pregnancy as recommended. Any fever, persistent illness, exposure to a contagious infection such as chickenpox, or symptoms of urinary or genital infection should be discussed promptly with a doctor or midwife. Advice should be sought before travel to areas with malaria, Zika, or other infection risks.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing or severe chest pain
    \item High fever with a severe headache, stiff neck, or rash
    \item Decreased fetal movements
    \item Vaginal bleeding or leaking of fluid
    \item Severe abdominal pain or signs of labour before term
    \item Collapse, confusion, or seizures
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Routine antenatal screening:} blood tests in early pregnancy for blood group, rubella, syphilis, hepatitis B, HIV, and other infections
    \item \textbf{Urine testing:} routine screening for bacteriuria and tests when symptoms occur
    \item \textbf{Cervical screening:} testing for chlamydia and gonorrhoea in at-risk women
    \item \textbf{Group B streptococcus testing:} vaginal and rectal swabs late in pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Serology:} blood tests to diagnose or confirm immunity to specific infections
    \item \textbf{Amniocentesis or fetal blood sampling:} in selected cases to diagnose fetal infection
    \item \textbf{Fetal ultrasound:} to assess growth, fluid, and features of fetal infection
    \item \textbf{Testing the newborn:} where maternal infection is present or suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} treatment of urinary, genital, and other bacterial infections, chosen to be safe in pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Antivirals:} treatment of influenza, herpes, and HIV to protect the mother and reduce transmission to the baby
    \item \textbf{Immunoglobulin and vaccination:} for exposure to chickenpox, and vaccination against influenza and whooping cough in pregnancy
    \item \textbf{Intrapartum care:} intravenous antibiotics during labour for group B streptococcus, and planned caesarean section for active genital herpes
    \item \textbf{Fetal monitoring:} ultrasound and other surveillance where fetal infection is a concern
    \item \textbf{Preventing mother-to-child transmission:} antiretroviral therapy for HIV and safe infant feeding advice
    \item \textbf{Public health follow-up:} contact tracing for sexually transmissible and notifiable infections
    \item \textbf{Paediatric review:} assessment of the newborn after birth when infection is present or suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Miscarriage, stillbirth, and preterm birth
    \item Congenital infection syndromes: hearing loss, vision problems, brain damage, and growth restriction
    \item Anaemia and hydrops fetalis with parvovirus
    \item Neonatal infection with group B streptococcus, herpes, or other pathogens
    \item Severe maternal illness, including pneumonia with influenza or sepsis with pyelonephritis
    \item Transmission of HIV, hepatitis B, or syphilis to the baby when untreated
    \item Psychological distress for families during investigation and uncertainty
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With antenatal screening and modern treatment, the outlook for most infections in pregnancy is excellent. Antibiotics cure urinary and many genital infections, antiretrovirals reduce HIV transmission to the baby to below one per cent, and treatment of syphilis prevents most fetal damage. Vaccination in pregnancy protects both mother and baby against influenza and whooping cough. For congenital infections such as cytomegalovirus, outcomes depend on timing and severity, and early detection allows monitoring and support. Prevention through vaccination, hygiene, and safe food practices remains the most effective strategy.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Be vaccinated against influenza and whooping cough in every pregnancy
    \item Ensure immunity to rubella, measles, and chickenpox before pregnancy
    \item Attend all antenatal appointments and screening tests
    \item Wash hands frequently and avoid contact with sick children's saliva and urine
    \item Avoid handling cat litter and raw meat to prevent toxoplasmosis
    \item Practise food safety and avoid unpasteurised dairy, deli meats, and pre-prepared salads
    \item Use condoms to prevent sexually transmissible infections
    \item Avoid travel to areas with malaria or Zika risk unless advised
    \item Report fever, rash, or exposure to infectious illness to a doctor or midwife promptly
\end{itemize}
