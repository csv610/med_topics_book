\chapter{AICD (Implantable Cardioverter Defibrillator)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An automated implantable cardioverter defibrillator (AICD or ICD) is a small, battery-powered device placed under the skin of the chest that continuously monitors the heartbeat and can deliver an electrical shock or pacing to stop life-threatening fast heart rhythms. It is used in people whose heart is at risk of developing ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation -- rhythms that can cause the heart to stop pumping blood effectively (sudden cardiac arrest).

The device does not cure the underlying heart condition, but it acts as a safety net that can restore a normal rhythm when a dangerous arrhythmia occurs. Modern devices can also act as pacemakers for dangerously slow heart rhythms and can be monitored remotely so that problems are detected early.

\section*{Epidemiology}
ICDs are implanted in hundreds of thousands of people worldwide each year. They are most commonly fitted in people with heart failure, a previous heart attack, or a weakened heart muscle (cardiomyopathy), because these conditions carry an increased risk of dangerous arrhythmias.

Men are affected by heart disease more often than women, and most recipients are over 50 years of age. However, people with inherited heart-rhythm conditions such as long QT syndrome or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy can receive a device at any age, including in childhood.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The device is a treatment rather than a disease. The conditions that lead to an ICD being needed include the following.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Previous heart attack:} scarring of the heart muscle creates abnormal pathways for electrical signals
    \item \textbf{Heart failure:} a weakened or enlarged heart is prone to arrhythmias
    \item \textbf{Cardiomyopathy:} diseases of the heart muscle, such as dilated or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
    \item \textbf{Inherited rhythm conditions:} such as long QT syndrome, Brugada syndrome, or arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy
    \item \textbf{Previous cardiac arrest or dangerous arrhythmia:} a history of these events makes another more likely
\end{itemize}

The device is connected to the heart by one or more leads threaded through a vein. It constantly senses the heart's electrical rhythm. If a dangerously fast rhythm occurs, it first attempts to pace the heart gently (anti-tachycardia pacing); if that fails, it delivers a defibrillation shock. It can also pace the heart when the rhythm becomes dangerously slow.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The device itself usually causes no symptoms. The features relate to the underlying heart condition and to the way the device behaves.

\begin{itemize}
    \item No symptoms when the device is monitoring normally
    \item A shock may be felt as a sudden thump in the chest, sometimes with a brief dizzy or faint feeling
    \item A beeping tone from the device can alert the person that a check is needed
    \item Symptoms of the underlying heart disease, such as breathlessness, fatigue, or palpitations
    \item A small bump under the skin where the device is implanted
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the device is a treatment, the clinical question is usually whether symptoms are due to the device, the underlying heart condition, or something else.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Heart rhythm problems not treated by the device, such as atrial fibrillation
    \item Device malfunction or an inappropriate shock
    \item Infection of the device pocket or leads
    \item Syncope or dizziness from other causes, such as low blood pressure, dehydration, or a seizure
    \item Panic or anxiety symptoms that mimic palpitations
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the device clinic or hospital promptly if a shock has occurred, and seek urgent assessment in the situations below.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item More than one shock in a short period, or a shock followed by collapse or continuing symptoms
    \item Fainting, severe dizziness, or chest pain
    \item Difficulty breathing
    \item Signs of infection at the implant site: redness, swelling, warmth, oozing, or fever
\end{itemize}

Also arrange a device check whenever a beeping warning tone is heard, even if the person feels well.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Before implantation, tests are performed to confirm that the device is needed and to plan the procedure.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Electrocardiogram (ECG):} records the heart's electrical activity
    \item \textbf{Echocardiogram:} ultrasound imaging to measure how well the heart pumps
    \item \textbf{Holter monitor or event recorder:} portable devices that record the heart rhythm over time
    \item \textbf{Exercise testing:} for some people, to see how the heart responds to exertion
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} to check heart function and other health problems
    \item \textbf{Cardiac MRI:} in some cases, to examine the heart muscle in detail
\end{itemize}

After implantation, the device is checked regularly by remote monitoring or in the clinic to review its battery, leads, and any recorded events.

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Implantation:} a procedure under local or general anaesthetic in which the device and leads are placed, usually taking about an hour
    \item \textbf{Device programming:} the specialist sets the heart-rate thresholds for pacing and shock delivery
    \item \textbf{Regular device checks:} at the clinic or remotely, to monitor battery life, lead function, and recorded rhythm events
    \item \textbf{Medication:} beta-blockers and other medicines are usually continued to treat the underlying heart condition
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle adjustments:} avoiding strong magnets, keeping phones away from the chest, and being cautious around electrical equipment
    \item \textbf{Safety:} temporary driving restrictions after implantation and after shocks, and avoidance of contact sports that could damage the device
    \item \textbf{Battery replacement:} a minor operation every several years when the battery runs low
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding, bruising, or infection at the implant site
    \item Damage to the vein or heart during lead placement
    \item Movement of the device, or dislodgement or fracture of a lead
    \item Inappropriate shocks, when the device misreads a normal rhythm as dangerous
    \item Pneumothorax (collapsed lung) as a rare complication of the procedure
    \item Anxiety, fear of shocks, and reduced quality of life in some people
    \item The device cannot prevent all sudden cardiac deaths
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
An ICD substantially improves survival for people at high risk of a dangerous arrhythmia and can be life-saving. It does not cure the underlying heart disease, so the long-term outlook also depends on the heart condition being treated and on the success of other treatments such as medications and cardiac rehabilitation.

Most people return to normal activities after recovery from the procedure, and the device can be lived with for many years with regular follow-up. Psychological support is important for the minority who find living with the device anxiety-provoking.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Underlying disease:} treat and control the underlying heart condition, including blood pressure, cholesterol, and diabetes
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} do not smoke, and limit alcohol
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} keep regular appointments and device checks
    \item \textbf{Identification:} carry a device ID card, and inform doctors and dentists about the device
    \item \textbf{Interference:} avoid strong magnetic fields, and keep phones away from the chest area
    \item \textbf{Driving:} follow driving advice and return to driving only when cleared
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} seek help if fear of shocks or anxiety is affecting daily life
\end{itemize}
