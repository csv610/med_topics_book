\chapter{Blepharoplasty}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Blepharoplasty is a surgical procedure that removes excess skin, muscle, and fat from the upper and/or lower eyelids. It is performed for cosmetic reasons -- to give the eyes a fresher, more alert appearance -- and for functional reasons, when drooping upper lids obstruct the visual field. It is one of the most commonly performed plastic surgery procedures in the world. Blepharoplasty should be distinguished from a brow lift, which elevates the eyebrows, and from ptosis repair, which corrects weakness of the levator muscle that opens the eye; these procedures may be combined when several problems coexist.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Blepharoplasty is performed most often in adults over 40 years of age, when eyelid skin becomes progressively laxer and orbital fat pads more prominent, and it is among the most popular cosmetic operations in both men and women. The functional variety, performed to relieve visual-field obstruction from upper-lid hooding, is more common in older adults whose drooping lids interfere with reading and driving. Rates have risen steadily with the growth of cosmetic surgery generally, and a small proportion of people undergo revision surgery in later years as ageing continues.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The eyelids age through a combination of thinning and loss of elasticity of the skin, weakening of the orbicularis muscle and supporting connective tissue, and redistribution of the orbital fat. The result is a set of characteristic changes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Excess, baggy upper-lid skin that may hood over the lashes and outer corner of the eye
    \item Puffy or bulging lower-lid fat bags (herniated fat)
    \item Fine wrinkles and laxity of the skin around the eyes
    \item Occasionally, stretching or dehiscence of the levator aponeurosis contributes to true ptosis
\end{itemize}

Genetics, cumulative sun exposure, smoking, and the age-related loss of fat and collagen all determine how early and how severely these changes develop, and they explain why the same person's upper and lower lids may age quite differently.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features that lead people to consider blepharoplasty include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Heavy, droopy upper eyelids that may obscure the outer part of the visual field
    \item A persistently tired or aged appearance despite adequate sleep
    \item Bags under the eyes from herniated lower-lid fat
    \item Excess, wrinkled skin over the lids
    \item Difficulty applying makeup or eye drops because of overhanging skin
    \item In severe cases, difficulty with driving or reading as the lid obstructs vision
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before surgery, the surgeon looks for conditions that can mimic or coexist with excess eyelid skin:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ptosis:} true drooping from a weak levator muscle, which requires a different operation
    \item \textbf{Brow ptosis:} sagging of the eyebrows, which is corrected with a brow lift rather than lid surgery alone
    \item \textbf{Dermatochalasis:} the medical term for the excess eyelid skin that is the target of the operation
    \item \textbf{Periorbital oedema:} fluid swelling of the lids, often from allergies, excess salt, or kidney disease
    \item \textbf{Thyroid eye disease:} which can cause puffiness, retraction, and prominence of the eyes
    \item \textbf{Eyelid tumours or cysts:} occasionally masquerading as simple skin excess
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most people considering blepharoplasty arrange a consultation with a suitably qualified surgeon directly. Urgent advice is needed for sudden eye symptoms, and for concerning problems after surgery:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe eyelid swelling or pain
    \item A red, painful, or light-sensitive eye
    \item A sudden change in vision or a curtain-like shadow across the vision
    \item After surgery: heavy bleeding, a wound that opens, or signs of infection (spreading redness, discharge, or fever)
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden loss of vision
    \item Severe eye pain after surgery
    \item Difficulty breathing or signs of anaphylaxis (swelling of the face, lips, or throat)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment for blepharoplasty is predominantly clinical, with targeted investigations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} cosmetic concerns, functional visual symptoms, previous eye or eyelid surgery, medications (including aspirin, anticoagulants, and supplements), and general health
    \item \textbf{Examination:} measurement of excess skin, assessment of lid position and levator function, and evaluation of brow position
    \item \textbf{Visual-field testing:} formal mapping of the field of vision, used to justify and document functional surgery
    \item \textbf{Eye assessment:} checking for dry eye, tear-film abnormalities, and other ocular disease that could complicate healing
    \item \textbf{Photography:} standardised pre-operative photographs for planning and comparison
    \item \textbf{Pre-operative checks:} blood pressure, clotting status, and review of medicines that increase bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Blepharoplasty is usually performed as a day procedure under local anaesthesia with sedation or, less commonly, general anaesthesia. Skin, and sometimes muscle and fat, is removed or repositioned through incisions hidden in the natural upper-lid crease or just below the lower-lash line. Management considerations include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgical technique:} upper-lid, lower-lid, or combined surgery; fat may be repositioned rather than removed to avoid a hollowed appearance
    \item \textbf{Non-surgical alternatives:} dermal fillers, laser resurfacing, or radiofrequency treatments for milder changes
    \item \textbf{Aftercare:} cold compresses, head elevation, and keeping the wounds clean
    \item \textbf{Medication:} lubricating eye drops or ointment to prevent dry eye while healing
    \item \textbf{Avoidance:} no heavy lifting or strenuous exercise for several weeks, and avoiding aspirin-like medicines around the operation
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} suture removal, review of healing and symmetry, and advice on when to resume normal activities
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Most blepharoplasties heal well, but complications can occur and are minimised by careful patient selection:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Bruising and swelling, which usually settle within one to two weeks
    \item Dry, gritty eyes, which are often temporary but occasionally persistent
    \item Bleeding, including a retrobulbar haematoma behind the eye (a rare surgical emergency)
    \item Infection of the wound or the eye itself
    \item Visible scarring, especially with the lower-lid approach
    \item Ectropion, where the lower lid turns outward and leaves the eye exposed
    \item Asymmetry or overcorrection, sometimes requiring revision surgery
    \item Rarely, injury to the extraocular muscles causing double vision
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people are pleased with the result, and the improvement in appearance is usually long-lasting, although the natural ageing process continues afterwards. Where surgery is performed for functional reasons, most people find that the obstructed visual field is substantially improved. Bruising settles over one to two weeks and the final result becomes fully apparent after several months. A good outcome depends heavily on careful patient selection, realistic expectations, and the skill and experience of the surgeon.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Protect the skin around the eyes from sun with sunglasses and sunscreen
    \item Avoid smoking, which accelerates skin ageing and impairs healing
    \item Choose a surgeon who is appropriately qualified, experienced, and accredited
    \item Stop smoking and discuss all medicines, including over-the-counter and herbal supplements, with the surgical team beforehand
    \item Follow post-operative instructions, including rest, ice packs, head elevation, and wound care
    \item Attend follow-up appointments and report any sudden pain, bleeding, or change in vision
\end{itemize}
