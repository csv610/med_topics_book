\chapter{Bursitis}
\index{Bursitis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bursitis is inflammation of a bursa, a small fluid-filled sac that cushions bones, tendons, and muscles where they move over one another. Bursae act as tiny shock absorbers, reducing friction at joints and pressure points throughout the body. When a bursa becomes inflamed, it swells and becomes painful, and the affected area is often tender and restricted in movement.

Bursitis most often affects the shoulder, elbow, hip, knee, and heel, and the condition is commonly named after its site -- for example, housemaid's knee (prepatellar bursitis of the knee) and student's elbow (olecranon bursitis of the elbow). Most cases are short-lived and respond well to rest, ice, and simple treatment, but bursitis can become chronic or infected and then requires medical care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bursitis is very common, particularly in people with physically demanding occupations or sports. Repeated kneeling or leaning on the elbows causes knee and elbow bursitis, reflected in the traditional names housemaid's knee and student's elbow. Shoulder and hip bursitis are common in middle-aged and older adults and are often linked to the way tendons move over bone as they age. Septic (infected) bursitis is less common and occurs most often at the elbow or knee after a break in the overlying skin. Overall, bursitis affects men and women at all ages, but the causes differ with age and activity.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Bursitis is usually caused by repeated friction, prolonged pressure, a direct injury, or an infection:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Repetitive friction:} repeated movements such as kneeling, sweeping, throwing, or climbing stairs irritate the bursa over time
    \item \textbf{Prolonged pressure:} leaning on the elbows or knees, or resting on hard surfaces
    \item \textbf{Direct injury:} a fall or knock can injure a bursa, causing bleeding and inflammation
    \item \textbf{Infection:} bacteria can enter through a cut or graze over the bursa, causing septic bursitis
    \item \textbf{Underlying joint or tendon problems:} arthritis, gout, or shoulder conditions such as rotator cuff disease can irritate adjacent bursae
    \item \textbf{Metabolic conditions:} gout and rheumatoid arthritis cause inflammation in and around joints, including bursae
\end{itemize}

A bursa normally contains a small amount of lubricating fluid. When irritated, its lining produces extra fluid, the sac swells, and the surrounding tissue becomes inflamed, producing pain and limited movement. In infection, the fluid itself may become infected and filled with pus.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A soft, sometimes tender swelling over a joint, such as the front of the knee, the tip of the elbow, or the outer hip
    \item Pain that worsens with movement, pressure, or resting on the area
    \item Redness and warmth over the swelling
    \item Stiffness or reduced range of movement of the nearby joint
    \item Pain at night or when lying on the affected side
    \item In septic bursitis: a red, hot, very tender swelling, often with fever and a recent break in the skin nearby
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cellulitis or septic arthritis:} infected tissue or joint, which is red, hot, and painful, usually with fever
    \item \textbf{Gout or pseudogout:} sudden, severe joint inflammation with intense pain and swelling
    \item \textbf{Tendonitis or tendon injury:} pain that is reproduced by specific movements
    \item \textbf{Rheumatoid arthritis or osteoarthritis flare:} joint inflammation that may coexist with bursitis
    \item \textbf{Ganglion or other benign cyst:} a firm swelling that is not usually painful
    \item \textbf{Fracture or stress fracture:} particularly after injury, with bone tenderness and pain on loading
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People should see a doctor if a joint swelling is red, hot, or very painful, if there is a fever, if the swelling follows a cut or graze, or if pain persists despite a week or two of rest and self-care.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe pain and swelling with fever and chills, which may indicate an infected joint
    \item Spreading redness, red streaks, or other signs of bloodstream infection
    \item Inability to use the joint or bear weight
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} occupation and activities, any injury or skin break, and whether the swelling developed gradually or suddenly
    \item \textbf{Examination:} palpation of the swelling, assessment of warmth and redness, and testing of joint movement
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} the best imaging test, showing the swollen bursa and its fluid content
    \item \textbf{Aspiration:} drawing fluid from the bursa to check for infection or gout, especially when septic bursitis is suspected
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} inflammatory markers and white cell count when infection is considered
    \item \textbf{X-ray:} sometimes used to exclude bone injury or underlying arthritis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Self-care:} rest the area, apply ice packs, use a soft pad or cushion when kneeling or leaning, and elevate the limb
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} paracetamol or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medicines such as ibuprofen, as directed
    \item \textbf{Compression:} a firm bandage may help reduce swelling
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} stretching and strengthening exercises to restore movement and correct the underlying strain
    \item \textbf{Injection and aspiration:} a doctor may drain the bursa or inject a small dose of corticosteroid to settle stubborn inflammation
    \item \textbf{Septic bursitis:} antibiotics and drainage of infected fluid, as prescribed by a doctor
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} rarely needed, to remove a chronically inflamed bursa that does not settle
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic bursitis that keeps recurring if the cause is not addressed
    \item Septic bursitis, an infection of the bursa requiring antibiotics and drainage
    \item Spread of infection to the bloodstream (sepsis) in untreated septic bursitis
    \item Stiffness and weakness of the nearby joint during recovery
    \item Scarring or a persisting lump after severe inflammation
    \item Side effects of treatment, such as skin thinning after repeated steroid injections
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most cases of bursitis settle within days to a few weeks with rest and simple treatment. Recurrence is common if the causative activity is not modified, for example if a worker continues kneeling without knee pads. Septic bursitis usually resolves with antibiotics and drainage provided it is treated promptly. Overall outcomes are excellent, and surgery is rarely required.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use knee pads when kneeling and padded rests or elbow pads when leaning
    \item Take regular breaks from repetitive or pressure activities
    \item Warm up before exercise and build up intensity gradually
    \item Keep muscles strong and joints flexible through regular activity
    \item Treat any cut or graze over a joint promptly and keep it clean
    \item Seek medical advice early if a swelling becomes red, hot, or painful, rather than waiting
\end{itemize}
