\chapter{Common Cold Treatment}
\index{Common Cold Treatment}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Common cold treatment refers to the clinical and self-care strategies employed to manage the symptoms of the common cold, a viral upper respiratory infection. Because there are no approved antiviral therapies that target common cold viruses directly, treatment is entirely supportive and palliative, aimed at reducing discomfort, preventing complications, and improving functional status. Effective management involves a combination of pharmacological agents, non-pharmacological therapies, and patient education to ensure safe usage, particularly in vulnerable populations such as paediatric, elderly, and pregnant patients.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The treatment of the common cold is a major driver of healthcare utilisation and consumer spending. Globally, billions of dollars are spent annually on over-the-counter (OTC) cough and cold preparations. In many countries, the average household purchases multiple OTC remedies each year. Inappropriate treatments, particularly the prescription of antibiotics for these viral infections, remain highly prevalent, contributing to global antimicrobial resistance. Furthermore, medication adverse events related to cough and cold products, especially accidental overdoses or side effects in children under 6 years of age, account for numerous emergency department presentations annually, highlighting the need for strict clinical guidelines on paediatric treatments.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The need for treatment arises from the host's inflammatory response to viral infection of the upper respiratory mucosa.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory mediator targets:} Viral replication triggers the release of bradykinin, prostaglandins, interleukins, and histamine, which cause the classic symptoms of nasal congestion, sore throat, and malaise. Symptomatic treatments target these specific pathways.
    \item \textbf{Vasodilation and congestion:} Prostaglandins and other mediators induce venous sinus engorgement in the nasal mucosa. Decongestants target alpha-adrenergic receptors to stimulate vasoconstriction and reduce swelling.
    \item \textbf{Nociceptor stimulation:} Bradykinin and prostaglandins sensitise local sensory nerve endings, causing pharyngeal pain and headache. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and paracetamol inhibit prostaglandin synthesis to alleviate this pain.
    \item \textbf{Cough reflex activation:} Post-nasal drip and direct inflammatory irritation of the laryngeal and tracheal mucosa stimulate the cough reflex. Demulcents coat the pharyngeal mucosa to reduce sensory input, while centrally acting antitussives raise the threshold of the cough centre in the brainstem.
    \item \textbf{Eustachian tube dysfunction:} Nasal mucosal swelling blocks the Eustachian tube and sinus ostia. Adequate treatment helps restore patency and mucociliary clearance, preventing secondary bacterial infections.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of patients seeking treatment is characterised by a constellation of upper respiratory symptoms, and monitoring the response to therapy is essential.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pain and discomfort:} Sore throat, headache, low-grade fever, and generalised muscle aches. Response to therapy: Analgesics and antipyretics typically reduce these within 30 to 60 minutes.
    \item \textbf{Nasal airway obstruction:} Severe congestion, rhinorrhoea, and post-nasal drip. Response to therapy: Topical decongestants provide rapid, temporary relief of airway resistance.
    \item \textbf{Coughing and throat irritation:} A dry, tickly cough that worsens at night. Response to therapy: Antitussives or soothing fluids help suppress spasms.
    \item \textbf{Adverse effects of treatment:} Drowsiness (from first-generation antihistamines), palpitations or insomnia (from oral decongestants), and rebound congestion (from overused nasal sprays).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When selecting a treatment regimen, it is critical to confirm that the symptoms are indeed due to a common cold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Allergic rhinitis:} Requires treatment with intranasal corticosteroids or non-sedating antihistamines, rather than decongestants or analgesics. Distinction: Marked by nasal pruritus, pale mucosa, and absence of systemic symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Influenza:} Requires supportive care and potentially neuraminidase inhibitors (e.g.\ oseltamivir) if started early in high-risk patients. Distinction: Severe systemic symptoms (high fever, severe myalgia).
    \item \textbf{Acute bacterial sinusitis:} May require oral antibiotics if symptoms are severe and prolonged. Distinction: Severe unilateral facial pain, purulent discharge, and "double sickening".
    \item \textbf{Streptococcal tonsillopharyngitis:} Requires antibiotic therapy (e.g.\ phenoxymethylpenicillin) to prevent rheumatic fever. Distinction: Lack of cough/coryza, tonsillar exudate, and positive throat swab.
    \item \textbf{Croup or bronchiolitis (in children):} May require systemic corticosteroids or bronchodilators, which are not used for standard colds. Distinction: Barking cough, inspiratory stridor, or wheezing.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most colds resolve with supportive home care within a week. Patients should seek medical advice if symptoms do not improve after 10 days, if they have pre-existing chronic respiratory conditions, or if they develop signs of complications.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rapid or laboured breathing, grunting, or chest wall retractions (especially in infants).
    \item Inspiratory stridor or persistent wheezing.
    \item Inability to swallow fluids, resulting in excessive drooling or severe dehydration.
    \item New-onset confusion, severe lethargy, or loss of consciousness.
    \item Gray or blue-tinted lips, tongue, or skin (cyanosis).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Investigations are rarely necessary to guide treatment of a simple common cold, but they are used to rule out other pathology or guide antiviral therapy in high-risk patients.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} Identification of typical symptoms and exclusion of signs pointing to bacterial infection or lower respiratory tract involvement.
    \item \textbf{Multiplex viral PCR swab:} Useful in clinical settings to differentiate rhinovirus from influenza, RSV, or COVID-19, particularly in patients who may benefit from specific antiviral treatments.
    \item \textbf{Pulse oximetry:} Performed if the patient has a cough or shortness of breath to ensure oxygen saturation is adequate ($>95\%$).
    \item \textbf{Throat swab culture or Rapid Antigen Test:} Used if streptococcal pharyngitis is suspected to avoid inappropriate antibiotic use for a viral cold.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is entirely symptomatic, combining pharmacological and non-pharmacological strategies tailored to the patient's age and health status.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Analgesics and antipyretics:} Paracetamol or ibuprofen to alleviate pain and fever. Aspirin must be avoided in children due to the risk of Reye's syndrome.
    \item \textbf{Nasal decongestants:} Topical nasal sprays (e.g.\ oxymetazoline or xylometazoline) for up to 3 to 5 days, or oral decongestants (e.g.\ pseudoephedrine) in adults to reduce nasal congestion. Contraindicated in patients with severe hypertension or uncontrolled cardiovascular disease.
    \item \textbf{First-generation antihistamines:} Combined with decongestants (e.g.\ phenylephrine and chlorpheniramine) to reduce rhinorrhoea and sneezing, though patients must be warned of sedative effects.
    \item \textbf{Cough remedies:} Centrally acting cough suppressants (e.g.\ dextromethorphan or pholcodine) for dry coughs. Expectorants (e.g.\ guaifenesin) are sometimes used but have limited clinical evidence of efficacy.
    \item \textbf{Paediatric restrictions:} OTC cough and cold medicines are \textbf{not recommended} for children under 6 years of age and should only be used in children aged 6 to 11 on medical advice, due to risks of toxicity.
    \item \textbf{Non-pharmacological therapies:} Saline nasal sprays or rinses, steam inhalation, and adequate fluid intake. Honey (for children over 12 months) has been shown to reduce cough frequency and severity.
    \item \textbf{Complementary therapies:} Zinc lozenges, Vitamin C, and Echinacea are popular, but evidence shows they provide at most a modest reduction in cold duration if started at the immediate onset of symptoms.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rhinitis medicamentosa:} Rebound nasal congestion caused by the overuse of topical decongestant sprays for more than 5 consecutive days.
    \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal distress:} Dyspepsia, nausea, or abdominal pain from overuse of NSAIDs (e.g.\ ibuprofen).
    \item \textbf{Accidental overdose:} Toxicity from taking multiple over-the-counter products containing the same active ingredients (e.g.\ paracetamol in both cold/flu tablets and separate analgesics).
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular stimulation:} Tachycardia, palpitations, and elevated blood pressure caused by oral sympathomimetic decongestants like pseudoephedrine.
    \item \textbf{Urinary retention:} Can occur in older men with benign prostatic hyperplasia who take antihistamines or decongestants.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With supportive treatment, the symptoms of the common cold typically peak on days 2 to 4 and resolve completely within 7 to 10 days. The cough may persist as a post-viral cough for up to 3 to 4 weeks. Treatment does not cure the infection but significantly reduces discomfort and improves quality of life. The prognosis is excellent, and normal functional capacity is restored quickly, provided that patients adhere to safe dosage guidelines.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Safe medication storage:} Store all OTC cold medicines out of sight and reach of children to prevent accidental ingestion.
    \item \textbf{Check active ingredients:} Carefully read labels on multi-symptom cold relief products to avoid doubling up on paracetamol or antihistamines.
    \item \textbf{Use honey for soothing:} Consuming warm water or tea with honey to soothe a throat and suppress coughs (only in individuals over 1 year of age).
    \item \textbf{Hydration:} Drink plenty of water, broth, or warm herbal teas to keep mucosal membranes moist and thin out mucus.
    \item \textbf{Proper dosing tools:} Always use the syringe or cup provided with liquid paediatric medications rather than a household spoon to ensure accurate dosing.
\end{itemize}
