\chapter{Intermittent Fasting}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Intermittent fasting is a way of eating that alternates periods of fasting with periods of normal eating, rather than restricting particular foods. Common patterns include the 16:8 method, in which all food is eaten within an eight-hour window each day, and the 5:2 method, in which a normal diet is eaten for five days and intake is restricted to about 2000 to 2500 kilojoules on two non-consecutive days. Intermittent fasting can help with weight loss by reducing overall energy intake, and research suggests it may also improve blood sugar, blood pressure, and cholesterol. It is safe for many people, but it is not suitable for everyone, including pregnant women, people with diabetes on certain medicines, and people with a history of eating disorders.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Interest in intermittent fasting has grown rapidly, and it is now one of the most popular approaches to weight management in Australia and worldwide. Around one in ten Australians attempting weight loss has tried intermittent fasting, and many report weight loss and health benefits. Obesity affects around two-thirds of Australian adults, driving demand for effective and sustainable approaches. Clinical trials show that intermittent fasting produces weight loss comparable to traditional daily calorie restriction in the short to medium term, though long-term data are still emerging, and adherence varies between individuals.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Intermittent fasting works primarily by creating an overall reduction in energy intake. During the fasting period, the body uses up its stored glucose (glycogen) and then shifts to burning fat, producing ketones as an alternative fuel. This metabolic switch is accompanied by lower insulin levels, improved insulin sensitivity, and changes in hormones that regulate appetite, including reduced ghrelin. Fasting also triggers cellular processes associated with repair and longevity in animal studies. The weight loss achieved depends on the energy deficit created, so the eating windows must not simply be used to eat more. Individual responses vary, and the pattern that a person can sustain matters more than the specific method.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Restricted eating windows, such as eating between 12 noon and 8 pm
    \item Fasting days with limited kilojoule intake, as in the 5:2 method
    \item Water, tea, and black coffee are allowed during fasting periods
    \item Hunger, especially in the first weeks as the body adapts
    \item Weight loss, typically 2 to 5 per cent of body weight over several months
    \item Improvements in blood sugar, blood pressure, and cholesterol in some people
    \item Possible fatigue, headaches, or irritability early on
    \item Some people find fasting easier to sustain than daily calorie counting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Choosing an eating approach should be matched to the individual's health and goals.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Daily calorie restriction:} a traditional, flexible approach
    \item \textbf{Mediterranean and DASH diets:} emphasising food quality rather than timing
    \item \textbf{Low-carbohydrate diets:} for people who find carbohydrate restriction effective
    \item \textbf{Commercial weight-loss programs:} structured support and accountability
    \item \textbf{Medicines for weight loss:} options for people with medical obesity
    \item \textbf{Bariatric surgery:} for severe obesity with complications
    \item \textbf{Medical conditions:} diabetes, pregnancy, and eating disorders need individualised advice
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with medical conditions, including diabetes, heart disease, kidney disease, or a history of eating disorders, should discuss intermittent fasting with a doctor before starting. Pregnant or breastfeeding women and children should not fast. Anyone who develops concerning symptoms, loses weight unintentionally, or struggles with the approach should seek advice. Regular review helps ensure that fasting remains safe and effective.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fainting, collapse, or severe confusion during fasting
    \item Very low blood sugar symptoms that do not respond to treatment in people with diabetes
    \item Chest pain or breathlessness
    \item Severe dehydration or inability to keep fluids down
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Health assessment:} weight, blood pressure, blood glucose, and relevant blood tests
    \item \textbf{Review of medicines:} some diabetes and blood pressure medicines require adjustment with fasting
    \item \textbf{Discussion of goals:} realistic expectations for weight and health
    \item \textbf{Nutritional review:} ensuring nutrient needs are met within eating windows
    \item \textbf{Screening for eating disorders:} important before recommending any restriction
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular weight and health checks while fasting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Choosing a pattern:} matching the method to lifestyle and preferences
    \item \textbf{Gradual start:} easing in with a shorter fast and building up
    \item \textbf{Fluid intake:} water, tea, and coffee during fasting periods
    \item \textbf{Nutritious meals:} emphasising protein, fibre, vegetables, and whole foods in eating windows
    \item \textbf{Avoiding overeating:} not compensating by eating more during eating periods
    \item \textbf{Physical activity:} continued exercise, timed sensibly around meals
    \item \textbf{Review and adjustment:} changing the pattern if it is not working or causing problems
    \item \textbf{Medical adjustment:} modifying medicines under medical advice when needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hunger, irritability, headaches, and poor concentration in the early weeks
    \item Low blood sugar in people with diabetes taking certain medicines
    \item Dehydration if fluid intake is neglected
    \item Nutrient deficiencies if eating windows contain poor-quality food
    \item Overeating on non-fasting days, negating the energy deficit
    \item Disordered eating in vulnerable people
    \item Reduced energy and performance in some athletes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Intermittent fasting is an effective weight-loss tool for many people in the short and medium term, with benefits for blood sugar and cardiovascular risk factors in those who lose weight. Its success depends on sustainability: people who find a pattern that fits their life tend to maintain results, while those who find fasting stressful are better served by another approach. Long-term data are still developing, but a healthy dietary pattern with adequate nutrition remains the foundation of lasting weight management.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discuss fasting with a doctor if you have any medical condition or take medicines
    \item Never fast during pregnancy or breastfeeding, or if you are a child or adolescent
    \item Choose a pattern you can sustain long term
    \item Prioritise protein, fibre, and vegetables in your eating window
    \item Drink plenty of water, tea, or coffee during fasting periods
    \item Eat slowly and stop when comfortably full
    \item Keep a food and fasting diary to stay accountable
    \item Seek support from a dietitian for individualised guidance
    \item Stop and seek advice if fasting causes distress or disordered eating
\end{itemize}
