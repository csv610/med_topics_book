\chapter{Friedreich's Ataxia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Friedreich's ataxia (FA) is a rare, inherited, progressive neurological disorder that damages the spinal cord, peripheral nerves, and the cerebellum, the part of the brain that coordinates movement. It typically begins in childhood or adolescence with unsteadiness when walking, which gradually worsens, and it also affects speech, coordination of the hands, and, importantly, the heart, causing cardiomyopathy in many people. Friedreich's ataxia is caused by a mutation in the FXN gene, which leads to a deficiency of a protein called frataxin, important for the energy-producing parts of cells. There is no cure, but the condition is managed with rehabilitation, treatment of heart and other complications, and emerging medicines. This chapter explains the condition, its features, and its management.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Friedreich's ataxia is the most common inherited ataxia, affecting around 1 in 50,000 people in Australia and worldwide, with roughly one person in a hundred carrying the faulty gene. Symptoms usually begin between ages 5 and 15, though some people have later onset. Around two-thirds of people with FA develop cardiomyopathy, which is the most common cause of death, and many also develop diabetes. Life expectancy is reduced, with most people living into their 30s to 50s, though outcomes vary. Advances in care, especially for the heart, are improving survival.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The FXN gene:} provides instructions for making frataxin, a protein concentrated in mitochondria, the energy-producing structures of cells
    \item \textbf{The mutation:} an expanded GAA repeat in the gene reduces frataxin production
    \item \textbf{Inheritance:} autosomal recessive; a child must inherit a faulty gene from both parents, who are usually unaffected carriers
    \item \textbf{Effects of frataxin deficiency:} mitochondria malfunction, iron accumulates abnormally in cells, and energy production fails, damaging tissues with high energy demands
    \item \textbf{The nervous system:} degeneration of the spinal cord tracts and peripheral nerves causes loss of coordination, sensation, and reflexes
    \item \textbf{The heart:} the heart muscle thickens and weakens (hypertrophic cardiomyopathy)
    \item \textbf{Other systems:} the pancreas can fail, causing diabetes; scoliosis and foot deformity are common
    \item \textbf{Progression:} the disorder is gradually progressive, with the rate varying between people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ataxia: unsteadiness and difficulty walking, which begins in childhood or adolescence and progressively worsens
    \item Clumsiness of the hands, tremor, and difficulty with fine movements
    \item Slurred, slow speech (dysarthria)
    \item Loss of sensation in the limbs, with absent ankle reflexes
    \item Poor balance and coordination, with frequent falls
    \item Scoliosis, foot deformity (pes cavus), and muscle weakness
    \item Heart problems: palpitations, breathlessness, and heart failure from cardiomyopathy
    \item Diabetes in a substantial proportion
    \item Visual and hearing problems in some people
    \item By adulthood, many people need a wheelchair, though the course is highly variable
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of childhood and adult ataxia must be considered.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Other inherited ataxias, including spinocerebellar ataxias and ataxia-telangiectasia
    \item Other causes of cerebellar disease, including tumours and multiple sclerosis
    \item Metabolic and mitochondrial disorders
    \item Vitamin E deficiency, which causes a similar ataxia and is treatable
    \item Cerebral palsy and other developmental conditions in children
    \item Genetic testing for the FXN gene confirms the diagnosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with progressive unsteadiness, clumsiness, or a family history of ataxia should be assessed by a doctor, with referral to a neurologist. Early diagnosis allows monitoring of the heart and other complications.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, severe breathlessness, or palpitations with fainting, which may indicate heart problems
    \item A fall with head injury
    \item Signs of a stroke or sudden neurological change
    \item High blood sugar with confusion or collapse in diabetes
    \item Any emergency symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} the combination of ataxia, absent reflexes, and sensory loss in a child or adolescent is characteristic
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} a blood test for the FXN repeat confirms the diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Neurological studies:} nerve conduction studies confirm peripheral neuropathy
    \item \textbf{Cardiac assessment:} echocardiogram and ECG to detect cardiomyopathy, repeated regularly
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} glucose and HbA1c for diabetes, and vitamin E levels to exclude treatable causes
    \item \textbf{Spinal imaging:} MRI to exclude other causes of ataxia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and speech therapy maintain function and manage symptoms
    \item \textbf{Mobility aids:} walking frames and wheelchairs, prescribed at the right time to maintain independence and safety
    \item \textbf{Heart care:} cardiology review, medicines for cardiomyopathy and arrhythmias, and monitoring
    \item \textbf{Diabetes management:} standard diabetes care, including medicines and monitoring
    \item \textbf{Scoliosis care:} bracing and, in some cases, surgery
    \item \textbf{Swallowing and nutrition:} speech and dietetic input, with feeding support if swallowing deteriorates
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} omaveloxolone is approved in some countries to slow progression; other treatments target symptoms such as spasticity and tremor
    \item \textbf{Emotional and practical support:} psychology, social work, and NDIS supports
    \item \textbf{Research participation:} clinical trials of therapies aimed at restoring frataxin are ongoing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, heart failure, and arrhythmias, the most serious complications
    \item Diabetes and its complications
    \item Falls and injuries
    \item Scoliosis and foot deformities
    \item Difficulty swallowing, with choking and aspiration
    \item Progressive disability requiring full-time care
    \item Reduced life expectancy, mainly from heart disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Friedreich's ataxia is a progressive condition, and most people require a wheelchair within 10--20 years of onset, though the pace varies widely. Survival into adulthood is the norm, with many people living into their 30s to 50s, and the most common cause of death is heart disease. Monitoring and treatment of the heart, diabetes, and swallowing problems improve survival and quality of life. Research is advancing rapidly, and therapies targeting frataxin deficiency offer hope of slowing the disease. With comprehensive care, people with FA live active, meaningful lives.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek genetic testing and counselling in families with the condition or a history of unexplained ataxia
    \item Attend cardiology reviews regularly, as heart problems can be silent
    \item Have regular diabetes screening
    \item Stay active with physiotherapy to maintain strength and balance
    \item Use mobility aids early enough to prevent falls
    \item Maintain good nutrition and seek swallowing review early
    \item Access NDIS supports, psychology, and patient organisations
    \item Discuss clinical trial options with the neurologist
\end{itemize}
