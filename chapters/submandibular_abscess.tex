\section*{Definition and Overview}
A submandibular abscess is a collection of pus that forms in the tissues beneath the floor of the mouth and under the jaw, in the submandibular space. It is a serious complication of dental infection and of infections of the tissues of the face and throat. The submandibular space is one of several potential spaces in the neck through which infection can spread, and an abscess here is important because it sits close to the airway. If it enlarges, it can push the tongue upward and backward, block the throat, and make breathing difficult or impossible. It is therefore managed as a potentially life-threatening emergency, even though it begins, in most cases, as an ordinary tooth infection.

Most submandibular abscesses arise from the lower molar teeth, whose roots sit immediately above this space, or from infections of the submandibular lymph nodes. Pus accumulates, the area becomes swollen, painful, and tender, and the person may develop fever and difficulty swallowing. Left untreated, the infection can spread downwards through the neck into the chest, and the airway can be compromised. This chapter explains how these abscesses form, who is at risk, how they are recognised, and how they are treated, with emphasis on the need for prompt care. Understanding the warning signs matters because the difference between a routine dental infection and a dangerous neck space infection is measured in hours.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Submandibular abscesses are among the commoner deep neck space infections, and they are seen most often in association with dental disease. In many countries and other developed countries, access to dental care has made some of the older, dramatic presentations less common, but significant numbers of people still present to emergency departments each year, particularly those with limited access to dental treatment. Deep neck space infections are somewhat more common in men, in adults in their middle decades, and in people with poor dental health, diabetes, or a weakened immune system. Children develop them too, often from infection of the lower back teeth or from an infected node draining a throat or tooth infection.

The broader significance lies in the trend: dental caries remains one of the most common diseases worldwide, and where it is untreated it can progress to facial swelling and space infection. The condition also carries substantial costs, in terms of emergency admissions, surgery, time off work or study, and the risk of severe complications, which makes prevention and early dental care genuinely important community health priorities. Populations with poorer oral health, lower socioeconomic status, and barriers to dental care carry a disproportionate share of these infections. The incidence of severe complications has fallen with antibiotic availability and emergency dental care, but submandibular abscesses still require admission to hospital in many cases, and they remain a leading cause of dental emergencies in hospitals across some countries.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The submandibular space lies below the floor of the mouth and is divided into a sublingual portion above the mylohyoid muscle and a submaxillary portion below it. The roots of the lower molar teeth are close to the upper (sublingual) part of this space, which explains why infection of these teeth is the most common source of a submandibular abscess. Infection can also reach the space through the lymph nodes that drain the teeth, gums, tongue, and face, and, less commonly, from infected salivary glands, skin infections, or after injury.

Once bacteria enter the space, the body's immune response produces inflammation, tissue breakdown, and pus, and the collection is contained by the layers of the neck fascia. If the pus is not drained, pressure rises, and the infection spreads along the fascial planes into adjacent spaces, including the airway region and, downward, toward the chest. Mixed aerobic and anaerobic bacteria are typically involved, reflecting the bacterial population of the mouth.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dental infection:} decay or infection of a lower back tooth, with the infection tracking through the bone and into the submandibular space
    \item \textbf{Lymph node infection:} the submandibular nodes enlarge and suppurate while fighting off a tooth, throat, or mouth infection
    \item \textbf{Salivary gland infection:} infection of the submandibular gland, sometimes with a blocked duct and a stone
    \item \textbf{Oral and facial infections:} spread from tonsils, the floor of the mouth, or an infected tongue
    \item \textbf{Skin trauma:} infection entering through a wound or an injection site in the area
    \item \textbf{Poorly controlled diabetes and immune compromise:} increase susceptibility and make infection harder to contain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Swelling under the jaw:} a tender, sometimes fluctuant swelling on one or both sides, just below the angle of the jaw
    \item \textbf{Pain:} aching or throbbing pain, worse with chewing, swallowing, or opening the mouth
    \item \textbf{Fever:} raised temperature with general malaise and feeling unwell
    \item \textbf{Difficulty swallowing:} painful swallowing, with drooling in severe cases
    \item \textbf{Difficulty opening the mouth:} trismus, or spasm of the jaw muscles, limiting mouth opening
    \item \textbf{Dental symptoms:} toothache, a loose tooth, or evidence of gross dental decay on the affected side
    \item \textbf{Neck stiffness:} pain and resistance when moving the neck, suggesting spreading infection
    \item \textbf{Redness and warmth:} the skin over the swelling may be red and hot
    \item \textbf{Later signs of airway compromise:} a muffled or changed voice, difficulty breathing, and the tongue pushed upward
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Submandibular sialadenitis:} infection of the submandibular gland, often with a stone, causing pain and swelling that worsens at mealtimes; usually less severe than an abscess and confirmed with ultrasound
    \item \textbf{Enlarged lymph nodes:} reactive or infected nodes from throat or dental infection enlarge and are tender but are firmer and mobile rather than fluctuant
    \item \textbf{Ludwig's angina:} a rapidly spreading infection of the whole floor of the mouth that causes both sides to swell, protrudes the tongue, and threatens the airway; treated with the same urgency as an abscess
    \item \textbf{Submandibular gland tumour:} a slow, painless swelling of the gland, without fever or tenderness, developing over months
    \item \textbf{Branchial cyst:} a congenital cyst in the neck that can become infected and enlarge, typically presenting in younger adults with no dental disease
    \item \textbf{Thyroglossal cyst:} a midline neck swelling, above the submandibular region, that moves when the tongue is protruded
    \item \textbf{Tonsillar or peritonsillar abscess:} infection higher in the throat causing sore throat, trismus, and a muffled voice, with swelling around the tonsil
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with a tooth infection who develops swelling under the jaw, fever, or difficulty swallowing should be assessed promptly by a general practitioner, a dentist, or an emergency department. Early treatment with antibiotics and dental review often resolves the infection before an abscess forms.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing or a feeling that the throat is closing
    \item A muffled or "hot potato" voice with drooling
    \item Rapidly enlarging swelling that spreads to both sides of the neck or upward into the face
    \item Inability to open the mouth, or high fever with confusion and feeling seriously unwell
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the clinician checks for dental disease, assesses the swelling for tenderness and fluctuance, examines the mouth and throat, and looks for trismus and airway signs
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} a full blood count and inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein help gauge the severity of the infection
    \item \textbf{Computed tomography (CT) scan:} the most useful imaging test; it confirms the abscess, shows its extent and depth, and reveals spread into adjacent neck spaces
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} used for superficial collections, especially in children, and to distinguish an abscess from simple swelling
    \item \textbf{Microbiology:} pus drained at surgery or aspirated is sent for culture to identify the bacteria and guide antibiotic choice
    \item \textbf{Dental imaging:} X-rays or panoramic imaging of the teeth identify the source tooth
    \item \textbf{Specialist referral:} ear, nose, and throat (ENT) surgeons and oral and maxillofacial surgeons manage confirmed abscesses
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Intravenous antibiotics:} broad-spectrum antibiotics covering the mixed mouth bacteria are started immediately, then adjusted to culture results
    \item \textbf{Surgical drainage:} the definitive treatment; the pus is drained under anaesthesia through a neck incision, and a drain is often left in place for a day or two
    \item \textbf{Source control:} the responsible tooth is treated, by extraction or root canal, either at the same operation or as a planned follow-up
    \item \textbf{Airway protection:} severe cases may require securing the airway before surgery, with an awake breathing tube or a tracheostomy, because pushing down the tongue can obstruct breathing during anaesthesia
    \item \textbf{Pain management:} regular analgesia, along with fluids and nutrition, often given intravenously until swallowing recovers
    \item \textbf{Oral hygiene and mouth care:} warm saline rinses and gentle mouth care support healing after drainage
    \item \textbf{Review of underlying health:} blood glucose control in diabetes and investigation of immune function if infections recur
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Airway obstruction:} the most feared complication; an enlarging abscess pushes the tongue back and can suffocate the person
    \item \textbf{Spread of infection:} tracking downward into the neck and chest causes mediastinitis, a life-threatening infection around the heart and lungs
    \item \textbf{Septicaemia:} bacteria entering the bloodstream cause severe sepsis with organ failure
    \item \textbf{Blood vessel complications:} the internal jugular vein can become infected and thrombosed
    \item \textbf{Nerve injury:} surgical drainage carries a small risk to the nerves near the space, including the nerve to the corner of the mouth and the tongue nerve
    \item \textbf{Recurrence:} if the source tooth is not treated or the pus is incompletely drained, the abscess can reform
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt recognition, antibiotics, and surgical drainage, the outlook for submandibular abscess is excellent, and most people make a full recovery within one to two weeks. The key factors determining outcome are the speed of treatment and the presence of complications at presentation. Patients who present early with a limited collection typically settle quickly with surgery and a short course of antibiotics. Those who present with airway compromise or spread into the chest have a much more serious illness that requires intensive care, but even then modern surgery, antibiotics, and respiratory support give the majority a good outcome. Long-term recovery depends on dealing with the underlying dental problem, because the risk of recurrence is otherwise substantial. Improving dental care, treating diabetes well, and seeking help early all reduce the chance of ever reaching this stage.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain good dental hygiene with regular brushing and flossing to prevent tooth decay
    \item See a dentist regularly, and treat dental decay and toothache early rather than waiting
    \item Do not delay seeking care for an infected or painful tooth, especially if you have diabetes or a weakened immune system
    \item If prescribed antibiotics for a dental infection, finish the full course as directed
    \item Keep good control of diabetes and other conditions that impair immunity
    \item After treatment, keep the wound clean and attend follow-up appointments for the source tooth
    \item Drink plenty of fluids and maintain good nutrition while recovering, and seek review promptly if swelling or pain recurs
\end{itemize}
