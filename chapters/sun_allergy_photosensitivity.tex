\section*{Definition and Overview}
Photosensitivity, sometimes loosely called "sun allergy", is an abnormal skin reaction to sunlight or other sources of ultraviolet (UV) radiation. In a normal response, sunlight causes tanning or sunburn, but in a photosensitive person the skin reacts with rashes, redness, itching, or blistering after a level of exposure that other people would tolerate easily. The word "allergy" is something of a simplification, because only some of these reactions are true allergies; many involve other mechanisms, including direct skin damage, immune reactions, and reactions triggered by chemicals in medicines or on the skin. All share one feature: the skin's response to light is abnormal and causes symptoms.

Photosensitivity matters in many countries more than almost anywhere else, because people live under some of the strongest UV radiation on earth. A reaction to the sun is not only uncomfortable; it can be mistaken for other skin conditions, limit outdoor life, and cause distress. Some forms are lifelong and require permanent sun avoidance, while others are caused by medicines or skin products and resolve when the cause is removed. This chapter explains the different types of photosensitivity, who gets them, how they are recognised, and how they are treated and prevented. It also covers the crucial point that a new sensitivity to sunlight can be a sign of an underlying illness, including lupus, and deserves proper assessment rather than self-treatment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Photosensitivity disorders are common, though their true frequency is hard to measure because many people never seek medical care. The most common form, called polymorphous light eruption, affects a substantial minority of people in temperate countries, and its prevalence is higher in northern European populations and in women, who may account for around three-quarters of cases. It typically begins in early adulthood and often improves with age. Solar urticaria, a rarer form in which hives appear within minutes of sun exposure, affects a much smaller number of people. Other photosensitive conditions are linked to medicines: many prescription drugs, from antibiotics to blood-pressure tablets and anti-inflammatory medicines, can make the skin reactive to sunlight, and the resulting rashes are a recognised and preventable side effect.

Photosensitivity is common in people with fair skin and a northern European background, but it can affect anyone, and it overlaps with other light-sensitive conditions. In many countries, sun-related skin disease is a major public health concern overall, though the photosensitivity disorders themselves are a distinct group. People with lupus, a condition in which UV light can trigger skin and systemic flares, form an important minority, and their management depends on scrupulous sun protection.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The skin reacts to light when UV radiation is absorbed by molecules in the skin and triggers a chain of events leading to inflammation. In photosensitive people, this chain is abnormally sensitive or abnormal in type. The exact mechanism differs between the forms: in polymorphous light eruption, it appears that the immune system reacts against skin proteins that have been altered by UV exposure, producing an itchy, bumpy rash; in solar urticaria, an immediate allergic-type reaction releases histamine and causes hives within minutes; and in phototoxic reactions, the culprit is usually a medicine or chemical that, when activated by UV light, directly damages skin cells, rather like a severe sunburn.

Photoallergic reactions are the true allergies: the UV light changes the structure of a chemical on the skin, and the immune system then treats the altered chemical as a foreign invader, producing an eczematous rash. The responsible chemical may be in a perfume, sunscreen, soap, or plant sap. Finally, some photosensitivity is part of systemic disease: in lupus and other connective tissue diseases, UV light triggers inflammation of the skin and sometimes of internal organs. In every form, the UV dose, the wavelength of light, and the individual's sensitivity determine whether, where, and how severely the reaction appears.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Polymorphous light eruption:} the common "sun allergy", an itchy bumpy or blistered rash appearing hours after UV exposure on the chest, arms, and legs
    \item \textbf{Solar urticaria:} hives that develop within minutes of sun exposure and fade within hours
    \item \textbf{Phototoxic drug reactions:} sunburn-like reactions caused by medicines such as some antibiotics, diuretics, and anti-inflammatory drugs when combined with UV
    \item \textbf{Photoallergic reactions:} eczematous rashes caused by chemicals in sunscreens, perfumes, soaps, or plants being altered by UV light
    \item \textbf{Lupus and connective tissue disease:} UV-triggered skin rashes, including the butterfly rash of lupus, in people with these autoimmune conditions
    \item \textbf{Chronic actinic dermatitis:} a rare, severe, persistent photosensitivity affecting mainly older people, sometimes triggered by allergy to sunscreens or plants
    \item \textbf{Inherited disorders:} rare genetic conditions such as xeroderma pigmentosum, in which UV damage cannot be repaired and skin cancers develop from childhood
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Itchy bumps and blisters:} the classic rash of polymorphous light eruption, appearing hours to days after exposure on areas the sun has reached, most often the chest, upper arms, and legs
    \item \textbf{Redness and burning:} resembling sunburn, seen in phototoxic reactions and in some drug reactions, often in a clear pattern matching the sun-exposed skin
    \item \textbf{Hives:} raised, itchy, red welts appearing within minutes in solar urticaria and settling within a few hours
    \item \textbf{Eczema-like patches:} scaly, itchy plaques in photoallergic reactions, often localised to the site where the offending chemical was applied
    \item \textbf{Rash confined to exposed skin:} the distribution following sun-exposed areas, sparing protected skin, is a key clue, often with a spring or summer pattern
    \item \textbf{Facial rash in lupus:} a red rash across the cheeks and nose, often triggered by sunlight, together with fatigue, joint pain, and other systemic symptoms
    \item \textbf{Recurrence:} symptoms returning each time the person is exposed to sufficient UV, with each form having its own timing of onset after exposure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sunburn:} simple overexposure to UV causes redness, pain, and later peeling, but without the bumpy rash or the sensitivity to modest exposure
    \item \textbf{Contact dermatitis:} a rash from a skincare product, plant, or fabric, which can resemble photoallergy but appears regardless of sun exposure
    \item \textbf{Eczema and atopic dermatitis:} itchy patches in typical sites such as elbow creases and knees, without the sun-related distribution
    \item \textbf{Psoriasis:} scaly plaques on knees, elbows, and scalp; sunlight often improves rather than worsens it
    \item \textbf{Pityriasis rosea:} a self-limiting rash of oval patches on the trunk that can be mistaken for polymorphous light eruption
    \item \textbf{Viral exanthems:} childhood viral rashes can mimic a sun rash in timing and appearance
    \item \textbf{Urticaria from other causes:} hives from food, drugs, or stress are not specifically triggered by sunlight, unlike solar urticaria
    \item \textbf{Systemic lupus and other autoimmune disease:} skin signs are the visible part of a broader illness requiring blood tests and rheumatology assessment
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who develops a rash after sun exposure should have it assessed, because the appearance alone does not reliably distinguish a harmless "sun allergy" from a drug reaction, an underlying autoimmune condition, or early skin cancer. See a doctor promptly if the rash is severe, painful, or widespread, if it appears after starting a new medicine, or if it is accompanied by fatigue, joint pain, fever, or other symptoms suggesting lupus.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A severe reaction with blistering over a large area, or signs of dehydration from extensive skin damage
    \item Swelling of the face, lips, or throat, difficulty breathing, or wheezing, which may indicate a severe allergic reaction
    \item Dizziness, collapse, or a widespread hives rash after sun exposure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the timing of the rash after sun exposure, its distribution, the products and medicines used, and the person's overall health are the foundation of diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Phototesting:} controlled exposure of small areas of skin to UV light in a specialist dermatology department reproduces the reaction and helps characterise the sensitivity
    \item \textbf{Photopatch testing:} testing the skin's reaction to products applied with and without UV light identifies photoallergic triggers such as sunscreens and perfumes
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} tests for lupus and other autoimmune conditions are arranged when systemic disease is suspected
    \item \textbf{Skin biopsy:} a small sample of skin examined under a microscope can confirm the type of reaction and exclude other conditions
    \item \textbf{Review of medicines:} a thorough check of all current medicines, including over-the-counter products, identifies possible photosensitising drugs
    \item \textbf{Allergy testing:} occasionally used for suspected allergic components of the reaction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sun protection:} the mainstay of management; broad-spectrum high-SPF sunscreen, protective clothing, hats, and sunglasses, with careful avoidance of peak UV hours
    \item \textbf{Gradual UV tolerance:} for polymorphous light eruption, gradual, carefully graded exposure in early spring, under medical guidance, can help some people build tolerance
    \item \textbf{Topical corticosteroids:} creams applied to the rash reduce itching and inflammation in mild to moderate reactions
    \item \textbf{Antihistamines:} helpful particularly in solar urticaria and itchy forms, though they treat symptoms rather than the sensitivity itself
    \item \textbf{Phototherapy:} specialist UV treatment under supervision can desensitise the skin in some severe forms
    \item \textbf{Treating the cause:} stopping or changing a photosensitising medicine, with the prescriber's advice, resolves most drug-induced reactions
    \item \textbf{Management of underlying disease:} people with lupus need coordinated care, including medicines such as hydroxychloroquine, from a rheumatologist or dermatologist
    \item \textbf{Stronger treatments for severe cases:} oral corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, or specialised agents are used for severe persistent photosensitivity under specialist care
    \item \textbf{Patient education:} understanding the condition, its triggers, and how to live well with it is essential, especially in many countries's high-UV climate
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chronic skin damage:} repeated reactions add to the long-term UV load on the skin, and photosensitive skin often cannot protect itself by tanning, increasing the risk of premature ageing and skin cancer
    \item \textbf{Skin infection:} broken blistered or scratched skin can become infected
    \item \textbf{Scarring:} severe blistering reactions can scar, particularly if scratched or infected
    \item \textbf{Psychological impact:} the need to avoid sun and the visibility of rashes can cause anxiety and reduced quality of life, especially in younger people
    \item \textbf{Missed systemic disease:} if lupus or another autoimmune condition is not recognised, the underlying illness progresses untreated, with risks to joints, kidneys, and other organs
    \item \textbf{Severe allergic reactions:} uncommon, but generalised urticaria or angioedema can be serious
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the form of photosensitivity. Polymorphous light eruption, the commonest type, often improves spontaneously over years, and many people find it bothers them less as they age, while others manage it well with sun protection and the spring tolerance-building described above. Drug-induced photosensitivity resolves completely once the offending medicine is stopped, provided the change is made with the prescriber's guidance. Solar urticaria and the rarer severe forms, such as chronic actinic dermatitis, tend to persist and need long-term specialist care, but modern treatments help most people control their symptoms and maintain a normal life. For people with lupus, the prognosis is that of the underlying disease, but rigorous sun protection substantially reduces skin flares and can limit the disease's activity overall. In all forms, the combination of accurate diagnosis, avoidance of triggers, and good sun protection gives most people an excellent quality of life, even in many countries's harsh sunlight.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Check the UV index daily and limit time outdoors when it is three or higher, which in most of some countries are most of the year
    \item Use broad-spectrum SPF50+ sunscreen generously and reapply every two hours and after swimming or sweating
    \item Wear sun-protective clothing, a wide-brimmed hat, and sunglasses, and seek shade rather than relying on sunscreen alone
    \item Keep a diary of rashes and the products, medicines, and exposures that preceded them, to help your doctor find the trigger
    \item If you start a new medicine, ask your doctor or pharmacist whether it increases sun sensitivity
    \item Choose sunscreens labelled for sensitive skin if you suspect your sunscreen itself is causing a reaction, and consider testing products on a small area first
    \item Manage spring exposure gradually rather than going from winter into full sun suddenly
    \item Moisturise and soothe affected skin with cool compresses and fragrance-free products during a flare
    \item See your GP early for a new or worsening reaction, and do not ignore a facial rash that could be lupus
\end{itemize}
