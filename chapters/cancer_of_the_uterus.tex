\chapter{Cancer of the Uterus}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Cancer of the uterus is a malignant growth that begins in the tissues of the womb. The vast majority of cases start in the lining of the uterus, the endometrium, and are known as \textbf{endometrial cancer}. A smaller number arise in the muscular wall of the womb and are known as uterine sarcomas, which behave differently and are treated differently.

Endometrial cancer tends to grow slowly and is often detected early because it typically causes abnormal vaginal bleeding. When found at an early stage, the outlook is generally very good. Treatment is planned by a multidisciplinary team and usually combines surgery with radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or hormone therapy according to the type and stage of the disease.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cancer of the uterus is one of the most common gynaecological cancers, and its incidence has been rising in many countries, in part reflecting increasing rates of obesity. It most often affects women after the menopause, with most diagnoses occurring between the ages of 55 and 75, although younger women can also be affected. Obesity, advancing age, and a longer lifetime exposure to oestrogen are all linked to higher rates of the disease. The overall risk by age 75 is roughly 1 in 100 women.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Most uterine cancers develop when the lining of the uterus is exposed to unopposed oestrogen, that is, oestrogen not balanced by progesterone. This can occur with:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Obesity:} body fat converts other hormones into oestrogen, raising its blood level
    \item \textbf{Polycystic ovary syndrome} or irregular ovulation, which reduces progesterone exposure
    \item \textbf{Hormone replacement therapy} that contains oestrogen without a progestogen
    \item \textbf{Late menopause or early first period}, which extend lifetime oestrogen exposure
    \item \textbf{Tamoxifen}, taken as breast cancer treatment
\end{itemize}

Under prolonged oestrogen stimulation the endometrial cells proliferate, and mutations can accumulate, leading first to abnormal overgrowth (endometrial hyperplasia) and then to cancer. A smaller group of tumours, including some high-grade and serous types, develops through different molecular pathways and is less clearly linked to oestrogen.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The most common symptom of uterine cancer is abnormal vaginal bleeding:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bleeding after the menopause}, however light or brief
    \item \textbf{Bleeding between periods} or unusually heavy periods in younger women
    \item A watery, pink, or blood-stained vaginal discharge
    \item Pelvic pain or pressure, especially with more advanced disease
    \item Pain during intercourse
    \item Unexplained weight loss, fatigue, or anaemia in advanced cases
\end{itemize}

Most cases are diagnosed early because the bleeding prompts a woman to see a doctor.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Several benign conditions can cause symptoms similar to uterine cancer:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Endometrial hyperplasia, an overgrowth of the uterine lining
    \item Endometrial polyps
    \item Uterine fibroids, which are benign muscle tumours
    \item Atrophic vaginitis or post-menopausal thinning of the tissues
    \item Cervical cancer or cervical polyps
    \item Infection of the cervix or pelvic organs
    \item Bleeding related to hormone treatment or the transition to menopause
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any vaginal bleeding after the menopause should be assessed by a doctor promptly, as should bleeding between periods or a significant change in menstrual pattern in a woman over 40:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding that recurs or persists after the menopause
    \item Unexpected bleeding in a woman taking tamoxifen
    \item Pelvic pain or pressure that is worsening
    \item Persistent watery or blood-stained discharge
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heavy, uncontrolled vaginal bleeding with faintness or dizziness
    \item Severe abdominal or pelvic pain
    \item Signs of infection or sepsis, such as high fever, chills, or feeling severely unwell
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pelvic examination:} to feel for masses and inspect the cervix and vagina
    \item \textbf{Transvaginal ultrasound:} to measure the thickness of the uterine lining and look for growths
    \item \textbf{Endometrial biopsy:} sampling of the lining in the consulting room or clinic, which is the main test for diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Hysteroscopy and curettage:} direct visualisation of the womb with a thin telescope and scraping of the lining under anaesthetic
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT, MRI, or PET scans to determine whether the cancer has spread and to plan treatment
    \item \textbf{Molecular testing:} on the biopsy tissue to look for markers that may guide treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is planned by a multidisciplinary team and depends on the type, grade, and stage of the cancer, as well as the woman's general health and whether she wishes to preserve fertility:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} hysterectomy, the removal of the womb, with removal of the fallopian tubes and ovaries, is the standard treatment for most early-stage cancers
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} to the pelvis may be used after surgery for higher-risk disease, or instead of surgery in women who cannot have an operation
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy:} for more advanced, recurrent, or certain high-risk types of cancer
    \item \textbf{Hormone therapy:} progesterone-based tablets may be used for advanced or recurrent disease, and occasionally for early disease in women who strongly wish to preserve fertility
    \item \textbf{Immunotherapy and targeted therapy:} newer medicines for specific molecular subtypes of advanced disease
    \item \textbf{Fertility-preserving options:} hormone treatment and close monitoring may be offered to selected young women with early, low-grade cancer, with surgery delayed until after childbearing
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular reviews and scans to check for recurrence
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Spread (metastasis) to the lymph nodes, peritoneum, liver, or lungs with advanced disease
    \item Heavy or persistent bleeding causing anaemia
    \item Blockage of the ureters or bowel by tumour in advanced cases
    \item Surgical complications, including infection, bleeding, damage to surrounding organs, and blood clots in the legs or lungs
    \item Side effects of radiotherapy or chemotherapy, including fatigue, nausea, bowel and bladder irritation, and lymphoedema
    \item Early menopause and loss of fertility after hysterectomy
    \item Psychological effects, including anxiety, depression, body-image concerns, and sexual difficulties
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for uterine cancer is generally favourable because it is often detected early. Around three in four women are diagnosed when the cancer is still confined to the womb, and most of these women are cured with surgery. Survival is lower for high-grade, advanced, or recurrent disease. Overall, more than 80\% of women survive at least five years after diagnosis. Regular follow-up, prompt treatment, and managing health problems such as diabetes and obesity all contribute to better outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Report any post-menopausal bleeding promptly, however light or brief
    \item Maintain a healthy weight through diet and regular physical activity
    \item Discuss the benefits and risks of hormone replacement therapy with a doctor
    \item Women taking tamoxifen should be aware of the need to report any vaginal bleeding
    \item Attend regular gynaecological check-ups, especially if there is a family history of bowel, womb, or ovarian cancer
    \item Consider genetic testing and risk-reducing surgery for women with an inherited condition such as Lynch syndrome
    \item Manage conditions such as polycystic ovary syndrome and diabetes with medical advice
\end{itemize}
