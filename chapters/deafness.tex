\chapter{Deafness}
\index{Deafness}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Deafness is the partial or complete loss of hearing. It ranges from mild difficulty hearing certain sounds to profound hearing loss, and it may be present from birth (congenital) or develop at any stage of life (acquired). Hearing loss is commonly divided into conductive types, caused by problems in the outer or middle ear, and sensorineural types, caused by damage to the inner ear or auditory nerve. Hearing is fundamental to communication, learning, and social connection, so hearing loss has wide-ranging effects on quality of life. Many causes of deafness are treatable or preventable, and modern hearing devices and rehabilitation allow most people to communicate effectively.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hearing loss is one of the most common chronic conditions worldwide. The World Health Organization estimates that over 430 million people, about 5\% of the global population, have disabling hearing loss, a figure projected to rise with population ageing. In many countries, more than one in six people have some degree of hearing loss, and the prevalence rises steeply with age, affecting around two-thirds of people over 70. Approximately 1--3 in 1,000 newborns are born with significant hearing loss, making newborn hearing screening a routine part of infant care. Occupational and recreational noise exposure is a leading preventable cause in younger adults.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conductive causes:} conditions blocking or impairing the passage of sound through the outer or middle ear, including wax, ear infections, fluid (glue ear), perforated eardrum, and otosclerosis
    \item \textbf{Sensorineural causes:} damage to the hair cells of the cochlea or the auditory nerve, from ageing (presbycusis), noise exposure, infections such as meningitis, ototoxic medicines, and Meniere's disease
    \item \textbf{Congenital causes:} genetic syndromes, infections during pregnancy such as cytomegalovirus or rubella, and birth complications
    \item \textbf{Sudden sensorineural hearing loss:} rapid loss of hearing, often in one ear, sometimes viral or vascular in origin, requiring urgent treatment
    \item \textbf{Mixed loss:} a combination of conductive and sensorineural components
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty following conversations, especially in background noise
    \item Asking others to repeat themselves and turning up the television volume
    \item Muffled hearing or the sense that others are mumbling
    \item Tinnitus, or ringing in the ears, which often accompanies hearing loss
    \item In children: delayed speech and language, inattention, or poor school progress
    \item Balance problems when the vestibular system is also affected
    \item Withdrawal from social situations due to communication difficulty
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Conductive versus sensorineural hearing loss, distinguished by examination and audiometry
    \item Ear wax impaction and middle ear fluid, which are reversible
    \item Otosclerosis, which is surgically treatable
    \item Sudden sensorineural hearing loss, which requires urgent assessment
    \item Auditory processing disorder, in which hearing thresholds are normal but sound is poorly interpreted
    \item Cognitive decline in older adults, which can mimic or coexist with hearing loss
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who notices reduced hearing, particularly sudden hearing loss, should see a doctor promptly. Children should have hearing assessed if speech is delayed or they appear not to hear. Adults should have hearing checked if it affects work, safety, or relationships. A GP can assess the ears and arrange audiometry and specialist referral.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, complete hearing loss in one or both ears (a medical emergency)
    \item Hearing loss with severe dizziness, facial weakness, or other neurological symptoms
    \item Hearing loss following a head injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} inspection of the ear canal and eardrum with an otoscope, and tuning fork tests (Weber and Rinne)
    \item \textbf{Pure tone audiometry:} measures hearing thresholds across frequencies and distinguishes conductive from sensorineural loss
    \item \textbf{Speech audiometry:} assesses the ability to understand speech
    \item \textbf{Tympanometry:} evaluates middle ear function and pressure
    \item \textbf{Newborn screening:} automated otoacoustic emissions and auditory brainstem response tests in infants
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT or MRI of the temporal bones for structural causes, tumours, or planned surgery
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} for congenital or familial hearing loss
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reversible causes:} removal of wax, treatment of ear infections, and grommets for glue ear
    \item \textbf{Hearing aids:} the mainstay of management for most people with sensorineural loss; modern devices are discreet and digitally programmable
    \item \textbf{Cochlear implants:} surgically implanted devices for people with severe-to-profound loss who gain limited benefit from hearing aids
    \item \textbf{Bone conduction devices:} options for conductive or mixed loss where conventional aids are unsuitable
    \item \textbf{Assistive technology:} amplified telephones, loop systems, captions, and alerting devices
    \item \textbf{Communication support:} lip-reading classes, Auslan (local Sign Language), and speech therapy
    \item \textbf{Medical and surgical treatment:} corticosteroids for sudden sensorineural loss, and surgery such as stapedectomy for otosclerosis
    \item \textbf{Audiological rehabilitation:} counselling and training to adapt to hearing devices and communication strategies
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Social isolation, depression, and reduced quality of life
    \item Cognitive decline in older adults, which is associated with uncorrected hearing loss
    \item Communication difficulties affecting education, employment, and relationships
    \item Safety risks from not hearing alarms, traffic, or warnings
    \item Tinnitus and balance disturbance in some types of hearing loss
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis depends on the cause and type of hearing loss. Conductive losses are frequently reversible or surgically correctable. Sensorineural loss from ageing or noise is permanent but is very well managed with hearing aids, which most users find significantly improve communication and quality of life. Cochlear implants restore useful hearing for many profoundly deaf people, including children, who can develop spoken language. Early detection and intervention, particularly in children, are associated with the best outcomes. People with hearing loss who use appropriate devices and rehabilitation generally maintain independent and socially connected lives.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Protect hearing from loud noise: wear earplugs or earmuffs at concerts, in noisy workplaces, and when using power tools
    \item Keep personal audio volume at safe levels and follow the "60/60" rule (no more than 60\% volume for 60 minutes)
    \item Have hearing checked regularly, especially with increasing age or noise exposure
    \item Avoid inserting objects into the ear canal and seek professional removal of wax
    \item Vaccinate against infections that can cause hearing loss, such as rubella, measles, and meningitis
    \item Manage conditions such as diabetes and high blood pressure that can affect hearing
    \item Seek help early: the sooner hearing loss is addressed, the better the outcome
\end{itemize}
