\chapter{Floods and Cyclones: Health and Safety}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Floods and cyclones are among the most destructive natural hazards in Australia, and protecting health during them means preparing before, acting safely during, and staying alert in the long aftermath. The health and safety priorities are clear: never enter floodwater, shelter correctly from the cyclone's winds, avoid carbon monoxide from generators, use safe water and food, protect wounds, manage chronic illness, and look after mental health. Each phase of a disaster has its own dangers, and each can be managed with preparation and sensible decisions. This chapter is a practical health and safety guide for floods and cyclones.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Cyclones threaten northern Australia each season, and floods affect communities across the continent, with major events recurring in Queensland, New South Wales, and Victoria. These events have caused hundreds of deaths in Australian history, and floods are among the most deadly natural hazards in the country. Drowning in vehicles is the single largest cause of flood deaths, and most cyclone deaths occur in the wind and surge zone. In the aftermath, communities see surges in gastroenteritis, skin infections, respiratory illness, and mental health problems that can persist for years. Preparation measurably reduces death and injury.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Floodwater dangers:} drowning, injury from submerged objects, contamination with sewage and chemicals, and infection through skin contact or ingestion
    \item \textbf{Cyclone dangers:} wind-borne debris, building collapse, storm surge, and flying glass cause trauma and death
    \item \textbf{Carbon monoxide:} generators and heaters used indoors produce the gas that poisons people, often fatally
    \item \textbf{Contaminated water and food:} flooding disrupts supply and refrigeration, causing waterborne and foodborne illness
    \item \textbf{Mould:} damp, flooded homes grow mould that aggravates asthma and allergies
    \item \textbf{Mosquitoes:} standing water breeds mosquitoes carrying Ross River and dengue viruses in affected areas
    \item \textbf{Disruption of care:} loss of power, medicines, and access to health services destabilises chronic illness
    \item \textbf{Psychological trauma:} the event and its losses produce anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress, and grief
    \item \textbf{Electrical and debris hazards:} downed power lines, damaged appliances, and sharp debris injure people during and after the event
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The phases of a disaster have distinct safety and health features.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Before: preparing a plan, emergency kit, medicines, water, food, and a safe shelter
    \item During: sheltering from wind and storm surge, evacuating flood zones, and never entering floodwater
    \item Immediately after: injury from debris and chainsaws, carbon monoxide poisoning, and contaminated water exposure
    \item Days to weeks after: gastroenteritis, wound infections, leptospirosis, and mosquito-borne disease
    \item Weeks to months after: mould-related respiratory illness and mental health conditions
    \item In people with chronic illness: decompensation from missed medicines and disrupted services
    \item The safety feature of a prepared household: knowing the risks and the plan before the event
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Illness after a disaster is often multifactorial and needs careful assessment.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Gastroenteritis from contaminated water versus food poisoning
    \item Carbon monoxide poisoning, which mimics flu with headache, nausea, and confusion
    \item Respiratory symptoms from mould versus asthma or infection
    \item Fever and muscle pain from leptospirosis, melioidosis, or mosquito-borne viruses
    \item Mental health symptoms versus the normal stress response
    \item Worsening chronic disease versus new illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical care for fever, vomiting, diarrhoea, breathing problems, infected wounds, or significant distress after a flood or cyclone, and re-establish care early for chronic conditions.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Drowning, unconsciousness, or breathing difficulty
    \item Severe bleeding, trauma, or suspected spinal injury
    \item Suspected carbon monoxide poisoning: headache, nausea, confusion, collapse
    \item Chest pain, breathlessness, or signs of stroke
    \item Suicidal thoughts or behaviour
    \item Signs of sepsis: fever, confusion, rapid heart rate, or collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} exposure, injuries, symptoms, and disruption to medicines
    \item \textbf{Infection testing:} stool and blood tests for waterborne, leptospiral, and mosquito-borne diseases
    \item \textbf{Carbon monoxide measurement:} for exposure to combustion indoors
    \item \textbf{Respiratory review:} for asthma and mould exposure
    \item \textbf{Chronic disease checks:} blood pressure, glucose, and kidney function after disruption
    \item \textbf{Mental health screening:} for anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Preparation:} emergency plan, evacuation routes, and a kit with water, food, medicines, first aid, and documents
    \item \textbf{Safe shelter:} follow cyclone warnings, shelter in the strongest part of the building, and evacuate flood zones when directed
    \item \textbf{Water safety:} never enter floodwater on foot or in a vehicle; treat all floodwater as contaminated
    \item \textbf{Carbon monoxide:} run generators and fuel appliances only outdoors, well away from windows and doors
    \item \textbf{Food and water:} boil water or use bottled supplies; discard food that may have spoiled or been contaminated
    \item \textbf{Wounds:} clean, cover, and seek care; update tetanus vaccination
    \item \textbf{Mould:} dry homes quickly, wear masks during clean-up, and treat respiratory symptoms early
    \item \textbf{Chronic disease:} restock medicines, resume care with the GP, and know how to reach services during the recovery
    \item \textbf{Mental health:} reach out for support early, use counselling services, and watch for symptoms in family members
    \item \textbf{Community recovery:} use recovery centres, financial assistance, and support services
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Death and disability from drowning, trauma, and carbon monoxide
    \item Outbreaks of infectious disease
    \item Worsening chronic disease with preventable complications
    \item Mould-related respiratory illness
    \item Post-traumatic stress, depression, and suicide risk
    \item Financial and housing stress prolonging recovery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The health outcomes of floods and cyclones are largely determined by preparation and early response. Households that prepare, heed warnings, and avoid floodwater dramatically reduce their risk of death and injury, and communities that restore safe water, food, and health services quickly limit the disease burden. Mental health support delivered early and sustained through the recovery reduces long-term harm. Most people recover fully, and communities with strong preparedness and support networks emerge healthier after each event.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Know your risk, your warnings, and your evacuation plan before the season begins
    \item Keep an emergency kit stocked with water, food, medicines, first aid, and important documents
    \item Never drive, walk, or ride through floodwater
    \item Shelter correctly for cyclones and evacuate when told to
    \item Run generators only outdoors and away from the house
    \item Boil water until authorities confirm safety, and discard spoiled food
    \item Restock medicines and re-establish care after the event
    \item Seek support for yourself and check on family and neighbours during recovery
\end{itemize}
