\chapter{Diphtheria}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Diphtheria is a serious bacterial infection caused by Corynebacterium diphtheriae, which can affect the throat, nose, or skin. The most common form, respiratory diphtheria, causes a thick grey membrane to form in the throat that can obstruct breathing, along with fever and a sore throat. The bacteria produce a toxin that can damage the heart and nerves, which can be fatal. Diphtheria is prevented by vaccination, and it is now very rare in Australia, but it remains a threat in countries with low vaccination rates. Prompt treatment with antitoxin and antibiotics is essential.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Diphtheria has been almost eliminated in Australia and other countries with high vaccination coverage, with only rare imported cases. Before vaccination, it was a major cause of childhood death. It remains common in parts of Asia, Africa, and South America, and travellers to these regions should be vaccinated. Outbreaks can occur in communities with low immunisation rates. Booster doses are recommended throughout life because immunity from childhood vaccination fades.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bacterium:} Corynebacterium diphtheriae, spread by respiratory droplets or contact with infected skin
    \item \textbf{Toxin:} the bacteria produce a powerful toxin that damages tissue and organs
    \item \textbf{Pseudomembrane:} a thick grey membrane forms in the throat, which can block the airway
    \item \textbf{Incubation:} symptoms appear 2--5 days after exposure
    \item \textbf{Complications:} the toxin can cause inflammation of the heart muscle (myocarditis) and nerve damage (neuropathy)
    \item \textbf{Immunity:} vaccination protects against the toxin, and infection does not always confer lasting immunity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sore throat, fever, and a general feeling of being unwell
    \item A thick grey membrane on the tonsils, throat, or nose
    \item Swollen neck glands and a "bull neck" appearance
    \item Difficulty swallowing and breathing in severe cases
    \item A hoarse voice and a barking cough
    \item Skin diphtheria: punched-out ulcers with a grey membrane, usually in travellers or tropical areas
    \item Complications appear days to weeks later, including heart and nerve damage
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Tonsillitis and pharyngitis from other bacteria and viruses
    \item Infectious mononucleosis (glandular fever)
    \item Other causes of a throat membrane, such as oral thrush
    \item Epiglottitis, a different airway emergency
    \item Other causes of neck swelling
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with a severe sore throat, a grey throat membrane, or suspected diphtheria needs urgent medical assessment, especially if they have recently travelled to a region where diphtheria occurs.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing or a feeling that the airway is closing
    \item Difficulty swallowing or drooling
    \item A grey membrane in the throat with a high fever
    \item Chest pain, irregular heartbeat, or weakness (possible heart involvement)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical suspicion:} the membrane and symptoms suggest the diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Swabs:} throat or wound swabs for culture and identification of the bacterium
    \item \textbf{Toxin testing:} laboratory testing confirms toxin production
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} inflammatory markers and organ function
    \item \textbf{ECG and heart monitoring:} for myocarditis
    \item \textbf{Neurological assessment:} for nerve complications
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antitoxin:} diphtheria antitoxin is given urgently to neutralise the toxin
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} penicillin or erythromycin eradicate the bacteria and stop spread
    \item \textbf{Isolation:} to prevent spread to others
    \item \textbf{Airway management:} hospital care with monitoring and, if needed, procedures to secure the airway
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} fluids, rest, and monitoring of heart and nerve function
    \item \textbf{Vaccination:} immunisation is given during recovery, as infection does not always produce lasting immunity
    \item \textbf{Contact tracing:} close contacts are assessed, vaccinated, and treated if needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Airway obstruction from the membrane
    \item Myocarditis causing heart failure and abnormal rhythms
    \item Peripheral neuropathy causing weakness and paralysis
    \item Kidney damage
    \item Breathing failure and death in severe cases
    \item Secondary pneumonia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With early treatment, the outlook for diphtheria is good, but the disease is serious: without antitoxin, around 5--10\% of cases are fatal, with higher rates in children. The toxin causes most of the damage, so prompt treatment is essential. Complications such as heart and nerve damage usually improve over weeks to months. Prevention through vaccination is highly effective, and diphtheria is almost entirely preventable.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ensure children receive the diphtheria vaccine as part of the national immunisation schedule
    \item Maintain adult booster doses, including the tetanus-diphtheria-pertussis booster recommended for adults
    \item Pregnant women are recommended the booster to protect the newborn
    \item Check vaccination status before travelling to regions with diphtheria
    \item Seek prompt medical care for severe sore throat with a membrane
    \item Complete prescribed antibiotic courses
    \item Keep immunisation records up to date
\end{itemize}
