\chapter{Mouth cancer}
\index{Mouth cancer}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Mouth cancer, clinically termed oral cavity cancer or oral squamous cell carcinoma, is a malignant neoplasm originating from the mucosal tissues of the oral cavity. The oral cavity encompasses the lips, the anterior two-thirds of the tongue, the buccal mucosa (inner lining of the cheeks), the floor of the mouth, the hard palate, the gingiva (gums), and the retromolar trigone. Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) accounts for over 90\% of all mouth cancers, arising from the thin, flat squamous cells that line the oral mucosa. Mouth cancer is a highly aggressive and potentially life-threatening disease associated with significant morbidity, as both the tumour and its treatments can profoundly impact speech, mastication, swallowing, taste, and facial appearance. It is strongly associated with lifestyle carcinogens, particularly tobacco use and heavy alcohol consumption.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Mouth cancers represent a major subset of head and neck malignancies globally. In many countries, oral cavity cancers are diagnosed in approximately 2,500 individuals annually. The disease demonstrates a strong gender predisposition, affecting males twice as frequently as females, which correlates with historical tobacco and alcohol use patterns. The majority of cases are diagnosed in individuals aged 50 years and older, with the risk rising progressively with age. However, there is a rising incidence in younger cohorts, partly linked to human papillomavirus (HPV) exposure and changing tobacco habits. In specific regions, such as South and Southeast Asia, the incidence is exceptionally high across all age groups due to the widespread cultural practice of chewing betel quid (areca nut).

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The carcinogenesis of mouth cancer involves the progressive accumulation of genetic mutations in mucosal epithelial cells exposed to chronic carcinogens:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tobacco consumption:} The most critical risk factor. Smoking cigarettes, cigars, or pipes, and using smokeless tobacco (chewing tobacco or snuff) directly exposes the oral mucosa to over 60 known chemical carcinogens.
    \item \textbf{Alcohol synergy:} Chronic, heavy alcohol consumption acts as a solvent, increasing mucosal permeability to tobacco carcinogens. Additionally, the primary metabolite of alcohol, acetaldehyde, is a potent local carcinogen that impairs DNA repair.
    \item \textbf{Betel quid and areca nut chewing:} A major cause of oral cancer and oral submucous fibrosis (a pre-malignant condition) in South Asian populations. The areca nut contains alkaloids that stimulate fibrosing and mutagenic pathways.
    \item \textbf{Ultraviolet (UV) radiation:} Excessive exposure to solar UV radiation is the primary cause of lip cancers, almost exclusively affecting the lower lip in individuals with outdoor occupations.
    \item \textbf{Human Papillomavirus (HPV):} High-risk strains, particularly HPV-16, are strongly linked to oropharyngeal cancers (tonsils and base of tongue) and play a minor role in anterior oral cavity squamous cell carcinomas.
    \item \textbf{Oral Potentially Malignant Disorders (OPMDs):} Transformation of pre-existing dysplastic lesions, such as leukoplakia (persistent white patches) or erythroplakia (velvety red patches, which carry a high rate of malignant transformation).
    \item \textbf{Chronic mechanical irritation:} Long-standing trauma to the mucosa from sharp, broken teeth, or ill-fitting dental prostheses, which can promote localized cellular hyperproliferation and DNA damage.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Mouth cancer often begins as a subtle lesion that may be painless in its early stages. Common clinical features include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Non-healing oral ulcer:} A persistent sore, ulcer, or fissure on the lip, tongue, or gum that does not heal within 2 to 3 weeks.
    \item \textbf{Mucosal patches:} Persistent white patches (leukoplakia), red patches (erythroplakia), or mixed red-and-white patches (speckled leukoplakia) within the mouth.
    \item \textbf{Indurated lump or thickening:} A firm, localized thickening, swelling, or nodule in the cheek, tongue, or gum, which may be felt during a physical or dental examination.
    \item \textbf{Persistent pain or numbness:} Unexplained pain, tenderness, or loss of sensation in the mouth, tongue, or lower lip.
    \item \textbf{Impaired oral mobility:} Difficulty chewing, swallowing (dysphagia), moving the tongue, or fully opening the jaw (trismus).
    \item \textbf{Dental changes:} Unexplained loosening of teeth, pain around the jaw, or bleeding from the gums or oral tissues without obvious dental cause.
    \item \textbf{Cervical lymphadenopathy:} A firm, painless, fixed lump in the neck, representing metastatic spread to the regional lymph nodes.
    \item \textbf{Halitosis and voice changes:} Persistent bad breath (due to tissue necrosis) or a slurred or altered voice (``hot potato'' voice).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because many benign inflammatory and infectious lesions occur in the oral cavity, mouth cancer must be distinguished from:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Aphthous stomatitis (canker sores):} Painful, superficial ulcers that resolve spontaneously within 10--14 days and lack induration or clinical margins.
    \item \textbf{Oral candidiasis (thrush):} A fungal infection presenting as white, curd-like patches that can be easily scraped off, leaving an erythematous, sometimes bleeding surface, which responds to antifungal therapy.
    \item \textbf{Oral lichen planus:} A chronic autoimmune condition presenting as bilateral, reticular white lines (Wickham's striae) or painful erosions on the buccal mucosa.
    \item \textbf{Traumatic ulcer:} An ulcer caused by a sharp tooth cusp or a broken filling, which typically heals within 10 days once the mechanical irritant is removed or smoothed.
    \item \textbf{Infectious ulcers:} Mucosal manifestations of primary syphilis (chancre) or tuberculosis, distinguished by serology, acid-fast bacilli staining, and biopsy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any sore, ulcer, lump, or red/white patch in the mouth or on the lip that does not heal or improve within 2 to 3 weeks should be evaluated by a dentist, general practitioner, or oral specialist.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe difficulty breathing, swallowing, or a sensation that the airway is closing.
    \item Massive, uncontrollable bleeding from the mouth or neck.
    \item Loss of consciousness, severe confusion, or sudden collapse.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A definitive diagnosis requires tissue biopsy, supplemented by high-resolution imaging to stage the extent of the disease:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} A thorough inspection and palpation of the entire oral cavity, oropharynx, and neck lymph nodes under optimal lighting.
    \item \textbf{Incisional biopsy:} The gold-standard diagnostic procedure, performed under local anaesthesia, where a small representative wedge of the lesion (including some adjacent normal tissue) is excised for histopathological analysis.
    \item \textbf{Contrast-enhanced CT or MRI of the head and neck:} Vital to determine the primary tumour size, depth of mucosal invasion, involvement of adjacent bones (mandible or maxilla), and presence of cervical lymph node metastases.
    \item \textbf{Orthopantomogram (OPG):} A panoramic dental X-ray used specifically to evaluate whether the tumor has invaded the jawbone.
    \item \textbf{PET-CT scan:} Indicated in advanced cases to identify distant metastatic disease (typically in the lungs, bones, or liver) and to detect synchronous primary tumours.
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic panendoscopy:} Direct visualization of the upper aerodigestive tract (larynx, pharynx, trachea, and oesophagus) to rule out secondary primary cancers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is highly complex and determined by a multidisciplinary head and neck oncology team, based on the stage, location, and patient preferences:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgical resection:} The cornerstone of therapy. Wide local excision of the primary tumour, which may involve partial tongue removal (glossectomy) or jaw removal (mandibulectomy/maxillectomy).
    \item \textbf{Neck dissection:} Surgical removal of the lymph nodes in the neck (selective or modified radical) to treat or prevent lymphatic metastasis.
    \item \textbf{Microvascular reconstructive surgery:} Transfer of autologous tissue flaps (e.g. radial forearm free skin flap, fibular free bone flap) to reconstruct complex defects, preserving speech and swallowing function.
    \item \textbf{Radiotherapy:} Adjuvant external beam radiation therapy, administered to the oral cavity and neck post-operatively in patients with high-risk features (e.g. close/positive margins, perineural invasion, or multiple positive nodes).
    \item \textbf{Systemic chemotherapy or immunotherapy:} Combined with radiotherapy (chemoradiotherapy) for advanced disease, or used as palliative therapy (e.g. pembrolizumab, cisplatin) for recurrent or metastatic disease.
    \item \textbf{Multidisciplinary rehabilitation:} Intensive support from speech pathologists, specialist dentists, clinical dietitians (especially if a gastrostomy [PEG] feeding tube is required), and microvascular physiotherapists.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The progression of mouth cancer and its intensive treatments lead to severe long-term complications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dysphagia and aspiration:} Impaired swallowing due to anatomical tissue loss or radiation scarring, raising the risk of aspiration pneumonia and requiring prolonged enteral nutrition.
    \item \textbf{Speech impairment:} Difficulty articulating words (dysarthria) following glossectomy or mandibular resection, requiring speech therapy.
    \item \textbf{Osteoradionecrosis:} A severe complication of jaw irradiation characterised by non-healing, exposed, necrotic bone, which requires pre-treatment dental clearance and hyperbaric oxygen therapy in some cases.
    \item \textbf{Xerostomia and rampant caries:} Permanent damage to the salivary glands from radiation, leading to chronic dry mouth, oral discomfort, loss of taste (dysgeusia), and rapid dental decay.
    \item \textbf{Trismus:} Scarring and fibrosis of the masticatory muscles post-radiation or post-surgery, limiting the patient's ability to open their mouth.
    \item \textbf{Facial disfigurement:} Significant changes to facial aesthetics following major resections, leading to profound psychological distress.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for mouth cancer is highly stage-dependent. Early-stage (Stage I and II) oral cavity cancers have an excellent prognosis, with 5-year survival rates exceeding 80--90\% following localized surgical resection. However, once the cancer has spread to the cervical lymph nodes (Stage III and IV), the 5-year survival rate drops to approximately 30--50\%. Because early-stage mouth cancer is often painless, many patients present with advanced disease. Early clinical detection by general practitioners and dentists during routine screenings is the single most critical factor in improving survival and reducing the need for highly invasive, disfiguring treatments.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Primary prevention and lifestyle modifications are highly effective at reducing the risk of oral cancer:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Complete tobacco cessation:} Quit smoking cigarettes, pipes, or cigars, and avoid all smokeless tobacco products.
    \item \textbf{Moderate alcohol consumption:} Limit alcohol intake to low-risk guidelines, and avoid the synergistic risk of concurrent tobacco and alcohol use.
    \item \textbf{Avoid betel quid/areca nut:} Eliminate the chewing of betel nut, areca nut, and related commercial preparations.
    \item \textbf{Daily lip protection:} Apply a broad-spectrum water-resistant lip balm with SPF 30 or higher before going outdoors, and wear a wide-brimmed hat.
    \item \textbf{Excellent oral hygiene:} Brush and floss teeth twice daily, and visit a dentist at least twice a year for professional dental care and oral mucosal screening.
    \item \textbf{Perform oral self-examinations:} Conduct monthly visual checks of your mouth (tongue, cheeks, gums, floor of mouth) in a well-lit mirror to identify any new sores or colour changes early.
\end{itemize}
