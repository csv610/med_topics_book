\chapter{Degenerative Arthritis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Degenerative arthritis, more commonly called osteoarthritis, is a condition in which the cartilage that cushions the ends of bones gradually wears away, leading to pain, stiffness, and reduced movement in the affected joints. It most often affects the knees, hips, hands, and spine. Osteoarthritis is the most common form of arthritis and is a leading cause of disability worldwide. While it cannot be cured, a great deal can be done to relieve symptoms and maintain function, including exercise, weight management, and joint care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Osteoarthritis affects around one in eleven Australians, with the prevalence rising steeply with age; it is present in over half of people over 65. Women are affected more often than men, particularly after menopause. The knees and hips are the most common large joints affected, and hand arthritis is very common. Risk factors include increasing age, obesity, joint injury, repetitive joint use, genetics, and previous joint surgery. Osteoarthritis is a major cause of joint replacement surgery, with tens of thousands of hip and knee replacements performed in Australia each year.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cartilage wear:} the smooth cartilage covering joint surfaces thins and develops fissures, exposing the underlying bone
    \item \textbf{Bone changes:} the bone beneath the cartilage thickens, and bony spurs (osteophytes) form at joint margins
    \item \textbf{Inflammation:} low-grade inflammation of the joint lining (synovium) contributes to pain and swelling
    \item \textbf{Mechanical stress:} excess weight, joint injury, and repeated loading increase the strain on joints
    \item \textbf{Ageing:} cartilage repair capacity declines with age
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} a family history increases susceptibility, especially in hand and hip arthritis
    \item \textbf{Synovial fluid changes:} reduced lubrication and shock absorption worsen friction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain in the affected joint, typically worsened by activity and relieved by rest
    \item Morning stiffness lasting less than 30 minutes, and stiffness after inactivity
    \item Reduced range of movement and difficulty with activities such as climbing stairs, kneeling, or gripping
    \item Swelling, joint tenderness, and a grating sensation or creaking on movement
    \item Bony swelling and deformity in the hands, such as Heberden's and Bouchard's nodes
    \item Pain that varies with weather and activity
    \item Disability affecting walking, work, and daily living in advanced disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rheumatoid arthritis, which causes symmetrical joint inflammation, prolonged morning stiffness, and systemic features
    \item Gout and pseudogout, which cause acute, intense attacks of joint pain
    \item Psoriatic arthritis and other inflammatory arthritides
    \item Septic arthritis, a medical emergency with hot, swollen joint and fever
    \item Fibromyalgia, which causes widespread pain without joint damage
    \item Bursitis or tendonitis around the joint
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with persistent joint pain, stiffness, or reduced function should see a doctor for diagnosis and management planning. A hot, red, swollen joint with fever requires urgent attention, as does pain after significant injury. Early diagnosis and management help maintain function and slow progression.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A hot, swollen, red joint with fever (possible septic arthritis)
    \item Sudden inability to move or bear weight on a joint after injury
    \item Sudden severe joint pain, which may indicate gout, fracture, or infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the pattern of pain, stiffness, and joint involvement usually suggests the diagnosis
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} show joint space narrowing, bone spurs, and other typical changes, although severity on X-ray does not always match symptoms
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} usually normal in osteoarthritis but help exclude inflammatory arthritis; inflammatory markers and rheumatoid factor may be checked
    \item \textbf{Joint fluid analysis:} aspiration may be performed to exclude gout or infection in swollen joints
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound and MRI:} useful in selected cases to assess soft tissues and cartilage
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Exercise:} the cornerstone of management; strengthening, stretching, and low-impact aerobic activity such as walking, swimming, and cycling improve pain and function
    \item \textbf{Weight management:} losing excess weight substantially reduces load on weight-bearing joints and relieves pain
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} tailored programmes, manual therapy, and gait advice
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} paracetamol, topical anti-inflammatory gels, and short courses of oral non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)
    \item \textbf{Heat and cold:} heat packs relax muscles and ease stiffness; cold packs reduce acute pain and swelling
    \item \textbf{Assistive devices:} walking aids, orthotics, jar openers, and other aids reduce joint stress
    \item \textbf{Injections:} corticosteroid injections for flare-ups and, in selected knees, viscosupplementation
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} joint replacement is highly effective for advanced arthritis when pain is severe and function poor despite conservative care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic pain, disability, and reduced quality of life
    \item Loss of independence and difficulty with mobility and self-care
    \item Muscle weakness and deconditioning from reduced activity
    \item Sleep disturbance and low mood
    \item Falls and fractures, especially with knee or hip arthritis
    \item Complications of joint replacement surgery, including infection and implant loosening
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Osteoarthritis is a chronic condition that tends to progress slowly, but progression is highly variable: many people remain stable for years, and some improve. Symptoms are often manageable with exercise, weight control, and simple treatments, and most people remain active. Joint replacement surgery gives excellent results for severe hip and knee arthritis, with most people experiencing marked pain relief and improved mobility. Early, sustained self-management offers the best long-term outlook.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a healthy weight to reduce joint load
    \item Stay active with a mix of strengthening, flexibility, and aerobic exercise
    \item Protect joints from injury with proper technique, warm-ups, and supportive footwear
    \item Use correct lifting and posture to reduce strain on knees, hips, and spine
    \item Listen to your body: pace activities and rest when joints flare
    \item Attend physiotherapy and follow a structured exercise plan
    \item Manage other health conditions such as diabetes and high blood pressure
\end{itemize}
