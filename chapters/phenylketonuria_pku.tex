\chapter{Phenylketonuria (PKU)}
\index{Phenylketonuria (PKU)}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{Educational information; PKU care should be coordinated by a metabolic specialist and dietitian.}

\section*{What it is}
Phenylketonuria is an inherited metabolic condition in which the body cannot process phenylalanine, an amino acid found in protein. Without treatment, high phenylalanine can damage the brain. Newborn screening allows early diagnosis in many health systems.

\section*{Diagnosis and monitoring}
Diagnosis uses newborn or blood testing and may be confirmed with metabolic and genetic assessment. Ongoing monitoring measures blood phenylalanine and considers growth, nutrition, development, school or work, mental health, and pregnancy plans.

\section*{Treatment}
Treatment usually involves an individualized low-phenylalanine diet, special formula or medical nutrition, and regular dietetic support. Some people may be eligible for medicines or other therapies. Protein restriction must be medically supervised so that calories, vitamins, minerals, and protein needs are met.

\section*{Pregnancy and adult care}
People who can become pregnant should discuss PKU before conception and maintain the recommended phenylalanine range during pregnancy to reduce fetal risk. Adults should not assume that treatment can stop when childhood ends; lifelong follow-up is commonly needed.

\section*{When to seek medical care}
Contact the metabolic team for difficulty following the diet, repeated high results, vomiting, poor growth, developmental change, pregnancy, or concerns about formula or medicine. Seek urgent care for severe vomiting, dehydration, altered consciousness, or a rapidly worsening illness.
