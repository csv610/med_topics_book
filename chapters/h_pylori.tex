\chapter{H. pylori}
\index{H. pylori}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
H. pylori (\textit{Helicobacter pylori}) is a spiral-shaped bacterium that colonises the lining of the stomach and is the most common cause of chronic stomach infection worldwide. It is responsible for most cases of peptic ulcers (stomach and duodenal ulcers) and is a major risk factor for stomach cancer and a type of lymphoma of the stomach. Most people infected with H. pylori have no symptoms, but some develop gastritis, ulcers, or, rarely, cancer. The infection is usually acquired in childhood and persists for life unless treated. Treatment with a combination of antibiotics and acid-suppressing medicines (eradication therapy) cures the infection in most people.

\section*{Epidemiology}
H. pylori infects around half of the world's population, making it one of the most common human infections. In many countries, prevalence is lower than in many developing countries, affecting around 15--25\% of the population, but rates are higher in older people, in migrants from high-prevalence countries, and in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities. Around 1 in 6 infected people develop peptic ulcers over their lifetime, and H. pylori is present in the majority of duodenal ulcers. Infection with H. pylori is estimated to cause around 90\% of non-cardia stomach cancers.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
H. pylori causes disease by colonising the stomach lining and evading the immune system.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} mainly person-to-person through saliva, vomit, or faecal-oral spread, usually in childhood
    \item \textbf{Colonisation:} the bacterium burrows into the mucous layer of the stomach and attaches to the cells beneath
    \item \textbf{Urease enzyme:} H. pylori produces urease, which neutralises stomach acid around it and allows survival
    \item \textbf{Gastritis:} infection causes chronic inflammation of the stomach lining (gastritis) in all infected people
    \item \textbf{Ulcers:} inflammation and acid damage can erode through the lining, causing gastric or duodenal ulcers
    \item \textbf{Cancer:} long-standing infection and inflammation can lead to precancerous changes and stomach cancer
    \item \textbf{Achlorhydria and atrophy:} in some people, long-term infection causes thinning of the stomach lining
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Most people with H. pylori have no symptoms. When symptoms occur, they relate to gastritis or ulcers.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Upper abdominal pain or discomfort, often described as burning or gnawing
    \item Pain that may improve or worsen with eating
    \item Bloating, burping, and a feeling of fullness after small meals
    \item Nausea and loss of appetite
    \item Unexplained weight loss
    \item Vomiting, which can be blood-stained in bleeding ulcers
    \item Black, tarry stools from bleeding ulcers
    \item Anaemia and fatigue from chronic blood loss
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of upper abdominal symptoms should be considered.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD)
    \item Functional dyspepsia, where symptoms occur without an identifiable cause
    \item Peptic ulcers from other causes, including NSAID medicines
    \item Gastritis from other causes
    \item Gallbladder disease, including gallstones
    \item Stomach cancer, which is rare but must be considered with alarm symptoms
    \item Pancreatic disease
    \item Coeliac disease and other causes of dyspepsia
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for persistent upper abdominal pain, indigestion, or other gut symptoms, especially if accompanied by weight loss, vomiting, or signs of bleeding. Testing for H. pylori is recommended for people with ulcers, confirmed gastritis, a family history of stomach cancer, or unexplained iron deficiency.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vomiting blood or material that looks like coffee grounds
    \item Black, tarry, or bloody stools
    \item Sudden severe abdominal pain, which may indicate a perforated ulcer
    \item Severe dehydration or an inability to keep down fluids
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Several tests can detect H. pylori, and the choice depends on the situation.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urea breath test:} a non-invasive test in which the patient drinks a labelled urea solution; the bacterium's urease releases labelled carbon dioxide, detected in the breath
    \item \textbf{Stool antigen test:} detects H. pylori proteins in a stool sample
    \item \textbf{Endoscopy with biopsy:} the most direct test, allowing visual inspection of the stomach and biopsy for the bacterium and for tissue changes
    \item \textbf{Blood antibody test:} detects past or present infection, but is less useful for confirming cure
    \item \textbf{Testing before and after treatment:} a non-invasive test after treatment confirms eradication
    \item \textbf{Culture and sensitivity:} performed when antibiotic resistance is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment aims to eradicate the infection and heal any damage.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Eradication therapy:} a combination of a proton pump inhibitor and two or three antibiotics, taken for 7--14 days
    \item \textbf{Common regimens:} such as triple therapy with amoxicillin, clarithromycin, and a PPI
    \item \textbf{Second-line treatment:} different antibiotic combinations for people who do not respond to the first course
    \item \textbf{Confirmation of cure:} a breath or stool test at least four weeks after finishing antibiotics and two weeks after stopping acid suppression
    \item \textbf{Treatment of ulcers:} acid-suppressing medicines continue until the ulcer heals
    \item \textbf{Addressing other causes:} stopping NSAIDs that contribute to ulcers
    \item \textbf{Family and household:} treatment of the index case is usually sufficient; testing asymptomatic family members is generally not recommended
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} repeat endoscopy in some cases to confirm healing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Peptic ulcers with bleeding, perforation, or obstruction
    \item Iron-deficiency anaemia from chronic blood loss
    \item Stomach cancer, the risk of which is reduced by eradication
    \item Gastric lymphoma (MALT lymphoma), which often regresses after eradication
    \item Antibiotic resistance, leading to failed treatment and the need for second-line therapy
    \item Side effects of treatment, including nausea, taste disturbance, and diarrhoea
    \item Re-infection, which is uncommon in adults in developed countries
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Eradication therapy cures H. pylori in around 80--90\% of people with standard treatment. Once eradicated, ulcers heal, and the risk of ulcer recurrence and stomach cancer falls substantially. People who are not treated remain at risk of ulcers and, over decades, stomach cancer. The long-term benefits of eradication are significant, which is why testing and treatment are recommended for those with symptoms, ulcers, or risk factors. Re-infection is uncommon in adults.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Practise good hand hygiene, especially after using the toilet and before preparing food
    \item Ensure clean drinking water and safe food handling
    \item Complete the full course of eradication therapy exactly as prescribed
    \item Attend the follow-up test to confirm the infection is cured
    \item Avoid NSAIDs if you have ulcers or ulcer symptoms, unless advised by a doctor
    \item Limit alcohol and do not smoke, as both worsen stomach damage
    \item See a doctor for persistent gut symptoms or any signs of bleeding
    \item If you have a family history of stomach cancer, discuss screening with your doctor
\end{itemize}
