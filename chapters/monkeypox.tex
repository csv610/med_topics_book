\chapter{Monkeypox}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Mpox (formerly known as monkeypox) is an infectious disease caused by the mpox virus, a double-stranded DNA virus of the genus \textit{Orthopoxvirus} within the family \textit{Poxviridae}. It is a zoonotic viral pathogen native to the tropical rainforests of Central and West Africa, but has gained global significance due to international outbreaks, leading to its declaration as a Public Health Emergency of International Concern by the World Health Organization in 2022. Mpox is characterised by a prodromal phase of fever, lymphadenopathy, and malaise, followed by the development of a distinctive vesiculopapular rash that progresses to pustules and crusts. Transmission occurs through close, personal, often skin-to-skin contact with infected lesions, respiratory droplets, or contaminated fomites. While most cases are mild and self-limiting, mpox can cause severe pain, secondary bacterial infections, and, in rare instances, fatal systemic complications, especially in immunocompromised individuals.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Historically, mpox was primarily endemic to rural areas of the Democratic Republic of the Congo and other West African nations, with transmission driven by contact with wild animal reservoirs (such as rodents and primates). However, the 2022--2023 global outbreak marked a significant shift, characterised by sustained human-to-human transmission outside endemic regions, disproportionately affecting gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men networks, though anyone exposed can contract the virus. In Australia, several hundred cases have been diagnosed since 2022. The basic reproduction number in the affected networks during outbreaks has been estimated around 1.5--2.0. A newer lineage, Clade Ib, emerged in late 2023, showing increased transmissibility and virulence, prompting renewed international surveillance and targeted vaccination campaigns using third-generation smallpox vaccines.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Mpox is caused by the mpox virus, which exists in two distinct genetic clades: Clade I (associated with higher virulence) and Clade II (associated with milder disease and the 2022 global outbreak).
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral entry and transmission:} The virus enters the host through broken skin (even microscopic abrasions), the respiratory tract, or mucosal membranes (oral, genital, or rectal mucosa).
    \item \textbf{Lymphatic dissemination:} Following entry, the virus replicates at the inoculation site and rapidly drains to regional lymph nodes, leading to prominent lymphadenopathy, a key differentiating feature from smallpox and chickenpox.
    \item \textbf{Viraemic phase:} Primary and secondary viraemia disseminate the virus throughout the reticuloendothelial system and to epithelial cells of the skin and mucous membranes, triggering the characteristic rash.
    \item \textbf{Cytopathic skin lesions:} Host cellular immune response to the viral replication in the epidermis leads to ballooning degeneration of keratinocytes, focal necrosis, and the formation of intraepidermal vesicles.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of mpox typically begins after an incubation period of 5--21 days, progressing through prodromal and eruptive stages.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A prodromal phase lasting 1 to 5 days, characterised by high fever, severe headache, muscle aches (myalgia), backache, extreme fatigue, and chills.
    \item Prominent lymphadenopathy, which can be unilateral or bilateral and typically affects the cervical, submandibular, axillary, or inguinal regions.
    \item A distinctive skin rash beginning 1 to 3 days after the fever. The lesions progress sequentially from macules to papules, vesicles, pustules (often umbilicated), and finally crusts that desquamate.
    \item Atypical rash presentations, common during the global outbreak, presenting as a few localised lesions in the genital, perianal, or oral areas, sometimes without a preceding systemic prodrome.
    \item Severe mucosal pain, including proctitis (rectal pain, tenesmus, bleeding) and tonsillitis or pharyngitis, making swallowing or defecating extremely painful.
    \item Healing of lesions typically takes 2 to 4 weeks, and a patient remains infectious until all scabs have fallen off, revealing a fresh layer of intact skin underneath.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The clinical features of mpox, particularly the rash, must be distinguished from other vesicular and ulcerative conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chickenpox:} Causes a similar vesicular rash, but chickenpox lesions are typically in different stages of development simultaneously, are smaller, more superficial, and are not accompanied by prominent lymphadenopathy.
    \item \textbf{Herpes simplex virus:} Causes painful, recurrent vesicular lesions on the mouth or genitals, but HSV lacks the generalised cephalocaudal progression, systemic prodrome, and widespread lymphadenopathy of classic mpox.
    \item \textbf{Syphilis:} Primary chancres or secondary papular rashes can mimic mpox lesions; however, syphilis is painless in the primary stage, lacks vesicular fluid-filled lesions, and is diagnosed via specific serological tests.
    \item \textbf{Hand, foot, and mouth disease:} Caused by Coxsackievirus; presents with vesicles on the hands, feet, and oral cavity in children, but lacks the pustular progression, severe mucosal pain, and inguinal/axillary lymphadenopathy.
    \item \textbf{Molluscum contagiosum:} A benign poxvirus causing firm, umbilicated, flesh-coloured papules, but these are painless, slow-growing, lack a fluid-filled stage, and do not cause systemic symptoms.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who has had close contact with a confirmed mpox case or who develops an unexplained rash with fever and swollen lymph nodes should seek medical assessment. They should call the clinic ahead to discuss isolation protocols.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, severe chest tightness, or confusion.
    \item Inability to swallow fluids or severe dehydration due to excruciating mouth pain.
    \item A seizure, altered consciousness, or severe neck stiffness.
    \item Signs of severe sepsis, such as rapid breathing, cold and clammy skin, or shivering, in a patient with infected skin lesions.
\end{itemize}

Other non-emergency reasons to seek medical attention include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Developing a new, unexplained blister-like rash, especially if you have had a new sexual partner or travelled to an outbreak area.
    \item Severe rectal pain, bleeding, or difficulty passing urine.
    \item Secondary bacterial infection of a skin lesion, marked by increasing redness, warmth, swelling, or drainage of pus.
    \item Needing to discuss vaccination if you are in a high-risk group.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnostic confirmation relies on molecular testing, as clinical features can mimic other infections.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Polymerase Chain Reaction test:} The gold standard diagnostic method. Swabs are taken directly from the fluid or scabs of vesicular/pustular lesions to detect mpox virus DNA.
    \item \textbf{Orthopoxvirus PCR:} Broad-spectrum PCR testing to identify the genus, followed by specific sequencing to confirm mpox and identify the clade.
    \item \textbf{Viral culture:} Can be performed in biosafety level 3 laboratories but is not routinely used for acute diagnosis.
    \item \textbf{Serological testing:} Limited utility for acute diagnosis due to cross-reactivity with other orthopoxviruses or prior smallpox vaccination, but useful for epidemiological studies.
    \item \textbf{Screening for co-infections:} A full sexually transmitted infection screen (including HIV, syphilis, gonorrhoea, chlamydia, and HSV) is indicated, as co-infections are common during outbreaks.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management focuses on symptom relief, preventing secondary infections, and using antivirals in severe cases.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Symptomatic care:} Adequate pain relief, including paracetamol, NSAIDs, and occasionally stronger analgesics for severe rectal or oral pain; warm sitz baths for genital or anal lesions.
    \item \textbf{Isolation and hygiene:} Patients must isolate at home until all lesions have scabbed, fallen off, and formed new skin (typically 2--4 weeks). Covering lesions with bandages helps reduce transmission risk.
    \item \textbf{Antiviral therapy:} An antiviral agent (such as tecovirimat) that inhibits viral envelope proteins, reserved for patients with severe disease, mucosal complications, or high risk of progression (such as immunocompromised individuals).
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} Oral or intravenous antibiotics only if secondary bacterial skin infection occurs.
    \item \textbf{Vaccination:} Post-exposure prophylaxis (within 4--14 days of exposure) or pre-exposure vaccination for high-risk groups using the third-generation smallpox vaccine.
    \item \textbf{Public health tracing:} Immediate notification of cases to local public health authorities to coordinate contact tracing and containment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Secondary bacterial skin infections:} Folliculitis, cellulitis, or abscesses resulting from scratching or poor wound hygiene.
    \item \textbf{Ocular complications:} Blepharitis or keratitis, which can lead to corneal ulceration and permanent vision loss if the virus is accidentally introduced into the eye.
    \item \textbf{Severe mucosal complications:} Urethral or rectal strictures due to severe ulcerative inflammation and subsequent scarring.
    \item \textbf{Respiratory complications:} Bronchopneumonia, which is more common in children and immunocompromised patients.
    \item \textbf{Sepsis:} Systemic bacterial spread from infected lesions, leading to organ dysfunction.
    \item \textbf{Neurological complications:} Encephalitis or transverse myelitis (rare but severe).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for mpox is generally favourable. For Clade II (the clade responsible for the global outbreak), the case-fatality rate is less than 0.1\%, with the vast majority of patients recovering fully within 2--4 weeks without long-term sequelae. However, Clade I is associated with a higher case-fatality rate (up to 10\% in historical African outbreaks), particularly in young children and immunocompromised hosts. Severe complications can prolong hospital stay and recovery. Some patients may experience permanent scarring or hyperpigmentation at lesion sites.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Receive the mpox vaccine if you are eligible or in a high-risk group; two doses given at least 28 days apart provide optimal protection.
    \item Avoid close, skin-to-skin contact (including sexual contact) with anyone who has a rash that looks like mpox or is a confirmed contact.
    \item Do not share bedding, towels, clothing, or eating utensils with an infected individual.
    \item Practice frequent hand hygiene by washing hands with soap and water or using an alcohol-based hand sanitiser.
    \item Cover any active skin lesions with bandages or long clothing if you must be in the same room as others, and wear a surgical mask.
    \item Avoid touching or scratching the lesions, as this can spread the virus to other parts of your body (such as your eyes) or introduce bacteria.
    \item Clean and disinfect contaminated household surfaces regularly using standard household disinfectants.
\end{itemize}
