\chapter{Warts treatment}
\index{Warts treatment}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Warts, clinically referred to as verrucae, represent benign, epidermal proliferations resulting from localized infection of keratinocytes by the Human Papillomavirus (HPV). These lesions can manifest on any cutaneous or mucosal surface of the body, characterized by cellular hyperplasia, hyperkeratosis, and papillary projections. Cutaneous warts represent a ubiquitous dermatological concern, presenting in various clinical morphologies including common warts (verrucae vulgares), plantar warts (verrucae plantares), flat warts (verrucae planae), and filiform warts. While benign and frequently self-limiting, these lesions represent a significant public health burden due to their infectious nature, capacity for autoinoculation, and potential to cause substantial physical pain, cosmetic disfigurement, and psychological distress.

The scope of warts treatment encompasses a diverse array of therapeutic modalities, ranging from conservative, patient-administered topical treatments to invasive dermatological procedures. Because warts are caused by an intraepidermal viral infection rather than a systemic process, treatment is predominantly localized and focused on either physical destruction of the infected tissue, induction of a localized inflammatory response, or chemical exfoliation of the hyperkeratotic layers. The significance of understanding warts treatment lies in the clinical challenge they present: no single treatment is universally effective, and recurrence rates remain high. Consequently, medical providers and consumer health advocates must understand the underlying pathophysiology, clinical presentation, and evidence-based therapeutic options to optimize patient care, manage expectations, and prevent complications.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of cutaneous warts demonstrates a high prevalence worldwide, affecting individuals across all geographic regions, ethnicities, and socioeconomic backgrounds. It is estimated that approximately 10\% to 12\% of the general population is actively infected with cutaneous warts at any given time. However, the distribution is not uniform across all demographics. The prevalence peaks significantly in children and young adults, with studies indicating that up to 20\% to 30\% of school-aged children (specifically between the ages of 12 and 16 years) are affected. This higher incidence in pediatric populations is attributed to immature cell-mediated immunity, increased physical contact in school and recreational environments, and a higher frequency of minor skin trauma that facilitates viral entry.

Transmission of the causative agent, HPV, occurs via direct skin-to-skin contact with infected individuals or through indirect contact with contaminated surfaces (fomites). Moist environments, such as swimming pool decks, public locker rooms, communal showers, and gymnastic mats, serve as primary vectors for indirect transmission, particularly for plantar warts. Autoinoculation, the mechanical spread of the virus from one site to another on the same individual via scratching, shaving, or nail-biting, is highly common. Certain occupational cohorts also exhibit elevated incidence rates; for example, meat, poultry, and fish handlers frequently develop a clinical variant known as ``butcher's warts'' (typically associated with HPV-7), which is exacerbated by prolonged exposure to moisture, cold, and minor occupational lacerations. Immunocompromised individuals, including organ transplant recipients on immunosuppressive regimens, patients undergoing chemotherapy, and individuals living with HIV, exhibit a significantly higher prevalence, with warts that are larger, more numerous, resistant to standard therapies, and prone to malignant transformation.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The development of warts is directly linked to infection by the Human Papillomavirus (HPV), a small, non-enveloped, double-stranded DNA virus belonging to the Papillomaviridae family. There are over 200 distinct HPV genotypes identified, which exhibit strict tissue specificity (tropism) for either cutaneous or mucosal epithelium. The primary mechanism of infection and subsequent disease progression involves a precise sequence of cellular and molecular events:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Human Papillomavirus (HPV) Genotypes}: Specific cutaneous manifestations are associated with distinct viral subtypes. Verrucae vulgares (common warts) are predominantly caused by HPV-2, HPV-4, HPV-27, and HPV-57. Verrucae plantares (plantar warts) are primarily linked to HPV-1. Verrucae planae (flat warts) are associated with HPV-3 and HPV-10, while mucosal lesions such as condylomata acuminata (genital warts) are caused by low-risk mucosal types HPV-6 and HPV-11.
    \item \textbf{Micro-abrasions and Viral Entry}: HPV cannot infect intact, healthy skin. The virus requires a disruption in the physical barrier of the skin, such as a micro-abrasion, laceration, or maceration, to gain access to the basal layer of the epidermis.
    \item \textbf{Basal Keratinocyte Infection}: The virus target cells are the slow-dividing stem cells of the basal layer (stratum basale). HPV binds to cell-surface receptors (primarily heparan sulfate proteoglycans) and enters the host cell via endocytosis. Once inside, the viral DNA remains extrachromosomal (episomal) within the basal cells, maintaining a low copy number.
    \item \textbf{Viral Replication and Epidermal Differentiation}: As the infected basal cells divide and differentiate, migrating upward through the spinous (stratum spinosum) and granular (stratum granulosum) layers, the expression of viral genes is sequentially activated. Late viral genes (L1 and L2) encode capsid proteins, which assemble with the viral genome in the upper layers of the epidermis to form infectious virions.
    \item \textbf{Hyperkeratosis, Acanthosis, and Papillomatosis}: HPV alters normal cell cycle regulation, promoting cellular proliferation in the epidermis. This leads to the characteristic histopathological features of warts: hyperkeratosis (thickening of the stratum corneum), acanthosis (thickening of the squamous layer), and papillomatosis (undulation of the epidermal-dermal junction).
    \item \textbf{Koilocytosis}: A pathognomonic feature of HPV infection is the presence of koilocytes in the upper epidermis. These are vacuolated keratinocytes with shrunken, hyperchromatic nuclei surrounded by a clear halo, representing viral cytopathic effects.
    \item \textbf{Host Immune Response}: Cell-mediated immunity, mediated by CD4+ helper T cells and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, is the primary host defense mechanism responsible for controlling HPV infection. Humoral immunity (antibodies) plays a role in preventing reinfection but is ineffective at clearing established intracellular lesions.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Cutaneous warts present with distinct morphological features depending on their anatomical location and the specific HPV genotype involved. A comprehensive understanding of these clinical presentations is essential for accurate diagnosis and selecting appropriate treatment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Common Warts (Verrucae Vulgares)}: These typically appear as firm, hyperkeratotic, exophytic papules or nodules. They have a rough, irregular, ``cauliflower-like'' surface and vary in size from a few millimeters to over a centimeter. They most commonly occur on the hands, fingers, knees, and elbows. A characteristic diagnostic sign is the presence of tiny black dots within the lesion, which represent thrombosed, dilated dermal capillaries that have been pulled upward by the proliferating papillae.
    \item \textbf{Plantar Warts (Verrucae Plantares)}: Located on the plantar surfaces of the feet, these lesions are subject to constant mechanical pressure during standing and walking. Consequently, they are pushed inward (endophytic) rather than outward. They are often covered by a thick layer of hyperkeratotic callus, which can obscure the underlying wart structure. Plantar warts can be extremely painful, particularly with lateral squeezing of the lesion rather than direct downward pressure. Unlike calluses, plantar warts disrupt the normal skin lines (dermatoglyphics) and reveal pinpoint bleeding when the superficial keratin is pared down.
    \item \textbf{Flat Warts (Verrucae Planae)}: These are small (1 to 4 millimeters), slightly elevated, flat-topped papules that are smooth and flesh-colored, pink, or light brown. They typically occur on the face, neck, dorsum of the hands, and shins. They often present in large numbers, sometimes numbering in the dozens or hundreds, and can spread linearly along scratch marks due to the Koebner phenomenon.
    \item \textbf{Filiform Warts}: Characterized by long, narrow, thread-like projections extending from a narrow base, these warts are most commonly found on the face, particularly around the eyelids, nose, lips, and neck. They are highly vascularized and can be cosmetically distressing to the patient.
    \item \textbf{Mosaic Warts}: This variant occurs when multiple plantar warts coalesce into a large, flat, tightly packed cluster. They cover a broad surface area of the foot and are notoriously resistant to standard treatments.
    \item \textbf{Periungual and Subungual Warts}: These warts develop around (periungual) or beneath (subungual) the fingernails or toenails. They can disrupt normal nail growth, lead to painful splitting of the nail plate, damage the nail matrix, and are particularly difficult to treat due to their anatomical proximity to the nail structure.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because many cutaneous lesions present with hyperkeratotic or papular features, it is critical to distinguish warts from other benign, premalignant, or malignant dermatological conditions to avoid inappropriate management.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Calluses and Corns (Helomas)}: Commonly mistaken for plantar warts. Calluses are broad areas of thickened skin caused by friction, whereas corns are smaller, concentrated areas of hyperkeratosis over bony prominences. Unlike plantar warts, calluses and corns preserve normal skin lines (dermatoglyphics), lack thrombosed capillary ``black dots,'' and are more painful upon direct downward pressure rather than lateral pinching.
    \item \textbf{Seborrheic Keratosis}: These are benign epithelial tumors that appear ``stuck-on'' the skin. They are typically brown, grey, or black, have a waxy or greasy texture, and occur in older adults on the trunk, face, or upper extremities. They lack the viral architecture and thrombosed capillaries of verrucae vulgares.
    \item \textbf{Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)}: A malignant neoplasm of keratinocytes that can closely mimic common warts, especially verrucous carcinoma, a low-grade variant of SCC. Any recalcitrant wart that fails to respond to multiple treatments, bleeds spontaneously, grows rapidly, or occurs on sun-damaged skin in an older or immunocompromised patient must undergo biopsy to rule out malignancy.
    \item \textbf{Lichen Planus}: A chronic inflammatory skin condition characterized by the ``6 Ps'': pruritic, purple, polygonal, planar, papules, and plaques. Flat-topped lichen planus lesions on the wrists or shins can resemble verrucae planae, but can be distinguished by the presence of Wickham's striae (fine white lines on the surface) and histopathological examination.
    \item \textbf{Molluscum Contagiosum}: A viral skin infection caused by a poxvirus, presenting as dome-shaped, smooth, pearly papules with a central umbilication. They occur primarily in children and can be distinguished from warts by their smooth surface and lack of hyperkeratotic scaling.
    \item \textbf{Actinic Keratosis}: A precancerous lesion caused by chronic ultraviolet radiation. They present as rough, scaly, erythematous patches on sun-exposed areas (scalp, face, forearms). They are thinner and more erythematous than common warts and occur in older demographics.
    \item \textbf{Arsenic Keratosis}: Rare, multiple hyperkeratotic papules that appear on the palms and soles years after chronic exposure to arsenic, mimicking plantar or common warts but presenting with a history of exposure and systemic signs.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Cutaneous warts are generally benign and rarely present an immediate, life-threatening emergency. However, specific clinical scenarios require evaluation by a qualified healthcare professional, and certain systemic symptoms mandate urgent or emergency medical care.

Individuals should schedule an appointment with a primary care physician or a dermatologist if they experience any of the following: the diagnosis of the lesion is uncertain (e.g., a suspicious lesion that could represent a skin malignancy); the wart is located on a sensitive area such as the face, eyelids, or genitals; the lesion does not respond to standard over-the-counter treatments after several weeks of consistent use; the wart causes significant pain that interferes with walking or daily activities; or the patient has a pre-existing medical condition such as diabetes or peripheral arterial disease, where minor foot lesions can lead to severe, non-healing ulcers.

Emergency medical evaluation is required if the wart or its surrounding tissue shows signs of severe secondary bacterial infection (such as cellulitis or lymphangitis). Patients must seek immediate care if they develop:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Spreading redness, warmth, or swelling extending rapidly from the site of the wart or treatment area.
    \item Red streaks tracking up a limb toward the body (lymphangitis).
    \item Systemic symptoms of infection, including high fever, chills, confusion, or a rapid heart rate.
    \item Uncontrolled bleeding from a wart or surgical site.
\end{itemize}
In the event of rapid onset of high fever, systemic illness, or signs of severe sepsis, patients should contact emergency services immediately by dialing the appropriate emergency number for their region, such as \textbf{local emergency services} in many countries, \textbf{911} in the United States, \textbf{999} in the United Kingdom, or \textbf{112} in the European Union.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of cutaneous warts is primarily clinical, relying on a detailed medical history and a thorough physical examination of the lesion's morphology, texture, and location. In the vast majority of cases, routine laboratory testing or imaging is not required.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visual Inspection and Palpation}: The clinician assesses the physical characteristics of the lesion, such as the hyperkeratotic surface, presence of capillary black dots, and disruption of normal skin markings. Palpation helps determine the level of tenderness, particularly with lateral compression.
    \item \textbf{Paring (Shaving)}: For hyperkeratotic lesions on the palms or soles, the clinician may use a sterile scalpel blade to gently pare down the superficial layers of dead keratin. This procedure is generally painless. In a plantar wart, paring reveals tiny pinpoint bleeding points from the underlying elongated dermal papillae containing thrombosed capillaries. In a callus or corn, paring reveals a translucent, central keratinous core without bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Dermoscopy}: A non-invasive diagnostic tool that utilizes a high-quality magnifying lens and polarized light to examine the microvascular structures of the skin. Under dermoscopy, common and plantar warts display a pathognomonic pattern of red, brown, or black dots or loops representing thrombosed capillaries, which disrupt the surrounding dermatoglyphics.
    \item \textbf{Skin Biopsy}: If the diagnosis remains ambiguous, or if there is clinical suspicion of a premalignant or malignant lesion (such as squamous cell carcinoma or verrucous carcinoma), a punch or shave biopsy should be performed. The tissue is fixed in formalin and sent for histopathological examination. Key histopathological features of verrucae include hyperkeratosis, parakeratosis, acanthosis, papillomatosis, and koilocytic cells in the upper epidermis.
    \item \textbf{Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)}: PCR assays and viral DNA typing can identify specific HPV genotypes. While highly sensitive, this molecular test is expensive and is reserved for research settings or clinical trials, rather than routine patient care.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of cutaneous warts involves a range of options, from conservative ``watchful waiting'' to highly destructive physical procedures. Because warts are benign and often self-limiting, the potential benefits of any intervention must be weighed against the risks of pain, scarring, and infection. The choice of therapy is guided by the patient's age, the location and number of warts, the duration of infection, and the patient's immune status.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Watchful Waiting (No Intervention)}: Since many warts undergo spontaneous resolution due to the host's natural immune response, observation is a reasonable option, particularly in children with asymptomatic, non-painful lesions.
    \item \textbf{Salicylic Acid}: A widely used, first-line chemical treatment available over-the-counter in concentrations ranging from 17\% to 40\% (liquids, gels, or plasters). Salicylic acid is a keratolytic agent that slowly dissolves the intercellular cement of the hyperkeratotic stratum corneum, exfoliating the infected tissue. It also induces mild localized irritation, which may stimulate a host immune response. Application requires daily compliance: the user must soak the wart in warm water, gently pare down the dead skin with a pumice stone or emery board, apply the acid, and cover the area. Treatment must be continued for up to 12 weeks.
    \item \textbf{Cryotherapy}: A common office procedure performed by dermatologists or primary care physicians using liquid nitrogen (at a temperature of $-196^{\circ}C$). The liquid nitrogen is applied via a spray gun or a cotton-tipped applicator to freeze the wart and a small margin of surrounding normal skin. This causes intracellular ice crystal formation, cellular lysis, and subsequent dermal-epidermal separation, leading to blister formation. Cryotherapy is typically performed in multiple cycles, spaced 2 to 4 weeks apart. Side effects include localized pain, blistering, hypopigmentation, and a small risk of scarring.
    \item \textbf{Cantharidin}: A naturally occurring blistering agent derived from the beetle family Meloidae. It is applied topically by a clinician directly to the wart and covered with a non-porous tape. Cantharidin causes intraepidermal acantholysis (separation of epidermal cells) without affecting the basal layer, leading to painless blister formation within 24 to 48 hours. The blister lifts the wart off the skin. Once the blister heals, the necrotic wart tissue is pared away.
    \item \textbf{Immunotherapy}: Indicated for widespread, recalcitrant, or deep-seated warts that have failed destructive therapies.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Intralesional Antigen Injections}: Injection of antigens (such as Candida antigen, mumps antigen, or Trichophyton antigen) directly into the wart. This stimulates a localized cell-mediated type IV hypersensitivity reaction, triggering the host immune system to recognize and clear the HPV virus globally.
        \item \textbf{Topical Sensitizers}: Compounds like diphencyprone (DPC) or squaric acid dibutyl ester (SADBE) are applied to the skin to induce a localized allergic contact dermatitis, recruiting lymphocytes to clear the wart.
        \item \textbf{Imiquimod}: A topical cream (typically 5\% concentration) that acts as an immune response modifier by binding to toll-like receptor 7 (TLR-7), stimulating the production of interferon-alpha and other cytokines to combat the viral infection.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Duct Tape Occlusion Therapy}: A simple, low-cost home remedy. A piece of standard duct tape is applied over the wart and left in place for six days. The tape is then removed, the area is soaked and pared with an emery board, left uncovered overnight, and a new piece of tape is applied the following morning. The mechanism is believed to involve occlusion-induced maceration and localized chemical irritation, which prompts an immune response.
    \item \textbf{Surgical Modalities}: Reserved for severe, recalcitrant cases due to the risk of scarring.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Curettage and Electrodesiccation}: The wart is scraped away using a sharp spoon-shaped instrument (curette) after local anesthesia, and the base is cauterized using electricity to control bleeding and destroy remaining viral particles.
        \item \textbf{Laser Therapy}: Carbon dioxide (CO\textsubscript{2}) lasers emit infrared light to vaporize the infected tissue, while pulsed dye lasers (PDL) target the underlying microvasculature, starving the wart of its blood supply.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Photodynamic Therapy (PDT)}: Involves the topical application of a photosensitizing agent, such as 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA), which is preferentially absorbed by the rapidly dividing infected cells. The area is subsequently exposed to a specific wavelength of light, generating singlet oxygen and other reactive oxygen species that destroy the target tissue.
    \item \textbf{Topical Retinoids}: Tretinoin cream or gel can be applied to flat warts to disrupt the abnormal keratinization process and promote cellular turnover, preventing the spread of the virus.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Although cutaneous warts are benign, complications can arise either from the progression of the untreated disease or as adverse effects of the diagnostic and therapeutic interventions.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chronic Pain and Discomfort}: Plantar warts can cause severe pain during ambulation, leading to compensatory alterations in gait. Over time, an abnormal gait can cause secondary musculoskeletal pain in the ankles, knees, hips, or lower back.
    \item \textbf{Permanent Scarring}: Aggressive physical modalities, particularly surgery, laser therapy, and deep cryotherapy, can cause thermal or mechanical damage to the dermis, resulting in scar tissue. Scarring on the soles of the feet (plantar aspect) can be particularly problematic, leading to permanent, painful pressure points that are difficult to treat.
    \item \textbf{Secondary Bacterial Infection}: Any treatment that breaks the epidermal barrier (such as blistering from cryotherapy or cantharidin, or surgical excision) creates a portal of entry for pathogenic bacteria. Common secondary infections include impetigo, cellulitis, or localized abscesses, typically caused by Staphylococcus aureus or Streptococcus pyogenes.
    \item \textbf{Nail Dystrophy and Matrix Damage}: Periungual and subungual warts that extend beneath the nail fold can exert pressure on the nail matrix. Aggressive treatment in this sensitive area can cause permanent nail dystrophy, splitting, or loss of the nail plate.
    \item \textbf{Malignant Transformation}: While rare for cutaneous warts, individuals with severe immunodeficiency (e.g., epidermodysplasia verruciformis, organ transplant recipients) are at risk of specific low-risk HPV lesions transforming into squamous cell carcinoma (Bowen's disease or verrucous carcinoma).
    \item \textbf{Psychological Distress and Social Isolation}: Warts on highly visible areas such as the face, neck, or hands can cause significant cosmetic concern, leading to low self-esteem, social anxiety, and embarrassment, particularly in adolescents and young adults.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for cutaneous warts is generally excellent, as they are benign and frequently self-limiting. In immunocompetent individuals, approximately 60\% to 70\% of warts will spontaneously resolve without any medical intervention within two years. In pediatric populations, the rate of spontaneous clearance is even higher, with many resolving within a few months due to active immune surveillance.

However, clinical outcomes can vary significantly depending on several host and viral factors. In adults, warts tend to persist longer and are less likely to resolve spontaneously. In immunocompromised patients, the prognosis is guarded; warts are frequently recalcitrant to multiple treatment modalities, spread rapidly across the body, and have a high rate of recurrence immediately after discontinuation of therapy.

Recurrence is a significant clinical challenge in warts management, with rates ranging from 20\% to 30\% across various treatment methods. This is primarily because HPV can persist in a subclinical, latent state in the histologically normal-appearing skin surrounding the treated lesion. When the local tissue environment changes or the host's immune system fluctuates, the virus can reactivate, leading to a recurrence of the wart at or near the original site. Adherence to treatment regimens, particularly patient-applied home therapies, is a key determinant of clinical success.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventative strategies and diligent self-care are essential components of managing cutaneous warts, reducing the transmission of HPV to others, and preventing autoinoculation.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Maintain Skin Barrier Integrity}: Since HPV enters through compromised skin, keeping the skin well-hydrated and moisturized helps prevent drying, cracking, and minor tears. Promptly treat underlying skin conditions like eczema or xerosis.
    \item \textbf{Avoid Direct and Indirect Contact}: Do not touch, scratch, or pick at active warts on yourself or others. Avoid sharing personal items that come into contact with the skin, such as towels, socks, shoes, pumice stones, nail files, and nail clippers.
    \item \textbf{Use Protective Footwear}: Wear waterproof sandals, flip-flops, or pool shoes when walking in high-risk, communal environments such as public swimming pool decks, locker rooms, public showers, and gymnasiums.
    \item \textbf{Practice Hand and Foot Hygiene}: Wash hands regularly, especially after touching a wart or applying medication. Keep the feet clean and dry, changing socks daily to prevent moisture accumulation (maceration) which makes the skin more vulnerable to viral entry.
    \item \textbf{Vaccination}: The HPV vaccine (such as the nonavalent vaccine Gardasil 9) is designed to protect against mucosal HPV types that cause cervical cancer and genital warts (specifically types 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58). While it is not specifically indicated for cutaneous warts (which are caused by different types like HPV-1 and HPV-2), clinical studies have shown some cross-protection and resolution of recalcitrant cutaneous warts following vaccination, likely due to a generalized upregulation of cell-mediated immunity.
    \item \textbf{Avoid Wet Shaving and Biting}: Shaving over areas with flat warts can spread the virus rapidly along the path of the razor (Koebner phenomenon). Biting or chewing fingernails adjacent to periungual warts can cause sublingual spread or oral transfer.
    \item \textbf{Disinfect Personal Care Tools}: Any tools used to pare down or treat warts (such as pumice stones, emery boards, or clippers) must be dedicated solely to that purpose, disinfected with rubbing alcohol after use, and never shared or used on healthy skin.
\end{itemize}
