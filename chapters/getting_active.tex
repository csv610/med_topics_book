\chapter{Getting Active}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Getting active means incorporating regular physical activity into daily life. Physical activity includes any movement of the body that uses energy, from brisk walking and cycling to gardening, dancing, swimming, and organised sport. Australian guidelines recommend that adults accumulate 2.5 to 5 hours of moderate-intensity activity each week, or 1.25 to 2.5 hours of vigorous activity, plus muscle-strengthening exercises on at least two days. Children and young people need at least 60 minutes of moderate to vigorous activity daily. Regular activity improves physical and mental health, reduces the risk of many chronic diseases, and supports healthy ageing. The challenge for many people is starting and sustaining an active routine.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Physical inactivity is a major public health problem. In Australia, about 4 in 10 adults do not meet the national physical activity guidelines, and inactivity contributes substantially to the burden of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, some cancers, and poor mental health. Rates of inactivity are higher in women, older people, people with lower incomes, and those living in rural or remote areas. The economic cost of physical inactivity in Australia is estimated in the billions of dollars each year. Even small increases in activity produce meaningful health benefits, and closing the activity gap is a priority for preventive health.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
This chapter concerns behaviour and lifestyle rather than a disease. The mechanisms by which activity improves health include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Metabolic effects:} exercise improves the body's handling of glucose and fats, reducing the risk of diabetes and cardiovascular disease
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular conditioning:} regular activity strengthens the heart and improves circulation and blood pressure
    \item \textbf{Musculoskeletal health:} weight-bearing and resistance exercise build and maintain muscle and bone, preventing frailty and osteoporosis
    \item \textbf{Mental health:} activity releases endorphins, reduces stress hormones, improves sleep, and protects against depression and anxiety
    \item \textbf{Weight regulation:} activity increases energy expenditure and helps maintain a healthy body composition
    \item \textbf{Cognitive benefits:} exercise supports memory, concentration, and brain health across the lifespan
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Signs that a person is inactive and could benefit from increasing activity include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Shortness of breath or fatigue with mild exertion
    \item Poor stamina and reduced ability to do daily tasks
    \item Weight gain and loss of muscle tone
    \item Stiffness, aches, and reduced flexibility
    \item Poor sleep quality and low mood
    \item Blood pressure, cholesterol, or blood glucose levels that are creeping up
    \item Feeling too tired to exercise, a common barrier that improves once activity starts
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before starting an exercise program, consider whether any underlying condition needs attention:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Unexplained chest pain, breathlessness, or palpitations, which warrant medical assessment before vigorous exercise
    \item Joint pain from arthritis or previous injury that may need tailored exercise advice
    \item Uncontrolled high blood pressure or diabetes
    \item Osteoporosis, which affects the type of exercise that is safe
    \item Cardiovascular risk factors that require a doctor's clearance for vigorous activity
    \item Depression or chronic fatigue that may need treatment alongside an activity program
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most people can start moderate activity such as walking without seeing a doctor. See a GP first if you have an existing health condition, are over 45 with risk factors, or have symptoms such as chest pain or breathlessness with minimal exertion.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, pressure, or tightness during or after activity
    \item Severe breathlessness, dizziness, or fainting during exercise
    \item Palpitations that are fast, irregular, or accompanied by collapse
    \item Any sudden severe symptom while exercising
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Getting active rarely requires tests, but assessment helps tailor a safe and effective plan.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Health check:} blood pressure, weight, and review of risk factors and any conditions affecting exercise
    \item \textbf{Pre-exercise screening:} simple questionnaires such as the Adult Pre-exercise Screening System (APSS) identify people who need medical clearance
    \item \textbf{Readiness assessment:} considering current fitness, time available, and personal preferences
    \item \textbf{Goal setting:} agreeing realistic, measurable goals, such as walking 30 minutes a day
    \item \textbf{Specialist input:} an exercise physiologist or physiotherapist can design programs for people with medical conditions or injuries
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The key to getting active is starting gradually, choosing activities you enjoy, and building a routine that fits your life.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Start small:} begin with short sessions, such as 10-minute walks, and build up gradually
    \item \textbf{Aim for the guidelines:} work toward 2.5--5 hours of moderate activity, or 1.25--2.5 hours of vigorous activity, weekly
    \item \textbf{Add strength training:} include muscle-strengthening activities on at least two days each week
    \item \textbf{Incorporate incidental activity:} take stairs, walk part of the way to work, and break up sitting time
    \item \textbf{Choose enjoyable activities:} you are far more likely to sustain exercise you enjoy
    \item \textbf{Set a routine:} schedule activity like an appointment and track progress
    \item \textbf{Use support:} exercising with a friend, joining a group, or using an accredited exercise professional improves adherence
    \item \textbf{Be flexible:} if you miss a day, resume the next day rather than giving up
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The risks of inactivity far outweigh the risks of exercise, but some cautions apply:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Overuse injuries from increasing activity too quickly
    \item Muscle soreness in the first few weeks, which usually settles
    \item Cardiac events during unaccustomed vigorous exercise in people with undiagnosed heart disease, which is why gradual progression and screening matter
    \item Aggravation of existing joint conditions if the wrong activities are chosen
    \item Falls in older people attempting unsuitable exercise without guidance
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The benefits of becoming active are rapid and substantial. Within weeks, fitness, mood, energy, and sleep improve. Long-term, regular activity substantially reduces the risk of heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes, many cancers, osteoporosis, and depression, and adds years to life expectancy. People who are inactive and then increase their activity gain the largest health benefits. The most important predictor of long-term success is finding activity that is enjoyable and sustainable rather than aiming for intensity.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Set a realistic goal and write it down
    \item Build activity into daily routines, such as walking after meals
    \item Choose activities that are practical, affordable, and enjoyable
    \item Start gradually and progress slowly to avoid injury
    \item Include variety to maintain interest and work different muscles
    \item Wear appropriate footwear and clothing
    \item Keep hydrated and be mindful of extreme heat or cold
    \item Track your progress with an app, pedometer, or diary
    \item Seek professional guidance if you have health conditions or injuries
    \item Celebrate milestones and focus on how activity makes you feel, not just numbers
\end{itemize}
