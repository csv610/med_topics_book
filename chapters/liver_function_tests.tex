\chapter{Liver function tests}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Liver function tests (LFTs) are a group of blood biochemical measurements designed to assess the physiological state of the liver. They are used to detect hepatic injury, evaluate hepatic synthetic capacity, monitor the progression of known liver diseases, and track the safety of hepatotoxic medications. Although named 'function tests', many of the parameters measured in a standard LFT panel actually serve as markers of cellular damage or biliary obstruction rather than functional capacity. A standard LFT panel includes enzymes that indicate hepatocyte injury (alanine aminotransferase [ALT] and aspartate aminotransferase [AST]), enzymes indicating biliary tract pathology (alkaline phosphatase [ALP] and gamma-glutamyl transferase [GGT]), and substances excreted or synthesised by the liver (bilirubin and albumin).

\section*{Epidemiology}
Liver function tests are among the most frequently requested laboratory investigations in clinical medicine. Abnormal LFT results are common, occurring in approximately 10\% of the healthy, asymptomatic population. In Australia and other Western nations, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD, formerly NAFLD) and alcohol-related liver disease represent the most common causes of incidentally discovered abnormal LFTs. Screening is highly utilized in populations with metabolic syndrome, diabetes, obesity, and those taking long-term medications.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The biological markers in an LFT panel reflect specific cellular compartments and pathophysiological processes in the liver and biliary system. Key markers and their clinical significance include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Alanine aminotransferase (ALT):} An enzyme found predominantly inside hepatocytes; its release into the bloodstream is a highly specific marker of hepatocyte necrosis or cell membrane damage.
    \textbf{Aspartate aminotransferase (AST):} An intracellular enzyme present in hepatocytes, but also in cardiac muscle, skeletal muscle, and red blood cells; elevations can occur in hepatic injury as well as extrahepatic conditions (e.g.\ myocardial infarction or rhabdomyolysis).
    \item \textbf{Alkaline phosphatase (ALP):} An enzyme located on the canalicular membrane of hepatocytes and biliary epithelial cells, but also found in bone, placenta, and intestines. Elevation typically indicates cholestasis (impaired bile flow) or high bone turnover.
    \item \textbf{Gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT):} A membrane-bound enzyme in the biliary tree; it is a highly sensitive marker of biliary irritation and is also induced by alcohol consumption and certain medications (e.g.\ phenytoin).
    \item \textbf{Bilirubin:} A breakdown product of red blood cell haemoglobin; it is conjugated in the liver and excreted in bile. Elevated bilirubin causes clinical jaundice and indicates hepatocyte dysfunction, biliary obstruction, or increased haemolysis.
    \item \textbf{Albumin:} A major plasma protein synthesised exclusively by the liver. Because albumin has a half-life of approximately 20 days, a decrease in its level is a marker of chronic liver dysfunction or systemic inflammation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
A patient requiring LFTs may be entirely asymptomatic, or may present with clinical features indicative of underlying hepatobiliary pathology. Clinical features include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Jaundice:} Yellow discolouration of the skin and sclera (scleral icterus) caused by accumulation of bilirubin in tissues, typically visible when serum bilirubin exceeds 35--50 $\mu$mol/L.
    \item \textbf{Excretory changes:} Dark, tea-coloured urine (due to renal excretion of conjugated bilirubin) and pale, clay-coloured stools (acholic stools, due to lack of stercobilin in the gut from biliary obstruction).
    \item \textbf{Pruritus:} Generalized, intense itching without a primary skin rash, caused by the deposition of bile salts in the skin.
    \item \textbf{Abdominal pain:} Right upper quadrant or epigastric pain, which may be constant (hepatic capsule stretch) or colicky (gallstone disease).
    \item \textbf{Systemic symptoms:} Chronic fatigue, nausea, anorexia, abdominal distension (ascites), and easy bruising or bleeding.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Interpreting abnormal LFTs requires categorising the abnormalities into distinct patterns to narrow down the differential diagnosis:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hepatocellular pattern (ALT/AST elevated out of proportion to ALP):} Indicates direct damage to liver cells, commonly caused by viral hepatitis (A, B, C, D, E), drug-induced liver injury, autoimmune hepatitis, alcoholic hepatitis, or ischaemic hepatitis.
    \item \textbf{Cholestatic pattern (ALP and GGT elevated out of proportion to ALT/AST):} Indicates obstruction or inflammation of the bile ducts, caused by choledocholithiasis (common bile duct stones), pancreatic cancer, primary biliary cholangitis (PBC), or primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC).
    \item \textbf{Isolated hyperbilirubinemia:} Elevated bilirubin with normal liver enzymes, which must be differentiated into unconjugated (commonly Gilbert's syndrome or haemolytic anaemia) and conjugated forms (e.g.\ Dubin-Johnson syndrome).
    \item \textbf{Isolated GGT elevation:} Characterised by normal transaminases and ALP, frequently associated with high alcohol intake, non-alcoholic fatty liver, or enzyme-inducing drugs.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients with stable, mildly abnormal LFTs should follow up with their general practitioner. However, certain clinical signs indicate acute hepatic failure or severe biliary infection.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rapidly developing jaundice associated with new-onset confusion, sleepiness, or personality changes (indicating acute liver failure and encephalopathy).
    \item High fever, severe right upper quadrant abdominal pain, and jaundice (Charcot's triad, indicating ascending cholangitis).
    \item Persistent vomiting that prevents oral hydration.
    \item Signs of severe coagulopathy, such as vomiting blood, passing black stools, or spontaneous, uncontrollable nosebleeds.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
If an initial LFT panel is abnormal, subsequent investigations are structured to confirm the diagnosis and assess liver architecture:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Coagulation panel (INR/PT):} The most sensitive functional test of acute liver synthetic capacity; a prolonged INR indicates severe hepatic impairment.
    \item \textbf{Viral hepatitis serology:} Testing for Hepatitis A antibodies, Hepatitis B surface antigen, and Hepatitis C antibodies to rule out viral infections.
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune liver screen:} Checking for antinuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-smooth muscle antibodies (ASMA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies (AMA).
    \item \textbf{Metabolic and iron studies:} Testing serum iron, transferrin saturation, and ferritin (for haemochromatosis), alongside caeruloplasmin (for Wilson's disease in younger patients).
    \item \textbf{Abdominal ultrasound:} The primary imaging modality to assess liver size, echogenicity (fatty change), presence of focal lesions, gallstones, and biliary tree dilation.
    \item \textbf{MRCP or ERCP:} Indicated in cholestatic patterns to detail biliary tract anatomy (MRCP) or perform therapeutic interventions (ERCP).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is directed at the underlying cause of the abnormal LFTs:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cessation of hepatotoxic agents:} Immediately stopping alcohol, suspected prescription medications, over-the-counter drugs, and herbal supplements.
    \item \textbf{Medical treatment of viral and autoimmune disease:} Administering antiviral agents for chronic Hepatitis B or C, or corticosteroids and immunosuppressants for autoimmune hepatitis.
    \item \textbf{Biliary decompression:} Endoscopic removal of obstructing gallstones or stenting of strictures via ERCP.
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle modifications:} Recommending weight loss, a balanced low-fat diet, and regular physical exercise for patients with abnormal LFTs secondary to MASLD.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Failure to investigate and manage abnormal LFTs can result in progressive, irreversible liver disease:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Progression to cirrhosis:} Chronic hepatocyte inflammation leading to widespread fibrosis, portal hypertension, and hepatic failure.
    \item \textbf{Acute hepatic encephalopathy:} Brain dysfunction due to accumulation of toxic substances (ammonia) normally cleared by the liver.
    \item \textbf{Ascending cholangitis and sepsis:} A life-threatening bacterial infection arising from untreated biliary obstruction.
    \item \textbf{Hepatocellular carcinoma:} Development of primary liver cancer, particularly in patients with chronically elevated transaminases due to HBV or HCV.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for individuals with abnormal LFTs depends entirely on the cause, the extent of liver damage, and the response to interventions. Transient elevations due to mild viral illnesses or self-limiting drug reactions have an excellent prognosis, with enzymes returning to baseline within weeks. If the abnormal LFTs reflect chronic diseases like cirrhosis or advanced malignancy, the prognosis is guarded and requires specialist management.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
To protect liver health and maintain normal liver enzyme levels, individuals should:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Limit alcohol consumption:} Follow national guidelines (e.g.\ no more than 10 standard drinks per week in Australia) and avoid binge drinking.
    \item \textbf{Review all medications and supplements:} Inform doctors of all herbal products and over-the-counter supplements, which are common causes of drug-induced liver injury.
    \item \textbf{Use paracetamol safely:} Strictly adhere to dosing guidelines (maximum 4 g per day in healthy adults) and avoid paracetamol when consuming alcohol.
    \item \textbf{Achieve and maintain a healthy weight:} Combine a balanced diet (such as the Mediterranean diet) with regular exercise to prevent fat deposition in the liver.
    \item \textbf{Prevent viral hepatitis:} Get vaccinated against Hepatitis A and B, and avoid sharing personal grooming items like razors or toothbrushes.
\end{itemize}
