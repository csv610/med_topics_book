\chapter{Animal Bites}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An animal bite is a wound caused by the teeth of an animal, most commonly a dog, a cat, or another human. Bites range from minor scratches and abrasions to deep puncture wounds with damage to skin, muscle, tendon, bone, or joints. Their principal medical importance is infection: the mouths of animals and humans carry large numbers of bacteria, and a bite can introduce these organisms deep into the tissues, where they are difficult to treat. Rare but serious diseases such as rabies and tetanus can also be transmitted by bites.

Most bites are survivable with simple care, and prompt first aid plus professional assessment substantially reduces the risk of complications. Assessment is particularly important for bites on the hands, face, or joints, for cat and human bites, and for bites from wild or unfamiliar animals.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Dog bites are the most common animal bites, with tens of millions occurring worldwide each year. They most often affect children and young adults, and the majority are inflicted by a family pet or a familiar dog. Cat bites are less frequent but carry a higher risk of infection because the sharp, slender teeth create deep puncture wounds that seal over, trapping bacteria inside. Human bites occur in fights, during sport, and in children's play, and a particular form, the "clenched-fist injury" caused when a fist strikes teeth, is notorious for serious infection. Bites from wild animals are less common but pose greater risks, including rabies in affected regions.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Bites cause harm through two combined mechanisms: direct mechanical injury and inoculation of bacteria into the wound.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dog bites:} often tearing and crushing injuries that damage skin, muscle, and vessels; the crushing component reduces local blood supply and increases infection risk
    \item \textbf{Cat bites:} sharp teeth produce deep, narrow puncture wounds that close over quickly, sealing bacteria inside
    \item \textbf{Human bites:} carry a heavy load of mouth bacteria; clenched-fist injuries are particularly prone to deep infection because the impact drives organisms into joints and tendons
    \item \textbf{Wild animal bites:} risk of rabies and other zoonotic infections depending on the animal and region
    \item \textbf{Common pathogens:} \textit{Pasteurella multocida} (cats and dogs), \textit{Staphylococcus aureus}, \textit{Streptococcus} species, and anaerobes; human bites also carry \textit{Eikenella corrodens} and other organisms
    \item \textbf{Local tissue damage:} crushing, devascularisation, and haematoma formation impair the ability of immune defences to clear organisms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The appearance and consequences of a bite depend on its site, depth, and the animal involved:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A visible wound: puncture, laceration, tear, scratch, or crush injury
    \item Pain, swelling, and bruising around the bite
    \item Bleeding, which can be heavy if a major vessel is injured
    \item Damage to tendons, nerves, bones, or joints, particularly over the hands, feet, and face
    \item Signs of infection developing over hours to days: increasing pain, spreading redness, heat, swelling, or purulent discharge
    \item Fever, chills, and a feeling of being unwell with more severe or spreading infection
    \item Rarely, features of systemic infection such as rabies (agitation, difficulty swallowing, confusion) or tetanus (muscle spasms and stiffness)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Considerations after a bite include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Simple contamination versus established wound infection
    \item Superficial injury versus damage to tendon, nerve, bone, or joint
    \item Cellulitis versus abscess, septic arthritis, tenosynovitis, or osteomyelitis
    \item A true human bite versus a friction blister or other injury sustained in a fight
    \item Rabies risk after bat, fox, or other wild animal bites
    \item Whether tetanus immunisation is up to date
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical assessment for any bite that is deep, located on the face or hands, made by a cat or a human, or inflicted by a wild or unfamiliar animal. Also see a doctor if there is heavy bleeding, if you cannot move a finger, toe, or joint normally, or if you are unsure whether your tetanus vaccination is current.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services), or attend an emergency department, for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bites with severe bleeding that will not stop with direct pressure
    \item Bites to the face, eyes, neck, or genitals
    \item Deep puncture wounds, especially cat bites or bites over a joint
    \item Bites from bats, foxes, or other wild animals (rabies risk)
    \item Signs of spreading infection: enlarging redness, swelling, fever, or discharge
    \item Numbness, weakness, or inability to move the affected area
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the type of animal, whether the bite was provoked, the time of the bite, tetanus and rabies vaccination status, and the person's medical history including diabetes, immune compromise, and medicines
    \item \textbf{Examination:} careful assessment of the wound, its depth, and the function of nearby tendons, nerves, and joints; palpation for tenderness over bone or joint
    \item \textbf{Wound swabs and cultures:} taken when infection is already present, to guide antibiotic choice
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} X-ray to detect fractures, embedded foreign bodies such as teeth, or gas in the tissues; ultrasound or MRI may help in suspected deep infection
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} inflammatory markers and blood cultures if systemic infection is suspected
    \item \textbf{Rabies risk assessment:} based on the animal species, the geographical region, and the circumstances of the exposure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immediate first aid:} wash the wound thoroughly with soap and running water, control bleeding with pressure, and cover with a clean dressing
    \item \textbf{Professional wound cleaning:} irrigation with large volumes of saline to remove bacteria, debris, and devitalised tissue
    \item \textbf{Debridement:} surgical removal of crushed or dead tissue; heavily contaminated wounds may be left open initially
    \item \textbf{Suturing decisions:} many bite wounds are not closed immediately, particularly punctures and hand wounds; closure may be delayed until infection has been excluded
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} preventive antibiotics are commonly given for cat and human bites, bites on the hands, and bites in people at higher risk (such as those with diabetes or immune suppression)
    \item \textbf{Tetanus prophylaxis:} a booster dose is given if the last dose was more than five to ten years ago, with immunoglobulin where indicated
    \item \textbf{Rabies prevention:} post-exposure vaccination, and sometimes rabies immunoglobulin, after high-risk exposures
    \item \textbf{Elevation and rest:} elevation of the affected limb reduces swelling, especially for hand and leg bites
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review within 24--48 hours to check for early signs of infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wound infection: cellulitis and abscess formation
    \item Deep infection: septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, tenosynovitis, or necrotising fasciitis
    \item Spread of infection to the bloodstream (sepsis)
    \item Rabies, which is almost always fatal once symptoms develop but is preventable with prompt post-exposure treatment
    \item Tetanus, which is preventable by vaccination
    \item Scarring, including disfiguring facial scars
    \item Injury to tendons, nerves, or joints with permanent loss of function
    \item Psychological effects, particularly after a dog attack involving a child
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most animal bites heal without significant problems, particularly when the wound is cleaned promptly and treated appropriately. When infection occurs it usually develops within 24--48 hours and resolves with antibiotics, although deep or hand infections may require drainage or surgery. The outlook is worse when treatment is delayed, when bites involve bones, joints, or the hand, or when the person has weakened immunity. With prompt and appropriate care, serious complications such as rabies and tetanus are almost entirely preventable, and full functional recovery is the norm.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Supervise children closely around animals, including family pets
    \item Learn to read animal body language and avoid startling, cornering, or teasing animals
    \item Do not approach or feed stray, wild, or unfamiliar animals
    \item Keep pets vaccinated and cared for appropriately, including rabies vaccination where relevant
    \item Never disturb a dog while it is eating, sleeping, or caring for puppies
    \item Avoid rough play that encourages biting
    \item Keep tetanus immunisation up to date
    \item After any bite, wash the wound thoroughly with soap and running water immediately, and seek medical advice for high-risk wounds
\end{itemize}
