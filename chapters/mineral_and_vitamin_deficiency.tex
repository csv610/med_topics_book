\chapter{Mineral and vitamin deficiency}
\index{Mineral and vitamin deficiency}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Mineral and vitamin deficiencies occur when the body does not obtain or absorb the required amount of essential micronutrients necessary for optimal physiological function, growth, and cellular repair. Vitamins are organic compounds that serve as coenzymes or regulators in metabolic pathways (divided into fat-soluble vitamins, such as A, D, E, and K, and water-soluble vitamins, such as the B-complex and vitamin C). Minerals are inorganic elements required in macro quantities (such as calcium, magnesium, and phosphorus) or micro quantities (trace minerals like iron, zinc, iodine, selenium, and copper). Deficiencies can lead to specific, well-characterised clinical syndromes—such as scurvy (vitamin C deficiency), rickets or osteomalacia (vitamin D deficiency), pellagra (vitamin B3 deficiency), and iron-deficiency anaemia—and can significantly impair immune response, cognitive function, and musculoskeletal health if left untreated.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Globally, micronutrient deficiencies represent a major public health challenge, frequently referred to as "hidden hunger," affecting more than two billion people. In many countries and other developed nations, while severe starvation-induced deficiency is rare, subclinical deficiencies remain highly prevalent in specific risk groups. Iron deficiency is the most common nutrient deficiency worldwide, particularly affecting menstruating females, pregnant women, and toddlers. Vitamin D deficiency is also widespread, with local surveys indicating that approximately 20--30\% of adults have mild, moderate, or severe deficiency, rising significantly during winter and among institutionalised or dark-skinned populations. Folate deficiency is less common due to mandatory fortification programmes, but vitamin B12 deficiency is frequently observed in elderly individuals (affecting up to 10--15\% of those over 65) and individuals adhering to strict vegan diets without supplementation.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Micronutrient deficiencies arise through several primary pathways, including inadequate dietary intake, impaired gastrointestinal absorption, increased physiological demands, or excessive loss.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inadequate dietary intake:} Diets low in nutrient-dense foods, such as fresh fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and lean proteins, or restrictive eating patterns (e.g., poorly planned veganism or severe calorie restriction) lead to direct intake deficits.
    \item \textbf{Malabsorption syndromes:} Gastrointestinal conditions such as coeliac disease, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, and short bowel syndrome impair the mucosal absorption of vitamins and minerals. Bariatric surgeries (e.g., gastric bypass) also severely restrict absorption surfaces.
    \item \textbf{Increased metabolic demand:} Rapid growth phases during infancy, childhood, and adolescence, as well as pregnancy and lactation, dramatically elevate the body's daily requirements for micronutrients like iron, folate, calcium, and iodine.
    \item \textbf{Excessive excretion or loss:} Chronic blood loss (e.g., from heavy menstruation, peptic ulcers, or gastrointestinal malignancies) depletes iron stores. Similarly, chronic alcohol consumption impairs the absorption and enhances the urinary excretion of thiamine (vitamin B1), folate, and magnesium.
    \item \textbf{Drug-nutrient interactions:} Certain long-term medications interfere with nutrient metabolism; for example, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) reduce stomach acid required for vitamin B12 and calcium absorption, while metformin can impair B12 absorption.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of mineral and vitamin deficiencies is highly variable, depending on the specific nutrient depleted, the severity of the deficiency, and the rate of depletion.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{General and constitutional symptoms:} Persistent fatigue, generalised weakness, lethargy, cold intolerance (common in iron deficiency), and unexplained weight loss or poor growth in children.
    \item \textbf{Dermatological signs:} Pallor of the skin and conjunctivae (iron or folate deficiency), dry skin, brittle nails, spoon-shaped nails (koilonychia in iron deficiency), easy bruising, petechiae (vitamin C deficiency), and poor wound healing.
    \item \textbf{Oral and mucocutaneous changes:} Glossitis (smooth, red, painful tongue), angular cheilitis (cracks at the corners of the mouth), and bleeding, swollen gums (vitamin C deficiency).
    \item \textbf{Neurological and cognitive manifestations:} Peripheral neuropathy, paraesthesia ("pins and needles"), memory impairment, irritability, and ataxia, which are hallmark signs of vitamin B12 or thiamine deficiency.
    \item \textbf{Musculoskeletal symptoms:} Diffuse bone pain, muscle weakness, and muscle spasms or tetany (indicative of calcium, magnesium, or vitamin D deficiency).
    \item \textbf{Visual changes:} Impaired night vision (night blindness) and dry eyes (xerophthalmia), characteristic of vitamin A deficiency.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the symptoms of micronutrient deficiency are often non-specific (such as fatigue or neuropathy), they must be carefully distinguished from other systemic and organic conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chronic fatigue syndrome:} Presents with profound fatigue and cognitive difficulties but is not corrected by micronutrient supplementation.
    \item \textbf{Hypothyroidism:} Characterised by fatigue, cold intolerance, dry skin, and weight gain, requiring differentiation via thyroid function tests.
    \item \textbf{Fibromyalgia:} Causes widespread musculoskeletal pain and tenderness similar to osteomalacia, but lacks the biochemical markers of vitamin D or calcium deficiency.
    \item \textbf{Diabetic peripheral neuropathy:} Presents with sensory loss and paraesthesia matching vitamin B12 deficiency, but is associated with chronic hyperglycaemia and normal B12 levels.
    \item \textbf{Depressive disorders:} Can manifest with low energy, poor concentration, and sleep disturbances, which overlap with B-complex deficiencies.
    \item \textbf{Primary haematological malignancies:} Such as myelodysplastic syndromes, which present with macrocytic anaemia similar to folate or vitamin B12 deficiency.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should seek medical assessment if they experience persistent fatigue, unexplained weight loss, sensory changes in their extremities, recurrent mouth ulcers, or visual changes.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, acute neurological changes, such as sudden confusion, disorientation, or inability to walk (suggestive of Wernicke's encephalopathy due to severe thiamine deficiency).
    \item Sudden loss of consciousness, seizures, or muscle tetany (which can occur with severe hypocalcaemia).
    \item Severe chest pain, shortness of breath, or palpitations associated with profound anaemia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnostic evaluation relies on a combination of detailed dietary history, physical examination, and targeted laboratory investigations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Full blood count (FBC) and blood film:} Identifies microcytic anaemia (suggestive of iron deficiency) or macrocytic anaemia (suggestive of B12 or folate deficiency), along with hypersegmented neutrophils.
    \item \textbf{Iron studies:} Includes serum ferritin (the most sensitive marker for iron stores), transferrin, total iron-binding capacity (TIBC), and transferrin saturation.
    \item \textbf{Serum vitamin levels:} Direct measurement of active serum vitamin B12, red cell folate, serum folate, vitamin D (measured as 25-hydroxyvitamin D), and vitamin A.
    \item \textbf{Serum electrolytes and minerals:} Measurement of serum calcium (corrected for albumin), magnesium, phosphate, and zinc levels.
    \item \textbf{Active metabolites and functional tests:} Methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine levels can confirm intracellular B12 or folate deficiency when serum levels are borderline.
    \item \textbf{Malabsorption testing:} Coeliac serology (tissue transglutaminase IgA) and endoscopic biopsy if malabsorption is suspected as the primary cause.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The primary goal of management is to correct the specific deficiency, address the underlying cause, and prevent future recurrences through dietary and medical interventions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Targeted supplementation:} Administration of oral, intramuscular, or intravenous nutrients depending on the severity and cause of the deficiency (e.g., oral ferrous sulphate for iron deficiency, intramuscular hydroxocobalamin for pernicious anaemia).
    \item \textbf{Dietary modification:} Collaborating with an accredited practising dietitian to develop a nutrient-dense, balanced eating plan that incorporates foods rich in the deficient nutrients.
    \item \textbf{Treating the underlying cause:} Managing gastrointestinal diseases (e.g., strict gluten-free diet for coeliac disease) or adjusting medications that impair absorption.
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} Encouraging safe sun exposure for vitamin D synthesis, reducing alcohol consumption, and avoiding food-drug combinations that inhibit absorption (e.g., consuming tea or coffee with iron-rich meals).
    \item \textbf{Regular monitoring:} Serial blood tests to track nutrient levels, ensure therapeutic efficacy, and monitor for potential toxicity from over-supplementation of fat-soluble vitamins.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated or chronic micronutrient deficiencies can result in significant long-term morbidity and irreversible physiological damage.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurological impairment:} Irreversible subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord (due to vitamin B12 deficiency) and permanent cognitive decline.
    \item \textbf{Osteopenia and osteoporosis:} Increased risk of bone fractures and skeletal deformities (such as rickets in children) from chronic calcium and vitamin D depletion.
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular strain:} Severe, chronic anaemia can lead to high-output cardiac failure and exacerbation of underlying coronary artery disease.
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy complications:} Neural tube defects in the foetus due to maternal folate deficiency, and low birth weight or premature birth due to iron deficiency.
    \item \textbf{Impaired immunity:} Increased susceptibility to severe, recurrent infections from deficiencies in vitamins A, C, and zinc.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With timely diagnosis and appropriate supplementation, the prognosis for the majority of mineral and vitamin deficiencies is excellent. Symptoms such as fatigue and mild anaemia typically begin to improve within weeks of starting therapy. Neurological symptoms from vitamin B12 deficiency may take several months to resolve, and in cases where the deficiency has been severe and long-standing, some residual neurological deficits may persist. Adherence to dietary recommendations and regular monitoring prevents relapse, ensuring long-term recovery and standard life expectancy.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventative measures are focused on maintaining a diverse diet and monitoring risk factors.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Eat a varied diet:} Incorporate a wide range of whole foods, including leafy green vegetables, citrus fruits, whole grains, nuts, seeds, lean meats, and dairy products.
    \item \textbf{Safe sun exposure:} Achieve adequate vitamin D levels through brief, safe sun exposure, following local cancer council guidelines to balance UV exposure and skin protection.
    \item \textbf{Proactive supplementation:} Use appropriate supplements if adhering to restrictive diets (e.g., vitamin B12 for vegans) or during key life stages such as pregnancy (folate).
    \item \textbf{Limit alcohol intake:} Minimise alcohol consumption to reduce the risk of nutritional depletion and impaired gastrointestinal absorption.
    \item \textbf{Understand food pairings:} Pair iron-rich foods with vitamin C (e.g., citrus or bell peppers) to enhance absorption, and avoid drinking tea or coffee during meals.
\end{itemize}
