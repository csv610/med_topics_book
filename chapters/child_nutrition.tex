\chapter{Child Nutrition}
\index{Child Nutrition}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Child nutrition is the study and practice of feeding infants, children, and adolescents so that they grow, develop, and stay healthy. Children need energy, protein, vitamins, minerals, and fluids in the right amounts for their age, and these needs change rapidly as they grow. Good nutrition in childhood supports brain development, strong bones, a healthy immune system, and a healthy weight, and it establishes eating habits that may last a lifetime. Poor nutrition -- whether from eating too little, eating too much, or eating too narrow a range of foods -- can harm growth and development at any age.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Nutrition problems affect children at both ends of the spectrum. Worldwide, millions of children under 5 are underweight or stunted, mainly in low-income countries, while in high-income countries about one in four or five children is overweight or obese. Iron deficiency is common in toddlers and adolescent girls, and vitamin D deficiency affects many children in regions with limited sun exposure. Picky eating affects a large proportion of toddlers and is usually a passing phase, but it can occasionally lead to poor growth. Food allergy and intolerance affect a smaller minority of children and require careful management.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The causes of poor child nutrition vary with the type of problem:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inadequate intake:} poverty, food insecurity, restrictive diets, or severe picky eating
    \item \textbf{Malabsorption:} conditions such as coeliac disease, chronic diarrhoea, or inflammatory bowel disease reducing nutrient uptake
    \item \textbf{Increased requirements:} periods of rapid growth, chronic illness, or recovery from infection
    \item \textbf{Excess intake:} large portions, frequent sugary drinks, and highly processed foods leading to overweight
    \item \textbf{Feeding difficulties:} problems with chewing, swallowing, or sensory aversion to certain textures
    \item \textbf{Hormonal and genetic factors:} rare disorders of growth or metabolism
\end{itemize}

When children do not get the nutrients they need, growth slows, immunity weakens, and development can be affected. When they take in more energy than they use, the excess is stored as fat, gradually promoting overweight and its metabolic consequences.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Signs that a child's nutrition may be a concern include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Poor weight gain, slow growth, or weight loss
    \item Constant tiredness, pale skin, or poor concentration (possible iron or other deficiencies)
    \item Fatigue, muscle cramps, or bone pain (possible vitamin D deficiency)
    \item Steady or rapid weight gain above the expected range
    \item Diarrhoea, bloating, or poor growth in coeliac disease
    \item Very restricted eating or distress at mealtimes
    \item Loose teeth, tooth decay, or gum problems from frequent sugar
\end{itemize}

Many children with poor nutrition show no obvious symptoms at first, which is why growth is measured routinely at child health checks.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Constitutional small stature or a slow growth phase compared with undernutrition
    \item Normal picky eating compared with an eating disorder or a sensory feeding disorder
    \item Coeliac disease or other gut disease compared with simple feeding problems
    \item Underlying illness (such as heart or kidney disease or chronic infection) causing poor growth
    \item Overweight compared with fluid retention or a hormonal condition such as an underactive thyroid
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor or child health nurse if your child is losing weight, falling off their growth curve, refusing many foods, has ongoing digestive symptoms, or if you are worried about what or how much they are eating. Seek advice early rather than waiting, since growth and development are time-sensitive.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of severe dehydration or malnutrition, such as a very dry mouth, sunken eyes, or little urine
    \item A child who cannot keep any fluids down or is unusually floppy and drowsy
    \item Choking or trouble breathing during eating
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} a detailed account of what and how the child eats, activity level, symptoms, and family growth patterns
    \item \textbf{Growth measurement:} weight, height or length, and head circumference plotted on growth charts over time
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} general appearance, body proportions, and signs of nutrient deficiency
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} iron studies, vitamin D levels, and other checks where deficiency is suspected
    \item \textbf{Investigations for malabsorption:} coeliac screening or other tests if gut disease is possible
    \item \textbf{Dietary review:} referral to a dietitian for a detailed assessment and an individualised plan
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Age-appropriate feeding:} breast milk or formula in infancy, with iron-rich solids introduced around 6 months
    \item \textbf{Dietary guidance:} a balanced plate with vegetables, fruit, grains, protein, and dairy or alternatives
    \item \textbf{Dietitian review:} for children who are underweight, overweight, or have restricted diets
    \item \textbf{Treating the underlying cause:} a gluten-free diet for coeliac disease, supplements for iron or vitamin D deficiency
    \item \textbf{Managing picky eating:} regular meal times, offering new foods without pressure, and patience
    \item \textbf{Family-based changes:} whole-family eating habits, limits on sugary drinks and screen-time snacking
    \item \textbf{Ongoing monitoring:} regular weight and height checks to track progress
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Poor growth and delayed development in undernutrition
    \item Weakened immunity and more frequent infections
    \item Iron deficiency anaemia, affecting energy and concentration
    \item Vitamin D deficiency and weakened bones (rickets in severe cases)
    \item In excess: overweight, obesity, and the conditions that follow, such as type 2 diabetes
    \item Tooth decay from frequent sugar and sugary drinks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With early help, most children with nutrition problems recover fully. Catching up on growth is usually possible when the cause is addressed, although prolonged undernutrition early in life can have lasting effects on height and development. Overweight children often respond well to family-based changes, and most can return to a healthy weight range. Feeding problems such as picky eating usually improve with a consistent, relaxed approach. Long-term success depends on changes that the whole family can sustain.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Offer a wide variety of foods from early on, including vegetables and fruit every day
    \item Breastfeed if possible; introduce iron-rich solids around 6 months
    \item Let children decide how much to eat; avoid forcing or bribing
    \item Limit sugary drinks, juice, and packaged snacks
    \item Eat meals together as a family whenever possible
    \item Give a safe, low-dose vitamin supplement only if advised, such as vitamin D in some settings
    \item Keep the child active and limit screen time
    \item Attend scheduled child health checks so growth is monitored
\end{itemize}
