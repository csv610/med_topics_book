\chapter{Breast Reconstruction With an Implant or Tissue Expander}
\index{Breast Reconstruction With an Implant or Tissue Expander}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Breast reconstruction is surgery to rebuild the shape of a breast after a \textbf{mastectomy} -- surgical removal of the breast -- most often as part of treatment for breast cancer, and occasionally after risk-reducing mastectomy. One of the most common methods is \textbf{implant-based reconstruction}, in which a silicone or saline implant recreates the breast mound. When the skin and muscle are not sufficient to accept an implant immediately -- as is often the case after a large mastectomy or when radiotherapy is planned -- a \textbf{tissue expander} is used first: a balloon-like device placed beneath the chest muscle and gradually filled with saline over weeks to months, stretching the overlying tissue until it can accommodate a permanent implant. Reconstruction may be performed at the same time as the mastectomy (immediate) or as a separate operation later (delayed), and it can be combined with, or chosen instead of, reconstruction using the person's own tissue. The choice depends on the cancer treatment plan, body habitus, and the individual's preferences, and is best made jointly with the surgical and oncology teams.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Implant-based reconstruction is the most commonly used method of breast reconstruction after mastectomy in many countries, and is performed more often than autologous (own-tissue) reconstruction, largely because it is a shorter procedure with a quicker recovery. Reconstruction rates vary widely between countries and regions, depending on access to plastic surgery, hospital facilities, and individual choice, and they have risen steadily as techniques have improved and as its psychological benefits have become better recognised. The procedure is performed in women of all ages after mastectomy for breast cancer, and increasingly in women at very high genetic risk who choose risk-reducing mastectomy. Because post-mastectomy radiotherapy has an important effect on the success of implant reconstruction, the timing and choice of reconstruction are closely coordinated with the oncology plan.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Reconstruction follows removal of the breast, and its purpose is to restore the breast mound and improve appearance, comfort, and quality of life. The tissue expander approach is used when there is not enough skin or muscle to accept an implant immediately. The expander is placed under the pectoral muscle at the time of, or after, the mastectomy, and is inflated at intervals through a small subcutaneous port; each fill stretches the skin and muscle, gradually creating the space needed. Once the desired size is reached and healing is complete -- usually after three to six months, and often after any chemotherapy or radiotherapy has finished -- a second operation replaces the expander with the permanent implant.

The success of reconstruction depends on an adequate blood supply to the overlying skin, freedom from infection, and uncomplicated healing. Radiotherapy, smoking, and obesity all increase the risk of poor healing, implant infection, and capsular contracture, and are central to the discussion of risks before surgery.

\section*{Clinical Features}
This section describes what to expect after surgery rather than the symptoms of a disease:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal recovery:} swelling, bruising, and soreness for the first few weeks, settling gradually
    \item \textbf{Drains:} small tubes may remain in place for a few days to remove excess fluid
    \item \textbf{Expansion sessions:} each fill makes the breast feel tight and fuller, usually settling within a day or two
    \item \textbf{Firmness:} the reconstructed breast often feels firmer and less natural than the other breast
    \item \textbf{Reduced sensation:} the breast and nipple area may feel numb, and sensation is often permanently altered
    \item \textbf{Final result:} the exchange to the permanent implant is usually performed a few months after the mastectomy, once expansion and any adjunctive treatment are complete
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
For problems after reconstruction, the main alternatives to be considered are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection versus normal post-operative inflammation
    \item Seroma or haematoma versus an early fluid collection requiring drainage
    \item Capsular contracture (tightening of scar tissue around the implant) versus normal firmness
    \item Implant rupture or leak versus a new breast lump
    \item Skin necrosis (dying skin from poor blood supply) versus delayed healing
    \item Recurrence of breast cancer versus a benign finding in or near the reconstructed breast
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the surgical team promptly if you have a fever, wound redness or discharge, increasing pain, or any sudden change in the shape or size of the reconstructed breast, including during the expansion phase. Early review allows problems such as infection and fluid collections to be treated before they threaten the reconstruction.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item High fever, chills, or spreading redness around the wound -- possible serious infection
    \item Wound breakdown with heavy bleeding or discharge
    \item Sudden severe pain or swelling in the chest or breast
    \item Difficulty breathing, chest pain, or calf pain and swelling -- possible blood clot
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical examination:} assessment of the wound, the expander or implant, and the surrounding skin
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} used to examine fluid collections, the implant, or a new lump
    \item \textbf{MRI:} the most reliable imaging test for detecting implant rupture
    \item \textbf{Aspiration and culture:} fluid from a suspected infection or a late collection can be aspirated and tested
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} of any suspicious lump to exclude recurrence of cancer
    \item \textbf{Breast cancer surveillance:} ongoing imaging of the remaining breast tissue and regular follow-up appointments, as planned by the team
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Planning:} a multidisciplinary discussion involving the breast surgeon, plastic surgeon, oncologist, and breast care nurse, covering immediate versus delayed reconstruction and implant versus own-tissue options
    \item \textbf{Tissue expansion:} a scheduled programme of fills until the desired size is reached
    \item \textbf{Implant exchange:} a second operation, usually three to six months later, to place the permanent implant
    \item \textbf{Nipple and areola reconstruction:} an optional further procedure to complete the appearance of the breast
    \item \textbf{Pain relief and wound care:} as directed after each operation
    \item \textbf{Support:} physiotherapy to maintain shoulder and arm movement and strength, and psychological support for body image and adjustment
    \item \textbf{Complication management:} antibiotics for infection, drainage of fluid collections, and surgery for capsular contracture, rupture, or implant failure
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular reviews of the reconstruction and continued breast cancer surveillance
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection of the wound, the expander, or the implant, which may require removal of the implant
    \item Seroma or haematoma after surgery
    \item Skin or tissue necrosis from poor blood supply, more likely in smokers
    \item Capsular contracture, causing a hard, tight, or painful breast
    \item Implant rupture or leakage, the likelihood of which increases over time
    \item Asymmetry, rippling, or an appearance that feels unnatural
    \item Pain, scarring, and reduced or altered sensation
    \item The effects of radiotherapy, which slow healing and increase the risk of complications
    \item Rarely, breast implant-associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma (BIA-ALCL), mainly with textured implants
    \item The need for further operations to revise, replace, or remove the reconstruction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most women are satisfied with implant-based reconstruction, and the procedure does not increase the risk of breast cancer recurrence or affect cancer survival. The tissue expander phase takes several months and requires patience, and the reconstructed breast is usually firmer and less natural-feeling than the other breast, with altered sensation. Many women will eventually need further surgery, because implants can harden, rupture, or require replacement over time, and outcomes are generally less favourable after radiotherapy, in smokers, and in those who are significantly overweight. Close follow-up allows problems to be detected and treated early, and the support of the breast care team helps most women adjust well to the result.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Stop smoking well before surgery, and maintain a healthy weight, to optimise healing and reduce complications
    \item Follow all pre-operative and post-operative instructions, including wound care and activity limits
    \item Attend every expansion session and follow-up appointment
    \item Report fever, redness, increasing pain, or a change in the breast promptly
    \item Perform the physiotherapy and shoulder exercises as advised to maintain movement
    \item Continue routine breast cancer surveillance, including imaging of the remaining breast tissue
    \item Seek support from the breast care nurse or a counsellor for body image and emotional adjustment
\end{itemize}
