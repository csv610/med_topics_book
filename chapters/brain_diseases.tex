\chapter{Brain Diseases}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The term \emph{brain diseases} encompasses the enormous range of conditions that can disturb the structure or function of the brain. Some, such as ischaemic stroke, are acute vascular emergencies; others, such as Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease, are chronic progressive disorders in which nerve cells are gradually lost. The group also includes infections such as meningitis and encephalitis, primary and metastatic brain tumours, epilepsy, multiple sclerosis, the many forms of traumatic brain injury, and a variety of genetic, developmental, metabolic, and toxic conditions. Because the brain integrates sensation, movement, thought, emotion, memory, and the control of breathing and swallowing, disease anywhere in it can produce effects across the whole body. This chapter provides an overview of the spectrum of brain diseases, their shared principles of investigation and management, and the warning signs that warrant urgent attention; detailed descriptions of individual disorders appear in their dedicated chapters.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Neurological disorders are collectively the leading cause of disability worldwide and the second leading cause of death. Stroke affects more than 12 million people globally each year and is a major cause of adult-acquired disability; dementia, of which Alzheimer's disease is the most common form, currently affects more than 55 million people, a number projected to rise sharply as populations age. Epilepsy affects roughly 50 million people of all ages, and migraine is among the most prevalent and disabling conditions encountered in primary care worldwide. Parkinson's disease affects about 8--10 million people, most over the age of 60, while multiple sclerosis typically begins in young adults and is more common in women. Primary brain tumours are comparatively uncommon but have a disproportionate impact because of their lethality and the disability they cause. The overall burden of brain disease increases steeply with age, and many individual disorders show sex, ethnic, and geographic variation.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Brain diseases arise from many distinct mechanisms, although the final common pathway is damage to neurones, their supporting cells, or the brain's blood supply:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vascular:} occlusion of a cerebral artery (ischaemic stroke), rupture of a vessel (intracerebral or subarachnoid haemorrhage), or chronic small-vessel disease contributing to vascular dementia
    \item \textbf{Degenerative:} progressive loss of neurones with accumulation of abnormal proteins, as in Alzheimer's disease (amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles) and Parkinson's disease (Lewy bodies)
    \item \textbf{Infectious:} bacterial or viral meningitis, encephalitis, brain abscess, and prion diseases
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory and autoimmune:} the immune system attacking myelin and neurones, as in multiple sclerosis, or antibody-mediated disorders
    \item \textbf{Neoplastic:} primary tumours such as gliomas and meningiomas, or metastases from cancers elsewhere
    \item \textbf{Traumatic:} concussion, cerebral contusions, diffuse axonal injury, and intracranial bleeding after head injury
    \item \textbf{Genetic and developmental:} inherited neurodegenerative and metabolic diseases, structural brain malformations, and epilepsy syndromes
    \item \textbf{Metabolic and toxic:} hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, hepatic or renal failure, deficiency states, and exposure to drugs or environmental toxins
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features depend on which brain region is affected and on the speed and pattern of onset. Acute processes such as stroke produce deficits that appear suddenly, whereas chronic disorders evolve gradually. Common manifestations include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Headache, which may be acute, persistent, or progressive
    \item Seizures, blackouts, or other transient neurological events
    \item Focal weakness, numbness, or paralysis affecting one side of the body
    \item Difficulty speaking, understanding language, swallowing, or walking
    \item Impaired balance, coordination, vision, or hearing
    \item Memory loss, confusion, disorientation, and personality or behavioural change
    \item Tremor, rigidity, slowness of movement, or involuntary movements
    \item Fatigue, sleep disturbance, mood change, and impaired concentration
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because symptoms overlap so heavily, many brain diseases are distinguished only after careful investigation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Stroke versus transient ischaemic attack versus migraine with aura
    \item Epilepsy versus syncope, cardiac arrhythmia, or psychogenic non-epileptic seizures
    \item Dementia versus depression, delirium, or treatable mimics such as vitamin B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism, or medication effects
    \item Parkinson's disease versus essential tremor, drug-induced parkinsonism, or atypical parkinsonian syndromes
    \item Multiple sclerosis versus other inflammatory, demyelinating, or vascular conditions
    \item Brain tumour versus abscess, haemorrhage, or cerebral infarction on imaging
    \item Meningitis versus encephalitis versus subarachnoid haemorrhage
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
New, persistent, or progressive neurological symptoms -- recurrent headaches, a first seizure, tremor, weakness, memory problems, or dizziness -- warrant medical review, because several brain diseases respond far better when treated early. Anyone with a chronic brain condition should also know the individual warning signs that require rapid assessment.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of stroke, using the FAST rule -- Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call emergency services
    \item A sudden, severe headache -- the worst headache you have ever had
    \item A seizure, especially a first seizure, one lasting more than five minutes, or one followed by impaired recovery
    \item Sudden loss of consciousness, confusion, or extreme drowsiness
    \item Sudden loss of vision, severe dizziness, or inability to walk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} a detailed account of the onset and progression of symptoms, vascular and family history, and a full neurological examination covering mental state, cranial nerves, strength, sensation, reflexes, and coordination
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, inflammatory markers, glucose, electrolytes, liver and renal function, and targeted tests for infection, vitamin deficiencies, autoimmune markers, or thyroid disease
    \item \textbf{CT and MRI:} imaging to detect stroke, haemorrhage, tumours, inflammation, atrophy, or structural abnormality; specialised sequences (diffusion-weighted MRI, MR angiography) add further diagnostic precision
    \item \textbf{Electroencephalography (EEG):} records cerebral electrical activity and is central to the diagnosis and classification of epilepsy
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture:} cerebrospinal fluid analysis for meningitis, encephalitis, subarachnoid haemorrhage, or demyelinating disease
    \item \textbf{Neuropsychological testing:} formal assessment of memory, attention, and executive function in dementia and after brain injury
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} for familial conditions, certain dementias, and inherited metabolic or neuromuscular disorders
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is disease-specific and is generally coordinated by a neurologist within a multidisciplinary team:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Emergency treatment:} rapid reperfusion for ischaemic stroke, surgical control of bleeding, and urgent antibiotics or antivirals for infection
    \item \textbf{Disease-modifying and symptomatic medicines:} antiepileptic drugs, acetylcholinesterase inhibitors for Alzheimer's disease, levodopa and related agents for Parkinson's disease, and disease-modifying therapies for multiple sclerosis
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} resection of tumours, drainage of abscesses or haematomas, relief of hydrocephalus, and epilepsy surgery in selected cases
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and speech and language therapy to restore and adapt function after stroke, injury, or surgery
    \item \textbf{Psychological and neuropsychiatric care:} treatment of depression, anxiety, and behavioural disturbance, and cognitive rehabilitation
    \item \textbf{Long-term and palliative support:} care planning, assistive technology, social care, and, where cure is not possible, palliative and end-of-life care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Permanent disability, including hemiparesis, aphasia, visual loss, or cognitive impairment
    \item Post-injury or post-stroke epilepsy, which may require lifelong treatment
    \item Aspiration pneumonia and respiratory complications from dysphagia
    \item Falls and fragility fractures from unsteadiness, seizures, or parkinsonism
    \item Loss of independence, with increasing reliance on carers and residential care
    \item Depression, anxiety, and significant carer burden
    \item Secondary complications of immobility, including venous thromboembolism, pressure injury, and infections
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Outcomes vary more widely across brain diseases than almost any other organ system, from full recovery after a minor transient event to progressive decline in neurodegenerative conditions. With rapid treatment, many people with ischaemic stroke make excellent recoveries, and infections such as bacterial meningitis respond well when treated early. Chronic conditions such as Parkinson's disease and Alzheimer's disease progress over years, but modern treatments can meaningfully slow decline and preserve independence and quality of life for long periods. The brain's plasticity allows some functional reorganisation after injury, so structured rehabilitation often produces substantial improvement even after significant damage. For incurable conditions, high-quality supportive and palliative care can maintain dignity and comfort, and advance care planning helps align care with the person's values.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Control blood pressure, cholesterol, and diabetes to protect the cerebral circulation
    \item Do not smoke, limit alcohol, and maintain a healthy weight
    \item Stay physically active, eat a balanced diet, and keep mentally and socially engaged
    \item Protect against head injury with seatbelts and helmets for cycling, riding, and contact sports
    \item Keep vaccinations up to date to prevent infections that can affect the brain
    \item Take prescribed medicines reliably and attend follow-up for conditions such as epilepsy, diabetes, and hypertension
    \item Seek medical attention promptly for new neurological symptoms rather than waiting for them to resolve
\end{itemize}
