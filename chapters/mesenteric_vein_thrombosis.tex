\chapter{Mesenteric Vein Thrombosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Mesenteric vein thrombosis is a rare but life-threatening vascular emergency characterised by the formation of a blood clot within the mesenteric venous system, which drains blood from the intestines. Most commonly, it affects the superior mesenteric vein, leading to venous congestion, tissue oedema, and, if unresolved, mesenteric ischaemia, bowel infarction, peritonitis, and death. Mesenteric vein thrombosis accounts for approximately 5--15\% of all cases of acute mesenteric ischaemia. It can present acutely, subacutely, or chronically. Acute mesenteric vein thrombosis requires rapid diagnosis and immediate therapeutic intervention (anticoagulation, thrombolysis, or surgical resection of necrotic bowel) to prevent catastrophic outcomes, while chronic mesenteric vein thrombosis is often associated with portal hypertension and collateral vessel formation.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Mesenteric vein thrombosis is an uncommon condition, estimated to occur in approximately 1 to 2 cases per 100,000 hospital admissions. It is more common in men than women and typically presents in the fifth to sixth decades of life. Unlike arterial mesenteric ischaemia, which is strongly associated with atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease in older adults, mesenteric vein thrombosis often occurs in younger patients and is strongly linked to inherited or acquired hypercoagulable states (thrombophilias), local inflammatory conditions, or portal hypertension. In Australia, the diagnosis has become more frequent and is made earlier due to the widespread availability of high-resolution computed tomography imaging, which has significantly improved survival rates over the past twenty years.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The pathophysiology of mesenteric vein thrombosis follows Virchow's triad of venous stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability. Blood clot formation in the superior or inferior mesenteric veins leads to venous outflow obstruction, bowel wall oedema, and microvascular ischaemia.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hypercoagulable states:} Inherited factors (such as Factor V Leiden mutation, Prothrombin G20210A mutation, Antithrombin III, Protein C, or Protein S deficiency) or acquired disorders (such as Antiphospholipid syndrome, myeloproliferative neoplasms like polycythaemia vera, or paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria).
    \item \textbf{Local inflammatory and infectious conditions:} Intra-abdominal pathology such as acute pancreatitis, appendicitis, diverticulitis, inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis), or pylephlebitis (septic thrombophlebitis of the portal vein).
    \item \textbf{Venous stasis and portal hypertension:} Liver cirrhosis leading to portal hypertension, congestive heart failure, or mechanical compression of mesenteric veins by intra-abdominal tumours.
    \item \textbf{Mechanical trauma:} Direct vessel injury from abdominal surgery (such as splenectomy, bariatric surgery, or liver transplantation) or blunt abdominal trauma.
    \item \textbf{Systemic factors:} Pregnancy, oral contraceptive pill use, or active malignancy, which promote a generalised prothrombotic state.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of mesenteric vein thrombosis is notoriously variable, ranging from severe abdominal pain to vague, nonspecific complaints, which often delays diagnosis.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe, acute central abdominal pain that is typically disproportionate to the physical findings on abdominal examination (pain out of proportion to exam) in the early stages.
    \item Postprandial abdominal pain, leading to food fear and weight loss, particularly in subacute or chronic presentations.
    \item Nausea, vomiting, anorexia, and abdominal distension resulting from venous congestion and bowel wall thickening.
    \item Diarrhoea, which may contain occult or macroscopic blood due to mucosal ischaemia and sloughing.
    \item Low-grade fever, tachycardia, and progressive abdominal tenderness as the disease advances towards bowel necrosis.
    \item Peritonitic signs, including abdominal rigidity, guarding, and rebound tenderness, which indicate bowel infarction and peritonitis.
    \item Chronic mesenteric vein thrombosis features, including splenomegaly, ascites, haematemesis from oesophageal varices, or features of portal biliopathy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The clinical features of mesenteric vein thrombosis overlap with many acute abdominal and vascular emergencies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mesenteric arterial embolism or thrombosis:} Represents the most common form of acute mesenteric ischaemia; typically occurs in older patients with atrial fibrillation or atherosclerosis, presenting with a more hyperacute onset and less bowel wall thickening on CT.
    \item \textbf{Acute pancreatitis:} Causes severe upper abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting, but is distinguished by marked elevation of serum amylase and lipase.
    \item \textbf{Acute diverticulitis or appendicitis:} Localised inflammatory conditions that cause focal abdominal pain and fever; can coexist with mesenteric vein thrombosis as a trigger, requiring CT to differentiate.
    \item \textbf{Bowel obstruction:} Presents with colicky pain, vomiting, and distension, but is distinguished by characteristic air-fluid levels and transition zones on imaging.
    \item \textbf{Perforated peptic ulcer:} Causes sudden, severe, peritonitic pain with free air under the diaphragm on imaging, whereas early mesenteric vein thrombosis lacks free air.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Acute mesenteric vein thrombosis is a medical emergency. Anyone experiencing sudden, severe abdominal pain that does not resolve should seek immediate emergency care.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe, or worsening abdominal pain, especially if accompanied by vomiting or fever.
    \item Vomiting blood or passing large amounts of blood or black, tarry stools.
    \item Collapse, dizziness, rapid heart rate, or signs of shock (cold, clammy skin, confusion).
    \item Abdominal pain with abdominal rigidity or severe tenderness to touch.
\end{itemize}

Other non-emergency reasons to seek medical attention include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Persistent, recurrent abdominal pain after eating, accompanied by unintended weight loss.
    \item Chronic abdominal bloating, changes in bowel habits, or persistent low-grade fever.
    \item Discussion of anticoagulation safety if you have a known thrombophilia or family history of blood clots.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Prompt radiological investigation is critical, as laboratory tests are non-specific.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Contrast-enhanced CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis:} The gold standard diagnostic investigation; reveals a filling defect in the superior mesenteric vein, bowel wall thickening, target-like mucosal enhancement, or pneumatosis intestinalis.
    \item \textbf{Duplex ultrasound of the abdomen:} Useful for evaluating flow in the portal and splenic veins, but limited in assessing the mesenteric veins due to overlying bowel gas.
    \item \textbf{MRI angiography:} Highly accurate and useful in patients with contraindications to iodinated contrast media.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} Full blood count (leucocytosis is common), venous lactate (elevated levels suggest bowel ischaemia or infarction), and liver function tests.
    \item \textbf{Thrombophilia screening:} Performed after the acute event and off anticoagulants to identify underlying genetic or acquired clotting disorders.
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic laparoscopy or laparotomy:} Indicated if clinical signs of peritonitis or bowel necrosis are present, allowing direct visualisation of the bowel loops and resection of non-viable tissue.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management focuses on preventing thrombus extension, restoring venous flow, and resecting non-viable bowel.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anticoagulation:} The cornerstone of treatment, initiated immediately with intravenous unfractionated heparin or low-molecular-weight heparin, followed by long-term oral anticoagulants for at least 3--6 months, or lifelong if a permanent thrombophilia is identified.
    \item \textbf{Intravenous fluids and bowel rest:} Aggressive fluid resuscitation to maintain intestinal perfusion, nil-by-mouth status, and nasogastric tube decompression to relieve bowel distension.
    \item \textbf{Broad-spectrum antibiotics:} Administered intravenously to prevent bacterial translocation across the ischaemic bowel wall and treat potential pylephlebitis.
    \item \textbf{Endovascular therapy:} Catheter-directed thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy via a transhepatic or transjugular route, reserved for clinically stable patients with extensive thrombosis who fail to improve on heparin.
    \item \textbf{Surgical exploration:} Urgent laparotomy is indicated if there are signs of peritonitis, bowel perforation, or infarction, involving resection of necrotic bowel loops and creation of stomas.
    \item \textbf{Second-look laparotomy:} Typically scheduled 12--24 hours after the initial resection to re-evaluate the viability of the remaining bowel loops and ensure no further necrosis has occurred.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bowel infarction and gangrene:} Complete venous occlusion leading to tissue death, perforation, and faecal peritonitis.
    \item \textbf{Short bowel syndrome:} A malabsorptive state occurring when extensive surgical resection of the small intestine (typically leaving less than 200 cm of viable bowel) is required.
    \item \textbf{Septic shock:} Life-threatening systemic infection resulting from bacterial translocation, peritonitis, or pylephlebitis.
    \item \textbf{Portal hypertension:} Chronic mesenteric vein thrombosis can lead to portal vein thrombosis, causing cavernous transformation of the portal vein, oesophageal varices, and splenomegaly.
    \item \textbf{Recurrent venous thromboembolism:} Increased risk of developing clots in the mesenteric system or deep veins of the legs and lungs.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of mesenteric vein thrombosis has improved significantly, with the 30-day mortality rate falling from over 30\% to approximately 5--10\% due to early CT diagnosis and rapid anticoagulation. Patients who receive timely treatment before the development of bowel infarction have excellent long-term survival. However, if bowel necrosis occurs and surgical resection is required, mortality increases to 20--30\%, and survivors are at risk of short bowel syndrome. Compliance with long-term anticoagulation and regular follow-up for portal hypertension are essential to prevent recurrence.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Adhere strictly to your prescribed oral anticoagulant medication schedule and attend all recommended blood tests.
    \item Inform all healthcare providers (including dentists) that you are taking anticoagulants before undergoing any procedures.
    \item Seek genetic counselling and screening for family members if you are diagnosed with an inherited thrombophilia.
    \item Avoid smoking and oestrogen-containing oral contraceptives, as these significantly increase the risk of blood clots.
    \item Manage chronic inflammatory conditions, such as inflammatory bowel disease or pancreatitis, in close consultation with your specialist.
    \item Seek immediate medical advice if you develop new or worsening abdominal pain, changes in bowel habits, or signs of bleeding.
\end{itemize}
