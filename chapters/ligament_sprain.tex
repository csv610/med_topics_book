\section*{Definition and Overview}
Ligament Sprain is a physical injury resulting from external force or trauma. Injuries may affect skin, soft tissue, bone, joints, nerves, blood vessels, or internal organs, and range from minor (requiring first aid only) to life-threatening. Trauma is a leading cause of death and disability globally, particularly in young adults. Prompt assessment and management follow the Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) principles of primary survey, resuscitation, and secondary survey.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Trauma is the leading cause of death in people aged 1--44 years in developed countries. Road traffic accidents, falls, and interpersonal violence account for the majority of serious injuries. Fall-related injuries are increasingly common in older adults. Sports injuries are frequent in adolescents and young adults. Occupational injuries remain a significant preventable burden.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blunt trauma:} motor vehicle crashes, falls, assaults -- causes tissue compression, shearing, and contusion
    \item \textbf{Penetrating trauma:} stab wounds, gunshot wounds -- causes direct tissue disruption along the projectile path
    \item \textbf{Thermal injury:} burns, frostbite -- tissue damage from extreme temperature
    \item \textbf{Chemical/electrical injury:} corrosive substances, electric current
    \item \textbf{Overuse:} repetitive stress leading to microtrauma and inflammation
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} tissue damage triggers inflammation, oedema, haemorrhage, and the systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) in severe trauma
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain, tenderness, or deformity at the injury site
    \item Swelling, bruising, or haematoma
    \item Loss of function, range of motion, or ability to bear weight
    \item Wound, laceration, or skin break
    \item Numbness, paraesthesia, or weakness (nerve injury)
    \item Pallor, coolness, or absent pulse distal to injury (vascular compromise)
    \item Deformity, shortening, or abnormal mobility (fracture/dislocation)
    \item Signs of internal injury: abdominal tenderness, chest instability, dyspnoea
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fracture vs.\ soft tissue injury (sprain, strain, contusion)
    \item Closed vs.\ open (compound) fracture
    \item Dislocation vs.\ subluxation
    \item Tendon rupture vs.\ tendonitis
    \item Internal organ injury vs.\ abdominal wall contusion
    \item Spinal injury vs.\ muscular back pain
    \item Pre-existing lesions unmasked by minor trauma (e.g.\ pathological fracture through a bone cyst)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
\textbf{Call 000 for major trauma, suspected spinal injury, severe bleeding, suspected fractures that are deformed or compound, head injury with confusion or loss of consciousness, or any injury that compromises breathing or circulation.}

Otherwise seek care if:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pain and swelling are severe or worsening
    \item The limb looks deformed or cannot be used
    \item There is numbness, tingling, coldness, or colour change below the injury (neurovascular compromise)
    \item A wound is deep, dirty, or has a foreign body
    \item Symptoms do not improve after 48--72 hours of self-care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Primary survey (ATLS):} Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure -- to identify immediately life-threatening problems
    \item \textbf{History:} mechanism of injury, time, loss of consciousness, pre-existing conditions, medications (anticoagulants)
    \item \textbf{Examination:} inspection, palpation, and neurovascular assessment of the injured region
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} X-ray for suspected fractures; CT for complex fractures and internal injuries; MRI for soft tissue, spinal cord, or ligamentous injury
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, coagulation, cross-match; blood gas and lactate in severe trauma
    \item \textbf{FAST scan:} focused assessment with sonography in trauma to detect intraperitoneal haemorrhage
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First aid:} RICE protocol (Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation) for soft tissue injuries; control bleeding with direct pressure
    \item \textbf{Wound management:} cleaning, debridement, suturing/stereostrips, and tetanus prophylaxis
    \item \textbf{Analgesia:} paracetamol, NSAIDs, or opioids for moderate-to-severe pain
    \item \textbf{Immobilisation:} splints, plaster casts, slings, or braces to protect and stabilise
    \item \textbf{Reduction:} manipulation of fractures or dislocations under appropriate analgesia/anaesthesia
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} internal fixation, wound exploration, repair of vessels/nerves/tendons, or laparotomy for internal injury
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy and graded return to function
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection (cellulitis, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis) -- especially with open fractures or contaminated wounds
    \item Compartment syndrome -- raised pressure within a muscle compartment causing ischaemia
    \item Fat embolism syndrome -- after long bone fractures
    \item Deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism -- due to immobility
    \item Non-union, malunion, or delayed union of fractures
    \item Chronic pain, stiffness, or post-traumatic arthritis
    \item Nerve or vascular injury with permanent deficit
    \item Tetanus or rabies (if contaminated wound or animal bite)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most minor injuries heal within 2--6 weeks. Simple fractures unite in 6--8 weeks (longer in older adults). Soft tissue injuries recover in days to weeks depending on severity. Rehabilitation is essential for optimal functional recovery. Long-term outcomes depend on the severity of injury, adequacy of treatment, and presence of complications. Post-traumatic arthritis may develop years after intra-articular fractures.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wear seatbelts, helmets, and recommended protective equipment for sport and work
    \item Follow road safety rules and avoid drink-driving
    \item Warm up and cool down when exercising; use correct technique
    \item Keep walkways, stairs, and lighting safe at home, especially for older adults
    \item Take breaks from repetitive tasks; use ergonomic equipment
    \item Maintain physical fitness, strength, and balance
    \item Ensure tetanus vaccination is up to date
\end{itemize}
