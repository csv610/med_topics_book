\chapter{Diverticular Disease}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Diverticular disease is a condition of the large intestine in which small bulging pouches (diverticula) form in the bowel wall, most commonly in the lower left side of the colon. Having diverticula without symptoms is called diverticulosis, and it is very common with age. When the pouches become inflamed or infected, it is called diverticulitis, which causes abdominal pain, fever, and changes in bowel habit. Most people with diverticulosis have no symptoms, and diverticulitis can usually be treated at home, although severe cases need hospital care. Diet, especially fibre, plays an important role in prevention and management.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Diverticulosis is one of the most common bowel conditions in Western countries, affecting about half of people over 60 and most people over 80. Only around 10--25\% of people with diverticula ever develop diverticulitis, and most cases are mild. The prevalence has risen with ageing populations and low-fibre diets. In Australia, diverticular disease is a common reason for GP visits and hospital admissions, particularly in older adults.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diverticula formation:} increased pressure in the bowel pushes the inner lining through weak spots in the muscle wall
    \item \textbf{Low-fibre diet:} low fibre intake causes constipation and high pressure in the colon, promoting pouch formation
    \item \textbf{Age:} the bowel wall weakens with age
    \item \textbf{Diverticulosis:} pouches present without inflammation or symptoms
    \item \textbf{Diverticulitis:} food or stool becomes trapped in a pouch, causing inflammation and infection
    \item \textbf{Complications:} abscess, perforation, bleeding, and narrowing of the bowel
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Most people with diverticulosis have no symptoms
    \item Some have bloating, cramping, and altered bowel habit
    \item Diverticulitis causes constant abdominal pain, usually in the lower left side
    \item Fever, nausea, vomiting, and a change in bowel habit
    \item Tenderness over the affected area
    \item Blood in the stool can occur with diverticular bleeding
    \item Severe cases cause an acute abdomen with guarding and rigidity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Irritable bowel syndrome, which causes similar cramping and bowel changes
    \item Inflammatory bowel disease, including Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis
    \item Bowel cancer, which can cause abdominal pain and changed bowel habit
    \item Appendicitis, when the pain is on the right side
    \item Gynaecological conditions in women
    \item Urinary tract infection and kidney stones
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for persistent abdominal pain, fever, or changes in bowel habit. People with known diverticular disease who develop severe pain, fever, or inability to tolerate food or fluids need urgent assessment.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe abdominal pain
    \item High fever with abdominal pain
    \item Vomiting that prevents keeping down fluids
    \item Significant bleeding from the bowel
    \item Signs of peritonitis: rigid abdomen, severe tenderness, and being very unwell
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} symptoms, tenderness, and vital signs
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} inflammatory markers and full blood count in suspected diverticulitis
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} the best test for diagnosing diverticulitis and its complications
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} used in some cases
    \item \textbf{Colonoscopy:} performed after recovery to exclude cancer and assess the extent of disease
    \item \textbf{Stool tests:} to exclude infection where relevant
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diverticulosis:} no treatment needed for most people; a high-fibre diet and adequate fluids are recommended
    \item \textbf{Mild diverticulitis:} home treatment with rest, fluids, a clear diet, and sometimes oral antibiotics
    \item \textbf{Severe diverticulitis:} hospital care with intravenous antibiotics and fluids
    \item \textbf{Abscess:} drainage by a radiologist or surgery
    \item \textbf{Perforation:} emergency surgery to remove the affected section of bowel
    \item \textbf{Bleeding:} hospital care, and treatment to stop bleeding if severe
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} colonoscopy after recovery and advice on preventing recurrence
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Abscess formation around the bowel
    \item Perforation causing peritonitis, which is life-threatening
    \item Bowel obstruction from scarring and narrowing
    \item Fistulas between the bowel and bladder or vagina
    \item Significant bleeding
    \item Recurrent attacks of diverticulitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people with diverticulosis never have symptoms, and most attacks of diverticulitis are mild and resolve with simple treatment. Around 15--30\% of people with one attack have further attacks, but the risk falls with a high-fibre diet and healthy lifestyle. Serious complications are uncommon, and surgery is needed only in a minority. The outlook for diverticular disease is good with appropriate care.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Eat a high-fibre diet with vegetables, fruit, wholegrains, and legumes
    \item Drink plenty of water to keep fibre working
    \item Exercise regularly and maintain a healthy weight
    \item Do not smoke
    \item Avoid straining on the toilet
    \item See a doctor promptly for abdominal pain with fever
    \item Follow medical advice after an attack to prevent recurrence
\end{itemize}
