\chapter{Help to Remain Living at Home}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
For people living with a chronic disease or serious illness, remaining at home with support is often the preferred and most achievable option. A wide range of services exists to make this possible, including home nursing, personal care, domestic assistance, meals, equipment and home modifications, respite care, and telehealth. In Australia, these services are accessed through general practitioners, hospitals, the My Aged Care system for older people, and the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS) for eligible people with disability. Planning early and coordinating services well helps people stay at home safely, comfortably, and with dignity.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Around half of all Australians live with a chronic condition, and chronic disease accounts for a large proportion of GP visits, hospital admissions, and disability. Most people with chronic illness prefer to live at home, and the vast majority do so with family support and community services. My Aged Care supports around a million older Australians each year, and an increasing number of people with serious illness are cared for at home with palliative care support. The population is ageing, making home-based support services an increasingly central part of the health system.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chronic diseases:} heart disease, diabetes, chronic lung disease, kidney disease, and neurological conditions create ongoing care needs
    \item \textbf{Serious illness:} advanced cancer, organ failure, and progressive neurological disease may require palliative and end-of-life care at home
    \item \textbf{Functional decline:} reduced mobility, frailty, and cognitive impairment increase the need for daily assistance
    \item \textbf{Caregiver strain:} family carers provide most home care, and their health and wellbeing are central to sustainability
    \item \textbf{Goals of care:} maintaining independence, comfort, symptom control, and quality of life
    \item \textbf{The aim of services:} to bridge the gap between the person's abilities and the demands of daily living
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty with daily activities: bathing, dressing, cooking, cleaning, and shopping
    \item Increasing dependence on family for personal care and medication management
    \item Falls, unsafe mobility, and frequent hospital admissions
    \item Poor symptom control: pain, breathlessness, fatigue, and nausea
    \item Social isolation and caregiver exhaustion
    \item Concerns about safety, nutrition, and adherence to treatment
    \item The person's own wish to avoid residential care and remain in familiar surroundings
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Needs vary enormously, and assessment distinguishes what care is required and which service provides it
    \item Older people are supported through My Aged Care; people with disability through the NDIS; others through hospital and community programs
    \item Temporary needs, such as after surgery, may be met by short-term care rather than long-term packages
    \item Palliative care supports people with life-limiting illness and can be provided alongside other services
    \item The choice between home support and residential care depends on safety, need, and the person's wishes
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact a GP or the relevant support program when needs change, after a hospital admission, or when care at home becomes difficult to maintain. Urgent medical care is needed for deterioration in the underlying illness.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Collapse, severe breathlessness, chest pain, or stroke symptoms
    \item Sudden confusion or reduced consciousness
    \item Severe pain or bleeding that cannot be managed at home
    \item A fall with a suspected fracture or head injury
    \item Signs of severe infection, such as high fever and drowsiness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Needs assessment:} comprehensive assessment of medical, functional, social, and environmental needs
    \item \textbf{My Aged Care assessment:} a government assessment determines eligibility and the level of home care package
    \item \textbf{NDIS planning:} eligibility and support planning for people with significant and permanent disability
    \item \textbf{Carer assessment:} the needs and capacity of family carers are assessed
    \item \textbf{Home environment review:} identifies safety hazards and equipment needs
    \item \textbf{Medical review:} the underlying illness and symptom control are reviewed regularly
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Home nursing:} wound care, medication, catheters, and clinical monitoring
    \item \textbf{Personal care:} help with bathing, dressing, and grooming
    \item \textbf{Domestic assistance:} cleaning, laundry, and meal preparation
    \item \textbf{Equipment and modifications:} walking aids, hoists, hospital beds, ramps, and bathroom modifications
    \item \textbf{Meals on wheels and allied health:} nutrition, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and speech therapy
    \item \textbf{Respite care:} planned breaks for family carers
    \item \textbf{Palliative care at home:} symptom management and end-of-life support
    \item \textbf{Coordination:} a care coordinator or case manager links services and communicates with the GP and specialists
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Falls, fractures, and head injuries at home
    \item Hospital admissions from poorly managed symptoms or infections
    \item Malnutrition, dehydration, and medication errors
    \item Pressure injuries in people with limited mobility
    \item Carer burnout, depression, and physical injury among family carers
    \item Social isolation for the person and the family
    \item Breakdown of home care arrangements, leading to crisis admissions or residential care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With appropriate support, most people with chronic disease can remain at home for many years, and most people receiving palliative care prefer to die at home, which is achievable for the majority with good community services. Outcomes depend on the underlying illness, the adequacy and reliability of support, and the capacity of family carers. Early planning, regular review, and flexible services that respond to changing needs are associated with fewer crises, fewer hospital admissions, and better quality of life for both the person and their carers.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Plan ahead: discuss future care needs with your GP before a crisis occurs
    \item Apply for assessments early, as waiting lists for home care packages can be long
    \item Keep medicines and appointments organised, and use medication aids if needed
    \item Make the home safe: remove trip hazards and install rails and non-slip flooring
    \item Maintain strength and mobility with physiotherapy and daily activity
    \item Accept help from family, friends, and services rather than waiting until exhausted
    \item Ensure family carers take regular respite and look after their own health
    \item Have an advance care plan documenting your preferences for care
\end{itemize}
