\chapter{Headache}
\index{Headache}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Headache is pain or discomfort in the head, face, or upper neck. It is one of the most common symptoms experienced by humans, and most headaches are harmless and short-lived. The most common types are tension-type headaches, migraine, and cluster headaches, along with headaches from colds, sinusitis, and dehydration. A small minority of headaches are symptoms of serious conditions such as meningitis, stroke, or bleeding in the brain, which is why certain "red flag" features require urgent care. Most headaches are managed with rest, fluids, simple pain relief, and trigger management, while recurring or severe headaches benefit from medical assessment and preventive treatment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Headache is extremely common: around 9 in 10 people have had a headache at some stage, and around half of adults have one each month. Migraine affects around 1 in 7 people in many countries, is three times more common in women, and is a leading cause of disability in young adults. Tension-type headache is the most common type. Medication-overuse headache affects up to 1 in 50 adults and is caused by frequent use of pain-relief medicines. Headache accounts for a large number of GP consultations, sick days, and presentations to emergency departments.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Headache arises from activation of pain-sensitive structures in and around the head.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pain-sensitive structures:} the membranes around the brain, blood vessels, muscles, and sinuses; the brain itself has no pain receptors
    \item \textbf{Tension-type headache:} muscle tension and tenderness in the scalp, neck, and shoulders, often with stress, poor posture, or lack of sleep
    \item \textbf{Migraine:} a complex neurological event involving the trigeminal nerve system and changes in brain chemicals, with a strong genetic basis
    \item \textbf{Triggers:} stress, fatigue, skipped meals, dehydration, certain foods, hormones, bright lights, and screen time
    \item \textbf{Cluster headache:} intense, one-sided pain around the eye, related to the hypothalamus
    \item \textbf{Medication-overuse headache:} frequent use of pain relievers makes the brain oversensitive to pain
    \item \textbf{Secondary headaches:} caused by conditions such as sinusitis, meningitis, stroke, and bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features differ by headache type.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Tension-type: mild to moderate pressure or tightness around both sides of the head, like a band
    \item Migraine: moderate to severe throbbing pain, often one-sided, with nausea, vomiting, and sensitivity to light, sound, and smell; some people have a visual aura
    \item Cluster: severe, excruciating pain on one side, around the eye or temple, with a red, watery eye and blocked nose, occurring in bouts
    \item Sinus headache: pain over the sinuses with a blocked or runny nose
    \item Medication-overuse: daily or near-daily headache that improves temporarily with pain relief
    \item Red flags: sudden severe ("thunderclap") headache, fever with stiff neck, headache after head injury, new headache after 50, headache with weakness or speech problems, and headaches that worsen over time
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Serious causes of headache must be considered.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Meningitis, which causes fever, stiff neck, and sensitivity to light
    \item Subarachnoid haemorrhage, which causes a sudden, severe thunderclap headache
    \item Stroke and transient ischaemic attack
    \item Brain tumour, which is rare but causes progressive headaches with neurological symptoms
    \item Giant cell arteritis, which causes headache with jaw pain in older people
    \item Glaucoma (acute angle-closure), which causes eye pain and visual disturbance
    \item Raised intracranial pressure and hydrocephalus
    \item Carbon monoxide poisoning
    \item High blood pressure and pre-eclampsia in pregnancy
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for frequent or severe headaches, headaches that interfere with daily life, new headaches after age 50, or headaches associated with concerning features. People with migraine should seek care to establish a treatment plan.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A sudden, severe headache described as the worst ever (thunderclap)
    \item Headache with fever, stiff neck, and sensitivity to light (possible meningitis)
    \item Headache with weakness, numbness, facial drooping, or difficulty speaking (possible stroke)
    \item Headache after a head injury with confusion, vomiting, or drowsiness
    \item The worst headache of your life, or a headache with seizures or collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Most headaches are diagnosed clinically, with tests only for red flags.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the pattern, location, severity, triggers, and associated symptoms are the key to diagnosis
    \item \textbf{Examination:} blood pressure, neurological examination, and assessment of the head, neck, and sinuses
    \item \textbf{Headache diary:} tracking headaches helps identify triggers and patterns
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT or MRI is used only when red flags or unusual features are present
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} for suspected underlying causes, including giant cell arteritis in older people
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture:} for suspected meningitis or subarachnoid haemorrhage
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a neurologist for complex or treatment-resistant headache
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on the headache type and frequency.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute treatment for tension-type:} paracetamol or ibuprofen, rest, and addressing stress and posture
    \item \textbf{Acute treatment for migraine:} early treatment with triptans and simple pain relievers, plus rest in a dark, quiet room
    \item \textbf{Preventive treatment for migraine:} medicines taken daily to reduce attack frequency, plus trigger management
    \item \textbf{Cluster headache:} oxygen therapy and specific medicines, with specialist referral
    \item \textbf{Stopping medication overuse:} reducing pain-relief use under medical supervision
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} regular sleep, meals, hydration, and exercise; limiting caffeine and alcohol
    \item \textbf{Stress management:} relaxation, mindfulness, and cognitive behavioural therapy
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} for tension headache related to neck and posture problems
    \item \textbf{Non-drug approaches:} acupuncture and other therapies for some people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Medication-overuse headache from frequent pain-relief use
    \item Reduced quality of life, work absence, and disability from frequent migraine
    \item Chronic daily headache
    \item Missed diagnosis of serious underlying conditions
    \item Psychological effects, including anxiety about headaches
    \item Complications of untreated serious causes, such as meningitis and stroke
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for headache is generally excellent. Most headaches are self-limiting, and simple measures control them. Migraine responds well to treatment: acute medicines stop attacks when taken early, and preventive medicines reduce frequency by half or more in most people. With trigger management and lifestyle measures, most people gain good control. The key is correct diagnosis, because treatment differs between headache types, and awareness of red flags, because rare serious causes are treatable when identified quickly.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep a regular sleep schedule and do not skip meals
    \item Stay well hydrated and limit caffeine and alcohol
    \item Exercise regularly
    \item Manage stress with relaxation and mindfulness
    \item Maintain good posture and take breaks from screens
    \item Identify and avoid personal headache triggers
    \item Take acute medicines early, but do not overuse them
    \item See a doctor for frequent, severe, or unusual headaches
    \item Keep a headache diary to support diagnosis
\end{itemize}
