\chapter{Allergic Rhinitis (Hay Fever)}
\index{Allergic Rhinitis (Hay Fever)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hay fever is the common name for seasonal allergic rhinitis, an allergic inflammation of the nasal lining driven by airborne pollens released at particular times of the year. The name is a historical misnomer: hay is not a trigger and fever is not a feature. The condition is triggered by tree, grass, and weed pollens, and it produces the classic combination of sneezing, nasal itching, rhinorrhoea, nasal congestion, and itchy, watery eyes (allergic conjunctivitis). Symptoms recur predictably in relation to pollen seasons and resolve or settle between seasons, which distinguishes hay fever from perennial allergic rhinitis caused by dust mites or animal dander. Although the condition is common and usually manageable at home, severe or prolonged symptoms can substantially affect sleep, concentration, and quality of life, and hay fever frequently coexists with asthma and eczema.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hay fever is one of the most common allergic conditions worldwide, affecting an estimated 10 to 25\% of the population in many developed countries. It often begins in childhood or the teenage years, and its prevalence has risen steadily over recent decades. Symptoms characteristically appear each pollen season, which in temperate regions peaks in spring (tree pollens), late spring and summer (grass pollens, the most common trigger), and late summer and autumn (weed pollens). The condition affects both sexes equally and is more common in urban than rural areas. People with hay fever are at increased risk of asthma, eczema, and food allergy, and there is a strong genetic component, with a positive family history of atopy being the most consistent risk factor. Many individuals find that symptoms become milder with age, although others experience persistence throughout adult life.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Hay fever is caused by an IgE-mediated hypersensitivity reaction to pollen proteins in a genetically susceptible person:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tree pollen:} released in early spring in many regions, with alder, birch, and plane being common triggers.
    \item \textbf{Grass pollen:} the most common seasonal allergen, peaking in late spring and summer in temperate climates.
    \item \textbf{Weed pollen:} including ragweed and nettle, often released later in summer and into autumn.
\end{itemize}

In a sensitised person, pollen proteins bind to allergen-specific IgE on mast cells in the nasal and ocular mucosa. Re-exposure triggers degranulation and the release of histamine and other mediators, causing vasodilation, increased mucus production, mucosal oedema, and reflex sneezing. The immediate symptoms are followed by a late-phase inflammatory response with eosinophils and other cells that maintains nasal congestion and hyperresponsiveness, so that symptoms persist as long as exposure continues. Weather strongly influences symptom severity, because pollen counts rise on warm, dry, windy days and fall during rain.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The classic features of hay fever include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bouts of sneezing:} often prominent in the morning or after going outdoors.
    \item \textbf{Rhinorrhoea:} profuse, clear, watery nasal discharge.
    \item \textbf{Nasal congestion:} a blocked nose that may disturb breathing and sleep.
    \item \textbf{Itching:} of the nose, palate, throat, and ears.
    \item \textbf{Ocular symptoms:} itchy, red, or watery eyes, often with swelling of the eyelids.
    \item \textbf{Secondary symptoms:} post-nasal drip, dry cough, tiredness, headache, and reduced concentration through the pollen season.
\end{itemize}

The hallmark of hay fever is the strict seasonal pattern: symptoms appear with the relevant pollen season, persist while exposure continues, and resolve when the season ends.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions may be confused with hay fever:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Perennial allergic rhinitis:} caused by dust mites, animal dander, or mould, with symptoms present all year round.
    \item \textbf{Non-allergic (vasomotor) rhinitis:} triggered by temperature change, smoke, strong odours, or stress, without an allergic basis.
    \item \textbf{Viral coryza:} the common cold, especially in its first few days, and often accompanied by fever and sore throat.
    \item \textbf{Chronic rhinosinusitis:} with or without nasal polyps, associated with facial pain and loss of smell.
    \item \textbf{Structural nasal obstruction:} a deviated nasal septum or enlarged adenoids in children.
    \item \textbf{Rhinitis medicamentosa:} rebound congestion from prolonged use of decongestant nasal sprays.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most hay fever is managed with self-care, but medical review is appropriate if symptoms are severe, last most of the season, or fail to respond to pharmacy treatments, and also for one-sided nasal blockage, loss of smell, recurrent nosebleeds, facial pain suggesting sinusitis, or asthma that worsens during the pollen season.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden wheezing or difficulty breathing that does not settle.
    \item Swelling of the face or throat, or difficulty swallowing.
    \item Collapse or loss of consciousness.
    \item Any signs of a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of hay fever is usually clinical and straightforward:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} a clear seasonal pattern of symptoms, timing in relation to local pollen seasons, and a personal or family history of atopy.
    \item \textbf{Examination:} inspection of the nose, throat, and eyes for the pale, oedematous nasal lining and conjunctival injection typical of allergy.
    \item \textbf{Skin prick testing:} to confirm sensitisation to grass, tree, or weed pollens when the trigger is unclear.
    \item \textbf{Serum specific IgE:} allergen-specific blood tests where skin testing is unavailable or unsuitable.
    \item \textbf{Pollen count correlation:} relating symptoms to published daily pollen counts supports the diagnosis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is aimed at controlling symptoms through the pollen season and is stepped according to severity:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Allergen avoidance:} reducing pollen exposure on high-count days using the practical measures described below.
    \item \textbf{Oral antihistamines:} effective for sneezing, itching, and a runny nose; second-generation agents are preferred for their lower sedation.
    \item \textbf{Intranasal corticosteroids:} the most effective treatment for a blocked and itchy nose, most effective when started before the season and used daily.
    \item \textbf{Intranasal antihistamine, alone or combined with steroid:} for rapid relief when nasal symptoms are severe.
    \item \textbf{Nasal saline irrigation:} to wash pollen and mucus from the nasal passages.
    \item \textbf{Eye drops:} antihistamine or mast-cell-stabilising drops for itchy, watery eyes.
    \item \textbf{Decongestants:} short-term use only, because prolonged use produces rebound congestion.
    \item \textbf{Allergen immunotherapy:} a disease-modifying option for people with severe symptoms that do not respond to pharmacotherapy, providing sustained benefit that may persist for years after a completed course.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated or poorly controlled hay fever can cause:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute or chronic sinusitis and nasal polyps.
    \item Middle ear problems, particularly glue ear in children.
    \item Disturbed sleep, snoring, and daytime fatigue.
    \item Worse asthma control during the pollen season.
    \item Reduced performance at school or work during peak season.
    \item Low mood and frustration from prolonged, disruptive symptoms.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Hay fever is a chronic condition without a permanent cure, but the great majority of people control their symptoms well with simple, safe treatments used through the season. The condition fluctuates from year to year, and many people find their symptoms become milder as they age. A minority experience severe, disabling symptoms that warrant specialist assessment and treatment with allergen immunotherapy. In appropriately selected people, immunotherapy produces significant, lasting improvement that commonly endures for years.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Practical measures can greatly reduce pollen exposure and symptom burden:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Check the daily pollen count and limit time outdoors on high-count days.
    \item Keep windows closed at home and in the car during the season, and use recirculated air when driving.
    \item Shower and change clothes after being outdoors to remove pollen from skin and hair.
    \item Wear wraparound sunglasses to protect the eyes.
    \item Dry clothes indoors rather than on an outdoor line.
    \item Start preventive treatments, such as steroid nasal sprays, before the season begins on medical advice.
    \item Vacuum and dust regularly, and consider a pollen filter or air purifier in the home.
\end{itemize}
