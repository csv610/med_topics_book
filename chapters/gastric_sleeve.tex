\chapter{Gastric Sleeve}
\index{Gastric Sleeve}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The gastric sleeve, formally called sleeve gastrectomy, is a weight-loss (bariatric) operation in which about 75--80% of the stomach is removed, leaving a narrow, tube-like "sleeve". The operation restricts how much food the stomach can hold and changes appetite hormones, reducing hunger. It is performed for people with severe obesity, typically a body mass index (BMI) of 40 or above, or 35 with serious obesity-related conditions, who have not achieved lasting weight loss otherwise. The gastric sleeve has become the most commonly performed bariatric operation in many countries because of its good results, relatively simple technique, and the fact that it does not change how food is absorbed. This chapter explains how the operation works, its benefits, and what it involves.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Severe obesity affects a large and growing proportion of people, and bariatric surgery is performed on thousands of people each year. The gastric sleeve is now the most common bariatric procedure in many countries, having overtaken the gastric band. Weight loss with the sleeve averages 50--70% of excess weight, with major improvements in type 2 diabetes, blood pressure, and sleep apnoea. The operation is less complex than gastric bypass and has a lower risk of nutritional deficiency, though it is irreversible. Serious complications are uncommon but include leaks and bleeding.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The operation:} most of the stomach, including the part that produces ghrelin, the hunger hormone, is removed, leaving a small vertical sleeve
    \item \textbf{Restriction:} the sleeve holds only a small amount of food, so the person feels full quickly and eats less
    \item \textbf{Hormonal changes:} removing the ghrelin-producing tissue reduces hunger, and gut hormones increase satiety
    \item \textbf{Weight loss:} gradual, sustained loss over 12--24 months, averaging 50--70% of excess weight
    \item \textbf{Metabolic effects:} improved blood sugar and diabetes control, which are partly independent of weight loss
    \item \textbf{No malabsorption:} unlike gastric bypass, the sleeve does not bypass the intestine, so nutrient absorption is largely preserved
    \item \textbf{Permanence:} the sleeve cannot be reversed, which is an important consideration
    \item \textbf{Lifelong changes:} smaller meals, chewing well, vitamin supplements, and follow-up are still required
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pre-operative assessment: medical, nutritional, and psychological evaluation
    \item The operation takes 1--2 hours, usually laparoscopically, with a 1--3 night stay
    \item Early fullness after small meals, and reduced appetite
    \item Weight loss that begins within weeks and continues for 1--2 years
    \item Improvements in diabetes, blood pressure, and sleep apnoea
    \item The need for daily vitamin and mineral supplements
    \item Vomiting or reflux if eating too much or too quickly
    \item Lifelong follow-up with the bariatric team
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The sleeve is one option among several.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Non-surgical weight management, including diet, exercise, and newer medicines
    \item Gastric bypass, which produces more weight loss and better diabetes remission but with more nutritional risks
    \item Gastric band, which is adjustable but less effective and now less popular
    \item The choice depends on the person's health, preferences, and eating behaviour
    \item The suitability assessment excludes medical and psychological contraindications
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone considering a gastric sleeve should have a comprehensive assessment by a bariatric team, including a GP, surgeon, dietitian, and psychologist. Significant decisions about surgery should never be rushed.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain, fever, or vomiting in the weeks after surgery, which may indicate a leak
    \item Chest pain or breathlessness
    \item Inability to keep fluids down
    \item Wound infection or opening
    \item Any emergency symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Weight and health assessment:} BMI and obesity-related conditions
    \item \textbf{Medical work-up:} blood tests, cardiac and respiratory review, and sleep studies
    \item \textbf{Nutritional assessment:} baseline vitamin and mineral levels
    \item \textbf{Psychological assessment:} eating behaviour and readiness for change
    \item \textbf{Endoscopy:} stomach examination before surgery, including assessment for reflux and Helicobacter pylori
    \item \textbf{Post-operative imaging:} contrast X-rays when a leak is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Before surgery:} weight loss, smoking cessation, and optimisation of medical conditions
    \item \textbf{The operation:} laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy
    \item \textbf{After surgery:} a staged diet from fluids to soft food to small regular meals
    \item \textbf{Vitamins:} lifelong supplements, including a multivitamin, iron, calcium, and vitamin D
    \item \textbf{Protein:} prioritising protein in each meal to preserve muscle
    \item \textbf{Exercise:} regular activity to support weight loss and health
    \item \textbf{Hydration:} small, frequent sips of fluid throughout the day
    \item \textbf{Long-term follow-up:} bariatric team and GP review, with blood tests
    \item \textbf{Reflux management:} medicines or, if severe, further surgery in some people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Leak from the staple line, the most feared early complication
    \item Bleeding and infection
    \item Stricture or narrowing of the sleeve, causing vomiting and difficulty eating
    \item Acid reflux, which can be worsened by the sleeve
    \item Nutritional deficiencies, less common than after bypass but still possible
    \item Vomiting from eating too quickly or too much
    \item Weight regain in a proportion of people
    \item Risks of anaesthesia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The gastric sleeve produces substantial and durable weight loss for most people, with major improvements in diabetes, blood pressure, sleep apnoea, mobility, and quality of life. It is generally well tolerated, and serious complications are uncommon. Because it does not cause malabsorption, the nutritional risks are lower than with gastric bypass, but supplements are still needed for life. The operation is irreversible, and success depends on permanent changes in eating and activity. For well-selected people, the long-term health benefits are significant.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Commit to lifelong lifestyle change; surgery is a tool, not a cure
    \item Follow the staged diet exactly after surgery
    \item Eat small, slow, protein-first meals and chew thoroughly
    \item Do not drink during meals, to avoid overfilling the sleeve
    \item Take all vitamins and minerals daily
    \item Attend all follow-up appointments and blood tests
    \item Exercise regularly
    \item Seek urgent care for fever, severe pain, or vomiting after surgery
\end{itemize}
