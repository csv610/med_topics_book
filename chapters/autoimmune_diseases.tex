\chapter{Autoimmune Diseases}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Autoimmune diseases occur when the immune system loses its ability to tell the body's own cells apart from foreign invaders and begins attacking healthy tissue. The result is chronic inflammation and damage that can affect one organ or many organs at once. The diseases are usually grouped into two broad categories: organ-specific diseases, which target a single organ (such as type 1 diabetes or Graves' disease), and systemic diseases, which involve many parts of the body (such as systemic lupus erythematosus or scleroderma).

More than 80 distinct autoimmune diseases are recognised. They share a common thread of immune misdirection, but each has its own triggers, target tissues and clinical course. Treatment has improved greatly in recent decades, and many people now manage these conditions successfully over a normal lifespan.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Autoimmune diseases together affect a large share of the population, with some estimates suggesting that up to one in ten people will develop one in their lifetime. They are among the most common chronic diseases in women, who account for roughly four out of five cases overall.

Most autoimmune diseases begin between adolescence and middle age, although some, such as type 1 diabetes, can appear in early childhood. Prevalence varies by region and ethnic background. Rates of many autoimmune diseases appear to be rising in developed countries, which may reflect better detection as well as environmental and lifestyle changes. Several diseases tend to run in families, and a person with one autoimmune disease is at increased risk of developing another.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Autoimmunity develops when genetic susceptibility meets an environmental trigger, and the usual safeguards that prevent immune attack on the body's own tissues fail.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} many genes contribute, especially the HLA genes that help the immune system recognise self from non-self; most people who carry them never develop disease
    \item \textbf{Infections:} some viruses and bacteria are thought to trigger autoimmunity, sometimes by confusingly resembling body tissue (molecular mimicry)
    \item \textbf{Smoking and other toxins:} cigarette smoking is a well-established risk factor for several diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis and lupus
    \item \textbf{Hormones:} the female predominance of most autoimmune diseases points to a hormonal influence
    \item \textbf{Gut microbiome:} the balance of bacteria in the gut may shape immune responses and influence risk
\end{itemize}

Once the process starts, the immune system produces antibodies and activated immune cells against self-tissue, causing inflammation. In organ-specific disease the attack is focused, whereas in systemic disease circulating immune complexes and widespread inflammation damage many organs, including the skin, joints, kidneys and blood vessels.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Because any tissue can be a target, the presentation is extremely varied. Common general symptoms and examples of typical organ involvement include the following.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Fatigue, malaise and low-grade fever that come and go
    \item Joint and muscle pain, swelling and stiffness, as in rheumatoid arthritis
    \item Skin and mucous membrane changes, such as the butterfly rash of lupus or mouth ulcers in Beh\c{c}et's disease
    \item Endocrine effects, such as high blood sugar (type 1 diabetes) or an overactive thyroid (Graves' disease)
    \item Bowel symptoms, such as diarrhoea and abdominal pain in inflammatory bowel disease
    \item Nervous system symptoms, such as weakness, numbness or visual disturbance in multiple sclerosis
    \item Flares followed by periods of relative wellness are characteristic
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Symptoms such as fatigue, fever and joint pain are shared with many other conditions, and misdiagnosis is common early on.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic infections, including viral hepatitis, tuberculosis and Lyme disease
    \item Malignancy, especially lymphoma and leukaemia
    \item Fibromyalgia and chronic fatigue syndrome
    \item Endocrine and metabolic disorders, such as hypothyroidism or vitamin D deficiency
    \item Adverse reactions to medicines
    \item Separate autoimmune diseases that present similarly (e.g.\ lupus versus rheumatoid arthritis)
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if you have persistent symptoms that are unexplained, such as prolonged tiredness, joint pain, rash or recurrent fever. Because autoimmune diseases are often diagnosed years after they start, early assessment can shorten that delay.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, difficulty breathing or coughing up blood
    \item Sudden weakness, facial droop or difficulty speaking
    \item Sudden visual loss or double vision
    \item Severe abdominal pain, vomiting or black stools
    \item High fever with confusion, a racing heart or a faint feeling (possible infection or flare)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is clinical and cumulative; it relies on a consistent pattern of symptoms, examination findings and test results over time.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} careful history and physical examination, often over several visits
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory markers:} ESR and C-reactive protein, though these are non-specific
    \item \textbf{Autoantibody panels:} ANA, anti-dsDNA, anti-CCP and others help support specific diagnoses
    \item \textbf{Full blood count and biochemistry:} to detect anaemia, kidney or liver involvement and organ dysfunction
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} X-rays, ultrasound, CT or MRI of affected joints and organs
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} tissue samples from the skin, kidney, bowel or other organs for microscopic analysis
    \item \textbf{Specialist input:} diagnosis is often confirmed by a rheumatologist, immunologist or organ specialist
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Autoimmune diseases are managed rather than cured, with the goal of controlling inflammation and preventing damage.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{First-line medicines:} NSAIDs for pain and inflammation; corticosteroids for rapid control of flares
    \item \textbf{Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs):} methotrexate, sulfasalazine or hydroxychloroquine to slow disease over the long term
    \item \textbf{Biologics and targeted therapies:} medicines such as TNF inhibitors or rituximab for disease that does not respond to DMARDs
    \item \textbf{Organ-specific care:} for example, insulin therapy in diabetes or levothyroxine in thyroid disease
    \item \textbf{Allied health:} physiotherapy, occupational therapy and psychological support
    \item \textbf{Lifelong monitoring:} regular blood tests, imaging and specialist reviews
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Permanent organ damage, including joint deformity, kidney failure or liver cirrhosis
    \item Cardiovascular disease, which is increased in many autoimmune diseases
    \item Serious infections from immunosuppressive treatment
    \item Osteoporosis and other corticosteroid side effects
    \item Complications of pregnancy, including miscarriage and preterm birth
    \item Depression and reduced quality of life from a long-term illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Autoimmune diseases are chronic, but the outlook has improved dramatically. Many people achieve remission or good control with treatment, and life expectancy is near-normal for most conditions when managed well. Earlier diagnosis and the availability of targeted therapies have made a real difference. Outcomes still vary: some people respond to simple treatment, while others need multiple medicines and experience damage despite therapy. Relapses are common, and treatment plans are usually adjusted over time.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
There is no reliable way to prevent autoimmune disease, but the following may reduce risk, limit flares and protect general health.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Do not smoke, and limit alcohol
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and stay active within your limits
    \item Eat a varied, balanced diet and avoid crash diets
    \item Protect skin from strong sunlight if you have a photosensitive disease
    \item Take medicines exactly as prescribed and attend regular reviews
    \item Keep up to date with recommended vaccinations after checking safety with your doctor
    \item Seek help early for new or worsening symptoms
    \item Manage stress and maintain social connections
\end{itemize}
