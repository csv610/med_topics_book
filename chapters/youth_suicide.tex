\chapter{Youth suicide}
\index{Youth suicide}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Youth suicide refers to suicide among children, adolescents, and young adults; this chapter uses the broad public-health range of 10--24 years. Suicide is death resulting from a self-directed injurious act with intent to die. Related but distinct terms include suicidal ideation and suicide attempt; non-suicidal self-injury does not involve intent to die, although it is associated with increased future risk.

The developmental transition from childhood to adulthood is characterized by rapid neurobiological maturation, psychological identity formulation, and shifting social dynamics. During this period, the prefrontal cortex---the brain region responsible for executive functioning, impulse control, risk assessment, and emotional regulation---is still undergoing significant synaptic pruning and myelination. Consequently, adolescents often possess heightened emotional reactivity coupled with an underdeveloped capacity for cognitive control, making them uniquely vulnerable to impulsive actions in response to acute stressors. Recognizing youth suicide as a multi-dimensional phenomenon rather than an isolated act of despair is critical for effective clinical intervention. It requires an understanding of how individual neurobiology, developmental psychology, and socio-environmental factors intersect to produce a state of intolerable psychological pain, often referred to as ``psychache.'' This chapter serves as a comprehensive medical reference, detailing the epidemiological trends, complex etiologies, clinical presentations, diagnostic approaches, and evidence-based management strategies required to address this critical issue in pediatric and adolescent medicine.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Globally, youth suicide is a leading cause of mortality among adolescents and young adults. Current WHO reporting identifies suicide as the third leading cause of death among people aged 15--29; rankings vary by year, country, age band, and data quality. The epidemiological profile is also shaped by sex, gender identity, socioeconomic conditions, access to care, and access to lethal means.

While completed suicide is relatively rare in children under the age of 10, the incidence escalates dramatically after puberty, peaking in late adolescence and young adulthood (ages 15 to 24). There is a well-documented epidemiological phenomenon known as the ``gender paradox'' in suicidal behavior. Female adolescents consistently report higher rates of suicidal ideation and engage in non-fatal suicide attempts more frequently than their male peers, with ratios ranging from 2:1 to 3:1. Conversely, male adolescents are significantly more likely to die by suicide, representing approximately 70\% to 80\% of all completed youth suicides in many developed nations. This disparity is primarily attributed to the choice of highly lethal methods (such as firearms or hanging) by males, compared to less immediately lethal methods (such as poisoning or self-cutting) more frequently utilized by females, as well as differences in help-seeking behaviors.

Certain subpopulations carry a disproportionately high burden of risk. Sexual and gender minority youth---including those identifying as lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer, or questioning (LGBTQ+)---experience rates of suicidal ideation and behavior that are two to four times higher than their cisgender, heterosexual peers. This elevated risk is strongly linked to minority stress, societal discrimination, familial rejection, and victimization. Indigenous youth in some countries, Canada, New Zealand, and the United States also face exceptionally high suicide rates, often three to five times higher than national averages, driven by the historical and ongoing impacts of colonization, systemic disadvantage, intergenerational trauma, and cultural dislocation. Socioeconomic status, geographic isolation (with rural youth having higher rates of completed suicide due to limited access to mental health services and greater access to lethal means), and exposure to suicide contagions (clusters of suicide within a school or community) further influence these epidemiological trends.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The etiology of youth suicide is multifactorial, arising from a complex interplay of genetic predispositions, neurobiological alterations, developmental vulnerabilities, psychiatric co-morbidities, and environmental stressors. No single factor is sufficient to cause suicide; rather, it is the cumulative burden of multiple risk factors that elevates vulnerability.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Biological and developmental factors:} Adolescence involves ongoing development of systems supporting impulse control, emotion regulation, and decision-making. Biological findings are complex and are not clinical tests that can predict an individual's suicide risk.
    \item \textbf{Psychiatric disorders:} Depression, bipolar disorder, anxiety, trauma-related disorders, substance use, eating disorders, psychosis, and other conditions may increase risk, but suicide is not caused by one diagnosis and many people with these conditions do not attempt suicide.
    \item \textbf{Genetic Factors:} Family, twin, and adoption studies indicate a strong genetic component to suicidal behavior, independent of the inheritance of psychiatric disorders. A family history of suicide or suicide attempts increases an adolescent's risk by approximately two- to four-fold. Epigenetic modifications, such as DNA methylation of genes regulating the stress response (e.g., NR3C1), resulting from childhood trauma or neglect, can permanently alter neurodevelopmental trajectories and increase later suicide risk.
    \item \textbf{Cognitive and Psychological Vulnerabilities:} Cognitive distortions, such as hopelessness, dichotomous (``black-and-white'') thinking, impaired problem-solving abilities, and high levels of perfectionism, limit an adolescent's ability to cope with life stressors. Psychological pain, or ``psychache,'' arising from frustrated psychological needs (e.g., lack of belongingness, perceived burdensomeness), is a critical proximal driver of suicidal intent.
    \item \textbf{Family and Social Environment:} Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), including physical, sexual, or emotional abuse, neglect, domestic violence, and parental divorce, are powerful predictors of youth suicide. Insecure attachment patterns, high family conflict, lack of parental warmth, and parental psychopathology (particularly maternal depression or parental substance abuse) compromise the adolescent's emotional safety net.
    \item \textbf{Peer and Societal Pressures:} Peer victimization, including severe bullying and cyberbullying, is a potent trigger for suicidal behavior. The modern digital landscape, while offering connection, also exposes vulnerable youth to cyberbullying, social comparison, and online forums that romanticize or instruct on self-harm. Academic distress, relationship breakups, and social isolation are also common precipitating events.
    \item \textbf{Access to Lethal Means:} The physical availability of highly lethal methods is a critical environmental risk factor. In households where firearms are stored loaded or unlocked, or where lethal prescription medications are easily accessible, the transition from suicidal impulse to fatal action is significantly facilitated.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of suicidal risk in youth can be subtle, varied, and frequently masked by normal adolescent developmental changes. While some adolescents communicate their intent directly, others display indirect behavioral, emotional, or cognitive warning signs that require astute clinical detection.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Direct and Indirect Verbalizations:} Expressing explicit wishes to die, disappear, or ``end it all'' must always be taken seriously. Indirect statements, such as ``I won't be a problem for you much longer,'' ``Nothing matters anymore,'' or ``I wish I could sleep and never wake up,'' are critical red flags.
    \item \textbf{Behavioral Changes:} A sudden withdrawal from peer groups, family activities, and previously enjoyed hobbies (anhedonia). Significant decline in academic performance, unexplained school absences, or neglect of personal hygiene. Giving away treasured possessions, writing farewell letters, or cleaning out one's room unexpectedly can indicate active preparation for death.
    \item \textbf{Affective and Mood Disturbances:} Pervasive sadness, irritability, frequent tearfulness, intense anger, hopelessness, or marked changes in sleep and appetite. Any sudden change in mood or behaviour warrants compassionate enquiry rather than assumptions about intent.
    \item \textbf{Impulsivity and Risk-Taking:} Engaging in reckless behaviors, such as substance abuse, unsafe practices, dangerous driving, or physical fights, reflecting a disregard for one's own safety.
    \item \textbf{Non-Suicidal Self-Injury (NSSI):} Behaviors such as cutting, burning, scratching, or hitting oneself without explicit suicidal intent. While NSSI is conceptually distinct from suicide, it is one of the strongest clinical predictors of future suicide attempts, representing a maladaptive coping mechanism for overwhelming affect.
    \item \textbf{Somatic Complaints:} Frequent, vague physical symptoms such as chronic headaches, abdominal pain, fatigue, or changes in appetite and sleep patterns (insomnia or hypersomnia).
    \item \textbf{Cognitive Indicators:} Expressing feelings of worthlessness, excessive guilt, self-loathing, or severe hopelessness about the future. The belief that one is a burden to family and friends is a particularly high-risk cognitive feature.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
In evaluating youth presenting with suicidal ideation or behaviors, it is essential to conduct a thorough differential diagnostic assessment to identify underlying psychiatric disorders, medical conditions, or developmental factors that may mimic, aggravate, or drive the suicidality.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Major Depressive Disorder (MDD):} Characterized by pervasive depressed or irritable mood, anhedonia, fatigue, and feelings of worthlessness. While suicidality is a diagnostic criterion for MDD, clinicians must distinguish between suicidal thoughts occurring as a symptom of depression versus a primary crisis driven by situational stressors without a full depressive syndrome.
    \item \textbf{Bipolar Disorder:} Adolescents presenting with suicidal behavior may be in a depressive phase of bipolar disorder or experiencing a mixed state. Mixed states, which combine depressive symptoms with manic energy, irritability, and impulsivity, carry an exceptionally high risk of lethal self-harm.
    \item \textbf{Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD) or Traits:} Marked by intense emotional instability, chronic feelings of emptiness, identity disturbance, and frantic efforts to avoid abandonment. Recurrent suicidal behavior, gestures, threats, or self-mutilating behavior are core features. Differentiating chronic, habituated self-harm in BPD from acute, high-intent suicidal behavior is critical for choosing the correct intervention.
    \item \textbf{Adjustment Disorders:} An emotional or behavioral reaction to an identifiable stressor (e.g., parental divorce, academic failure) occurring within three months of the onset of the stressor. The suicidality in adjustment disorders is often acute and highly reactive, but can still be highly lethal.
    \item \textbf{Substance-Induced Mood Disorder:} Alcohol, cannabis, stimulants, and prescription drug misuse can induce severe depressive symptoms and disinhibition, leading to impulsive suicidal behavior. Symptoms must be evaluated in relation to active intoxication or withdrawal states.
    \item \textbf{Schizophrenia and Psychotic Disorders:} Although less common in younger adolescents, emerging psychosis can present with command hallucinations (voices telling the individual to harm themselves) or persecutory delusions that drive suicidal actions to escape perceived threats.
    \item \textbf{Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD):} Impulsivity and emotional dysregulation associated with ADHD can lead to sudden, unplanned suicide attempts in response to minor stressors or frustrations, even in the absence of a comorbid depressive disorder.
    \item \textbf{Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD):} Autistic youth may experience high rates of depression and suicidal ideation, which can present atypically (e.g., through increased rigid behaviors, mutism, or intense distress over changes in routine) and can be difficult to assess due to communication differences.
    \item \textbf{Underlying Medical Conditions:} Chronic illnesses (e.g., autoimmune diseases, chronic pain syndromes, poorly controlled diabetes), neurological conditions (e.g., epilepsy, traumatic brain injury), or endocrine disorders (e.g., hypothyroidism) can cause organic mood symptoms or place a severe psychosocial burden on the youth, contributing to suicidal distress.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Immediate medical or psychiatric intervention is required if an adolescent displays signs of imminent danger to themselves. Do not wait for an acute crisis to resolve without assessment.

You must seek immediate emergency medical care or go to the nearest emergency department if the youth:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Threatens or talks about wanting to hurt or kill themselves.
    \item Actively looks for ways to kill themselves, such as searching online for methods or acquiring weapons, ligatures, or toxic substances.
    \item Writes or talks about death, dying, or suicide, when these actions are out of the ordinary for them.
    \item Displays extreme agitation, panic, or rage, or expresses a feeling of being trapped or in unbearable pain with no way out.
    \item Has engaged in an act of self-harm, regardless of how minor the physical injury appears, as the intent and medical risk may be high.
\end{itemize}

In life-threatening situations or acute crises:
\begin{itemize}
    \item In many countries, call the local emergency number or go to the nearest emergency department. Use a local crisis service when available.
    \item In the United States and Canada, call 911 for an emergency or call or text 988 for the Suicide \& Crisis Lifeline.
    \item In the United Kingdom, call 999 for emergency services, or call 111 for non-emergency medical advice. You can also call the Samaritans on 116 123.
    \item In Europe, call 112 for emergency services.
\end{itemize}
Never leave a youth who is actively suicidal unattended. Remove all potentially lethal objects (medications, sharp objects, ropes, firearms) from their immediate environment while waiting for help.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of suicide risk is entirely clinical, as there are no biological markers or diagnostic laboratory tests for suicidality. A comprehensive assessment requires a sensitive, non-judgmental, and developmentally appropriate clinical interview with the youth, complemented by collateral information from parents, guardians, and school staff where appropriate.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical Interview and Risk Assessment:} The clinician must directly ask about suicidal thoughts, plans, intent, and access to lethal means. Inquiring about suicide does not implant the idea in a young person's mind; rather, it provides relief and a safe space to share distress. Standardized clinical tools can structure the assessment:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS):} A widely validated tool that assesses the severity of ideation, intensity of ideation, behavior, and lethality of attempts.
        \item \textit{ASQ (Ask Suicide-Screening Questions):} A brief 4-item tool developed by the NIMH, highly effective in pediatric emergency and outpatient settings.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive Psychiatric Evaluation:} Assessing for comorbid mental health conditions (e.g., depression, bipolar disorder, anxiety, substance use) using criteria from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5-TR).
    \item \textbf{Evaluation of Safety and Protective Factors:} Identifying protective factors, such as strong family support, positive peer relationships, therapeutic alliance, religious or cultural beliefs against suicide, and future-oriented goals.
    \item \textbf{Physical Examination:} A complete physical exam should be performed to assess for physical consequences of self-harm (e.g., scars, fresh lacerations, burns, ingestion symptoms) and to rule out underlying organic illnesses.
    \item \textbf{Laboratory Investigations:} There is no blood test that diagnoses suicide risk. Investigations are guided by the history and examination, or by the suspected injury or ingestion. They may include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item targeted blood tests, including glucose, electrolytes, liver or kidney tests, when clinically indicated;
        \item toxicology or drug-specific tests when an overdose or intoxication is suspected; and
        \item pregnancy testing when relevant to the medical evaluation or treatment decisions.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Collateral Interviews:} Interviewing parents or caregivers separately to understand the youth's baseline behavior, family history of mental illness, recent stressors, and the family's capacity to supervise and support the patient.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Managing youth suicide risk requires a multi-layered, coordinated approach involving the patient, their family, mental health specialists, pediatricians, and educational institutions. The primary goal is to ensure immediate safety, followed by long-term psychotherapeutic and pharmacological interventions to address underlying vulnerabilities.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Immediate Safety Planning and Restricting Means:} A collaborative ``Safety Plan'' (such as the Stanley-Brown Safety Planning Intervention) must be co-created with the patient. This plan lists personal warning signs, internal coping strategies, social contacts who can offer distraction, family members or adults to contact in crisis, professional help resources, and steps to make the environment safe. Parents must be instructed to secure all medications (especially over-the-counter painkillers and psychiatric drugs), lock away firearms and ammunition separately, and restrict access to sharp instruments and household poisons.
    \item \textbf{Level of Care Determination:} Clinicians must decide whether the patient can be managed safely in the community (outpatient care) or requires intensive psychiatric treatment (inpatient hospitalization, partial hospitalization, or intensive outpatient programs). Inpatient admission is indicated for youth with high suicidal intent, a specific and lethal plan, lack of a safe or supportive home environment, or severe psychiatric agitation.
    \item \textbf{Psychotherapy:} Psychotherapy is the cornerstone of long-term management for youth at risk.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Suicide Prevention (CBT-SP):} Focuses on identifying cognitive distortions, developing adaptive coping strategies, and improving emotion regulation and problem-solving skills.
        \item \textit{Dialectical Behavior Therapy for Adolescents (DBT-A):} An evidence-based option for some adolescents with recurrent self-harm or suicidal behaviour. It teaches mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness and commonly includes family skills training.
        \item \textit{Family-Based Therapies:} Interventions such as Attachment-Based Family Therapy (ABFT) aim to repair trust, improve parent-child communication, and resolve family conflicts that contribute to the youth's distress.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pharmacotherapy:} Medication is indicated to treat underlying psychiatric disorders such as depression or anxiety, but must always be combined with psychotherapy.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs):} Fluoxetine is often the first-line pharmacotherapy for youth depression, as it has the strongest evidence base in this population. Sertraline and escitalopram are also commonly used.
        \item \textit{Monitoring for Treatment-Emergent Suicidality:} The US FDA warning concerns an increased risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviours in children, adolescents, and young adults taking antidepressants. Prescribers should arrange close follow-up after starting or changing treatment, explain warning symptoms to the young person and family, and never stop medication abruptly without medical advice. Treating depression remains important and the balance of benefit and risk must be reviewed individually.
        \item \textit{Atypical Antipsychotics or Mood Stabilizers:} May be prescribed as adjuncts in cases of bipolar disorder, severe impulsivity, psychotic symptoms, or treatment-resistant depression.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Continuous Follow-Up and Support:} Regular, frequent appointments during transitions of care (e.g., post-discharge from a psychiatric unit) are critical, as the period immediately following discharge carries the highest risk of re-attempting.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The consequences of suicidal behavior and untreated suicide risk extend far beyond the immediate threat to life, leading to profound physical, psychological, and social complications for the individual, their family, and the wider community.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physical Morbidity and Disability:} Non-fatal suicide attempts can result in severe, permanent physical injuries. Overdoses can cause irreversible organ damage (e.g., acute liver failure from paracetamol toxicity, renal failure, or cardiac arrhythmias). Violent attempts, such as jumps or firearm use, can lead to traumatic brain injury, spinal cord injury, permanent paralysis, disfigurement, or chronic pain.
    \item \textbf{Psychological Trauma:} Repeated self-harm and suicidal crises exacerbate feelings of hopelessness, shame, and self-stigma. Surviving an attempt can lead to post-traumatic stress symptoms related to the event itself or the medical/psychiatric interventions that followed.
    \item \textbf{Social and Educational Impairment:} Ongoing mental health crises often lead to prolonged school absences, academic underachievement, and social isolation. The disruption of normal developmental milestones (e.g., graduating, forming peer relationships) can have long-term socioeconomic impacts.
    \item \textbf{Impact on Families and Caregivers:} Families of suicidal youth experience chronic, severe stress, anxiety, guilt, and depression. The emotional and financial strain of navigating mental health systems, coupled with constant hypervigilance to ensure the youth's safety, can lead to caregiver burnout, marital conflict, and family breakdown.
    \item \textbf{Complicated Grief and Suicide Contagion:} A completed youth suicide leaves behind ``suicide survivors'' (family, friends, classmates) who are at high risk for complicated grief, PTSD, and depression. Furthermore, youth are uniquely susceptible to ``suicide contagion,'' where exposure to suicide within a peer group, school, or through sensationalized media reporting increases the risk of suicidal behavior in other vulnerable young people.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for youth experiencing suicidal ideation or behaviors varies widely, depending heavily on the timing of intervention, the presence of supportive relationships, and access to evidence-based mental health care.

With timely, comprehensive, and continuous intervention, the prognosis is generally favorable. Many adolescents who experience a suicidal crisis during their teenage years go on to achieve full recovery, developing robust coping skills that serve them well into adulthood. The resolution of suicidal ideation and the cessation of self-harming behaviors are achievable clinical outcomes, especially when family support is strong and the youth is engaged in therapies like DBT-A or CBT-SP.

However, the risk of recurrence remains a significant concern. A previous suicide attempt is one of the strongest known risk factors for another attempt or suicide. Risk is often especially high after an attempt or discharge from psychiatric care, so follow-up should be prompt and continuous. Long-term outcomes vary: many young people recover, while others experience recurrent depression, interpersonal difficulties, self-harm, or substance-use problems. Factors associated with a poorer prognosis include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Early age of onset of self-harming behavior.
    \item Co-occurring substance use disorders, which increase impulsivity and impair judgment.
    \item Lack of a supportive family environment or persistent exposure to abuse, neglect, or severe bullying.
    \item Poor treatment adherence, including premature termination of psychotherapy or discontinuation of psychiatric medications.
    \item High levels of impulsivity and severe hopelessness.
\end{itemize}
Active, long-term monitoring and fostering resilience are essential to sustaining positive outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventing youth suicide requires a comprehensive, multi-tiered approach that spans universal public health strategies, targeted interventions for at-risk groups, and individual self-care and family practices.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Universal and School-Based Prevention Programs:} Implementing evidence-based programs in schools, such as the ``Youth Partners in Care'' or ``Sources of Strength,'' which teach emotional literacy, distress tolerance, and how to identify and help peers in crisis. Providing mental health education helps destigmatize mental illness and encourages early help-seeking.
    \item \textbf{Gatekeeper Training:} Training teachers, school counselors, coaches, and youth leaders to recognize the early warning signs of suicidal distress and safely connect youth with professional services.
    \item \textbf{Restricting Access to Lethal Means:} As a highly effective public health measure, securing household firearms, regulating package sizes of over-the-counter pain medications, and installing safety barriers on high bridges or transit platforms significantly reduces impulsive suicide rates.
    \item \textbf{Fostering Strong Social Connections:} Encouraging positive, supportive relationships with parents, peers, teachers, and mentors. Promoting participation in structured extracurricular activities, sports, or community groups to reduce social isolation.
    \item \textbf{Promoting Healthy Habits (Self-Care):} Fostering healthy lifestyle choices that support brain development and emotional regulation:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Adequate Sleep:} Ensuring adolescents maintain a regular sleep schedule, as sleep deprivation is strongly linked to irritability, emotional instability, and impulsive behaviors.
        \item \textit{Physical Activity:} Regular exercise has proven antidepressant effects and helps manage stress.
        \item \textit{Balanced Nutrition:} A healthy diet supports overall physical and mental well-being.
        \item \textit{Limiting Screen Time:} Encouraging healthy boundaries with social media and digital devices, including avoiding screen use before bedtime and monitoring for online harassment.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Developing Coping Strategies:} Helping youth build a repertoire of healthy coping mechanisms, such as journaling, mindfulness meditation, artistic expression, or practicing deep breathing techniques when experiencing intense emotions.
    \item \textbf{Open Communication:} Creating a family environment where emotions are validated and open discussions about mental health are encouraged. Parents should actively listen to their children without immediate judgment or dismissiveness.
\end{itemize}
