\chapter{First Aid Basics}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
First aid basics are the core skills everyone should know to help someone who is injured or suddenly unwell. They form the foundation of all first aid and cover the most common life-threatening emergencies: cardiac arrest, choking, severe bleeding, unconsciousness, and burns. The basics are simple and memorable: check for danger, check the person's response, call 000, and then manage the airway, breathing, and circulation. Mastering these skills takes a few hours of training, and they can be applied anywhere. This chapter sets out the essential first aid basics in plain language, suitable for beginners and as a refresher.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Most cardiac arrests, choking episodes, and serious injuries happen in homes and public places, where professional help takes minutes to arrive. Each year in Australia, thousands of people die from out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, and survival is roughly two to three times higher when a bystander starts CPR. Choking kills several people a year, and severe bleeding, burns, and drowning cause further preventable deaths. First aid training is common, but many Australians have never learned the basics or have forgotten them. The people most likely to need first aid are family members, which is why home skills matter.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The ABCs:} Airway, Breathing, Circulation are checked in order, because each failure kills quickly and must be corrected before the next can be addressed
    \item \textbf{Airway:} an unresponsive person's tongue can fall back and block the throat; the head-tilt, chin-lift opens the airway
    \item \textbf{Breathing:} in cardiac arrest, breathing stops; CPR keeps oxygenated blood moving to the brain
    \item \textbf{Circulation:} the heart pumps blood carrying oxygen; cardiac arrest stops the pump, and severe bleeding empties the system
    \item \textbf{The chain of survival:} early recognition and call for help, early CPR, early defibrillation, and early advanced care
    \item \textbf{The recovery position:} side-lying in an unconscious person who is breathing keeps the airway clear
    \item \textbf{Time is critical:} brain damage begins within minutes without oxygen, so immediate action beats perfect action
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The basics follow a standard sequence.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Check the scene for danger before approaching
    \item Check response: talk to and gently shake the person
    \item If unresponsive: call 000, put the phone on speaker, and follow instructions
    \item Check breathing: look, listen, and feel for up to 10 seconds
    \item If not breathing or only gasping: start CPR and use an AED if available
    \item If breathing: place in the recovery position and monitor until help arrives
    \item Control severe bleeding with firm, direct pressure
    \item Cool burns with running water for 20 minutes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The basic assessment distinguishes emergencies.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Responsive versus unresponsive, which decides whether to call 000 and whether CPR is needed
    \item Breathing normally versus not breathing, which decides between the recovery position and CPR
    \item Gasping, which is not normal breathing and is treated as cardiac arrest
    \item Fainting (brief, recovers) versus cardiac arrest (does not recover)
    \item Mild choking (coughing) versus severe choking (silent)
    \item Major bleeding versus minor bleeding
    \item Burns requiring hospital care versus minor burns
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
The basics are about knowing when to act and when to call for help. Call 000 immediately for unconsciousness, abnormal breathing, severe bleeding, chest pain, choking, or any situation that looks life-threatening.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item An unresponsive person
    \item A person not breathing or only gasping
    \item Severe bleeding that does not stop with pressure
    \item Severe choking
    \item Chest pain or pressure
    \item Signs of stroke
    \item Any injury or illness you believe is an emergency
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Danger check:} the scene must be safe for the first aider
    \item \textbf{Response check:} tap and shout to assess consciousness
    \item \textbf{Airway:} open with head-tilt, chin-lift
    \item \textbf{Breathing check:} look, listen, feel, and watch the chest move
    \item \textbf{Ongoing assessment:} an unconscious person can stop breathing at any time and must be rechecked regularly
    \item \textbf{Handover:} tell ambulance officers what happened, what was done, and what the person's normal health is
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CPR:} 30 compressions to 2 rescue breaths, at 100--120 compressions per minute, pressing down 5--6 cm in the centre of the chest; for infants and children, compress about one-third of the chest depth
    \item \textbf{AED:} as soon as available, turn it on and follow the voice prompts; pads go on the bare chest
    \item \textbf{Recovery position:} for unconscious, breathing casualties, roll onto the side, tilt the head back, and monitor
    \item \textbf{Bleeding:} direct pressure with a clean dressing; if blood soaks through, add more dressings without removing the first
    \item \textbf{Choking:} encourage coughing; if severe, 5 back blows then 5 abdominal thrusts, repeating the cycle
    \item \textbf{Burns:} running cool water for 20 minutes, then cover with cling film; never use ice or creams
    \item \textbf{Reassurance:} talk calmly to the casualty, keep them warm, and stay until help arrives
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hesitation and inaction, which cost lives in cardiac arrest
    \item Performing CPR incorrectly but partially, which still helps
    \item Moving a casualty with a suspected spinal injury unnecessarily
    \item Infection in wounds not properly treated
    \item Exhaustion and distress in the first aider, who should ask for help and be relieved
    \item Relying on memory rather than refreshing skills
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Learning and applying first aid basics changes outcomes in a measurable way. CPR and defibrillation within the first minutes of cardiac arrest give the best chance of survival, and even compression-only CPR by an untrained bystander is far better than no action. The recovery position prevents avoidable deaths from airway obstruction, and pressure stops fatal bleeding. These skills are simple, require no equipment, and can be applied anywhere. Anyone who has completed a basic course and refreshes it periodically is equipped to act in the emergencies most likely to occur around them.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take a basic first aid and CPR course; skills need updating every few years
    \item Keep the 000 number and Poisons Information Centre number accessible
    \item Learn where the nearest AED is in your home, workplace, and community
    \item Keep a first aid kit stocked and know how to use it
    \item Practise the recovery position and CPR on a manikin
    \item Teach older children the basics of calling for help
    \item Review the current guidelines from the Australian Resuscitation Council
\end{itemize}
