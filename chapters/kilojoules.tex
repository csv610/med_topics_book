\chapter{Kilojoules}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A kilojoule is a unit of energy used to measure the energy in food and drink and the energy the body uses. In Australia, food labels and dietary advice use kilojoules (kJ), while calories, used in many other countries, are a different unit: one calorie equals about 4.2 kilojoules. The body needs a certain number of kilojoules each day to stay the same weight, and weight change occurs when intake does not match the energy used. Understanding kilojoules helps people choose foods and portion sizes that match their needs. This chapter explains kilojoule requirements, how to read the numbers on labels, and how to use them for weight management.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Most Australians consume more kilojoules than they need, and around two-thirds of adults and one in four children are overweight or obese. Discretionary foods, which are not needed for health, contribute around a third of the kilojoules Australians eat. Portion sizes and energy-dense foods have increased, while physical activity has declined, creating the energy surplus behind rising weight. Understanding and moderating kilojoule intake is central to preventing and managing obesity, and food labelling and public health campaigns aim to help people make informed choices.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Energy balance is the relationship between kilojoules consumed and kilojoules used. The body uses energy for its basal metabolism, the work of keeping organs and cells functioning; for physical activity; and for digesting food. Energy needs vary with age, sex, body size, muscle mass, and activity, with active young men needing the most and older, less active people needing less. When intake exceeds use, the surplus is stored as fat, and when intake is below use, stored fat is used, producing weight loss. Because foods differ greatly in energy density, a small portion of a high-kilojoule food can contain as much energy as a large meal of vegetables, which is why food choices and portions matter so much.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Food labels show kilojoules per serve and per 100 grams or millilitres
    \item Average adult needs are around 8,700 to 10,000 kilojoules daily, varying widely
    \item One calorie equals about 4.2 kilojoules
    \item A kilogram of body fat stores about 37,000 kilojoules
    \item Cutting about 2,000 kilojoules a day produces weight loss of about half a kilogram a week
    \item Energy-dense foods: fried food, chips, cakes, and sugary drinks
    \item Nutrient-dense foods: vegetables, fruit, legumes, and lean proteins
    \item Reading labels compares products and portions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Kilojoule advice is individualised.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Energy needs:} vary by age, sex, size, and activity
    \item \textbf{Weight goals:} maintenance, loss, or gain require different intakes
    \item \textbf{Life stages:} pregnancy, breastfeeding, growth, and older age change needs
    \item \textbf{Medical conditions:} diabetes, and metabolic conditions affect advice
    \item \textbf{Quality versus quantity:} a kilojoule is not everything, as food quality matters
    \item \textbf{Sustainable change:} small, consistent adjustments work better than extreme diets
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People wanting to lose weight or manage their weight should seek dietetic advice, especially with medical conditions such as diabetes or heart disease, and children's weight should be managed with professional support. Anyone with an eating disorder, or concerned about restrictive dieting, should seek help. A doctor or dietitian can calculate individual energy needs and plan healthy change.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, sudden weakness, or difficulty speaking, which may indicate heart attack or stroke
    \item Very high blood sugar with vomiting, abdominal pain, or confusion
    \item Collapse, fainting, or confusion
    \item Severe dehydration or inability to keep fluids down
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary assessment:} review of typical intake and portion sizes
    \item \textbf{Calculation of energy needs:} using age, sex, size, and activity
    \item \textbf{Weight and body mass index:} tracking
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} glucose, cholesterol, and other measures as indicated
    \item \textbf{Review of eating patterns:} habits, triggers, and emotional eating
    \item \textbf{Dietetic referral:} for individualised advice
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reading labels:} comparing kilojoules per serve and per 100 grams
    \item \textbf{Portion control:} serving sensible amounts and avoiding supersizing
    \item \textbf{Food quality:} prioritising vegetables, fruit, legumes, and lean protein
    \item \textbf{Limiting discretionary foods:} to small amounts
    \item \textbf{Reducing sugary drinks:} an easy way to cut kilojoules
    \item \textbf{Increasing activity:} to use more energy and build fitness
    \item \textbf{Small consistent changes:} that are sustainable
    \item \textbf{Professional support:} dietitians and weight management programs
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} tracking weight and adjusting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Weight gain and obesity
    \item Heart disease, diabetes, and fatty liver
    \item Dental decay from sugary foods and drinks
    \item Nutritional deficiencies if kilojoule cutting ignores quality
    \item Disordered eating from extreme restriction
    \item Muscle loss with crash dieting
    \item Regaining weight after unsustainable diets
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Understanding kilojoules helps people manage their weight sustainably. A modest, consistent energy deficit produces gradual weight loss that is more likely to be maintained than crash dieting, and combining intake control with activity gives the best results. Food quality matters alongside quantity, and a diet rich in vegetables, fruit, and whole foods supports health while controlling energy. With individualised advice, most people can achieve and maintain a healthy weight.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Learn your approximate daily energy needs
    \item Read food labels and compare kilojoules
    \item Serve sensible portions and avoid second helpings
    \item Drink water instead of sugary drinks
    \item Fill half the plate with vegetables
    \item Limit takeaway and packaged snacks
    \item Stay active most days
    \item Weigh yourself regularly but not obsessively
    \item Seek dietetic advice for sustainable change
    \item Avoid extreme diets and fads
\end{itemize}
