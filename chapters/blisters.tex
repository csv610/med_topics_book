\chapter{Blisters}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A blister is a small bubble of fluid that forms within the top layers of the skin. The fluid -- usually clear serum, but sometimes blood or pus -- cushions and protects the damaged skin beneath while it heals. Blisters may be caused by friction, heat, cold, chemicals, infections, or skin disease, and most are harmless and short-lived. However, blisters that are widespread, painful, recurrent, infected, or that appear without an obvious cause may point to an underlying condition requiring medical assessment.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Blisters are extremely common. Friction blisters affect walkers, runners, and hikers and are among the most frequent injuries in military and endurance settings. Sunburn blisters occur after prolonged exposure to strong sunlight, especially in people with fair skin. Viral blistering rashes such as chickenpox are common in childhood, and cold sores (herpes simplex) affect most adults at some time. Autoimmune blistering diseases such as pemphigus and bullous pemphigoid are uncommon and mostly affect older adults, while inherited conditions such as epidermolysis bullosa are rare.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Blisters form when the layers of the skin separate and fluid collects in the space created. The principal causes are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Friction:} rubbing of skin against footwear, tools, or sports equipment splits the upper skin layers
    \item \textbf{Thermal injury:} burns, scalds, sunburn, and frostbite damage the skin and cause fluid accumulation
    \item \textbf{Chemical and allergic irritation:} contact with irritants or allergens such as poison ivy can cause blistering
    \item \textbf{Viral infection:} herpes simplex (cold sores), herpes zoster (shingles), chickenpox, and hand, foot, and mouth disease
    \item \textbf{Bacterial infection:} impetigo and other staphylococcal skin infections can produce blisters
    \item \textbf{Medications and allergic reactions:} certain drugs, and rarely severe reactions such as Stevens--Johnson syndrome, cause widespread blistering
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune disease:} pemphigus and bullous pemphigoid, in which the immune system attacks the skin's adhesion structures
    \item \textbf{Genetic conditions:} inherited disorders such as epidermolysis bullosa cause skin that blisters at the slightest trauma
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The typical features depend on the cause, but the common elements are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A raised, round or oval bubble filled with clear, cloudy, bloody, or purulent fluid
    \item Pain or tenderness, especially with pressure or friction
    \item Redness of the surrounding skin
    \item In friction blisters, a sensation of warmth or a tender \textquotedblleft hot spot\textquotedblright\ before the blister appears
    \item Itching, which is common with allergic and viral blisters
    \item Crusting and peeling as the blister dries and heals
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A blister may be the main feature of several distinct conditions that should be distinguished:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Friction blister versus burn blister versus sunburn
    \item Cold sore (herpes simplex) versus impetigo versus aphthous ulcer
    \item Chickenpox versus insect bites versus scabies
    \item Shingles versus early impetigo versus allergic contact dermatitis
    \item Bullous pemphigoid versus pemphigus versus drug eruption
    \item Blisters on the feet in people with diabetes or poor circulation
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most blisters can be managed at home, but medical advice is warranted in certain situations:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of infection: increasing redness, warmth, pain, pus, or red streaks spreading from the blister
    \item Blisters that are widespread, unexplained, or keep recurring
    \item Blisters in the mouth or eyes
    \item Blistering after a significant burn, chemical exposure, or a new medication
    \item A person with diabetes, poor circulation, or a weakened immune system develops a foot blister
    \item A child has a blistering rash and is unwell or feverish
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:} blistering with difficulty breathing or swelling of the face and lips (severe allergic reaction), a large or deep burn, or widespread skin peeling with fever and severe illness (possible Stevens--Johnson syndrome).

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Most blisters are diagnosed from the history and appearance alone. Further assessment is needed when the cause is unclear:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} how the blister started, recent sun or chemical exposure, footwear, medications, and general health
    \item \textbf{Examination:} the distribution, size, and fluid content of the blisters
    \item \textbf{Swabs:} of blister fluid or broken skin to identify bacterial infection
    \item \textbf{Viral testing:} polymerase chain reaction or culture for suspected herpes or other viral rashes
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} autoantibody and other investigations when an autoimmune cause is suspected
    \item \textbf{Skin biopsy:} a small sample sent for histology and immunofluorescence to diagnose autoimmune blistering disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Most blisters heal on their own and need little more than protection:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Leave intact blisters alone:} the unbroken skin is a natural, sterile dressing
    \item \textbf{Protection:} cover with a soft dressing or blister plaster and relieve pressure on the area
    \item \textbf{Draining:} large, tense, or very painful blisters may be drained under sterile conditions by a health professional, keeping the roof intact
    \item \textbf{Cleaning:} wash broken blisters with soap and water, and do not peel off the blister roof
    \item \textbf{Antiseptic:} applied to broken blisters to prevent infection
    \item \textbf{Treating the cause:} antivirals for herpes and shingles, antibiotics for bacterial infection, and specialist treatment for autoimmune blistering disease
    \item \textbf{Burn care:} cool a fresh thermal burn with running water for at least 20 minutes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Infection of the blister (cellulitis), which can be serious in people with diabetes or poor circulation
    \item Delayed healing if the blister roof is removed too early
    \item Scarring or pigment changes, especially after deeper burns
    \item Secondary spread of infection from viral blisters
    \item Chronic, painful, or infected blisters that restrict walking and daily activities
    \item Systemic complications of untreated autoimmune blistering disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Friction, sunburn, and most minor blisters heal within a few days to a week without treatment, and the skin returns to normal. Cold sores and shingles settle within one to two weeks, although the responsible viruses remain dormant in the body and can reactivate. Blisters that become infected take longer to heal and may require antibiotics. Autoimmune blistering diseases are chronic but can usually be controlled with treatment, and people with epidermolysis bullosa require lifelong specialist skin care.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wear well-fitting, broken-in shoes and moisture-wicking socks
    \item Use protective gloves for tools and gardening
    \item Apply lubricants or specialist blister plasters to high-friction areas before activity
    \item Use sunscreen and limit sun exposure to prevent sunburn blisters
    \item Stop the activity as soon as a hot spot or soreness develops
    \item Keep the skin clean, dry, and moisturised
    \item Do not pop intact blisters, and seek care early if signs of infection develop
\end{itemize}
