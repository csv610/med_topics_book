\chapter{Mechanical Ventilation}
\index{Mechanical Ventilation}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Mechanical ventilation is a life-support intervention designed to assist or completely replace spontaneous breathing in patients with severe impairment of gas exchange or respiratory muscle pump failure. It functions by delivering a controlled flow of oxygenated gas into the lungs, facilitating the clearance of carbon dioxide and improving arterial oxygenation. Mechanical ventilation can be categorised into non-invasive ventilation (NIV), which utilizes a tight-fitting face mask or nasal interface (e.g. continuous positive airway pressure [CPAP] or bilevel positive airway pressure [BiPAP]), and invasive mechanical ventilation, which requires securing the airway with an endotracheal tube or a tracheostomy tube. It is a critical component of emergency medicine, anaesthesia, and intensive care, aimed at reducing the work of breathing, preventing respiratory muscle fatigue, and allowing time for the underlying respiratory pathology to resolve.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Mechanical ventilation is one of the most common interventions performed in intensive care units (ICUs) worldwide. In many countries, approximately 30--40\% of all ICU admissions require mechanical ventilation, representing tens of thousands of patients annually. The epidemiology reflects the incidence of life-threatening respiratory, cardiovascular, and neurological illnesses. Invasive mechanical ventilation is most frequently initiated in elderly populations and patients with significant chronic comorbidities, who are at higher risk of respiratory failure. Non-invasive ventilation is commonly employed in emergency departments and general wards for acute exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and acute cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, significantly reducing the rates of subsequent endotracheal intubation.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Mechanical ventilation is indicated when a patient's respiratory system fails to meet the metabolic demands of the body. The pathophysiological processes and clinical indications include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure (Type I):} Characterised by severe ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) mismatch or right-to-left shunt, seen in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), severe multilobar pneumonia, near-drowning, or massive pulmonary embolism.
    \item \textbf{Acute hypercapnic respiratory failure (Type II):} Alveolar hypoventilation leading to retention of carbon dioxide and respiratory acidosis, commonly caused by acute exacerbations of COPD, status asthmaticus, or severe obesity hypoventilation syndrome.
    \item \textbf{Neuromuscular pump failure:} Impairment of the respiratory muscles or nerves, preventing adequate thoracic expansion, as seen in Guillain-Barré syndrome, myasthenia gravis crisis, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), or high cervical spinal cord injury.
    \item \textbf{Central respiratory drive depression:} Insufficient neural signal to breathe, resulting from acute traumatic brain injury, brainstem stroke, or toxic drug overdose (especially opioids or sedatives).
    \item \textbf{Intraoperative airway management:} The necessity to secure the airway and control ventilation during major surgical procedures under general anaesthesia involving neuromuscular blockade.
    \item \textbf{Airway protection:} Securing the airway in patients with a Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score of 8 or less, preventing the aspiration of gastric contents or blood (e.g. in severe trauma or hepatic encephalopathy).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation involves both the pre-ventilation signs of respiratory failure and the unique features observed in a patient undergoing active mechanical ventilation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Signs of respiratory distress (pre-ventilation):} Severe tachypnoea (respiratory rate $>30$ breaths/min), use of accessory muscles (sternocleidomastoid, scalene), intercostal recession, nasal flaring, and paradoxical abdominal movement.
    \item \textbf{Manifestations of hypoxaemia:} Cyanosis (blue discoloration of lips and skin), agitation, confusion, tachycardia, and a progressive drop in peripheral oxygen saturation ($SpO_2 <90\%$).
    \item \textbf{Manifestations of hypercapnia:} Progressive somnolence, bounding peripheral pulses, warm extremities, and asterixis (flapping tremor).
    \item \textbf{Patient-ventilator dyssynchrony (during ventilation):} Evidence of the patient ``fighting the ventilator,'' characterized by agitation, coughing, tachypnoea, accessory muscle use while ventilated, and high-pressure ventilator alarms.
    \item \textbf{Altered respiratory parameters:} Changes in airway pressures (peak and plateau), lung compliance, and resistance displayed on the ventilator monitor, along with variations in sedation-induced consciousness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
In the management of a patient requiring or undergoing mechanical ventilation, clinicians must differentiate the primary causes of respiratory failure and diagnose sudden ventilator-related deteriorations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cardiogenic vs. non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema:} Distinguishing left ventricular failure from ARDS, which is crucial as ARDS requires low tidal volume lung-protective ventilation and higher positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP).
    \item \textbf{Invasive vs. non-invasive ventilation appropriateness:} Differentiating patients who can safely undergo a trial of NIV (alert, haemodynamically stable, cooperative) from those requiring immediate endotracheal intubation (unconscious, copious secretions, haemodynamic instability).
    \item \textbf{Acute deterioration on the ventilator (DOPES mnemonic):} Differentiating the cause of sudden hypoxia or high airway pressures: \textbf{D}isplacement of the endotracheal tube; \textbf{O}bstruction of the tube (mucus plug or biting); \textbf{P}neumothorax (barotrauma); \textbf{E}quipment failure (ventilator malfunction); or \textbf{S}tacked breaths (auto-PEEP or air trapping).
    \item \textbf{Sedation-induced unconsciousness vs. neurological event:} Distinguishing a patient who is slow to wake due to accumulation of sedatives from one who has suffered an intraoperative stroke or hypoxic-ischaemic brain injury.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Mechanical ventilation is predominantly managed within a hospital. For patients on long-term home mechanical ventilation (via tracheostomy or NIV mask), carers and patients must be trained to recognize critical events.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Accidental decannulation (pulling out) of the tracheostomy tube or endotracheal tube.
    \item Sudden malfunction or power failure of the home ventilator with failure of the backup battery.
    \item Blueness of the lips, face, or fingernails (cyanosis) or sudden loss of consciousness.
    \item Acute, severe shortness of breath, choking, or chest pain in a ventilated patient.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Ongoing monitoring and diagnostic investigations are essential to tailor ventilator settings and detect complications:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis:} The gold-standard investigation to evaluate oxygenation ($PaO_2$), alveolar ventilation ($PaCO_2$), and acid-base balance (pH, bicarbonate).
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} Performed immediately after intubation to confirm the endotracheal tube tip is 3--5 cm above the carina, and repeated daily or during clinical deterioration to monitor for pneumothorax, atelectasis, or lobar collapse.
    \item \textbf{Continuous capnography:} Monitoring of end-tidal carbon dioxide ($EtCO_2$) to confirm tube position and continuously assess alveolar ventilation.
    \item \textbf{Ventilator graphics and loops:} Interpretation of flow-volume and pressure-volume loops to detect air leaks, secretions, auto-PEEP, or compliance changes.
    \item \textbf{Microbiological cultures:} Endotracheal aspirates or bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) fluid cultures when ventilator-associated pneumonia is suspected clinically (fever, purulent secretions, new chest X-ray infiltrate).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management involves securing the airway, configuring ventilator settings, maintaining sedation, and planning for weaning:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Airway management:} Endotracheal intubation via direct or video laryngoscopy under rapid sequence induction (RSI), with tube placement confirmed by capnography.
    \item \textbf{Mode configuration:} Choosing between volume-targeted modes (e.g. assist-control volume control) or pressure-targeted modes (e.g. pressure control, pressure support) depending on the patient's lung mechanics and respiratory drive.
    \item \textbf{Lung-protective ventilation settings:} Limiting tidal volume to 6 mL/kg of predicted body weight (to prevent volutrauma) and maintaining plateau pressure below 30 cm $H_2O$ (to prevent barotrauma) in patients with ARDS.
    \item \textbf{PEEP titration:} Applying positive end-expiratory pressure to keep alveoli open at the end of expiration, preventing atelectrauma and improving oxygenation.
    \item \textbf{Sedation and analgesia:} Continuous infusion of intravenous agents (e.g. propofol, midazolam, dexmedetomidine) and analgesics (e.g. fentanyl, morphine) to ensure comfort and prevent dyssynchrony, targeting a light level of sedation where possible.
    \item \textbf{Weaning protocol:} Daily Spontaneous Awakening Trials (SATs, turning off sedation) and Spontaneous Breathing Trials (SBTs, testing the patient's ability to breathe with minimal ventilator support) to assess readiness for extubation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The use of mechanical ventilation carries substantial risks of secondary injury and infection:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP):} A nosocomial lung infection occurring more than 48 hours after endotracheal intubation, associated with high morbidity.
    \item \textbf{Ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI):} Alveolar damage due to overdistension (volutrauma), high transpulmonary pressures (barotrauma, resulting in pneumothorax or surgical emphysema), or cyclic alveolar recruitment-derecruitment (atelectrauma).
    \item \textbf{Haemodynamic compromise:} Increased intrathoracic pressure reduces venous return (preload) to the right atrium, which can cause hypotension and reduced cardiac output, particularly in hypovolaemic patients.
    \item \textbf{Airway and vocal cord trauma:} Laryngeal injury, tracheal stenosis, or vocal cord paralysis caused by pressure from the endotracheal tube cuff.
    \item \textbf{Oxygen toxicity:} Production of damaging reactive oxygen species due to prolonged exposure to high fractions of inspired oxygen ($FiO_2 >0.6$).
    \item \textbf{ICU-acquired weakness:} Significant muscle wasting and diaphragmatic atrophy resulting from prolonged immobilization, sedation, and neuromuscular blockade.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of a patient requiring mechanical ventilation depends primarily on the age of the patient, their pre-existing functional reserve, and the cause of the respiratory failure. Patients ventilated for acute, reversible conditions such as a drug overdose or post-operative recovery have a very high survival rate (exceeding 90--95\%) and are typically extubated within 24 hours. Conversely, patients ventilated for severe ARDS, multi-organ failure, or septic shock face a mortality rate of 30--50\%. Prolonged mechanical ventilation (exceeding 14 days) is associated with prolonged physical and cognitive rehabilitation, with many survivors experiencing post-intensive care syndrome (PICS).

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
In the hospital and home settings, strategies to prevent complications and support recovery include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{VAP prevention bundle:} Elevating the head of the bed to 30--45 degrees, performing regular oral care with chlorhexidine, subglottic secretion drainage, and daily assessment for weaning.
    \item \textbf{Early physical rehabilitation:} Initiating passive range-of-motion exercises, in-bed cycling, or assisted sitting and standing as soon as haemodynamically stable to prevent ICU-acquired weakness.
    \item \textbf{Humidification and suctioning:} Ensuring adequate humidification of the ventilator circuit to keep secretions thin, and suctioning the airway when necessary to prevent mucus plugging.
    \item \textbf{Tracheostomy site maintenance:} Meticulous aseptic cleaning of the tracheostomy stoma daily and replacing the inner cannula to prevent infection and tube blockage.
    \item \textbf{Device and alarm testing:} For home ventilators, conducting daily functional checks of backup batteries, circuit integrity, and audio alarms.
\end{itemize}
