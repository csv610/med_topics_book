\chapter{Hysteroscopy}
\index{Hysteroscopy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hysteroscopy is a procedure that allows a doctor to look inside the uterus using a hysteroscope, a thin, lighted telescope passed through the vagina and cervix. It can be used for diagnosis, to investigate abnormal bleeding, pain, or fertility problems, and for treatment, to remove polyps, fibroids, scar tissue, or a displaced intrauterine device. Diagnostic hysteroscopy may be performed in a clinic with local anaesthesia or none at all, while operative hysteroscopy is usually performed under general anaesthesia as day surgery. Because it provides direct vision of the uterine cavity, it is more accurate than ultrasound for detecting problems within the cavity itself.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hysteroscopy is one of the most common procedures in gynaecology. Abnormal uterine bleeding affects up to a quarter of women of reproductive age and is the most frequent reason for the procedure. Endometrial polyps are found in up to one in four women having hysteroscopy for abnormal bleeding, and submucous fibroids and intrauterine adhesions are also common findings. With the growth of outpatient (office) hysteroscopy, many diagnostic procedures are now performed without general anaesthesia, reducing costs and recovery times. The procedure is also used increasingly in the assessment of infertility and recurrent miscarriage.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The uterine cavity is normally a narrow, triangular space lined by the endometrium. Structural abnormalities that cause symptoms include polyps, which are fleshy overgrowths of the lining; submucous fibroids, which are benign muscle tumours bulging into the cavity; adhesions (Asherman syndrome), which are bands of scar tissue, usually from previous surgery or infection; and a uterine septum, a congenital band of tissue dividing the cavity. Hysteroscopy allows these abnormalities to be seen directly and, in many cases, removed at the same sitting. The procedure uses a distending fluid, such as saline, to open the cavity so that the telescope can pass and the surgeon can see clearly.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Abnormal uterine bleeding: heavy, prolonged, or irregular periods
    \item Bleeding between periods or after intercourse
    \item Post-menopausal bleeding
    \item Pelvic pain or a feeling of pressure
    \item Difficulty conceiving or recurrent miscarriage, prompting assessment of the cavity
    \item Symptoms related to a lost or embedded intrauterine device
    \item After the procedure: mild cramping and light spotting, usually settling within a day or two
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Findings at hysteroscopy are distinguished from other causes of the same symptoms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Endometrial polyps:} the most common finding in abnormal bleeding
    \item \textbf{Submucous fibroids:} muscle tumours distorting the cavity
    \item \textbf{Endometrial hyperplasia and cancer:} abnormal proliferation of the lining
    \item \textbf{Intrauterine adhesions:} scar tissue, typically after surgery or infection
    \item \textbf{Uterine septum:} a congenital structural variant
    \item \textbf{Adenomyosis and endometriosis:} endometrial tissue in the muscle wall or pelvis, which may coexist
    \item \textbf{Normal findings:} many women with bleeding have a structurally normal cavity, indicating hormonal causes
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Women should seek gynaecological review for heavy, irregular, or post-menopausal bleeding, bleeding between periods or after intercourse, or pelvic pain that is persistent or worsening. Women experiencing fertility problems or recurrent miscarriage may be offered hysteroscopy as part of their assessment.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain, heavy bleeding, or fever after hysteroscopy
    \item Foul-smelling discharge that may indicate infection
    \item Collapse, faintness, or dizziness accompanied by bleeding or pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} menstrual pattern, bleeding symptoms, pain, fertility history, and medication use
    \item \textbf{Pelvic examination and ultrasound:} initial assessment of the uterus and ovaries
    \item \textbf{Hysteroscopy:} direct visual inspection of the cavity, with photographs taken
    \item \textbf{Endometrial biopsy:} sampling of the lining at hysteroscopy or separately, especially after menopause
    \item \textbf{Operative hysteroscopy:} removal of polyps, fibroids, or adhesions under direct vision
    \item \textbf{Full blood count and iron studies:} for anaemia from chronic bleeding
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy test:} to exclude pregnancy as a cause of bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic hysteroscopy:} usually in the outpatient clinic, taking about 15 minutes, with local anaesthetic if needed
    \item \textbf{Polypectomy:} removal of polyps with scissors, graspers, or electrosurgery
    \item \textbf{Myomectomy:} resection of submucous fibroids through the hysteroscope
    \item \textbf{Adhesiolysis:} division of intrauterine adhesions to restore the cavity
    \item \textbf{Septum resection:} division of a uterine septum to improve fertility outcomes
    \item \textbf{Aftercare:} simple analgesia, rest for a day, and avoiding tampons and intercourse for a few weeks as advised
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review of pathology results and symptom response, with further treatment if needed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Uterine perforation, which is uncommon and usually heals without intervention
    \item Infection of the uterus or pelvis
    \item Heavy bleeding after operative procedures
    \item Fluid overload from the distending solution, more likely in long procedures
    \item Damage to the cervix or, rarely, to the bladder or bowel
    \item Failed or incomplete removal of fibroids or polyps, requiring a repeat procedure
    \item Anaesthesia-related risks in operative cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Diagnostic hysteroscopy is safe and well tolerated, and most women return to normal activities the same or next day. Removal of polyps improves bleeding in most women, and resection of submucous fibroids and division of adhesions or a septum improve symptoms and fertility outcomes in appropriately selected cases. Complications are rare in experienced hands. When hysteroscopy shows a normal cavity, attention turns to hormonal or systemic causes of the symptoms.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek timely review of abnormal bleeding so that treatable causes are found early
    \item Attend for any biopsy or imaging recommended, as results guide treatment
    \item Use simple analgesia and rest after the procedure as advised
    \item Avoid tampons, swimming, and intercourse for the period recommended after operative hysteroscopy
    \item Report fever, heavy bleeding, or severe pain promptly
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and treat anaemia, which supports general gynaecological health
    \item Keep follow-up appointments so that results and recovery are reviewed
\end{itemize}
