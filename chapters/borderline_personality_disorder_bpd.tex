\chapter{Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD)}
\index{Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Borderline personality disorder (BPD), also known as emotionally unstable personality disorder (EUPD), is a mental health condition characterised by a pervasive pattern of instability in emotions, interpersonal relationships, self-image, and behaviour. The name derives from an older view that the condition lay "on the border" between neurosis and psychosis, but it is now understood as a specific disorder of emotional regulation. People with BPD experience intense and rapidly shifting emotions, an unstable sense of identity, a chronic feeling of emptiness, frantic efforts to avoid abandonment, impulsive actions, and, in a large proportion, self-harm and suicidal thoughts. Periods of stress can trigger paranoid ideas or dissociation. BPD is not a judgement of character: it is a treatable condition, and the majority of people with the diagnosis improve significantly with appropriate psychological therapy and support.

\section*{Epidemiology}
BPD affects approximately 1--2\% of the general population, making it one of the most common personality disorders. It is diagnosed more often in women in clinical settings, although community studies suggest the true sex distribution may be more balanced. The condition is over-represented among people receiving mental health care, particularly in crisis and inpatient services, and it carries the highest rate of self-harm and suicide of any personality disorder. Onset is typically in adolescence or early adulthood, and the diagnosis is most often made in the twenties and thirties. BPD frequently co-occurs with depression, anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder, eating disorders, and substance use problems.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} twin and family studies indicate a substantial heritable component, with emotional reactivity and impulsivity being partly inherited traits.
    \item \textbf{Childhood adversity:} emotional, physical, or sexual abuse, neglect, and repeated invalidation of a child's feelings are among the strongest risk factors for BPD.
    \item \textbf{Attachment disruption:} early separation from or loss of caregivers can impair the development of secure relationships and emotional self-regulation.
    \item \textbf{Neurobiological changes:} imaging studies show heightened amygdala reactivity to emotional and threatening stimuli, together with reduced top-down control from the prefrontal cortex, producing intense responses that are slow to settle.
    \item \textbf{Neurotransmitter function:} disturbances in serotonin and other signalling systems contribute to impulsivity, aggression, and mood instability.
    \item \textbf{Stress sensitivity:} people with BPD are highly sensitive to perceived rejection or abandonment, and minor interpersonal triggers can precipitate overwhelming distress.
    \item \textbf{Environmental stress in adulthood:} ongoing conflict, instability, and invalidation can trigger and worsen symptoms.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The core features of BPD can be grouped into instability across four domains -- emotions, behaviour, self-image, and relationships:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Intense emotional swings lasting hours to days, often triggered by interpersonal events
    \item A chronic feeling of emptiness and a rapidly shifting, unstable sense of self and identity
    \item Frantic efforts to avoid real or imagined abandonment
    \item A pattern of unstable relationships alternating between idealisation and devaluation
    \item Impulsive behaviour in spending, sex, eating, substance use, or driving
    \item Repeated self-harm, such as cutting or burning
    \item Recurrent suicidal thoughts, gestures, or attempts
    \item Difficulty controlling anger, leading to outbursts or physical conflict
    \item Stress-related paranoid ideas or dissociation, such as feeling detached from reality
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bipolar disorder:} characterised by sustained episodes of mania and depression lasting days to weeks, rather than brief, situation-triggered swings in mood.
    \item \textbf{Depressive and anxiety disorders:} chronic low mood or anxiety can resemble, and commonly coexist with, BPD.
    \item \textbf{Post-traumatic stress disorder:} may follow abuse or violence and shares symptoms of emotional reactivity; it is defined by re-experiencing and avoidance of the trauma.
    \item \textbf{Other personality disorders:} narcissistic, histrionic, and antisocial personality disorders share some features of unstable self-image and behaviour.
    \item \textbf{Adult attention deficit hyperactivity disorder:} impulsivity and emotional reactivity can be mistaken for BPD.
    \item \textbf{Alcohol and drug misuse:} intoxication and withdrawal mimic impulsivity and mood swings.
    \item \textbf{Medical conditions:} thyroid disease and other physical illnesses can cause mood changes and warrant exclusion.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who regularly feels overwhelmed by emotions, hurts themselves, or struggles to keep relationships, study, or work on track because of mood instability should seek a professional assessment from a GP, psychologist, or psychiatrist. Early help is associated with better outcomes, and a diagnosis of BPD leads to structured, effective treatment rather than a label of being "difficult". Family members who are concerned about a loved one's self-harm or safety should encourage and facilitate assessment.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Thoughts of suicide or a plan to act on them
    \item An overdose or deliberate self-harm that has occurred
    \item Bleeding or other injury from self-harm that needs medical treatment
    \item A situation in which the person cannot be kept safe, or there is a risk to others
\end{itemize}
Crisis lines such as a local crisis service are available around the clock for support and guidance.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detailed history:} a longitudinal assessment of mood, relationships, impulsivity, self-image, self-harm, and any history of trauma, gathered sensitively over one or more sessions.
    \item \textbf{Mental state examination:} assessment of current mood, affect, thinking, perception, and risk of harm.
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic interview:} structured interviews and validated questionnaires identify the pattern of personality traits and confirm that the required features are present and long-standing.
    \item \textbf{Information from family:} with consent, collateral history is useful, since people with BPD may be unaware of or minimise certain patterns.
    \item \textbf{Physical review:} examination and blood tests to exclude medical causes of mood instability, such as thyroid disease, and to assess the effects of alcohol or drugs.
    \item \textbf{Risk assessment:} careful, repeated evaluation of suicide and self-harm risk, which is the highest clinical priority in BPD.
    \item \textbf{Co-morbidity screening:} assessment for depression, anxiety, PTSD, eating disorders, and substance use, which affect treatment planning.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Psychological therapy is the foundation of treatment, with medication playing a limited supportive role:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT):} the most extensively researched therapy for BPD; it teaches mindfulness, emotion regulation, distress tolerance, and interpersonal effectiveness, and reduces self-harm and hospitalisation.
    \item \textbf{Mentalisation-based therapy (MBT):} helps people understand their own mental states and those of others, improving emotion regulation and relationships.
    \item \textbf{Cognitive behaviour therapy and schema therapy:} address unhelpful beliefs and lifelong patterns.
    \item \textbf{Group and day programs:} deliver skills training and structured support alongside individual therapy.
    \item \textbf{Medication:} no drug is specifically licensed for BPD; medication is used only short-term for co-morbid depression, anxiety, or insomnia, or for severe distress.
    \item \textbf{Crisis and safety planning:} an agreed, written plan of early warning signs, coping strategies, and contacts for use during crises.
    \item \textbf{Psychoeducation and family support:} educating partners and families reduces conflict and improves outcomes.
    \item \textbf{Case management:} a named key worker coordinates care across primary, community, and crisis services.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Suicide and non-fatal self-harm, the most serious complications; around 10\% of people with BPD die by suicide
    \item Alcohol and substance use disorders
    \item Depression, anxiety, and eating disorders
    \item Relationship breakdown, family conflict, and social isolation
    \item Financial problems from impulsive spending and unstable employment
    \item Occupational difficulties and long-term unemployment
    \item Repeated hospital admissions and interruptions to education and career
    \item Physical complications of self-harm, including scarring and infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
BPD has a far better long-term outlook than is commonly assumed. Longitudinal studies show that most people no longer meet full diagnostic criteria after ten to twenty years, and the intensity of impulsivity and emotional instability typically softens with age. Symptoms improve gradually and can flare during stressful periods, but sustained engagement in structured therapy, a stable support network, and an effective crisis plan are strongly associated with good outcomes. In the short term, DBT significantly reduces suicidal behaviour and self-harm within months. Many people with BPD go on to complete education, hold stable jobs, and build lasting relationships, and the possibility of genuine recovery should always be the framework for treatment.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek assessment early if emotional instability or self-harm is emerging.
    \item Practise the mindfulness and emotion-regulation skills taught in therapy between sessions.
    \item Keep a written crisis plan with trusted contacts and safe activities, and review it regularly.
    \item Maintain a stable routine of regular sleep, meals, and physical activity.
    \item Avoid alcohol and recreational drugs, which destabilise mood and increase impulsivity.
    \item Stay in therapy and attend sessions even when feeling better.
    \item Connect with peer support groups for people with BPD and their families.
    \item Learn to recognise early warning signs and take action before a crisis develops.
\end{itemize}
