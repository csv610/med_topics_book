\chapter{Nettle rash}
\index{Nettle rash}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Nettle rash, medically known as urticaria (and commonly referred to as hives), is a prevalent dermatological condition characterised by the sudden appearance of itchy, raised, red or skin-coloured wheals (welts). These lesions are caused by the release of histamine and other vasoactive mediators from mast cells in the superficial dermis, leading to localised intradermal oedema and vasodilation. Nettle rash can be acute (lasting less than six weeks) or chronic (lasting six weeks or longer), and it may occur in isolation or alongside angioedema (deep subcutaneous or submucosal tissue swelling). Understanding nettle rash is crucial for clinicians to identify potential systemic allergic triggers and rule out anaphylaxis.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Nettle rash is a very common condition, affecting approximately 15\% to 20\% of the population at least once in their lifetime. It can occur in individuals of any age, gender, or ethnic background. Acute urticaria is more common in children and young adults, often triggered by viral infections or allergic reactions. Chronic urticaria is more prevalent in adults, particularly women (affecting them twice as often as men), and frequently has an autoimmune basis. In many countries, urticaria accounts for a notable proportion of general practice consultations and emergency presentations for allergic reactions.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The primary pathophysiological event in nettle rash is mast cell degranulation, which can be triggered by immunological or non-immunological pathways, leading to the release of histamine, prostaglandins, and leukotrienes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Allergic triggers:} IgE-mediated hypersensitivity to foods (e.g.\ nuts, shellfish, eggs), medications (e.g.\ penicillin, sulphonamides), or insect stings.
    \item \textbf{Infectious agents:} Viral upper respiratory tract infections (especially in paediatric cases), or bacterial infections such as \textit{Helicobacter pylori}.
    \item \textbf{Physical stimuli:} Mechanical pressure, cold exposure, heat, sunlight (solar urticaria), exercise (cholinergic urticaria), or friction (dermographism).
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune mechanisms:} Autoantibodies directed against the high-affinity IgE receptor (Fc$\epsilon$RI) or IgE itself, causing spontaneous mast cell activation.
    \item \textbf{Chemical triggers:} Non-immunological degranulation caused by certain medications, such as NSAIDs, aspirin, or ACE inhibitors.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Nettle rash presents with characteristic cutaneous lesions and associated sensations that can fluctuate rapidly.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pruritic wheals:} Raised, intensely itchy, erythematous or white welts that vary in size from millimetres to several centimetres.
    \item \textbf{Transient nature:} Individual wheals typically appear rapidly and resolve completely within 24 hours without scarring, though new wheals may continuously form elsewhere.
    \item \textbf{Angioedema:} Deeper swelling of the subcutaneous or submucosal tissues, commonly affecting the lips, eyelids, hands, feet, or genitalia.
    \item \textbf{Dermographism:} The appearance of a linear wheal along the line of firm stroking or scratching of the skin.
    \item \textbf{Systemic signs (rare):} Mild fever, fatigue, or joint pain in cases associated with underlying systemic vasculitis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Nettle rash must be distinguished from other inflammatory dermatoses and systemic conditions that present with urticarial-like lesions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urticarial vasculitis:} Wheals that persist for more than 24 hours, often burn or pain rather than itch, and leave residual hyperpigmentation.
    \item \textbf{Erythema multiforme:} Targetoid lesions representing a hypersensitivity reaction, usually triggered by infections (e.g.\ herpes simplex) or drugs.
    \item \textbf{Atopic dermatitis (eczema):} Chronic, dry, scaly plaques located in flexural distributions, rather than transient, migratory wheals.
    \item \textbf{Bullous pemphigoid:} Prodromal urticarial plaques that eventually develop tense subepidermal blisters.
    \item \textbf{Insect bites (papular urticaria):} Persistent, grouped papules with a central punctum, rather than transient, migratory wheals.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should seek medical advice if the rash is widespread, causing significant discomfort, or does not improve with over-the-counter antihistamines.
\hfill \break
\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Swelling of the tongue, throat, or mouth, causing difficulty breathing or swallowing.
    \item Shortness of breath, wheezing, or a persistent, barking cough.
    \item Dizziness, lightheadedness, or feeling faint (signs of anaphylactic shock).
    \item Nausea, vomiting, or severe abdominal pain accompanying the rash.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is primarily clinical, based on the appearance and transient nature of the lesions. Investigations are generally reserved for chronic or severe cases.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Detailed clinical history:} Focusing on potential triggers, duration of individual wheals, drug history, and systemic symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Full blood count and inflammatory markers:} CRP and ESR to screen for underlying systemic inflammatory or infectious diseases in chronic cases.
    \item \textbf{Allergy testing:} Skin prick tests or serum specific IgE tests if a specific IgE-mediated food, environmental, or venom allergy is suspected.
    \item \textbf{Autoantibody screening:} Thyroid autoantibodies and antinuclear antibodies (ANA) in chronic spontaneous urticaria.
    \item \textbf{Skin biopsy:} Indicated if lesions persist for more than 24 hours, are painful, or leave bruising, to rule out urticarial vasculitis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management aims to control symptoms, reduce pruritus, and avoid identified triggers.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{H1-antihistamines:} Second-generation non-sedating H1-antihistamines (e.g.\ cetirizine, fexofenadine, loratadine) are first-line, with doses up to four times the standard dose in refractory cases.
    \item \textbf{Short-course oral corticosteroids:} Used for acute exacerbations or severe urticaria to achieve rapid control.
    \item \textbf{Monoclonal antibody therapy (Omalizumab):} An anti-IgE biologic treatment indicated for chronic spontaneous urticaria refractory to high-dose antihistamines.
    \item \textbf{Trigger avoidance:} Identifying and avoiding physical triggers, specific foods, or drugs like NSAIDs.
    \item \textbf{Cool compresses and topical agents:} Applying cool, damp cloths or calamine lotion to soothe intense itching.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sleep deprivation and chronic fatigue due to nocturnal pruritus.
    \item Significant impairment in quality of life and psychiatric comorbidities (anxiety and depression).
    \item Anaphylaxis when nettle rash is part of a systemic IgE-mediated allergic reaction.
    \item Secondary skin infections due to excoriation (scratching).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Acute urticaria generally has an excellent prognosis, resolving spontaneously within days to a few weeks in the vast majority of cases. Chronic urticaria is more challenging, often running a fluctuating course. However, approximately 50\% of patients with chronic spontaneous urticaria experience remission within 12 months, and most can be well-controlled with modern pharmacological therapies, particularly second-generation antihistamines and biological agents.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Avoid known triggers, including specific foods, medications, or physical stimuli like hot showers.
    \item Wear loose-fitting, breathable clothing to minimise friction and pressure on the skin.
    \item Keep skin moisturised and cool to reduce the sensation of itch.
    \item Manage stress, as emotional stress can exacerbate urticaria flares.
    \item Keep a symptom diary to help identify potential triggers and track treatment efficacy.
\end{itemize}
