\chapter{Ehrlichiosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Ehrlichiosis is a bacterial infection transmitted by the bite of infected ticks. The bacteria, from the Ehrlichia and Anaplasma families, infect white blood cells and cause a febrile illness with headache, muscle aches, and fatigue. In Australia, the disease caused by the related bacterium Anaplasma platys is sometimes called human anaplasmosis or Australian tick typhus-like illness, and it has been increasingly recognised following tick bites. Most people recover fully with prompt antibiotic treatment, but the illness can be severe in older people and those with weakened immune systems.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Ehrlichiosis occurs in regions where the ticks that carry the bacteria are found. In the United States, human monocytic ehrlichiosis is most common in the south-eastern and south-central states, while anaplasmosis occurs mainly in the north-east and upper mid-west. In Australia, a form of ehrlichiosis associated with the paralysis tick Ixodes holocyclus has been identified along the eastern seaboard. Cases increase in warmer months when ticks are active and people spend time outdoors.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Ehrlichiosis is caused by obligate intracellular bacteria that infect blood cells.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Organisms:} Ehrlichia chaffeensis causes human monocytic ehrlichiosis, and Anaplasma phagocytophilum causes human granulocytic anaplasmosis; Anaplasma platys is the organism recognised in Australian cases
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} the bacteria are transmitted through the bite of an infected tick, with transmission requiring the tick to be attached for a period of time
    \item \textbf{Reservoirs:} wild animals, particularly deer and rodents, maintain the bacteria in nature
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} the bacteria infect white blood cells and multiply within them, triggering an immune response and inflammation
    \item \textbf{Effects:} fever, low blood counts, and in severe cases effects on the liver, kidneys, and nervous system
    \item \textbf{Incubation:} symptoms usually begin one to two weeks after the tick bite
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The illness ranges from mild to severe.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever, chills, and severe headache
    \item Muscle aches, joint pain, and fatigue
    \item Nausea, vomiting, and loss of appetite
    \item A rash, which occurs in a minority of people
    \item Cough and breathing difficulty in some cases
    \item Confusion or neurological symptoms in severe cases
    \item Low blood pressure in severe disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Tick-borne and other infections cause similar symptoms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Other tick-borne diseases, including Q fever, Lyme disease, and spotted fever rickettsioses
    \item Influenza and other viral infections
    \item Meningitis and encephalitis
    \item Other causes of fever with low blood counts, including viral infections and sepsis
    \item Malaria in travellers
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with a fever and other symptoms following a tick bite, or after time in tick habitat, should see a doctor and mention the tick exposure. Prompt treatment prevents serious complications.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item High fever with confusion, seizures, or reduced consciousness
    \item Difficulty breathing
    \item Severe headache with neck stiffness
    \item Collapse or very low blood pressure
    \item A spreading rash with fever
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is suggested by the history of tick exposure and confirmed with laboratory tests.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} tick bite or outdoor exposure and the pattern of symptoms
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count may show low platelets and white cells, and liver enzymes are often raised
    \item \textbf{Specific tests:} PCR testing of blood detects the bacterial DNA and is most useful early in the illness
    \item \textbf{Antibody testing:} blood tests for antibodies, which appear later
    \item \textbf{Treatment on suspicion:} antibiotics are often started before the diagnosis is confirmed, because early treatment is important
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is straightforward and effective when started early.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} doxycycline is the treatment of choice for both adults and children
    \item \textbf{Early treatment:} starting antibiotics on suspicion, without waiting for laboratory confirmation
    \item \textbf{Symptom relief:} fluids, rest, and paracetamol for fever and pain
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} for severe cases, with supportive care for complications
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} blood tests to follow recovery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe illness with hospitalisation in older people and those with weakened immunity
    \item Meningitis and encephalitis
    \item Respiratory failure
    \item Kidney and liver dysfunction
    \item Bleeding problems from very low platelets
    \item Death, which is rare with prompt treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt antibiotic treatment, the outlook is excellent, and most people recover fully within a few weeks. Delayed treatment increases the risk of severe disease and complications. As with other tick-borne diseases, prevention through avoiding tick bites is the most important strategy.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Avoid walking through long grass and bushy areas in tick season
    \item Wear long sleeves, long pants, and insect repellent containing DEET or picaridin
    \item Check for ticks after outdoor activity and remove any promptly
    \item Remove ticks correctly with fine-tipped tweezers, pulling steadily without crushing
    \item Check pets and children for ticks
    \item Seek medical advice promptly after a tick bite with symptoms
\end{itemize}
