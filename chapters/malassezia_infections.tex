\chapter{Malassezia Infections}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Malassezia infections are skin and systemic conditions caused by the overgrowth or pathogenic transition of the genus \textit{Malassezia} (formerly known as \textit{Pityrosporum}), which are lipophilic (lipid-loving), commensal yeasts forming part of the normal human cutaneous microflora. Under the influence of specific host and environmental factors, these yeasts can transform from a benign yeast phase into a pathogenic pseudohyphal phase, leading to a variety of dermatological disorders. These include pityriasis versicolor, Malassezia folliculitis, seborrheic dermatitis (including dandruff), and neonatal cephalic pustulosis. In rare instances, particularly in immunocompromised individuals or neonates receiving lipid infusions via central venous catheters, \textit{Malassezia} can cause life-threatening systemic fungaemia. Management primarily involves topical or systemic antifungal therapies alongside strategies to reduce skin surface lipids.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Malassezia-associated dermatoses are highly prevalent worldwide. Pityriasis versicolor is particularly common in tropical and subtropical regions, where hot and humid conditions support yeast growth, affecting up to 40\% of the population in these areas compared to roughly 1\% to 2\% in temperate climates. It predominantly affects adolescents and young adults, coinciding with the peak activity of sebaceous glands and increased sebum production. Seborrheic dermatitis has a bimodal distribution, occurring in infants (cradle cap) and adults aged 30 to 60, with an estimated general population prevalence of 3\% to 5\%. However, in immunocompromised cohorts—particularly individuals living with HIV/AIDS—the prevalence of seborrheic dermatitis rises dramatically to between 35\% and 85\%, often presenting with severe, refractory symptoms.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The genus \textit{Malassezia} currently comprises over 14 distinct species, with \textit{M. globosa}, \textit{M. restricta}, and \textit{M. sympodialis} being the most frequently isolated in human dermatoses.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lipid dependence:} Because \textit{Malassezia} yeasts lack fatty acid synthase genes, they are unable to synthesise long-chain fatty acids and must rely on host sebum. They colonise sebum-rich areas of the body, such as the face, scalp, chest, and upper back.
    \item \textbf{Transformation factors:} Excessive sweating (hyperhidrosis), high ambient humidity, occlusion of the skin (e.g. from tight clothing or greasy cosmetics), systemic corticosteroid use, and immunosuppression trigger the multiplication and pathogenic transformation of the yeast.
    \text{\item \textbf{Inflammatory response:}} The yeast produces extracellular lipases and phospholipases that hydrolyse sebum triglycerides into free fatty acids (such as oleic acid). These breakdown products irritate the epidermis, disrupt the skin barrier, and recruit inflammatory mediators, driving the lesions of seborrheic dermatitis.
    \item \textbf{Pigment alteration:} In pityriasis versicolor, the yeast produces dicarboxylic acids (most notably azelaic acid), which act as competitive inhibitors of tyrosinase, the enzyme responsible for melanin synthesis. This leads to hypopigmentation. Conversely, hyperpigmentation can occur due to post-inflammatory changes and melanocyte stimulation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical manifestations vary depending on the specific dermatological condition caused by the yeast overgrowth.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pityriasis versicolor:} Fine, scaly, macules and patches on the chest, back, neck, and upper arms. The lesions can be hypopigmented (pale), hyperpigmented (brown), or erythematous (pink). A hallmark feature is that these lesions do not tan upon sun exposure.
    \item \textbf{Malassezia folliculitis:} Itchy, monomorphic (uniform in appearance) papules and pustules located predominantly on the upper chest, back, shoulders, and upper arms, which frequently flare after sweating or sun exposure.
    \item \textbf{Seborrheic dermatitis:} Greasy, yellowish scales overlying erythematous plaques in sebum-rich regions, including the scalp, eyebrows, glabella, nasolabial folds, retroauricular areas, and presternal region.
    \item \textbf{Neonatal cephalic pustulosis:} Inflammatory papules and pustules on the face and scalp of infants, peaking around 3 weeks of age, characterised by the absence of comedones (blackheads/whiteheads).
    \item \textbf{Systemic fungaemia symptoms:} Fever, respiratory distress, and thrombocytopenia in patients with central lines receiving parenteral lipid emulsions.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because \textit{Malassezia} infections can present in multiple ways, they must be distinguished from other infectious and inflammatory dermatoses.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pityriasis versicolor vs.\ vitiligo:} Vitiligo lesions are completely depigmented (chalk-white), lack any surface scale, have a predilection for acral and periorificial areas, and do not show fluorescence under Wood's lamp.
    \item \textbf{Pityriasis versicolor vs.\ pityriasis rosea:} Pityriasis rosea features a larger "herald patch", followed by smaller oval plaques aligned along skin cleavage lines in a "Christmas tree" pattern, and is not associated with lipophilic yeast.
    \item \textbf{Malassezia folliculitis vs.\ acne vulgaris:} Acne vulgaris presents with polymorphic lesions (comedones, papules, pustules, cysts) and is not typically pruritic, whereas Malassezia folliculitis is intensely itchy and monomorphic without comedones.
    \item \textbf{Seborrheic dermatitis vs.\ plaque psoriasis:} Psoriasis plaques are typically well-demarcated with silvery, micaceous scale, occurring on extensor surfaces (elbows, knees), and are less oily than seborrheic dermatitis.
    \item \textbf{Seborrheic dermatitis vs.\ contact dermatitis:} Allergic or irritant contact dermatitis is associated with a clear exposure history and presents with vesicles, intense pruritus, and localized eczema.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should consult a general practitioner or dermatologist if skin lesions are widespread, cosmetically distressing, do not respond to over-the-counter topical treatments, or are associated with severe itching.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item High fever, chills, and signs of sepsis (e.g. rapid heart rate, confusion, low blood pressure) in an immunocompromised patient or a neonate with a central venous catheter.
    \item Symptoms of a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) to antifungal medications, such as facial swelling, hives, and difficulty breathing.
    \item Spreading skin redness, warmth, severe pain, and swelling, suggesting secondary bacterial cellulitis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is primarily clinical but can be rapidly confirmed using simple office-based procedures.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Potassium hydroxide (KOH) preparation:} Microscopic examination of skin scrapings from pityriasis versicolor lesions reveals the classic "spaghetti and meatballs" (or "bananas and grapes") appearance, representing short, thick hyphae mixed with clusters of round yeast cells.
    \item \textbf{Wood's lamp examination:} Exposure of pityriasis versicolor lesions to ultraviolet light in a darkened room reveals a characteristic pale yellow-gold or yellow-green fluorescence.
    \item \textbf{Skin biopsy:} Rarely required, but in atypical cases of Malassezia folliculitis, histopathology shows dilated follicles containing abundant round and oval budding yeast cells within the follicular infundibulum, highlighted by PAS or Grocott stains.
    \item \textbf{Culture:} Not routinely performed because \textit{Malassezia} requires specialized media enriched with lipids (e.g. Dixon's agar or olive oil overlay) to grow, and its presence does not prove infection as it is a normal commensal.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is directed at reducing the yeast population and addressing predisposing factors.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Topical antifungal therapy:} The first-line treatment for localized infections. This includes ketoconazole 2\% cream or shampoo, selenium sulfide 2.5\% lotion/shampoo, ciclopirox olamine 1\% cream, or terbinafine 1\% spray. Shampoos are applied to the affected skin, left on for 5 to 10 minutes, and then rinsed off.
    \item \textbf{Oral antifungal therapy:} Indicated for extensive, recurrent, or topically refractory pityriasis versicolor and Malassezia folliculitis. Preferred agents are oral itraconazole (200 mg daily for 7 days) or oral fluconazole (300 mg weekly for 2 to 3 weeks). \textit{Note: Oral terbinafine is ineffective for Malassezia infections.}
    \item \textbf{Maintenance therapy:} Because recurrence is common (up to 80\% within a year), patients may require prophylactic application of topical ketoconazole or selenium sulfide once or twice a week, or monthly oral antifungals.
    \item \textbf{Keratolytic agents:} Salicylic acid or sulfur-containing soaps to help strip away the scaly skin layers and reduce sebum accumulation.
    \item \textbf{Addressing predisposing factors:} Advising patients to avoid occlusive clothing, heavy oils, and greasy moisturisers.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
While predominantly a cosmetic and pruritic nuisance, Malassezia infections can lead to specific complications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Post-inflammatory pigmentary changes:} Persistent hypopigmentation or hyperpigmentation that can remain for several months after the active yeast infection has been successfully eradicated.
    \item \textbf{Secondary bacterial infection:} Excoriations (scratch marks) from pruritic folliculitis or seborrheic dermatitis can introduce bacteria, leading to impetigo or cellulitis.
    \item \textbf{Catheter-associated fungaemia:} A life-threatening systemic infection occurring when \textit{Malassezia} colonises central venous catheters, particularly in premature infants or severely ill adults receiving lipid emulsions.
    \item \textbf{Psychosocial distress:} Significant cosmetic embarrassment, social anxiety, and reduced quality of life due to highly visible facial or body rashes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for cutaneous Malassezia infections is excellent, with rapid resolution of scaling, itching, and pustules within 2 to 4 weeks of starting appropriate antifungal therapy. However, the normalisation of skin pigmentation in pityriasis versicolor is slow, often taking several months of sun exposure for melanocyte function to recover. Recurrence is extremely common due to the yeast's commensal nature, and many patients require ongoing maintenance therapy, especially during hot, humid summer months.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventive self-care aims to minimise the warmth, humidity, and oiliness that facilitate yeast overgrowth.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Wear breathable clothing:} Choosing loose-fitting, lightweight, and natural fabrics (such as cotton or linen) to reduce sweat accumulation.
    \item \textbf{Dry skin thoroughly:} Ensuring the skin is completely dry after bathing, swimming, or intense physical exercise.
    \item \textbf{Avoid greasy skin products:} Refraining from using heavy oils, thick body lotions, or oil-based sunscreens on areas prone to breakouts.
    \item \textbf{Prophylactic shampoo use:} Utilizing an anti-dandruff shampoo containing ketoconazole, selenium sulfide, or zinc pyrithione once a week during warm, humid seasons.
    \item \textbf{Prompt showering:} Showering immediately after sweating to remove excess sebum and sweat from the skin surface.
\end{itemize}
