\index{Meditation}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Meditation is a broad term for a range of mind-body practices characterised by the intentional training of attention and awareness to foster mental clarity, emotional regulation, and physiological relaxation. Rooted in ancient philosophical and spiritual traditions, modern secular meditation practices---most notably mindfulness-based interventions---have been extensively integrated into clinical medicine and psychology. As a complementary therapeutic modality, meditation aims to reduce the physiological effects of chronic stress, manage anxiety and depressive symptoms, alleviate chronic pain, and improve overall subjective wellbeing by modulating central nervous system activity and stress-response pathways.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Meditation has experienced a substantial surge in popularity globally over the past two decades. In many countries, national health surveys indicate that meditation is one of the most widely practised complementary health therapies, utilised by approximately 15\% to 20\% of the adult population. It is practised across all socioeconomic groups, with a higher prevalence of use among females, university-educated individuals, and middle-aged adults. Its clinical integration is supported by a growing body of evidence, leading to its inclusion in therapeutic guidelines for stress, anxiety, and chronic disease management.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The health benefits of meditation are mediated through neurobiological structural changes and downregulation of the autonomic nervous system.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Autonomic nervous system regulation:} Meditation activates the parasympathetic nervous system (the ``rest and digest'' response) while suppressing sympathetic nervous system activity, resulting in reductions in heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, and systemic cortisol levels.
    \item \textbf{Amygdala downregulation:} Regular practice is associated with decreased grey matter density and activity in the amygdala (the brain's fear and stress centre), leading to reduced reactivity to emotional stressors.
    \item \textbf{Prefrontal cortex activation:} Increased cortical thickness and activity in the prefrontal cortex, which enhances executive functioning, sustained attention, and emotional regulation.
    \item \textbf{Default mode network (DMN) modulation:} Meditation reduces activity in the DMN (the brain network active during mind-wandering, self-referential thought, and rumination), which is often hyperactive in individuals with depression and anxiety.
    \item \textbf{Systemic anti-inflammatory effects:} Downregulation of pro-inflammatory gene expression (such as NF-$\kappa$B pathways), leading to reduced levels of inflammatory cytokines (e.g.\ IL-6 and C-reactive protein) in chronic practitioners.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Meditation practices are diverse, and patients participating in these programmes can experience several subjective and objective clinical shifts.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diverse modalities:} Practices include mindfulness meditation (open monitoring), focused attention meditation (e.g.\ transcendental meditation or breath awareness), loving-kindness meditation, and movement-based practices (e.g.\ yoga, tai chi, or qigong).
    \item \textbf{Cognitive flexibility:} Increased ability to observe internal thoughts, emotions, and physical sensations without immediate reactivity or judgement.
    \item \textbf{Enhanced attention control:} Improved capacity to sustain focus, resist distractions, and redirect attention back to a chosen anchor (such as the breath).
    \item \textbf{Relaxation response:} Immediate physical signs of relaxation, including slower breathing, reduced muscle tension, and warmth in the extremities.
    \item \textbf{Transient emotional challenges:} Occasionally, novice practitioners may experience brief periods of heightened anxiety, emotional release, or restlessness as they become more aware of suppressed thoughts and feelings.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
While meditation is a supportive practice rather than a diagnostic condition, clinical assessment is required to differentiate its appropriate use from situations requiring primary psychiatric or medical intervention.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Major depressive disorder (MDD):} Meditation is an effective relapse-prevention tool (e.g.\ Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy) but should not replace pharmacotherapy or psychotherapy in acute, severe major depression.
    \item \textbf{Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD):} Meditation can reduce anxiety symptoms, but standard medical therapies (CBT, SSRIs) remain first-line for moderate-to-severe diagnostic GAD.
    \item \textbf{Psychosis or schizophrenia:} Extreme mindfulness or intensive meditation retreats can trigger depersonalisation, derealisation, or exacerbate psychotic symptoms in predisposed individuals, necessitating caution.
    \item \textbf{Chronic pain syndromes:} Mind-body techniques help manage the emotional distress of chronic pain, but structural, neurological, or autoimmune causes must be formally ruled out and managed concurrently.
    \item \textbf{Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD):} Unguided meditation can trigger flashbacks or panic in patients with severe PTSD; trauma-sensitive mindfulness, guided by a trained therapist, is required.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Individuals should consult a general practitioner or mental health professional before commencing intensive meditation programmes, especially if they have pre-existing mental health conditions.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute psychiatric emergencies, including severe panic attacks, psychotic episodes (hallucinations or delusions), or acute suicidal crises that can occasionally be triggered by intensive or unguided psychological stressors.
    \item Sudden, severe physical symptoms (e.g.\ chest pain, severe dyspnoea) occurring during or after practice, which require emergency cardiovascular assessment.
\end{itemize}
Other reasons to see a doctor: If meditation increases your feelings of anxiety, depression, depersonalisation, or derealisation, stop the practice and seek advice from a medical practitioner or psychologist.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Before prescribing or recommending meditation within a clinical setting, a brief assessment is helpful to ensure safety and fit.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Psychiatric screening:} Evaluating for a history of trauma, psychosis, or severe active depression to determine if standard, modified, or therapist-guided meditation is appropriate.
    \item \textbf{Expectation setting:} Assessing the patient's goals, preferences, and willingness to practise, which are key determinants of adherence and clinical success.
    \item \textbf{Symptom baseline documentation:} Using standardised questionnaires (e.g.\ K10 for psychological distress or DASS-21) to monitor progress and clinical response.
    \item \textbf{Physical assessment:} For movement-based meditation practices (such as yoga or tai chi), assessing joint mobility, cardiovascular stability, and balance to prevent musculoskeletal injury.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Meditation is integrated into healthcare through structured clinical programmes, digital applications, and self-directed practice.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR):} An evidence-based, 8-week group programme combining mindfulness meditation, gentle yoga, and group discussion, developed by Jon Kabat-Zinn.
    \item \textbf{Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT):} Combining mindfulness practices with cognitive therapy principles, specifically indicated to prevent relapse in recurrent depression.
    \item \textbf{Daily self-directed practice:} Encouraging 10 to 20 minutes of daily practice using structured breathing, body scans, or guided digital applications (e.g.\ Headspace, Smiling Mind).
    \item \textbf{Clinical integration:} Incorporating meditation as an adjunct to standard pharmacological and psychological treatments for chronic pain, cardiovascular disease, oncology, and sleep disorders.
    \item \textbf{Professional guidance:} Recommending instruction from qualified mindfulness teachers, psychologists, or certified yoga instructors to ensure proper technique and support.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Although generally safe, intensive or inappropriate meditation practice can lead to adverse psychological effects, particularly in vulnerable individuals.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Increased anxiety or panic:} The focused attention on internal sensations can sometimes cause hyper-awareness, triggering panic or somatic anxiety.
    \item \textbf{Depersonalisation and derealisation:} A transient feeling of detachment from one's mind, body, or surroundings, which can be distressing if it occurs unexpectedly.
    \item \textbf{Exacerbation of trauma:} Silent or unguided meditation may allow traumatic memories or flashbacks to surface without adequate coping mechanisms.
    \item \textbf{Delayed medical treatment:} Over-reliance on meditation as a primary cure for physical or severe psychiatric conditions, leading to dangerous delays in seeking conventional medical care.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
For most individuals, regular meditation practice yields significant, long-term improvements in stress tolerance, emotional stability, sleep quality, and overall quality of life. Clinical studies demonstrate that MBSR and MBCT produce therapeutic outcomes comparable to cognitive behavioural therapy and maintenance antidepressant medication in reducing distress and preventing depressive relapse. The benefits are dose-dependent, with regular daily practice leading to more pronounced structural brain changes and clinical improvements than occasional practice.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Integrating meditation into a healthy lifestyle supports long-term mental resilience and stress prevention.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Start small:} Begin with short sessions (e.g.\ 3 to 5 minutes daily) and gradually increase the duration as comfort and concentration improve.
    \item \textbf{Establish a routine:} Anchor the practice to a regular daily habit, such as meditating immediately after waking up or before going to bed.
    \item \textbf{Create a quiet space:} Find a comfortable, distraction-free environment to practise, using a chair or cushion to maintain a straight, relaxed posture.
    \item \textbf{Use guided resources:} Utilise reputable apps, audio recordings, or local classes to help guide the mind and learn fundamental techniques.
    \item \textbf{Adopt an attitude of patience:} Accept that the mind will wander naturally; the practice consists of gently returning focus without self-criticism.
\end{itemize}
