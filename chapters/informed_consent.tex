\chapter{Informed Consent}
\index{Informed Consent}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Informed consent is the process by which a person voluntarily agrees to a medical test, treatment, or procedure after receiving and understanding the relevant information. It is both an ethical requirement and a legal principle based on the right of every person to decide what happens to their own body. Consent must be given voluntarily, by a person with capacity, after adequate information about the nature, benefits, risks, and alternatives of the proposed care, including the option of doing nothing. Consent may be verbal, written, or implied, and it can be withdrawn at any time. The process is a conversation, not merely a signed form, and it respects the person's values, culture, and preferences.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Informed consent is relevant to every healthcare encounter, from a blood test to major surgery, and its importance has grown with the rise of complex treatments, research participation, and patient-centred care. Complaints and legal claims about inadequate consent are not uncommon, and courts have established clear duties on clinicians to disclose material risks: risks that a reasonable person in the patient's position would want to know. Vulnerable groups, including children, people with cognitive impairment, people from culturally and linguistically diverse backgrounds, and people with low health literacy, are at particular risk of inadequate consent and need additional support in the process.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Consent has no biological mechanism, but its ethical foundations rest on the principle of autonomy: the right of a competent person to make decisions about their own body. Valid consent requires three elements: capacity, meaning the ability to understand, retain, weigh, and communicate a decision; voluntariness, meaning freedom from coercion or undue influence; and information, meaning disclosure of the nature of the treatment, its expected benefits, material risks, alternatives, and consequences of no treatment. Barriers to valid consent include language and communication difficulties, cognitive impairment, emergency situations, and systems that treat consent as a formality rather than a process.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A discussion with the clinician before any significant test, treatment, or procedure
    \item Information about the diagnosis, the proposed treatment, and how it is performed
    \item Disclosure of expected benefits, material risks, and common side effects
    \item Discussion of alternatives, including the option of no treatment
    \item An opportunity to ask questions and time to consider
    \item Written consent forms for surgery, anaesthesia, research, and other significant interventions
    \item Verbal or implied consent for everyday procedures such as blood tests
    \item Documentation of the consent discussion in the medical record
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The validity of consent may be questioned in several circumstances.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lack of capacity:} from dementia, delirium, intellectual disability, or intoxication
    \item \textbf{Coercion:} pressure from family, carers, or clinicians
    \item \textbf{Inadequate information:} undisclosed risks that the person would have considered material
    \item \textbf{Emergency treatment:} where consent cannot be obtained and treatment is necessary to save life
    \item \textbf{Children:} consent by a parent or guardian, with assent of the child considered; mature minors may consent independently
    \item \textbf{Culturally or linguistically complex situations:} requiring interpreters and cultural awareness
    \item \textbf{Advance care directives:} where a person's prior wishes govern their care
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People should not hesitate to ask questions before any treatment and should be offered enough time and information to decide comfortably. Anyone who feels pressured, confused, or inadequately informed about a proposed procedure should raise their concerns and can seek a second opinion. Carers and families should support people who have difficulty understanding or communicating their wishes, and involve the healthcare team in decisions.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Any medical emergency, where treatment proceeds to save life and consent is sought as soon as practical
    \item Situations where a person without capacity is at immediate risk of harm
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Assessment of capacity:} the clinician tests the ability to understand, retain, weigh, and communicate a decision
    \item \textbf{Structured consent discussions:} using decision aids, patient information leaflets, and interpreters
    \item \textbf{Documentation:} recording the discussion, questions raised, and the person's decision
    \item \textbf{Written consent forms:} for procedures, surgery, anaesthesia, and research
    \item \textbf{Legal and ethical review:} by ethics committees for research, and tribunals for decisions involving people without capacity
    \item \textbf{Second opinions:} encouraged when decisions are difficult or disputed
    \item \textbf{Advance care planning:} documenting future wishes for care
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Effective communication:} clear language, appropriate for the person's level of understanding
    \item \textbf{Patient information:} written material and decision aids in suitable languages and formats
    \item \textbf{Interpreters:} professional interpreters for people with limited English
    \item \textbf{Involvement of family and carers:} with the person's agreement, respecting their support
    \item \textbf{Time and opportunity to ask questions:} without pressure
    \item \textbf{Respect for refusal:} a competent person's right to refuse treatment, even against medical advice
    \item \textbf{Withdrawal of consent:} recognised at any time, before or during treatment
    \item \textbf{Support for decision-making:} guardians and tribunals where a person lacks capacity
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Treatment performed without valid consent may amount to battery or negligence
    \item Undisclosed risks that eventuate can found a claim for damages
    \item Loss of trust between patient and clinician
    \item Distress, confusion, and feelings of being disrespected
    \item Poorer adherence to treatment when people feel uninformed
    \item Avoidable harm from proceeding without adequate discussion of risks
    \item Ethical and legal consequences for clinicians and institutions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
When consent is obtained properly, people are more satisfied, more likely to adhere to treatment, and better able to cope with outcomes, including complications they were prepared for. Shared decision-making is associated with better outcomes and fewer complaints. The legal standard requires disclosure of material risks and is well established. As healthcare becomes more patient-centred, informed consent is increasingly regarded as a continuous conversation rather than a one-off formality, and this benefits both patients and clinicians.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ask questions until you understand the proposed treatment and its risks
    \item Take time to decide; you are not obliged to agree immediately
    \item Bring a support person or ask for an interpreter if needed
    \item Ask what happens if you choose no treatment
    \item Read information sheets and ask for clarification
    \item Seek a second opinion for major decisions if you have doubts
    \item Document your wishes through advance care planning
    \item Remember that you can withdraw consent at any time
    \item Report any concerns about pressure or inadequate information
\end{itemize}
