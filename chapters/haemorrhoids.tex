\chapter{Haemorrhoids}
\index{Haemorrhoids}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Haemorrhoids, commonly called piles, are swollen and enlarged veins in the lower rectum and anus. Everyone has anal cushions of vascular tissue that help control bowel movements, but when these become swollen, stretched, or prolapsed, they cause symptoms such as bleeding, discomfort, itching, and pain. Haemorrhoids are very common and are usually caused by straining during bowel movements, constipation, prolonged sitting, pregnancy, and obesity. Most haemorrhoids are mild and improve with diet, fibre, fluids, and simple treatments, while more troublesome ones can be treated with office procedures or surgery. They are not dangerous and do not cause cancer.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Haemorrhoids are among the most common conditions affecting the anal area. Around 1 in 20 people have haemorrhoids at any time, and up to half of adults experience them at some stage. They become more common with age, peaking between 45 and 65, and are more common in people who are obese, pregnant, or have chronic constipation. Both men and women are affected. Many people do not seek medical care for haemorrhoids, so the true prevalence is higher than reported.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Haemorrhoids develop when the anal cushions become enlarged under pressure.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anatomy:} the anal canal contains cushions of blood vessels and connective tissue that help maintain continence
    \item \textbf{Increased pressure:} straining at stool, chronic constipation, and prolonged sitting raise pressure in the anal veins
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} the enlarging uterus and hormonal changes increase pressure and are a common cause
    \item \textbf{Obesity:} increased abdominal pressure
    \item \textbf{Internal haemorrhoids:} arise above the dentate line and usually bleed or prolapse without pain
    \item \textbf{External haemorrhoids:} arise below it, cause discomfort, and can become thrombosed (clotted) and painful
    \item \textbf{Grading:} internal haemorrhoids are graded 1--4 by whether and how they prolapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on the type and severity of the haemorrhoids.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Bright red bleeding on the toilet paper, in the toilet bowl, or coating the stool
    \item Itching and irritation around the anus
    \item Discomfort, soreness, or a feeling of fullness in the anal area
    \item A lump protruding from the anus, which may need to be pushed back
    \item Pain, especially with thrombosed external haemorrhoids, which form a tender, blue lump
    \item Mucus discharge
    \item Symptoms worse with constipation, straining, and prolonged sitting
    \item Bleeding that is painless, which distinguishes haemorrhoids from anal fissures
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of anal bleeding and symptoms must be considered.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Anal fissure, a painful tear in the anal lining
    \item Anal fistula and anal abscess
    \item Rectal polyps and colorectal cancer
    \item Inflammatory bowel disease, including ulcerative colitis and Crohn disease
    \item Rectal prolapse
    \item Pruritus ani (anal itching) from other causes
    \item Skin tags
    \item Proctitis and other anal conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for any rectal bleeding, especially in people over 50, as bleeding can indicate more serious conditions. Seek review for persistent pain, a lump that will not go back in, or symptoms that do not improve with self-care.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heavy rectal bleeding or bleeding that does not stop
    \item Sudden severe anal pain with a tender, swollen lump, which may be a thrombosed or strangulated haemorrhoid
    \item Signs of severe infection, including fever and spreading redness
    \item An inability to pass urine or stool with severe pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Haemorrhoids are diagnosed by examination, and tests may be needed to exclude other causes.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the pattern of bleeding and symptoms
    \item \textbf{Inspection:} external haemorrhoids and skin changes
    \item \textbf{Digital rectal examination:} checks internal haemorrhoids and the prostate in men
    \item \textbf{Proctoscopy:} visual inspection of the anal canal
    \item \textbf{Colonoscopy:} recommended for people with bleeding, especially those over 50, to exclude polyps and cancer
    \item \textbf{Full blood count:} if there has been significant bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Most haemorrhoids are managed conservatively, with procedures for persistent cases.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diet and fluids:} a high-fibre diet, plenty of water, and regular exercise prevent constipation and straining
    \item \textbf{Topical treatments:} creams, ointments, and suppositories reduce pain and itching
    \item \textbf{Sitz baths:} warm baths soothe the area
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} paracetamol or ibuprofen
    \item \textbf{Rubber band ligation:} a band is placed around the base of an internal haemorrhoid, which shrinks and falls off
    \item \textbf{Sclerotherapy and infrared coagulation:} other office procedures for small internal haemorrhoids
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} haemorrhoidectomy for large, prolapsing, or recurrent haemorrhoids
    \item \textbf{Managing contributing factors:} weight loss, treating constipation, and avoiding prolonged sitting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Anaemia from chronic bleeding, which is uncommon but possible
    \item Thrombosed haemorrhoids causing severe pain
    \item Prolapsed haemorrhoids that cannot be replaced (strangulation), requiring urgent care
    \item Infection, which is rare
    \item Complications of treatment procedures, including bleeding and pain
    \item Recurrence, especially if constipation and straining continue
    \item Delayed diagnosis of more serious conditions when bleeding is assumed to be haemorrhoids
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Haemorrhoids are not dangerous and, with appropriate management, most people improve or resolve completely. Mild haemorrhoids respond well to diet and lifestyle measures, and office procedures are effective for moderate cases. Surgery cures most severe cases. The main challenges are recurrence, which is prevented by avoiding constipation and straining, and the importance of investigating bleeding to exclude other conditions. With sensible self-care, most people with haemorrhoids are free of significant symptoms.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Eat a diet high in fibre, including fruits, vegetables, and whole grains
    \item Drink plenty of water
    \item Exercise regularly to promote regular bowel movements
    \item Do not delay going to the toilet when you feel the urge
    \item Avoid straining, and limit time on the toilet
    \item Avoid prolonged sitting; take breaks and move regularly
    \item Maintain a healthy weight
    \item Keep the anal area clean and dry
    \item See a doctor for any rectal bleeding, especially if over 50
    \item Follow treatment advice to prevent recurrence
\end{itemize}
