\chapter{Favism}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Favism is a severe acute haemolytic anaemia triggered by eating fava (broad) beans, or by inhaling their pollen, in people who have a deficiency of an enzyme called glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD). G6PD deficiency is an inherited condition, passed on the X chromosome, in which red blood cells are unusually vulnerable to oxidative stress. When a person with the deficiency eats fava beans or takes certain medicines, their red blood cells can break down rapidly, causing anaemia, jaundice, and dark urine. Favism is the classic example of this reaction and takes its name from the fava bean. The condition is preventable by avoiding the trigger, and it usually resolves once the exposure stops, though it can be life-threatening if severe.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Favism occurs wherever G6PD deficiency and fava bean consumption overlap. G6PD deficiency is one of the most common human enzyme disorders, affecting an estimated 400--500 million people worldwide, with high prevalence in Africa, the Middle East, Asia, the Mediterranean, and among people of these ancestries, including Aboriginal Australians. Because the gene is on the X chromosome, males are affected much more often and more severely than females. In regions where fava beans are a dietary staple, such as the Mediterranean and the Middle East, favism is a well-recognised seasonal emergency in spring when beans are harvested. In Australia, favism is seen mainly in migrants and their descendants from these regions.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{G6PD deficiency:} the enzyme G6PD protects red blood cells from oxidative damage by maintaining a supply of a protective molecule called NADPH; without it, cells are defenceless against oxidants
    \item \textbf{Fava beans:} contain compounds, including vicine and convicine, that generate oxidant stress in susceptible red blood cells
    \item \textbf{Mechanism of haemolysis:} oxidant damage makes red blood cells rigid and fragile, so they are destroyed prematurely, mostly in the spleen, producing acute haemolytic anaemia
    \item \textbf{Inheritance:} X-linked recessive, so sons inherit it from their mothers; carrier women are usually mildly affected but can pass it on
    \item \textbf{Other triggers:} certain medicines (such as some antimalarials, antibiotics, and painkillers) and infections can cause haemolysis by the same mechanism
    \item \textbf{Oxidative haemolysis:} the haemoglobin released from broken cells is broken down to bilirubin, causing jaundice, and iron released in the process can injure the kidneys
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms begin within hours to a few days of eating fava beans.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden fatigue, weakness, and breathlessness on exertion from acute anaemia
    \item Pallor, with pale lips and nail beds
    \item Jaundice, a yellow tinge to the skin and whites of the eyes, from rapid red cell breakdown
    \item Dark, tea- or cola-coloured urine from haemoglobin released into the urine
    \item Abdominal or back pain, nausea, and vomiting
    \item In severe cases: rapid heart rate, collapse, and features of acute kidney injury
    \item In infants, the same reaction can be triggered by other oxidants and may present with prolonged newborn jaundice
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Acute haemolysis has several causes that must be distinguished.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Other inherited red cell disorders, such as hereditary spherocytosis
    \item Immune haemolytic anaemia, in which antibodies attack red cells
    \item Haemolysis from other drugs or chemicals in susceptible people
    \item Acute infection, such as malaria, in endemic regions
    \item Sickle cell crisis in people with sickle cell disease
    \item Other causes of dark urine, including severe dehydration, muscle breakdown, and bilirubinuria from liver disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with known G6PD deficiency should see a doctor promptly if they develop dark urine, jaundice, or unusual fatigue after eating beans or taking new medicines. Babies with prolonged jaundice should be checked for G6PD deficiency.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe breathlessness, rapid heart rate, or collapse
    \item Confusion or reduced responsiveness
    \item Passing very little or no urine, which may indicate acute kidney injury
    \item Any severe reaction in a child
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood count and film:} reveals falling haemoglobin and characteristic fragmented or "bite" red cells (Heinz bodies)
    \item \textbf{Reticulocyte count:} raised in response to the marrow trying to replace destroyed cells
    \item \textbf{Biochemistry:} raised bilirubin and LDH, and falling haptoglobin, confirming haemolysis
    \item \textbf{Urine tests:} haemoglobin or haemosiderin in the urine, and tests of kidney function
    \item \textbf{G6PD enzyme assay:} measures enzyme activity in red cells; it is best done after the acute episode has settled, because young red cells made during recovery can have misleadingly normal levels
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} confirms the diagnosis and identifies the specific variant, useful for family counselling
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Remove the trigger:} stop eating fava beans immediately and avoid the offending medicines
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} rest, fluids to protect the kidneys, and monitoring of blood counts and kidney function
    \item \textbf{Blood transfusion:} needed when anaemia is severe or the patient is unstable
    \item \textbf{Treat infection:} any coexisting infection that is driving haemolysis should be treated promptly
    \item \textbf{Avoid risky medicines:} people with G6PD deficiency should carry a list of medicines to avoid, including dapsone, some sulfonamides, and certain antimalarials
    \item \textbf{Family testing and counselling:} test at-risk relatives, including newborn babies of affected families
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe anaemia requiring transfusion and, rarely, heart failure from profound anaemia
    \item Acute kidney injury from the breakdown products of haemolysis
    \item Gallstones from chronic, low-grade haemolysis in some people
    \item Newborn jaundice severe enough to require phototherapy or exchange transfusion
    \item Rarely, death in severe untreated episodes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Favism is a self-limited condition once the trigger is removed: the red cell count typically begins to recover within days, and most people recover fully within a few weeks with supportive care. Recurrences are prevented by avoiding fava beans and other oxidant triggers. Between episodes, people with G6PD deficiency are usually entirely well. The main risk is that a severe episode occurs before the diagnosis is known, which is why testing at-risk families and carrying medical identification are so important. With avoidance, life expectancy is normal.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item If you have G6PD deficiency, never eat fava beans in any form, and avoid their pollen during flowering season
    \item Learn which medicines to avoid and check every new prescription, including over-the-counter products
    \item Tell doctors, pharmacists, and dentists about the deficiency before any treatment
    \item Wear a medical alert bracelet or carry a card stating the condition
    \item Have family members tested, including babies
    \item Seek urgent medical attention for dark urine, jaundice, or fatigue after any possible exposure
    \item Newborn screening programs in Australia identify many affected babies early
\end{itemize}
