\chapter{Joint Inflammation}
\index{Joint Inflammation}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Joint inflammation, medically called arthritis, is the swelling, pain, redness, and warmth of a joint caused by an inflammatory process within it. It can be acute, as in gout, infection, or injury, or chronic, as in rheumatoid arthritis and psoriatic arthritis. Inflammatory arthritis differs from osteoarthritis, which is mostly a wear-and-tear condition without significant inflammation, and the distinction matters because inflammatory arthritis is treated with medicines that suppress the immune system and prevent joint damage. A hot, swollen joint with fever is a medical emergency, as it may be septic arthritis.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Inflammatory joint conditions affect a substantial number of people. Rheumatoid arthritis affects around 1 per cent of the population, is two to three times more common in women, and most often begins between ages 30 and 50. Gout affects around 7 per cent of men and is increasingly common. Psoriatic arthritis affects around 30 per cent of people with psoriasis. Other forms include reactive arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, and juvenile idiopathic arthritis in children. Early diagnosis is important, because modern treatment can prevent the joint damage that once was common.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Inflammation of a joint occurs when immune cells enter the synovium, the joint lining, releasing chemicals that cause swelling, pain, and destruction of cartilage and bone. In rheumatoid arthritis, the immune system mistakenly attacks the joint lining, producing chronic symmetrical inflammation, often with antibodies such as rheumatoid factor. In gout, uric acid crystals in the joint trigger intense acute inflammation. In septic arthritis, bacteria invade the joint and cause rapid destruction. The pattern of joints involved, the presence of morning stiffness, and blood tests distinguish the causes. In most inflammatory arthritides, the driving process is autoimmune or metabolic, and suppressing it early prevents damage.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Swelling, pain, redness, and warmth over the affected joints
    \item Morning stiffness lasting more than 30 minutes
    \item Symmetrical joint involvement, often the hands and wrists, in rheumatoid arthritis
    \item Sudden severe pain in a single joint, as in gout
    \item A hot, swollen joint with fever, which may indicate septic arthritis
    \item Fatigue, malaise, and weight loss in chronic inflammatory disease
    \item Stiffness and difficulty with daily tasks
    \item Nodules, skin rashes, or nail changes in associated conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The specific cause of joint inflammation must be identified.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rheumatoid arthritis:} symmetrical small joint inflammation with morning stiffness
    \item \textbf{Gout and pseudogout:} sudden flares with crystals in the joint
    \item \textbf{Septic arthritis:} a single hot, swollen joint with fever, requiring urgent treatment
    \item \textbf{Psoriatic arthritis:} joint inflammation with psoriasis
    \item \textbf{Reactive arthritis:} after gut or genital infection
    \item \textbf{Ankylosing spondylitis:} inflammation of the spine and sacroiliac joints
    \item \textbf{Osteoarthritis:} use-related pain without significant swelling or stiffness
    \item \textbf{Injury and crystal disease:} trauma, and other crystal forms
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Joint swelling, pain, and stiffness that persist beyond a few weeks warrant assessment, and features such as morning stiffness, symmetrical involvement, or systemic symptoms should prompt early referral. A hot, swollen, red joint, especially with fever, requires urgent medical care to exclude septic arthritis.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A hot, swollen, red joint with fever, which may indicate septic arthritis
    \item Severe joint pain after injury with deformity or inability to move the joint
    \item Difficulty breathing or chest pain, which may indicate a blood clot in inflammatory disease
    \item Collapse, confusion, or severe illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the joints involved, pattern of stiffness, and systemic features
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} inflammatory markers, rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP antibodies, and uric acid
    \item \textbf{Joint aspiration:} examination of joint fluid for crystals and infection
    \item \textbf{X-rays:} for joint damage and to exclude fracture
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound and MRI:} to detect inflammation and early erosion
    \item \textbf{Imaging of the spine and sacroiliac joints:} where spondylitis is suspected
    \item \textbf{Referral:} to a rheumatologist for confirmation and treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Disease-modifying medicines:} methotrexate and other drugs for rheumatoid and related arthritis
    \item \textbf{Biological therapies:} targeted medicines for severe or resistant disease
    \item \textbf{Gout treatment:} anti-inflammatory medicines for flares and urate-lowering therapy
    \item \textbf{Septic arthritis:} urgent antibiotics and joint drainage
    \item \textbf{Pain and stiffness relief:} anti-inflammatories and simple analgesics
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} strengthening, stretching, and joint protection
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} exercise, weight management, and smoking cessation
    \item \textbf{Joint injections:} corticosteroids for flares
    \item \textbf{Early specialist care:} to prevent joint damage
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Joint destruction and deformity in untreated inflammatory arthritis
    \item Loss of function and work capacity
    \item Osteoporosis from inflammation and steroid use
    \item Increased risk of heart disease in inflammatory arthritis
    \item Complications of treatment, including infection risk
    \item Chronic pain, fatigue, and depression
    \item Sepsis and joint destruction from septic arthritis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for inflammatory joint disease has transformed. With early diagnosis and modern medicines, most people with rheumatoid arthritis achieve remission or low disease activity and avoid joint damage, maintaining work and quality of life. Gout is fully controllable, and septic arthritis is curable with urgent treatment. The key is not to delay: persistent joint inflammation should be assessed early so that treatment can prevent the damage that was once inevitable.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek medical care early for joint swelling and stiffness
    \item Take disease-modifying medicines as prescribed and attend reviews
    \item Stay active with joint-friendly exercise
    \item Protect joints with pacing and correct technique
    \item Manage weight and stop smoking
    \item Attend physiotherapy and self-management programs
    \item Report fever, a hot joint, or new symptoms promptly
    \item Seek support for the emotional effects of chronic disease
\end{itemize}
