\chapter{Laparoscopic hysterectomy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Laparoscopic hysterectomy is a minimally invasive surgical procedure performed to remove the uterus, and potentially adjacent structures such as the fallopian tubes and ovaries, using a laparoscope (a thin telescope with a light and camera) and specialised surgical instruments inserted through several small abdominal incisions. This procedure is indicated for a variety of benign and malignant gynaecological conditions, including uterine fibroids, severe endometriosis, adenomyosis, abnormal uterine bleeding refractory to medical therapy, uterine prolapse, and early-stage gynaecological cancers. Compared to traditional open abdominal hysterectomy, the laparoscopic approach offers significant clinical benefits, including reduced postoperative pain, minimal scarring, shorter hospital stays, and a faster return to daily activities.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hysterectomy is one of the most common major gynaecological surgeries performed globally. In Australia, approximately 30,000 hysterectomies are performed annually, with a shifting trend away from open abdominal procedures toward minimally invasive techniques, including laparoscopic and vaginal approaches. The peak incidence for undergoing a hysterectomy is among women aged 40 to 50 years, primarily driven by benign conditions such as symptomatic uterine fibroids and adenomyosis. Gynaecological malignancies account for approximately 10\% of cases, where laparoscopic staging and hysterectomy play a vital therapeutic role. The adoption rate of laparoscopic hysterectomy continues to rise due to advances in surgical training and technology.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Laparoscopic hysterectomy is indicated when pathological processes within the female reproductive tract fail to respond to conservative treatments or require definitive surgical removal. The underlying causes leading to this intervention include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Uterine fibroids (leiomyomas):} Benign monoclonal smooth muscle tumours of the myometrium that cause pelvic pain, pressure symptoms, and heavy menstrual bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Adenomyosis:} The ectopic presence of endometrial glands and stroma within the myometrium, leading to reactive hypertrophy, severe dysmenorrhea, and chronic pelvic pain.
    \item \textbf{Endometriosis:} Ectopic endometrial tissue outside the uterine cavity causing chronic inflammation, pelvic adhesions, and deep infiltrating lesions that may require hysterectomy as part of comprehensive surgical clearance.
    \item \textbf{Abnormal uterine bleeding (AUB):} Persistent, heavy, or irregular menstrual bleeding that is refractory to pharmacological therapies or intrauterine device insertion.
    \item \textbf{Uterine prolapse:} Descent of the uterus into the vaginal canal due to pelvic floor muscle and ligamentous laxity.
    \item \textbf{Gynaecological malignancies:} Early-stage endometrial, cervical, or ovarian cancers where removal of the pelvic reproductive organs is a cornerstone of curative oncological treatment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Patients presenting for laparoscopic hysterectomy typically display a clinical presentation related to their underlying gynaecological pathology. Common clinical features include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Heavy menstrual bleeding:} Significant blood loss during menstruation, often leading to iron-deficiency anaemia and fatigue.
    \item \textbf{Chronic pelvic pain:} Persistent discomfort, pressure, or fullness in the lower abdomen or pelvis.
    \item \textbf{Severe dysmenorrhea:} Painful menstrual cramps that interfere with daily activities and are unresponsive to standard analgesia.
    \item \textbf{Dyspareunia:} Deep pelvic pain experienced during or after sexual intercourse.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic pressure symptoms:} Urinary frequency, urgency, or constipation due to mechanical compression of the bladder or bowel by an enlarged uterus.
    \item \textbf{Palpable pelvic mass:} An enlarged uterus or fibroids detectable on physical examination.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before proceeding to surgery, other non-surgical and surgical etiologies must be considered and ruled out. The differential diagnosis includes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Endometrial hyperplasia or polyps:} Benign mucosal overgrowth causing abnormal bleeding, which may be treated via hysteroscopic resection.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID):} Chronic pelvic infection causing pain and adhesions, treated primarily with antibiotics.
    \item \textbf{Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS):} Gastrointestinal dysfunction presenting with chronic pelvic discomfort and bloating.
    \item \textbf{Interstitial cystitis:} Chronic bladder pain syndrome mimicking pelvic pain and urinary pressure symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Ovarian pathology:} Ovarian cysts or neoplasms causing localised pelvic pain and mass effect.
    \item \textbf{Adenomyoma vs. leiomyoma:} Localised adenomyotic lesions mimicking uterine fibroids on pelvic imaging.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should undergo regular gynaecological evaluations if they experience abnormal menstrual bleeding, chronic pelvic pain, or pressure symptoms. Following laparoscopic hysterectomy, patients must receive clear instructions on post-surgical warning signs.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Shortness of breath:} Sudden onset of dyspnoea or chest pain, which may indicate a pulmonary embolism.
    \item \textbf{Profuse vaginal bleeding:} Heavy, active bright red bleeding or passing large clots.
    \item \textbf{Signs of shock:} Extreme dizziness, fainting, rapid heart rate, or loss of consciousness.
    \item \textbf{Severe, unremitting abdominal pain:} Pain that is not controlled by prescribed analgesics, which may indicate internal haemorrhage or bowel injury.
\end{itemize}
Other reasons to contact the surgeon or visit a clinic include a fever over 38 degrees Celsius, spreading redness or purulent discharge from the incision sites, persistent nausea and vomiting, or inability to pass urine.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of conditions requiring laparoscopic hysterectomy relies on a thorough clinical history, physical examination, and targeted investigations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transvaginal ultrasound (TVUS):} The primary imaging modality used to assess uterine size, locate fibroids, measure endometrial thickness, and evaluate the ovaries.
    \item \textbf{Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):} Utilised for detailed pre-operative planning to differentiate between fibroids and adenomyosis, or for staging gynaecological cancers.
    \item \textbf{Endometrial biopsy:} Performed to exclude endometrial hyperplasia or malignancy in patients presenting with abnormal uterine bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Full blood count (FBC):} Assesses for iron-deficiency anaemia secondary to chronic heavy menstrual bleeding.
    \item \textbf{Hysteroscopy:} Direct visual examination of the uterine cavity to identify mucosal abnormalities, polyps, or submucosal fibroids.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Laparoscopic hysterectomy is a definitive surgical treatment option managed by specialist gynaecologists:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgical approach:} Performed under general anaesthesia. The abdomen is insufflated with carbon dioxide gas to create a working space. A laparoscope and instruments are inserted through small port sites (usually 5 to 12 mm in size). The uterus is dissected from its vascular supply and ligamentous attachments and extracted vaginally or via morcellation.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological management:} Pre-operative GnRH agonists may be used to shrink large fibroids. Postoperative care includes multimodal analgesia (paracetamol, NSAIDs, and short-term oral opioids) and venous thromboembolism (VTE) prophylaxis.
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle and supportive care:} Early mobilisation postoperatively to prevent deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and promote bowel motility, avoidance of heavy lifting (typically greater than 5 kg) for 6 weeks, and pelvic floor exercises once cleared by the surgeon.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Potential risks and complications of laparoscopic hysterectomy include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Haemorrhage:} Intra-operative or postoperative bleeding requiring blood transfusion or return to theatre.
    \item \textbf{Adjacent organ injury:} Accidental damage to the bladder, ureters, or bowel, which may require intra-operative repair.
    \item \textbf{Infection:} Vaginal vault abscess, pelvic cellulitis, urinary tract infection, or superficial wound infection.
    \item \textbf{Thromboembolism:} Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or pulmonary embolism (PE) secondary to pelvic surgery and immobility.
    \item \textbf{Vaginal vault dehiscence:} A rare but serious complication where the vaginal incision separates, potentially leading to bowel evagination.
    \item \textbf{Anaesthetic risks:} Adverse reactions to general anaesthetic agents, airway injury, or cardiovascular events.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for patients undergoing laparoscopic hysterectomy is generally excellent. The majority of patients report significant improvements in quality of life, physical comfort, and emotional well-being due to the resolution of chronic pain and abnormal bleeding. The typical hospital stay is 1 to 2 days, and most women return to normal light activities within 2 to 4 weeks, with a full recovery expected by 6 weeks. Long-term satisfaction rates are high, particularly when appropriate pre-operative counselling has been provided regarding the loss of fertility and potential onset of menopause (if bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy is performed).

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
While the need for surgery cannot always be prevented, postoperative self-care is critical for optimising recovery:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Wound care:} Keep laparoscopic incision sites clean and dry, monitoring for signs of infection such as redness, swelling, or discharge.
    \item \textbf{Graded activity:} Slowly increase walking distance each day while avoiding strenuous exercise, running, or heavy lifting for 6 weeks.
    \item \textbf{Bowel management:} Eat a high-fibre diet and drink plenty of fluids to avoid constipation, which can strain pelvic incisions.
    \item \textbf{Pelvic rest:} Refrain from sexual intercourse, douching, and tampon use for at least 6 weeks postoperative to allow the vaginal vault to heal completely.
    \item \textbf{Medication compliance:} Take prescribed analgesics and stool softeners as directed, and complete any course of prophylactic medications.
\end{itemize}
