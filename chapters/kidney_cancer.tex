\chapter{Kidney Cancer}
\index{Kidney Cancer}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Kidney cancer, also called renal cell carcinoma, is a cancer that begins in the cells of the kidney. It is the most common type of kidney cancer in adults, and it often grows silently, causing no symptoms until it is advanced. Many kidney cancers are now found incidentally on scans performed for other reasons, when they are small and highly curable. The main symptom when present is blood in the urine, sometimes with a flank mass or pain. Treatment depends on the stage: small tumours may be removed by surgery, larger or advanced cancers are treated with surgery, targeted medicines, immunotherapy, and other therapies. Early detection and modern treatments have substantially improved outcomes.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Kidney cancer affects around 4,000 people each year and is about twice as common in men as in women. It usually occurs after age 50, with the average age at diagnosis around 64. Risk factors include smoking, obesity, high blood pressure, family history, and inherited conditions such as von Hippel-Lindau disease. Chronic kidney disease and long-term dialysis also increase risk. The incidence has risen over recent decades, largely because more cancers are found incidentally on imaging, but survival has improved as a result of earlier diagnosis and better treatments.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Kidney cancer arises from the cells lining the tiny tubes of the kidney, most commonly the clear cells of the proximal tubule. Genetic changes, some inherited and most acquired, drive these cells to grow out of control. The most common genetic pathway involves loss of the VHL gene, which normally helps control cell growth and oxygen sensing. Smoking and obesity increase risk through cellular damage, inflammation, and hormonal effects. Kidney cancers grow within the kidney and can invade the surrounding fat, the renal vein, and lymph nodes, and they spread most often to the lungs, bones, liver, and brain. Tumours often grow for years before causing symptoms or being detected.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Blood in the urine, the most common symptom, which may be visible or found on testing
    \item A lump or mass in the flank or abdomen
    \item Flank pain, which may be dull and constant
    \item Fatigue, weight loss, and poor appetite
    \item Fever, night sweats, and anaemia
    \item High blood pressure in some cases
    \item Many cancers cause no symptoms and are found incidentally on scans
    \item Symptoms of spread: cough, bone pain, or neurological symptoms in advanced disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions cause blood in the urine and flank masses.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urinary tract and kidney infection:} fever, pain, and urinary symptoms
    \item \textbf{Kidney stones:} colicky pain and blood
    \item \textbf{Benign kidney tumours:} such as oncocytomas and angiomyolipomas
    \item \textbf{Kidney cysts:} common and usually benign
    \item \textbf{Bladder cancer:} blood in the urine without flank features
    \item \textbf{Other retroperitoneal masses:} lymphoma and adrenal tumours
    \item \textbf{Glomerular disease:} blood and protein in the urine
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Blood in the urine always requires assessment, and anyone with a flank mass, persistent flank pain, or unexplained weight loss and fatigue should be seen. People with a family history or inherited risk should discuss screening. Incidental kidney masses found on scans should be assessed by a specialist, as most small cancers are highly curable.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heavy blood in the urine with difficulty passing urine
    \item Sudden severe flank or abdominal pain
    \item Coughing blood, severe breathlessness, or bone pain that may indicate spread
    \item Collapse, confusion, or signs of severe illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urine tests:} microscopy for blood and cytology
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} kidney function, full blood count, and calcium
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} the first test for a suspected kidney mass
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} the key imaging for diagnosis and staging
    \item \textbf{MRI:} in selected cases
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} in some cases to confirm the diagnosis before treatment
    \item \textbf{Staging scans:} chest and bone imaging for spread
    \item \textbf{Genetic assessment:} for younger people and those with family history
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Partial nephrectomy:} removal of the tumour while preserving the rest of the kidney
    \item \textbf{Radical nephrectomy:} removal of the whole kidney, often by keyhole surgery
    \item \textbf{Active surveillance:} monitoring small tumours in older or frail people
    \item \textbf{Ablation:} freezing or heating small tumours
    \item \textbf{Targeted therapies:} medicines that block the growth pathways of kidney cancer
    \item \textbf{Immunotherapy:} medicines that help the immune system attack the cancer
    \item \textbf{Metastatic disease:} combination treatments that can control the disease for years
    \item \textbf{Palliative care:} symptom management and support
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular imaging to detect recurrence
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Spread to the lungs, bones, liver, or brain
    \item Blood in the urine and anaemia
    \item Loss of kidney function, especially in advanced disease
    \item High blood pressure and hormonal effects of the tumour
    \item Complications of surgery and treatments
    \item Recurrence after treatment
    \item Effects of the disease and treatment on quality of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for kidney cancer has improved significantly. Small, localised cancers found incidentally are cured in over 90 per cent of cases with surgery. Even metastatic disease can now be controlled for years with targeted and immunotherapy combinations, and some people achieve long-term remission. Survival depends on the stage at diagnosis, the tumour grade, and the response to treatment, and regular follow-up detects recurrence early.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Do not smoke
    \item Maintain a healthy weight
    \item Manage blood pressure
    \item Report blood in the urine promptly, even if it is painless
    \item Attend follow-up imaging after treatment
    \item Discuss family history with your doctor
    \item Protect the remaining kidney: hydration, blood pressure control, and avoiding medicines that harm the kidney
    \item Access cancer support services
    \item Seek urgent care for new symptoms that may indicate spread
\end{itemize}
