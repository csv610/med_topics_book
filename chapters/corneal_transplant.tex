\chapter{Corneal Transplant}
\index{Corneal Transplant}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A corneal transplant (keratoplasty) is an operation to replace part or all of the cornea -- the clear, dome-shaped front window of the eye -- with healthy donor tissue. The cornea focuses light onto the lens and retina, so when it becomes cloudy, scarred, or misshapen, vision is blurred or lost. A corneal transplant aims to restore clarity, improve vision, and relieve pain. It is one of the most common and successful types of transplant surgery, and in most cases donor tissue is sourced from deceased donors through eye banks. Several techniques exist, ranging from replacing the full thickness of the cornea to replacing only the layers that are diseased.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Corneal transplantation is performed in many thousands of people each year worldwide. The most common reasons include keratoconus, where the cornea becomes progressively thinned and cone-shaped; corneal scarring after infection, injury, or previous surgery; and corneal failure after cataract surgery. In many countries, the number of procedures performed annually runs into the thousands, and waiting times depend on donor availability. The success rate is high: more than 9 in 10 grafts remain clear at one year, and many remain functional for a decade or more, although the outlook depends on the underlying condition and the type of transplant.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The cornea is made up of several layers: the outer epithelium, the stroma, and the inner endothelium, which pumps fluid out of the cornea to keep it clear. Disease in any layer can cloud the cornea and reduce vision.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Keratoconus:} progressive thinning and bulging of the cornea that distorts vision
    \item \textbf{Bullous keratopathy:} endothelial failure, often after cataract surgery, causing fluid accumulation and cloudy, painful corneas
    \item \textbf{Corneal scarring:} from infection (such as herpes simplex), trauma, or chemical burns
    \item \textbf{Corneal dystrophies:} inherited conditions in which deposits accumulate in the cornea
    \item \textbf{Fuchs' endothelial dystrophy:} a common cause of progressive corneal clouding in older adults
    \item \textbf{Previous graft failure:} rejection or recurrence of disease in an earlier transplant
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms reflect the loss of corneal clarity and the change in the shape of the eye.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Blurred, cloudy, or distorted vision that worsens over time
    \item Glare and halos around lights, especially at night
    \item Reduced vision that is not fully correctable with glasses
    \item Sensitivity to light
    \item Eye pain, tearing, or a feeling of something in the eye in some conditions
    \item A visible grey-white haziness of the cornea in advanced cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Not every cause of cloudy vision requires a transplant, and the diagnosis is made by an ophthalmologist.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cataract, in which the lens inside the eye becomes cloudy rather than the cornea
    \item Refractive error that can be corrected with glasses or contact lenses
    \item Glaucoma or retinal disease causing reduced vision
    \item Dry eye or corneal surface disease causing fluctuating vision
    \item Corneal infection that may respond to medical treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See an optometrist or ophthalmologist if you have persistent blurred vision, increasing glare, or any change in your sight. If you already have a corneal condition, keep your scheduled reviews, because the decision to transplant is based on the rate of progression and the impact on daily life.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden loss of vision or severe eye pain
    \item Eye injury with a sharp object or chemical exposure
    \item A red, painful eye with discharge, which may be a serious infection
    \item Sudden flashes, floaters, or a curtain-like shadow over vision, which may indicate retinal detachment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment is performed by an ophthalmologist to confirm the diagnosis, decide whether surgery is needed, and plan the procedure.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} how long vision has been affected, prior eye surgery, eye infections, and general health
    \item \textbf{Visual acuity:} measurement of how well you can see at distance and near
    \item \textbf{Slit-lamp examination:} a microscope examination of the front of the eye to identify which corneal layer is diseased
    \item \textbf{Corneal topography:} mapping of the corneal shape, particularly for keratoconus
    \item \textbf{Pachymetry:} measurement of corneal thickness
    \item \textbf{Endothelial cell count:} assessment of the health of the inner corneal layer
    \item \textbf{General health checks:} blood tests and medical review to ensure fitness for surgery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The choice of transplant technique depends on which layers of the cornea are affected.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Penetrating keratoplasty:} full-thickness replacement of the cornea, used when all layers are diseased
    \item \textbf{Deep anterior lamellar keratoplasty (DALK):} replacement of the outer layers while preserving the healthy endothelium
    \item \textbf{Endothelial keratoplasty (DSEK/DMEK):} replacement of only the inner endothelial layer through a small incision, used in Fuchs' dystrophy and bullous keratopathy
    \item \textbf{Eye bank donor tissue:} carefully screened and matched donor corneas
    \item \textbf{Immunosuppressive eye drops:} steroids and other drops used for many months to prevent rejection
    \item \textbf{Suture removal and adjustments:} as the cornea heals, stitches may be tightened or removed
    \item \textbf{Optical correction:} glasses or contact lenses after healing to optimise vision
    \item \textbf{Treatment of the underlying condition:} control of herpes simplex, glaucoma, or other disease to protect the graft
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Graft rejection, in which the immune system attacks the donor tissue; it is treatable if detected early
    \item Infection of the graft
    \item Raised pressure inside the eye (glaucoma)
    \item Cataract formation or progression
    \item High or irregular astigmatism affecting the quality of vision
    \item Failure of the graft to clear or late graft failure
    \item Bleeding, leakage, or slow healing of the wound
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Corneal transplantation is one of the most successful transplant operations. Vision usually continues to improve for six to twelve months after surgery as the cornea heals and stitches settle, and glasses or contact lenses are often needed for best vision. Grafts for keratoconus and other conditions in which the eye is otherwise healthy have an excellent outlook, with most remaining clear for many years. Grafts performed for infection or chemical injury have a less certain course. Long-term follow-up with an ophthalmologist is essential, because problems such as rejection can usually be treated if they are caught early.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend all follow-up appointments so complications such as rejection are detected early
    \item Use prescribed eye drops exactly as directed, even after the eye feels comfortable
    \item Protect the eye from injury with glasses or goggles during sport and gardening
    \item Report any new redness, pain, reduced vision, or sensitivity to light immediately
    \item Avoid rubbing the eye, and use protective wear at night if advised
    \item Maintain good control of any underlying disease, such as herpes or glaucoma
    \item Consider organ donor registration so that others can benefit from corneal donation
\end{itemize}
