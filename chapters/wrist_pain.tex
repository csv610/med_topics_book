\chapter{Wrist pain}
\index{Wrist pain}
\section*{Definition and Overview}
Wrist pain may arise from bone, joint, tendon, ligament, nerve, skin, or referred pain. Common causes include sprain, fracture, tendinopathy, arthritis, carpal tunnel syndrome, ganglion cyst, and overuse.
\section*{Clinical Features}
Important features include injury mechanism, site, swelling, bruising, deformity, stiffness, numbness, weakness, clicking, fever, and effect on grip or movement. Pain after a fall onto the hand can indicate a fracture even when the wrist looks normal.
\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Consider fracture or dislocation, ligament sprain, tendon injury or tenosynovitis, osteoarthritis, inflammatory arthritis, gout, infection, ganglion, carpal tunnel syndrome, nerve injury, and referred neck or elbow pain.
\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Emergency assessment is needed for deformity, severe swelling, an open injury, pale or blue fingers, loss of sensation or movement, severe pain after trauma, or a hot swollen wrist with fever. Arrange assessment for persistent pain, suspected fracture, recurrent symptoms, or weakness.
\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment includes examination of the wrist, hand, fingers, nerves, blood supply, and nearby joints. X-ray is used when fracture is suspected; ultrasound, MRI, nerve studies, or blood tests are selected for persistent or atypical symptoms.
\section*{Management}
For a recent minor injury, rest from aggravating activity, use a wrapped cold pack, elevate the hand, and consider a short period of support. Use simple analgesia only if safe. Do not immobilise a painful wrist for a prolonged period without assessment; guided movement and rehabilitation are often important. Fractures, infection, tendon injury, and inflammatory disease require specific treatment.
\section*{Complications}
Complications include stiffness, chronic pain, instability, nerve compression, tendon rupture, malunion, complex regional pain syndrome, and loss of grip or function.
\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Many sprains and overuse problems improve over several weeks. Recovery after fracture, surgery, nerve compression, or inflammatory disease varies and may require hand therapy.
\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Increase activity gradually, use ergonomic tools and protective equipment, take breaks from repetitive tasks, strengthen forearm and hand muscles when recovered, and prevent falls.
