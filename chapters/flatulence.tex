\chapter{Flatulence}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Flatulence is the passage of gas from the digestive system through the back passage, commonly called passing wind. It is a completely normal bodily function: everyone produces gas from swallowing air and from the breakdown of food by bacteria in the large bowel, and passing gas 5--15 times a day is normal. Flatulence becomes a problem when it is excessive, foul-smelling, or accompanied by bloating, cramps, or embarrassment. In most cases the causes are dietary and easily modified, but persistent or painful wind can be a sign of digestive conditions such as irritable bowel syndrome, food intolerance, or coeliac disease. This chapter explains why gas forms, what increases it, and how to manage it.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Flatulence affects everyone, but a significant proportion of people report troublesome wind. Around 10--25\% of people experience bloating or excessive flatulence regularly, and it is one of the most common digestive complaints presenting to GPs. Gas-related symptoms are particularly common in irritable bowel syndrome, which affects around 10--15\% of the population, and in lactose intolerance, which affects around 1 in 20 Australians of European descent but much higher proportions of some other populations. Excessive wind causes social embarrassment and distress out of proportion to its medical significance, and many people seek dietary advice or over-the-counter remedies.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Swallowed air:} aerophagia from eating quickly, drinking through straws, chewing gum, fizzy drinks, and talking while eating
    \item \textbf{Bacterial fermentation:} undigested carbohydrates reach the large bowel, where bacteria ferment them, producing hydrogen, methane, and carbon dioxide
    \item \textbf{Dietary causes:} beans, lentils, broccoli, cabbage, onions, wheat, and high-fibre foods are the classic gas producers
    \item \textbf{Food intolerance:} lactose in dairy and fructose in fruit and some sweeteners are poorly absorbed by many people
    \item \textbf{Digestive conditions:} irritable bowel syndrome, coeliac disease, small intestinal bacterial overgrowth, and pancreatic insufficiency increase gas
    \item \textbf{Constipation:} slow transit allows more fermentation and gas accumulation
    \item \textbf{Odour:} sulphur-containing gases from proteins, eggs, and some vegetables cause smell, and certain foods and medicines alter it
    \item \textbf{The mechanism of bloating:} the bowel's sensitivity to stretch, not just the amount of gas, determines how distended a person feels
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Passing wind from several to a dozen or more times a day
    \item Bloating and a feeling of abdominal fullness, sometimes with visible distension
    \item Rumbling noises from the abdomen (borborygmi)
    \item Cramps and discomfort that ease after passing wind
    \item Wind related to specific foods, meals, or fizzy drinks
    \item The absence of serious features: no severe pain, weight loss, or bleeding
    \item Embarrassment and anxiety, which can affect social and work life
    \item In constipation, wind is accompanied by infrequent, hard stools
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Persistent gas and bloating can reflect more than diet.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Irritable bowel syndrome, with pain, bloating, and changed bowel habit
    \item Food intolerances: lactose, fructose, and other fermentable carbohydrates (FODMAPs)
    \item Coeliac disease, with bloating, diarrhoea, fatigue, and weight loss
    \item Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth
    \item Constipation, which retains gas and stool
    \item Pancreatic insufficiency, with fatty stools and malabsorption
    \item Less commonly, bowel obstruction or cancer, which must be considered with weight loss, bleeding, or severe pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Troublesome wind warrants a doctor's review, especially when combined with bloating, changed bowel habit, weight loss, or abdominal pain, or when it began recently in an older person. Most causes are benign and treatable.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal pain, especially with vomiting and an inability to pass wind or stool, which may indicate bowel obstruction
    \item Sudden severe abdominal pain
    \item Blood in the stool, black stools, or unexplained weight loss with abdominal symptoms
    \item Collapse or signs of a serious acute abdomen
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} dietary patterns, food triggers, bowel habit, and associated symptoms such as pain, weight loss, or fatigue
    \item \textbf{Examination:} abdominal examination to check for distension, tenderness, or masses
    \item \textbf{Food and symptom diary:} identifying triggers over a few weeks
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} coeliac serology, full blood count, and thyroid function where indicated
    \item \textbf{Breath tests:} for lactose intolerance and small intestinal bacterial overgrowth
    \item \textbf{Stool tests:} for inflammation or infection where indicated
    \item \textbf{Colonoscopy:} for alarm features such as bleeding, weight loss, or changed bowel habit in older adults
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Eat and drink slowly:} reduce swallowed air by not rushing meals, chewing gum, or using straws
    \item \textbf{Modify trigger foods:} reduce beans, lentils, cabbage, onions, and fizzy drinks in a gradual, individualised way
    \item \textbf{Address intolerances:} trial a dairy-free period for suspected lactose intolerance, or seek dietitian-guided FODMAP elimination
    \item \textbf{Treat constipation:} increase fibre and fluids gradually if constipation contributes
    \item \textbf{Exercise:} regular activity stimulates bowel movement and gas passage
    \item \textbf{Over-the-counter remedies:} simethicone, activated charcoal, and some products reduce symptoms for some people
    \item \textbf{Treat underlying conditions:} coeliac disease responds to a gluten-free diet; irritable bowel syndrome responds to dietary, lifestyle, and sometimes medical treatment
    \item \textbf{Probiotics:} may help some people, though evidence is mixed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Social embarrassment and avoidance of social situations
    \item Anxiety about the symptom, which can itself worsen it
    \item Distension and discomfort that interfere with work and sleep
    \item Missed diagnosis of coeliac disease or irritable bowel syndrome when the cause is not investigated
    \item Rarely, complications of the underlying condition if left untreated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Flatulence is normal, and excessive wind is almost always manageable. Dietary changes, slower eating, and treatment of constipation help most people within weeks, and those with food intolerances or irritable bowel syndrome usually improve substantially with tailored plans and professional guidance. The condition is harmless in the overwhelming majority of cases, and the main treatment goal is often reassurance: knowing that wind is a healthy sign of a functioning digestive system relieves much of the distress. For the minority with an underlying condition, diagnosis and treatment produce lasting improvement.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Eat slowly, chew well, and avoid talking while eating
    \item Limit fizzy drinks, chewing gum, and drinking through straws
    \item Identify personal trigger foods with a diary, and modify them gradually
    \item Increase fibre and fluids slowly to avoid gas
    \item Exercise regularly
    \item Use over-the-counter products when needed
    \item See a doctor if wind is severe, persistent, or accompanied by warning symptoms
    \item Remember that passing wind is normal and healthy; most people pass it many times a day
\end{itemize}
