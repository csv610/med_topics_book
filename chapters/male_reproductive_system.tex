\chapter{Male reproductive system}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The male reproductive system is a complex network of internal and external organs that function synergistically to produce, maintain, and transport sperm (male gametes) and protective fluid (semen), synthesise and secrete androgenic hormones (primarily testosterone), and facilitate copulation and reproduction. The system is anatomically categorised into the external genitalia, which include the penis and scrotum containing the testes, and the internal structures, comprising the epididymides, ductus (vas) deferens, ejaculatory ducts, and urethra, alongside accessory glands such as the seminal vesicles, prostate gland, and bulbourethral (Cowper's) glands. Regulated by the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis, the system is critical for endocrine health and reproductive capacity. Pathological processes within these structures can significantly impact fertility, urological function, and overall systemic health.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Disorders of the male reproductive system are highly prevalent worldwide, showing marked variation across different age groups. Congenital anomalies, such as cryptorchidism (undescended testes), affect approximately 1\% to 3\% of full-term male infants. In young adults, testicular cancer represents the most common solid malignancy, peaking in incidence between 15 and 39 years. In older males, benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is exceedingly common, affecting over 50\% of men aged over 60 years and up to 90\% of men aged over 80 years. In Australia, prostate cancer is the most frequently diagnosed non-cutaneous malignancy in men. Additionally, male-factor infertility is a significant global issue, contributing to approximately 50\% of all cases of couple infertility.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Pathologies of the male reproductive system can arise from congenital, genetic, endocrine, infectious, vascular, or neoplastic causes. Key pathophysiological mechanisms include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hormonal dysregulation:} Impairment of the HPG axis, such as hypogonadotropic hypogonadism or primary testicular failure (e.g. Klinefelter syndrome), leading to deficient testosterone synthesis and impaired spermatogenesis.
    \item \textbf{Infectious and inflammatory processes:} Bacterial or viral infections (such as Escherichia coli or Chlamydia trachomatis) causing epididymitis, orchitis, or prostatitis, which can result in scarring, ductal obstruction, and direct tissue damage.
    \item \textbf{Anatomical and mechanical defects:} Conditions such as varicoceles (abnormal dilatation of the pampiniform venous plexus, raising testicular temperature and inducing venous stasis) or inguinal hernias that compromise scrotal anatomy.
    \item \textbf{Vascular compromise:} Acute conditions such as testicular torsion, where rotation of the spermatic cord cuts off venous drainage and arterial supply, causing rapid testicular ischaemia and necrosis if untreated.
    \item \textbf{Neoplastic transformation:} Genetic mutations and cellular changes leading to benign hyperplasia (BPH) or malignancies (such as testicular germ cell tumours or prostatic adenocarcinoma), which are influenced by age, ethnicity, and genetic predispositions.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of male reproductive disorders depends on the underlying pathology, ranging from acute scrotal symptoms to chronic urinary or endocrine changes. Clinical features include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scrotal pain and swelling:} Acute, severe unilateral pain (typical of testicular torsion or epididymitis) or chronic, dull discomfort (typical of varicocele or hydrocele).
    \item \textbf{Urogenital masses:} A painless, firm testicular nodule (suspicious for testicular cancer) or a soft, transilluminating fluid collection surrounding the testis (hydrocele).
    \item \textbf{Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS):} Urinary hesitancy, weak stream, nocturia, urinary urgency, and a feeling of incomplete bladder emptying, primarily caused by prostatic enlargement.
    \item \textbf{Sexual dysfunction:} Erectile dysfunction, premature or delayed ejaculation, or a reduction in libido, which are often related to vascular disease or androgen deficiency.
    \item \textbf{Infertility:} Inability to achieve pregnancy after 12 months of regular, unprotected intercourse, which may be the sole presenting feature in an otherwise asymptomatic individual.
    \item \textbf{Systemic hypogonadism:} Fatigue, decreased muscle mass, increased visceral adiposity, mood changes, and reduced bone mineral density due to low serum testosterone.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Clinical evaluation must distinguish between benign and malignant conditions, as well as acute surgical emergencies and medical issues. The differential diagnosis includes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Testicular torsion vs. Epididymo-orchitis:} Testicular torsion is a surgical emergency characterized by sudden, severe pain and an absent cremasteric reflex, whereas epididymo-orchitis has a more gradual onset, is associated with fever and dysuria, and presents with a preserved cremasteric reflex.
    \item \textbf{Benign prostatic hyperplasia vs. Prostate cancer:} Both present with LUTS and elevated prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels, but prostate cancer may present with hard, asymmetric prostatic nodules on digital rectal examination, bone pain from osteoblastic metastases, or specific histopathological changes.
    \item \textbf{Varicocele vs. Hydrocele vs. Spermatocele:} A varicocele is a compressible scrotal mass resembling a "bag of worms", a hydrocele is a transilluminating fluid pocket within the tunica vaginalis, and a spermatocele is a painless, sperm-containing cyst arising from the epididymis.
    \item \textbf{Psychogenic vs. Organic erectile dysfunction:} Psychogenic ED often has an abrupt onset, variable presentation, and intact morning erections, while organic ED has a gradual onset and is associated with chronic cardiovascular risk factors.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Individuals should consult a general practitioner if they notice new testicular lumps, changes in urinary habits, persistent pelvic discomfort, or sexual dysfunction.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute scrotal pain:} Sudden, severe testicular pain, which is a potential sign of testicular torsion and requires immediate surgical exploration (ideally within 6 hours) to prevent permanent loss of the testis.
    \item \textbf{Acute urinary retention:} Complete inability to pass urine accompanied by severe, escalating lower abdominal pain and suprapubic distension.
    \item \textbf{Priapism:} A painful, persistent erection lasting longer than 4 hours, which is a medical emergency requiring urgent corporal aspiration to avoid ischaemic fibrosis.
    \item \textbf{Urosepsis:} High fever, chills, rapid heart rate, confusion, or severe lethargy associated with a urinary tract or prostatic infection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A structured diagnostic approach combines physical examination with laboratory, imaging, and functional tests. Investigations include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Digital rectal examination (DRE):} Palpation of the prostate gland to assess size, symmetry, consistency, and the presence of nodules or tenderness.
    \item \textbf{Scrotal ultrasound (with Doppler):} The gold standard imaging modality to differentiate intratesticular from extratesticular lesions, confirm testicular perfusion (to rule out torsion), and assess scrotal fluid.
    \item \textbf{Semen analysis:} The primary test for fertility assessment, evaluating sperm concentration, total motility, morphology, and semen volume.
    \item \textbf{Serum hormone panel:} Measurement of early morning total testosterone, luteinising hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and prolactin to evaluate endocrine status.
    \item \textbf{Prostate-specific antigen (PSA):} A serum biomarker used to screen for and monitor prostate disease, which must be interpreted alongside patient age and risk factors.
    \item \textbf{Serum tumour markers:} Measurement of beta-hCG, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) in cases of suspected testicular malignancy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management strategies range from emergency surgical interventions to long-term pharmacological therapies, lifestyle adjustments, and oncological procedures:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Emergency surgery:} Scrotal exploration with bilateral orchidopexy for testicular torsion, or surgical detumescence for ischaemic priapism.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacotherapy for prostate enlargement:} Alpha-blockers (such as tamsulosin) to relax prostatic and bladder neck smooth muscle, or 5-alpha reductase inhibitors (such as finasteride) to reduce prostate volume.
    \item \textbf{Androgen replacement therapy:} Testosterone replacement (via transdermal gels, intramuscular injections, or subcutaneous implants) in patients with confirmed hypogonadism, provided there are no contraindications.
    \item \textbf{Antimicrobial therapy:} Targeted course of oral or intravenous antibiotics for epididymitis, orchitis, or bacterial prostatitis.
    \item \textbf{Oncological management:} Radical inguinal orchidectomy with or without chemotherapy/radiotherapy for testicular cancer; active surveillance, radical prostatectomy, radiotherapy, or androgen deprivation therapy for prostate cancer.
    \item \textbf{Varicocele repair:} Surgical varicocelectomy or percutaneous embolisation in patients with chronic scrotal pain, testicular atrophy, or abnormal semen parameters.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Untreated or severe disorders of the male reproductive system can lead to significant physical and functional morbidity. Complications include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Permanent infertility:} Caused by irreversible germ cell damage from testicular torsion, late-stage cryptorchidism, chronic infections, or gonadotoxic therapies.
    \item \textbf{Testicular atrophy:} Irreversible loss of testicular parenchyma, resulting in impaired spermatogenesis and reduced hormone production.
    \item \textbf{Renal dysfunction:} Bladder outlet obstruction from severe prostatic hyperplasia leading to bilateral hydronephrosis, urinary tract infections, and post-renal kidney injury.
    \item \textbf{Sexual and urinary morbidity:} Erectile dysfunction, loss of ejaculation, or urinary incontinence, which can occur as side effects of surgical or radiation therapies for prostate cancer.
    \item \textbf{Androgen deficiency sequelae:} Osteoporosis, metabolic syndrome, increased cardiovascular risk, and severe muscle loss in patients with long-term untreated hypogonadism.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for disorders of the male reproductive system is generally positive with timely intervention. Testicular torsion has a salvage rate of over 90\% if detorsion occurs within 6 hours, dropping to less than 10\% after 24 hours. Testicular cancer has an excellent prognosis, with a 5-year survival rate exceeding 95\% due to its high responsiveness to modern oncological treatment. Pharmacological and surgical management of BPH provides effective and durable symptom relief for the majority of patients. Prostate cancer outcomes are highly dependent on stage and grade at diagnosis, with localised disease having a near 100\% 5-year survival rate, whereas metastatic disease requires long-term palliative systemic therapy.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Preventative measures and self-care strategies aim to reduce the incidence of reproductive tract disorders and support early detection. Recommendations include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Testicular self-examination:} Regular, monthly self-palpation of the testes starting in adolescence to detect changes in shape, size, or consistency early.
    \item \textbf{Safe sex practices:} Consistent use of barrier methods (condoms) to prevent sexually transmitted infections that can cause painful epididymo-orchitis and subsequent tubal scarring.
    \item \textbf{Prostate screening discussion:} Consulting a general practitioner about the risks and benefits of PSA testing starting at age 50 (or 45 for individuals with a family history of prostate cancer).
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle optimisation:} Maintaining a healthy weight, exercising regularly, and limiting alcohol intake to support cardiovascular health and reduce the risk of erectile dysfunction.
    \item \textbf{Avoiding scrotal hyperthermia:} Restricting prolonged exposure to saunas, hot tubs, or tight underwear in individuals attempting to conceive, to optimise spermatogenic conditions.
\end{itemize}
