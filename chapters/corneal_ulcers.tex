\chapter{Corneal Ulcers}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A corneal ulcer is an open sore on the cornea, the clear front surface of the eye. It usually develops when the outer layer of the cornea is damaged and becomes infected, most often by bacteria, but viruses, fungi, and amoebae can also be responsible. A corneal ulcer is a serious condition: if it is not treated promptly, it can spread deeper into the cornea, cause scarring, and permanently reduce vision. The hallmark symptoms are a painful, red eye with discharge and reduced vision, and the condition is treated as an emergency by eye specialists.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Corneal ulcers are a leading cause of preventable blindness worldwide, and are particularly common in tropical and developing regions where eye trauma and poor access to care are more frequent. In developed countries such as Australia, the most important risk factor is contact lens wear, especially overnight or poor hygiene use. Other groups at risk include people with dry eyes, those with eyelid problems, people with diabetes, and those taking steroid eye drops. The incidence has risen with the popularity of contact lenses, and microbial keratitis is estimated to affect a small but significant proportion of contact lens wearers each year.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
A corneal ulcer forms when the corneal surface is breached and organisms invade the underlying tissue.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bacteria:} the most common cause; \textit{Pseudomonas aeruginosa} is strongly linked to contact lens use, while \textit{Staphylococcus} and \textit{Streptococcus} follow corneal injury or surface disease
    \item \textbf{Herpes simplex virus:} causes recurrent dendritic ulcers and can scar the cornea
    \item \textbf{Fungi:} more common after injury with plant material or in tropical climates
    \item \textbf{Acanthamoeba:} a rare but serious amoebic infection linked to contact lens use with contaminated water
    \item \textbf{Contact lens problems:} poor hygiene, overnight wear, or lens overwear damage the surface and introduce organisms
    \item \textbf{Corneal surface disease:} dry eye, exposure, or trichiasis (in-growing lashes) predispose to ulceration
    \item \textbf{Immune suppression:} steroid drops, diabetes, and immunodeficiency impair healing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms usually develop quickly and worsen over hours to days.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe eye pain and a gritty or foreign-body sensation
    \item Redness of the eye
    \item Watery or purulent discharge
    \item Blurred or reduced vision
    \item Sensitivity to light (photophobia)
    \item A visible white or grey spot on the cornea
    \item Eyelid swelling
    \item Excessive tearing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of a painful red eye must be distinguished from a corneal ulcer.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Viral conjunctivitis or keratitis without ulceration
    \item Iritis or uveitis, inflammation inside the eye
    \item Acute glaucoma, with severe pain and raised pressure
    \item Corneal abrasion, a scratch without infection
    \item Foreign body in the eye
    \item Scleritis or episcleritis
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
A red, painful eye with reduced vision should be assessed urgently, ideally the same day, by a doctor or optometrist and referred to an ophthalmologist if a corneal ulcer is suspected.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe eye pain or sudden loss of vision
    \item Eye injury with a sharp object, chemical, or high-speed foreign body
    \item A contact lens wearer with severe pain and discharge, which may indicate a serious infection
    \item Any penetrating eye injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is clinical, supported by laboratory testing of material scraped from the ulcer.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} contact lens use, eye injury, exposure to contaminated water, and previous eye disease
    \item \textbf{Slit-lamp examination:} the ulcer is inspected with a microscope and fluorescein dye, which stains the damaged surface
    \item \textbf{Corneal scrapings:} samples taken with a small blade and sent for microscopy and culture
    \item \textbf{Microbiology:} Gram stain and culture to identify bacteria, fungi, or amoebae
    \item \textbf{PCR testing:} for herpes simplex and other viruses
    \item \textbf{Review:} of the contact lens case and solutions, which may also be cultured
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Corneal ulcers are treated urgently, often with admission to hospital for intensive treatment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Empirical antibiotic drops:} fortified broad-spectrum drops applied very frequently, often hourly at first
    \item \textbf{Culture-directed therapy:} treatment adjusted once the organism is identified
    \item \textbf{Antiviral or antifungal drops:} for viral and fungal ulcers
    \item \textbf{Cycloplegic drops:} to relax the eye and reduce pain
    \item \textbf{Stopping contact lens wear:} until the ulcer has healed
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} oral analgesia as needed
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} occasionally required to repair a perforated cornea or remove severe infection
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} frequent review until healing, then long-term monitoring for scarring
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Corneal scarring, causing permanent blurred vision
    \item Corneal perforation, where the ulcer erodes through the full thickness of the cornea
    \item Infection spreading inside the eye (endophthalmitis)
    \item Glaucoma from scarring and inflammation
    \item Irregular astigmatism from uneven healing
    \item The need for corneal transplant in severe cases
    \item Loss of vision or the eye in advanced cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt treatment, most small corneal ulcers heal within days to a few weeks, leaving a faint scar that may not affect vision. Larger, central, or delayed-treated ulcers carry a much higher risk of permanent scarring and visual loss, and a significant proportion of severe cases need corneal transplant later. Outcomes depend on the organism, the depth of the ulcer, and the speed of treatment, which is why a painful red eye should never be left to settle on its own.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Practise good contact lens hygiene: wash hands, use fresh solution, and never sleep in lenses unless prescribed for that purpose
    \item Replace contact lens cases regularly and keep them clean and dry
    \item Never rinse lenses or cases in tap water
    \item Remove lenses and seek advice promptly if the eye becomes red, painful, or sensitive to light
    \item Wear protective eyewear for work, sport, and gardening
    \item Keep dry eye and eyelid conditions treated
    \item See an optometrist or ophthalmologist regularly
\end{itemize}
