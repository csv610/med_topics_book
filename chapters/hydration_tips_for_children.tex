\chapter{Hydration Tips for Children}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hydration is the state of having enough water in the body to function normally. Children are more vulnerable to dehydration than adults because they have higher water turnover, smaller fluid reserves, and may not recognise or communicate thirst. Keeping children well hydrated supports concentration, energy, digestion, and health, and is especially important in hot weather, during illness, and with physical activity. Good hydration habits established in childhood, with water as the main drink, also protect against sugary drink consumption and its health effects.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Many children drink less than recommended and consume significant amounts of sugary drinks, which add energy without hydration benefits. Soft drink, juice, and sports drinks contribute to childhood obesity, tooth decay, and poor dietary patterns. Dehydration is common during gastroenteritis and fever, and it is a leading cause of childhood hospitalisation in Australia. Heat-related dehydration and illness are increasing with hotter summers. Encouraging water as the default drink is a key public health message.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Water needs:} children need around 4--8 cups of fluid daily depending on age, activity, and weather
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology of dehydration:} loss of water and electrolytes through sweat, fever, vomiting, and diarrhoea reduces blood volume and impairs organ function
    \item \textbf{Thirst lag:} children may not feel thirsty until already dehydrated
    \item \textbf{Signs of dehydration:} thirst, dry mouth, dark urine, fewer wet nappies, lethargy, and sunken eyes
    \item \textbf{Illness:} fever increases fluid needs; vomiting and diarrhoea increase losses
    \item \textbf{Sugary drinks:} provide fluid but with excess sugar, and can worsen diarrhoea
    \item \textbf{Breastfed babies:} continue breastfeeding; formula-fed babies need appropriately made formula
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Passing urine regularly with pale urine is a good sign of hydration
    \item Mild dehydration: thirst, dry lips, and slightly reduced urine
    \item Moderate dehydration: fewer wet nappies, no tears when crying, and dry mouth
    \item Severe dehydration: lethargy, sunken eyes, cold hands and feet, and rapid breathing
    \item In babies: a sunken fontanelle (soft spot) and reduced activity
    \item Children in hot weather or with activity may need more fluids
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dehydration from gastroenteritis, fever, heat, or inadequate intake
    \item Thirst can be a symptom of diabetes and other conditions
    \item Frequent urination may indicate a urinary infection or diabetes
    \item Excessive water intake can be harmful in rare cases
    \item Medical assessment distinguishes these
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if a child is not drinking enough, is producing little urine, or is unwell with vomiting or diarrhoea, or if you are concerned about dehydration.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A baby or child who is lethargic, floppy, or difficult to wake
    \item No wet nappies for 6 hours in a baby or 8 hours in a child
    \item Sunken eyes, sunken fontanelle, or very dry mouth
    \item Rapid breathing, cold hands and feet, or mottled skin
    \item Vomiting everything with an inability to keep fluids down
    \item Blood in vomit or stool with dehydration
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} hydration status is judged by examination
    \item \textbf{Urine output:} wet nappy and toilet frequency
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} electrolytes and kidney function in moderate to severe cases
    \item \textbf{Weight:} acute weight loss reflects fluid deficit
    \item \textbf{Review of intake:} fluids offered and consumed
    \item \textbf{Underlying illness:} gastroenteritis, diabetes, and infections are assessed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Water as the main drink:} offer water regularly throughout the day
    \item \textbf{Age-appropriate amounts:} babies need breast milk or formula; toddlers and children need water and milk
    \item \textbf{During illness:} offer small, frequent sips of fluid and oral rehydration solution for gastroenteritis
    \item \textbf{In hot weather:} increase fluids and dress children lightly
    \item \textbf{With activity:} offer water before, during, and after exercise
    \item \textbf{Limit sugary drinks:} water, milk, and occasional juice are the best choices
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} intravenous fluids for severe dehydration
    \item \textbf{Teach thirst cues:} children learn to drink before they feel thirsty
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dehydration causing hospitalisation, especially in gastroenteritis
    \item Electrolyte imbalance, including hyponatraemia
    \item Kidney injury in severe cases
    \item Poor concentration, headaches, and reduced school performance with mild dehydration
    \item Obesity and tooth decay from sugary drinks
    \item Heat illness in hot weather
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Mild dehydration resolves quickly with fluids, and severe dehydration is treated effectively in hospital. Most children recover fully, and good hydration habits prevent most problems. Children who learn to drink water as the default have better weight, dental, and overall health. With simple, consistent practices, dehydration is almost entirely preventable.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep a drink bottle filled with water available
    \item Offer water with meals and snacks
    \item Make water the default: keep it visible and accessible
    \item Limit juice and soft drink, and dilute juice if offered
    \item Increase fluids in heat and with activity
    \item During illness, offer small frequent sips and oral rehydration solution
    \item Check wet nappies and urine colour
    \item Model good hydration habits as a parent
\end{itemize}
