\chapter{Naloxone}
\index{Naloxone}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Naloxone is a synthetic morphinan derivative that acts as a potent, competitive opioid receptor antagonist. It has a high affinity for $\mu$-opioid receptors, rapidly displacing opioid agonists and reversing their clinical and physiological effects. Clinically, naloxone is primarily used for the emergency reversal of opioid overdose, characterised by respiratory depression, sedation, and miosis. It is a critical, life-saving agent included in the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines and is increasingly distributed through public health take-home programmes to prevent overdose fatalities.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Globally, opioid overdose is a major cause of preventable death. Risk affects all ages and is shaped by the local drug supply, prescribed medicines, loss of tolerance, and use with alcohol or sedatives. Take-home naloxone programmes allow people likely to witness an overdose—including friends, family, peers, and community workers—to provide life-saving treatment while emergency care is being arranged.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Naloxone reverses the effects of opioids by competing for active receptor sites in the central nervous system.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Opioid receptor antagonism:} Naloxone acts as a competitive antagonist at $\mu$, $\kappa$, and $\delta$-opioid receptors, with the highest affinity at the $\mu$-receptor.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacokinetics:} Following intravenous administration, naloxone acts within 2 minutes; subcutaneous and intramuscular routes take 2 to 5 minutes, and intranasal administration takes 8 to 13 minutes. It has a short half-life (30 to 90 minutes), which is often shorter than the half-life of the opioid being reversed, necessitating close monitoring.
    \item \textbf{Hepatic metabolism:} It is metabolised primarily in the liver via glucuronidation to naloxone-3-glucuronide, with renal excretion of metabolites.
    \item \textbf{Precipitated withdrawal:} In opioid-dependent individuals, rapid displacement of opioids from receptors can trigger acute, intense withdrawal symptoms.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of opioid overdose and the subsequent response to naloxone administration show characteristic features.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Opioid toxidrome:} Characterised by severe respiratory depression (respiratory rate < 8 breaths per minute), pinpoint pupils (miosis), and depressed conscious state or coma.
    \item \textbf{Reversal signs:} Restoration of adequate spontaneous respiration, dilation of pinpoint pupils, and return of consciousness or responsiveness.
    \item \textbf{Precipitated withdrawal features:} Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal cramps, piloerection (goosebumps), yawning, rhinorrhoea, lacrimation, severe anxiety, tachycardia, and hypertension.
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular changes:} Tachycardia, hypertension, or arrhythmia may occur, particularly if withdrawal is rapidly precipitated.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When managing suspected opioid overdose or evaluating the response to naloxone, other conditions must be considered.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Non-opioid sedative overdose:} Overdose of benzodiazepines, alcohol, GHB, or barbiturates will not respond to naloxone, though mixed ingestions are common.
    \item \textbf{Hypoglycaemia:} Can present with coma and altered mental status but is ruled out with blood glucose testing.
    \item \textbf{Acute stroke or intracranial haemorrhage:} Presents with altered consciousness and pupillary changes, but does not improve with naloxone.
    \item \textbf{Severe hypothermia:} Causes bradycardia, hypoventilation, and pupillary dilation or constriction, mimicking overdose.
    \item \textbf{Post-ictal state:} Stupor or coma following a seizure, which may have been secondary to opioid-induced hypoxia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
If an opioid overdose is suspected, emergency medical assistance must be sought immediately.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unresponsiveness or inability to wake a person up.
    \item Extremely slow, shallow, or stopped breathing.
    \item Snorting, gurgling, or choking sounds from the airway.
    \item Pale, cold, clammy skin, or blue-grey discolouration of the lips and fingernails.
    \item Pinpoint pupils in an unconscious individual.
\end{itemize}
Even if naloxone has been successfully administered and the patient has revived, emergency services must still be called because the duration of action of naloxone may be shorter than that of the opioid, leading to a recurrence of life-threatening respiratory depression.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis of opioid overdose is primarily clinical, based on the classic triad of symptoms, but investigations can support management.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical evaluation:} Assessment of airway patency, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, conscious state (Glasgow Coma Scale), and pupillary response.
    \item \textbf{Point-of-care blood glucose:} To immediately rule out hypoglycaemia as a cause of coma.
    \item \textbf{Arterial or venous blood gas:} To evaluate respiratory acidosis, hypercapnia, and oxygenation status.
    \item \textbf{Toxicology screening:} Urine or blood screening for opioids, benzodiazepines, paracetamol, and other common co-ingested substances, although treatment should never be delayed for these results.
    \item \textbf{Electrocardiogram (ECG):} To monitor for QTc prolongation or arrhythmias associated with certain opioids (e.g. methadone) or severe hypoxia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The immediate goal in treating opioid overdose is restoring adequate ventilation, with naloxone serving as the primary pharmacological intervention.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Airway and ventilation support:} Positioning the patient (recovery position), clearing the airway, and providing rescue breathing or bag-valve-mask ventilation with supplemental oxygen.
    \item \textbf{Naloxone administration:} Administered via intramuscular injection, intranasal spray, or intravenous injection. Dosing is titrated to restore adequate respiration rather than full consciousness.
    \item \textbf{Repeat dosing:} Repeat doses only according to the product instructions or emergency-dispatcher guidance if breathing does not improve or worsens. Potent or long-acting opioids may require more than one dose.
    \item \textbf{Continuous infusion:} A continuous intravenous infusion of naloxone may be required for overdoses involving long-acting opioids (e.g. methadone, sustained-release formulations).
    \item \textbf{Observation and monitoring:} Emergency medical assessment and observation are required after naloxone because its effect may wear off before the opioid. Follow local emergency guidance for the duration of monitoring.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Recurrent respiratory depression and coma if naloxone metabolises before the opioid.
    \item Acute withdrawal syndrome, causing distress, agitation, and potentially aggressive behaviour.
    \item Aspiration pneumonia due to depressed airway protective reflexes during overdose.
    \item Non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema, a rare but serious reaction to rapid opioid reversal.
    \item Cardiovascular instability, including severe hypertension, arrhythmias, or cardiac arrest, typically triggered by massive catecholamine release during precipitated withdrawal.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Outcome is best when breathing is restored promptly with basic life support and naloxone. Naloxone reverses opioid effects but cannot reverse injury caused by prolonged lack of oxygen, and recurrent respiratory depression can occur. Follow-up should include non-judgemental assessment of overdose risk and access to evidence-based treatment for opioid dependence where available.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ask a pharmacist, clinician, or local harm-reduction service whether take-home naloxone is available, and learn how to use the formulation supplied in your country.
    \item Learn to recognise the signs of an opioid overdose, such as slow breathing and pinpoint pupils.
    \item Avoid using opioids alone; ensure someone is present who can administer naloxone and call emergency services.
    \item Do not mix opioids with other sedatives, alcohol, or benzodiazepines, as this greatly increases overdose risk.
    \item Participate in opioid substitution therapy (e.g. methadone or buprenorphine) and harm reduction education.
\end{itemize}
