\section*{Definition and Overview}
A colostomy is a surgically created opening (stoma) that brings part of the large bowel (colon) through the abdominal wall so that faeces can leave the body without passing through the anus. The stoma is usually a small, red, moist circle on the surface of the abdomen, and a disposable bag worn over it collects the stool. A colostomy may be temporary or permanent, depending on the reason it was created and the health of the remaining bowel. Temporary colostomies are often closed after the underlying problem has healed, whereas permanent colostomies are needed when the lower bowel or anus must be removed or bypassed permanently.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bowel cancer is one of the most common cancers worldwide and is the most frequent reason a colostomy is created. Around 1--2\% of the general population will have a stoma at some point, and the number rises with age. Conditions such as diverticulitis, inflammatory bowel disease, trauma, and obstetric injuries account for a substantial share of the remaining cases. Colostomies are created in both men and women, most often in people over the age of 50, when colorectal disease is most common. Advances in surgery, including joined-up reconstruction (anastomosis) and bowel-conserving techniques, mean that an increasing proportion of stomas are temporary.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
A colostomy is a treatment, not a disease, and is usually created for one of several reasons:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Removal of the rectum and lower bowel for cancer, which leaves no way for stool to pass normally
    \item Protection of a new bowel join (anastomosis) while it heals, so that faeces do not leak through it
    \item Relief of obstruction or infection, such as a severe attack of diverticulitis or an obstructing tumour
    \item Management of trauma, penetrating injury, or severe pelvic injury where the bowel is damaged
    \item Treatment of birth defects affecting the anus or lower bowel in infants
    \item Bypass of a section of bowel too damaged or inflamed to function, as in some cases of inflammatory bowel disease
\end{itemize}

After the procedure, the end of the colon is brought to the skin surface and sutured in place, where it opens to allow faeces to pass. The stool that emerges depends on how much bowel remains above the stoma: a colostomy from the left side of the colon (the most common site) produces fairly formed stool, while more proximal colostomies produce looser output.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The stoma itself and its output are the main features to understand:

\begin{itemize}
    \item A moist, pink--red stoma on the abdominal wall, which may bleed slightly and bruise in the first days
    \item Passage of faeces and flatus through the stoma into the collecting bag rather than through the anus
    \item Output that is semi-formed to formed for left-sided colostomies and looser for right-sided ones
    \item Absence of bowel motions from the anus, unless the lower bowel was left in place
    \item Early swelling of the stoma that usually subsides over several weeks
    \item A need to wear a collection bag that is emptied and changed regularly
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
For a person living with a colostomy, symptoms that need careful sorting out include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Stoma blockage vs.\ constipation and thick output that will not pass
    \item Parastomal hernia (bulge around the stoma) vs.\ normal swelling
    \item Stoma prolapse (the stoma lengthens outward) vs.\ normal appearance
    \item Stomal infection or skin breakdown vs.\ simple skin irritation from the bag
    \item Stoma retraction (pulls inward) vs.\ normal healing changes
    \item Bowel obstruction vs.\ severe constipation
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the stoma nurse, GP, or hospital promptly if you notice new or worrying changes around the stoma, including:

\begin{itemize}
    \item No output, flatus, or bowel sounds for more than 6--8 hours, with bloating, cramping, or vomiting
    \item A stoma that becomes dark, blue, or black in colour, which may indicate a loss of blood supply
    \item The stoma pulling inward below skin level or separating from the skin around it
    \item A painful, tense bulge around the stoma (possible parastomal hernia)
    \item Persistent bleeding from the stoma that does not settle
    \item Increasing redness, pain, discharge, or spreading skin breakdown around the stoma
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heavy or persistent bleeding from the stoma
    \item Severe abdominal pain with vomiting and no stoma output (possible obstruction)
    \item A stoma that turns black and is extremely painful (possible loss of blood supply)
    \item Sudden collapse, severe dehydration, or signs of sepsis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment of a person with a colostomy focuses on the stoma, the bowel, and any underlying condition:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} when the stoma was created, output pattern, recent changes, medications, and the reason for the colostomy
    \item \textbf{Stomal examination:} inspection of colour, size, surrounding skin, and fit of the appliance
    \item \textbf{Palpation:} feeling around the stoma for hernias, retraction, or tenderness
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT scan or water-soluble enema to check for obstruction, stricture, or anastomotic leak
    \item \textbf{Endoscopy:} sigmoidoscopy or colonoscopy to inspect the bowel above the stoma where needed
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, electrolytes, and kidney function in cases of poor output or dehydration
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Care of a colostomy is a partnership between the person, the stoma nurse, and the surgical team:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stoma care:} learning to measure, fit, and change the bag and skin barrier correctly, usually with the support of a specialist stoma nurse
    \item \textbf{Skin care:} cleaning the surrounding skin with water, keeping it dry, and treating irritation early
    \item \textbf{Diet and fluids:} chewing food well, drinking enough fluid, and adjusting intake to manage output consistency
    \item \textbf{Bowel regulation:} dietary fibre, and occasionally medicines such as loperamide to firm up loose output, under medical guidance
    \item \textbf{Deodorant products:} pouch deodorants or diet adjustments to reduce odour and gas
    \item \textbf{Appliances and supplies:} a regular supply of bags and accessories arranged through the stoma service
    \item \textbf{Reversal surgery:} planned closure of a temporary colostomy once the bowel has healed and the person is fit
    \item \textbf{Psychological support:} counselling, peer support, and stoma support groups to adjust to life with a stoma
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
A range of complications can affect the stoma, the surrounding skin, or the bowel:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Skin irritation and excoriation around the stoma from leaking or poor appliance fit
    \item Parastomal hernia, where the abdominal wall bulges around the stoma
    \item Stoma prolapse, where the bowel telescopes outward beyond its normal length
    \item Stomal stenosis, a narrowing of the opening that can obstruct the passage of stool
    \item Stomal retraction, where the stoma sinks below the skin surface
    \item Bowel obstruction, from adhesions, hernia, or thickened stool
    \item Ischaemia or necrosis of the stoma if its blood supply fails
    \item Psychological distress, body image concerns, and reduced quality of life
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people with a colostomy adapt well and return to a full range of activities, including work, exercise, and social life, usually within several weeks to a few months of surgery. Where the colostomy was created for cancer, the long-term outlook depends mainly on the stage of the cancer rather than the stoma itself. Temporary colostomies are successfully closed in the majority of cases, and most people regain normal bowel function afterwards, though the frequency of motions may be increased at first. With good stoma care and support, complications can usually be recognised early and treated.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Learn and practise stoma care with your stoma nurse before leaving hospital
    \item Change the bag when about a third full and keep the surrounding skin clean and dry
    \item Check the stoma daily for colour, size, and skin condition
    \item Drink plenty of fluids and chew food well to avoid blockage
    \item Avoid heavy lifting for several months to reduce the risk of a parastomal hernia, and seek advice on safe exercise
    \item Carry a spare bag and emergency supplies when out
    \item Maintain a balanced diet and eat on a regular schedule to make output more predictable
    \item Attend scheduled follow-up appointments and contact the stoma service early with any concerns
\end{itemize}
