\chapter{Ulcerative colitis}
\index{Ulcerative colitis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic, relapsing-remitting inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) characterized by diffuse, continuous mucosal inflammation and ulceration starting in the rectum and extending proximally into the colon. Unlike Crohn's disease, which can affect any part of the gastrointestinal tract and is characterized by transmural inflammation and skip lesions, ulcerative colitis is strictly confined to the large intestine and affects only the innermost mucosal layer of the bowel wall. The disease invariably starts in the rectum (proctitis) and spreads continuously to involve the sigmoid colon (proctosigmoiditis), left side of the colon up to the splenic flexure (left-sided colitis), or the entire colon (extensive colitis or pancolitis).

Clinically, ulcerative colitis manifests as chronic, bloody diarrhea, rectal bleeding, bowel urgency, tenesmus, and lower abdominal cramping. It represents a major global healthcare burden, as it is a lifelong, incurable condition that primarily affects young individuals. The clinical course is unpredictable, defined by unpredictable flares of inflammation followed by periods of remission. Beyond the severe gastrointestinal symptoms, the chronic systemic inflammatory state in ulcerative colitis can lead to multi-system complications, significant nutritional deficiencies, impaired quality of life, and a long-term risk of colorectal malignancy. Successful management requires a multidisciplinary approach combining pharmacotherapy, nutritional support, lifestyle adjustments, and, in refractory or complicated cases, surgical intervention.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of ulcerative colitis has shifted significantly over the past century. Historically, UC was considered a disease of highly industrialized, Western countries. However, with the rapid urbanization and Westernization of lifestyles in developing countries, its incidence is rising globally, particularly in Asia, South America, and the Middle East.

The highest prevalence rates remain in North America and Northern and Western Europe, where prevalence estimates reach as high as $250$ to $300$ cases per $100{,}000$ individuals, and annual incidence rates range between $9$ and $20$ cases per $100{,}000$ person-years. In contrast, newly industrialized regions report lower but rapidly increasing rates, highlighting the role of environmental and lifestyle factors in the disease's pathogenesis.

The demographic distribution of ulcerative colitis demonstrates a bimodal age of onset. The primary and largest peak occurs in late adolescence and young adulthood, typically between the ages of $15$ and $30$ years. A second, smaller peak in incidence is observed in older adults, specifically between the ages of $50$ and $70$ years. The disease affects males and females relatively equally during early adulthood, though some studies report a slight male predominance in cases diagnosed later in life. Pediatric-onset ulcerative colitis accounts for approximately $10\%$ to $15\%$ of all diagnosed cases. Furthermore, significant ethnic disparities exist, with individuals of Ashkenazi Jewish descent demonstrating a three- to fourfold higher risk of developing the disease compared to other populations, suggesting a strong underlying genetic susceptibility.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The precise cause of ulcerative colitis is unknown, but the current medical consensus is that it is a multifactorial disease. It arises from an inappropriate, dysregulated mucosal immune response to the commensal gut microbiota in genetically predisposed individuals, triggered by environmental factors.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Genetic Factors}: Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified more than $200$ susceptibility loci associated with inflammatory bowel disease, some shared with Crohn's disease and others specific to ulcerative colitis. Key genetic markers include polymorphisms in the human leukocyte antigen (HLA) complex, particularly class II alleles like HLA-DRB1*1502 and HLA-DRB1*0103. Genes regulating epithelial barrier function, cytokine signaling (such as IL-23R, IL-10, and STAT3), and autophagy are also linked. Having a first-degree relative with the disease increases a person's risk tenfold.
  \item \textbf{Epithelial Barrier Dysfunction}: The colonic mucosal barrier consists of a thick mucus layer and a single layer of epithelial cells joined by tight junctions. In patients with ulcerative colitis, this barrier is structurally compromised. There is a reduction in the production of the protective mucin MUC2, leading to a thin, discontinuous mucus layer. Additionally, alterations in tight junction proteins, such as claudins and occludin, lead to increased paracellular permeability. This "leaky gut" allows luminal bacteria and antigens to cross the barrier, entering the lamina propria and triggering a continuous immune response.
  \item \textbf{Dysregulated Immune System}: Once luminal antigens penetrate the epithelial barrier, they are presented by dendritic cells to CD4+ T helper cells. In ulcerative colitis, this triggers an atypical T-helper 2 (Th2) cell immune response, characterized by the secretion of interleukin-13 (IL-13) and interleukin-5 (IL-5). IL-13 acts as a direct cytotoxic agent to epithelial cells, worsening barrier dysfunction. Other pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-alpha), interleukin-1beta (IL-1beta), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), are produced in high quantities, recruiting neutrophils to the site. The neutrophils migrate through the epithelium into the crypts, leading to cryptitis and the formation of crypt abscesses, which are hallmarks of UC.
  \item \textbf{Gut Dysbiosis}: The gastrointestinal tract of patients with active ulcerative colitis displays a loss of microbial diversity and a shift in the microbial balance, known as dysbiosis. There is a reduction in beneficial, short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria, particularly those belonging to the phyla \textit{Firmicutes} and \textit{Bacteroidetes}. Conversely, there is an increase in potentially inflammatory bacteria, such as \textit{Enterobacteriaceae}. SCFAs, especially butyrate, are the main energy source for colonocytes and are critical for maintaining mucosal integrity. A depletion of SCFAs impairs cell repair and promotes mucosal inflammation.
  \item \textbf{Environmental Triggers}: Numerous lifestyle and environmental factors influence the risk of developing ulcerative colitis. Interestingly, active smoking is negatively associated with the disease; it is protective, and many patients experience their first flare or develop the disease shortly after quitting smoking. Other triggers include the frequent use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), which disrupt mucosal prostaglandin synthesis, psychological stress, and diets high in animal protein, refined sugars, and polyunsaturated fats.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features of ulcerative colitis depend on the extent of colonic involvement and the severity of the mucosal inflammation. The disease typically follows a chronic relapsing-remitting pattern, with acute flare-ups followed by periods of relative or absolute clinical remission.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Gastrointestinal Symptoms}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Bloody Diarrhea}: This is the cardinal symptom of ulcerative colitis. The stool is typically loose or watery and mixed with macroscopically visible blood, mucus, and pus. The frequency of bowel movements ranges from fewer than four per day in mild disease to more than ten per day in severe, life-threatening flares.
    \item \textit{Rectal Bleeding}: Active inflammation of the rectal mucosa (proctitis) leads to frequent rectal bleeding, which can occur independently of stool passage.
    \item \textit{Urgency and Tenesmus}: Rectal inflammation impairs the rectum's compliance and capacity to hold stool, leading to a sudden, painful, and frequent urge to defecate, often accompanied by tenesmus (a sensation of incomplete bowel evacuation).
    \item \textit{Abdominal Pain}: Cramping abdominal discomfort is common, typically localized to the lower left quadrant or the suprapubic region, and is often relieved temporarily by defecation.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Systemic Symptoms}: Systemic features reflect the severity of systemic inflammation and are present during moderate-to-severe disease flares. These include:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Fatigue}: A profound, persistent exhaustion caused by chronic inflammatory cytokine activity, sleep disturbances from nocturnal bowel movements, and anemia.
    \textit{Anemia}: Microcytic, iron-deficiency anemia resulting from chronic, ongoing mucosal blood loss, combined with the anemia of chronic disease.
    \item \textit{Weight Loss and Malnutrition}: Caused by reduced oral intake (due to fear of eating triggering pain and diarrhea), malabsorption, and protein-losing enteropathy across the inflamed, ulcerated mucosa.
    \item \textit{Fever}: Low-grade fever is common in moderate flares, while high, spiking fevers indicate severe, fulminant colitis or localized complications.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Extraintestinal Manifestations}: Approximately $30\%$ of patients with ulcerative colitis develop inflammatory conditions outside the gastrointestinal tract, which can occur during active bowel flares or during remission:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Musculoskeletal}: Peripheral arthritis (typically asymmetric and non-deforming) and axial arthropathies (sacroiliitis and ankylosing spondylitis).
    \item \textit{Dermatological}: Erythema nodosum (tender, red subcutaneous nodules typically located on the shins) and pyoderma gangrenosum (painful, rapidly spreading skin ulcers).
    \item \textit{Ocular}: Episcleritis (painless redness of the eye) and anterior uveitis or iritis (painful red eye, photophobia, and blurred vision, requiring urgent ophthalmologic care).
    \item \textit{Hepatobiliary}: Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), a chronic cholestatic liver disease causing inflammation and scarring of the bile ducts. Around $2\%$ to $8\%$ of UC patients develop PSC, and up to $80\%$ of patients with PSC have underlying UC.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the clinical features of ulcerative colitis overlap with many other gastrointestinal diseases, a careful differential diagnosis is essential.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Crohn's Disease}: The main differential diagnosis. Crohn's disease features transmural inflammation, skip lesions, granulomas, perianal disease, and can affect any part of the digestive tract. Ulcerative colitis, by contrast, is confined to the colon, presents with continuous mucosal-only inflammation, and always involves the rectum.
  \item \textbf{Infectious Colitis}: Acute infections can present identically to a UC flare. Pathogenic bacteria include \textit{Salmonella}, \textit{Shigella}, \textit{Campylobacter}, and enterohemorrhagic \textit{Escherichia coli} (O157:H7). \textit{Clostridioides difficile} infection must always be ruled out, especially in patients with recent antibiotic exposure or those already diagnosed with IBD who experience a sudden, severe flare. Viral causes like cytomegalovirus (CMV) must be considered in immunocompromised patients.
  \item \textbf{Ischemic Colitis}: Caused by reduced blood flow to the colon, typically affecting older adults with cardiovascular disease. It presents with sudden, sharp left-sided abdominal pain followed by bloody diarrhea, characteristically sparing the rectum.
  \item \textbf{Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)}: A functional disorder presenting with abdominal pain and altered bowel habits. It is distinguished from UC by the absolute absence of blood in the stool, normal inflammatory markers, and a normal endoscopic appearance of the mucosa.
  \item \textbf{Microscopic Colitis}: Includes collagenous and lymphocytic colitis. It presents with chronic, watery, non-bloody diarrhea. The colon looks normal during endoscopy, and the diagnosis is made by identifying microscopic mucosal inflammation on biopsy.
  \item \textbf{Diverticular Colitis}: Segmental colitis associated with diverticula (SCAD) causes left-sided pain and bleeding, but the inflammation is restricted to the interdiverticular mucosa, sparing the rectum and proximal colon.
  \item \textbf{Medication-Induced Colitis}: Caused by drugs like NSAIDs, mycophenolate mofetil, and immune checkpoint inhibitors used in cancer immunotherapy.
  \item \textbf{Radiation Colitis}: Seen in patients with a history of pelvic radiation therapy. It presents with rectal bleeding and urgency, showing mucosal pallor and telangiectasias on endoscopy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients diagnosed with ulcerative colitis require ongoing medical supervision. However, certain acute signs and symptoms indicate a severe flare-up or a life-threatening complication that requires immediate clinical evaluation.

Patients should contact their gastroenterologist or primary care physician promptly if they experience any of the following:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A sudden increase in stool frequency (more than six bloody stools per day).
  \item Unexplained, rapid weight loss or persistent, severe loss of appetite.
  \item A persistent low-grade fever lasting more than $48$ hours.
  \item Worsening, severe abdominal pain or cramping that is not relieved by bowel movements.
  \item Signs of dehydration, such as extreme thirst, dry mouth, little to no urine output, or feeling dizzy upon standing.
\end{itemize}

Emergency medical services must be contacted immediately (e.g., by calling local emergency services in many countries, 911 in the United States, or 999 in the United Kingdom) if the patient exhibits any of the following life-threatening symptoms:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A high, spiking fever (above $38.5$ degrees Celsius or $101.3$ degrees Fahrenheit) combined with severe abdominal swelling, rigidity, and extreme tenderness. This suggests toxic megacolon or bowel perforation.
  \item Sudden, excruciating, and widespread abdominal pain, indicating peritonitis.
  \item Massive, continuous rectal bleeding, causing symptoms of shock (rapid heart rate, pale skin, cold sweat, confusion, or loss of consciousness).
  \item Inability to keep fluids down due to persistent vomiting, leading to severe dehydration and electrolyte imbalances.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of ulcerative colitis requires a combination of clinical assessment, laboratory testing, imaging studies, and endoscopic evaluation with biopsy.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Physical Examination}: The clinical examination evaluates the patient's general health, signs of malnutrition, and systemic toxicity. In mild cases, the exam may be normal. In severe flares, findings include abdominal tenderness (most severe in the left lower quadrant), abdominal distension, tachycardia, hypotension, fever, and pallor. A digital rectal exam (DRE) may confirm rectal tenderness and visible blood.
  \item \textbf{Laboratory Investigations}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Full Blood Count (FBC)}: Done to detect anemia and leukocytosis, which points to active inflammation or infection. Platelets may be elevated as an acute-phase reactant.
    \item \textit{Inflammatory Markers}: Serum C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) are checked. Elevated levels correlate with disease activity, though normal levels do not rule out localized proctitis.
    \item \textit{Biochemistry}: Serum albumin is checked; low levels indicate severe disease, malnutrition, and protein loss in the gut. Serum electrolytes and renal function are monitored in patients with severe diarrhea.
    \item \textit{Stool Tests}: Fecal calprotectin is a highly sensitive biomarker for gut inflammation. Stool cultures, microscopic examination, and PCR assays for \textit{Clostridioides difficile} toxins must be performed to rule out bacterial, viral, or parasitic infections.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Endoscopic Evaluation}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Colonoscopy with Ileoscopy}: The primary diagnostic tool. It allows direct visualization of the mucosa and determines the extent of the disease. Key findings in active UC include continuous inflammation starting at the rectum, loss of vascular patterns, redness, mucosal friability (bleeding when touched by the scope), and superficial ulcers. In chronic disease, mucosal healing can lead to pseudopolyps and a shortened colon.
    \item \textit{Histology (Biopsy)}: Biopsies must be taken from multiple segments of the colon. Histological criteria for UC include inflammation limited to the mucosa, crypt architectural distortion (shortening and branching of crypts), cryptitis, crypt abscesses, goblet cell depletion, and plasma cell or neutrophil infiltration in the lamina propria.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Imaging}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Abdominal X-ray}: Indicated in severe acute colitis to rule out toxic megacolon (transverse colon diameter greater than 6 cm with loss of haustration) and free air under the diaphragm (bowel perforation).
    \item \textit{CT or MR Enterography}: These cross-sectional imaging techniques help assess colonic wall thickening and check the small bowel to rule out Crohn's disease.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The goals of treating ulcerative colitis are to induce clinical and endoscopic remission, maintain remission without using corticosteroids, prevent complications, reduce the risk of colon cancer, and improve overall quality of life.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Pharmacological Therapy}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{5-Aminosalicylates (5-ASAs)}: Mesalazine, sulfasalazine, olsalazine, and balsalazide are the primary treatments for mild-to-moderate UC. For proctitis, topical 5-ASA (suppositories) is the first-line therapy. For left-sided or extensive colitis, a combination of oral and rectal 5-ASA (enemas or foams) is most effective. 5-ASAs are also used for long-term maintenance.
    \item \textit{Corticosteroids}: Used to induce remission in moderate-to-severe active disease. Options include oral prednisolone, budesonide MMX (a multi-matrix formulation that releases the drug in the colon to minimize systemic side effects), and intravenous methylprednisolone or hydrocortisone for severe cases. Corticosteroids must never be used for maintenance therapy due to severe side effects like osteoporosis, infection risk, hypertension, and adrenal suppression.
    \item \textit{Immunomodulators}: Thiopurines (azathioprine and 6-mercaptopurine) are oral medications used to maintain remission in patients with steroid-dependent disease. They take $8$ to $12$ weeks to work and require regular blood count monitoring due to risks of myelosuppression and liver toxicity.
    \item \textit{Biologic Therapies}: For moderate-to-severe disease refractory to conventional treatments:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Anti-TNF-alpha Agents}: Infliximab (IV), adalimumab (subcutaneous), and golimumab (subcutaneous). Infliximab is also used as a rescue therapy in acute severe colitis.
      \item \textbf{Anti-Integrin Monoclonal Antibodies}: Vedolizumab blocks alpha-4 beta-7 integrin, preventing white blood cells from entering the gut mucosa. Its gut-selective action offers a low systemic infection risk.
      \item \textbf{Anti-IL-12/23 and Anti-IL-23 Antibodies}: Ustekinumab and newer selective IL-23 blockers like mirikizumab are highly effective at inducing and maintaining remission.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textit{Targeted Synthetic Small Molecules}:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Janus Kinase (JAK) Inhibitors}: Tofacitinib, upadacitinib, and filgotinib are oral medications that block inflammatory cytokine pathways, offering rapid symptom relief.
      \item \textbf{Sphingosine-1-Phosphate (S1P) Receptor Modulators}: Ozanimod and etrasimod are oral drugs that prevent inflammatory white blood cells from leaving lymph nodes, reducing migration to the colon.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Surgical Intervention}: Surgery is considered curative for UC because it removes the diseased colon and rectum. About $10\%$ to $15\%$ of patients require surgery in their lifetime.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Indications}: Acute severe colitis unresponsive to medical rescue therapy, toxic megacolon, bowel perforation, severe bleeding, or dysplasia/cancer.
    \item \textit{Procedures}:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item \textbf{Restorative Proctocolectomy with Ileal Pouch-Anal Anastomosis (IPAA)}: The gold standard procedure. The colon and rectum are removed, and a reservoir (pouch) is made from the end of the small intestine and connected to the anus, preserving bowel continence without a permanent stoma bag.
      \item \textbf{Total Proctocolectomy with End Ileostomy}: Removal of the colon and rectum with the creation of a permanent stoma (ileostomy) on the abdomen. This is preferred in older patients or in emergency situations.
    \end{itemize}
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Nutritional and Support Care}: A low-residue diet can minimize stool volume during active flares. Deficiencies in iron, vitamin D, calcium, and vitamin B12 must be corrected. Patients should strictly avoid NSAIDs, which can trigger flares. Psychological support and stress management are highly beneficial.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Ulcerative colitis can cause severe colonic and systemic complications, especially if inflammation is not controlled.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Toxic Megacolon}: A life-threatening dilation of the colon (transverse colon diameter greater than 6 cm) with signs of systemic toxicity (fever, rapid heart rate, low red blood cell count). It carries a high risk of bowel perforation and peritonitis, requiring urgent decompression or surgery.
  \item \textbf{Bowel Perforation}: A rupture in the colonic wall, occurring most commonly during a severe, fulminant flare or as a result of toxic megacolon. It is a surgical emergency that leads to peritonitis and sepsis.
  \item \textbf{Severe Gastrointestinal Hemorrhage}: Massive mucosal bleeding can lead to shock, requiring urgent blood transfusions, endoscopic therapy, or surgery.
  \item \textbf{Colorectal Cancer}: The risk of colon cancer increases with the duration of the disease (typically rising after $8$ to $10$ years), the extent of colonic involvement (highest in pancolitis), and the presence of primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC). Regular screening colonoscopies with biopsies are recommended every $1$ to $3$ years.
  \item \textbf{Venous Thromboembolism (VTE)}: Active colitis increases the risk of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE). Prophylactic blood thinners are standard for hospitalized patients during flares.
  \item \textbf{Systemic Complications}: Chronic steroid use and malabsorption can lead to bone loss (osteoporosis and osteopenia). Pediatric patients may experience growth retardation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis of ulcerative colitis has improved significantly due to modern medical therapies, optimized surgical techniques, and structured surveillance programs. The disease is characterized by a lifelong, unpredictable course of active flares and remissions.

Approximately $50\%$ of patients are in clinical remission at any given time, while $30\%$ experience a mild-to-moderate relapsing course, and $20\%$ suffer from chronic, active disease. The risk of requiring a colectomy is highest in the first year after diagnosis, with about $10\%$ to $15\%$ of patients undergoing surgery within $10$ years.

Factors associated with a poorer prognosis and a higher likelihood of requiring advanced therapies or surgery include:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Age at diagnosis younger than $30$ years.
  \item Extensive colonic involvement (pancolitis) at presentation.
  \item Severe mucosal ulceration seen during initial endoscopy.
  \item Requirement for systemic steroids at the time of diagnosis.
  \item Coexisting primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC).
  \item Low serum albumin levels during active flares.
\end{itemize}

Life expectancy for individuals with ulcerative colitis is generally similar to that of the general population. However, mortality is elevated in patients who develop severe complications like toxic megacolon, bowel perforation, colorectal cancer, or advanced liver disease. While quality of life can be affected by chronic pain, urgency, and the psychological burden, early and personalized medical care allows the majority of patients to achieve long-term remission and lead active, productive lives.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
While the onset of ulcerative colitis cannot be prevented, self-care strategies focus on preventing flare-ups, managing side effects, and supporting overall health.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Medication Adherence}: The most effective way to prevent flares and reduce colon cancer risk is consistent adherence to prescribed maintenance therapies, even when completely asymptomatic. Stopping medications is a primary trigger for severe disease relapse.
  \item \textbf{Symptom Monitoring}: Keeping a symptom log (tracking stool frequency, presence of blood, and abdominal pain) helps identify early signs of a flare, allowing for timely medical adjustments.
  \item \textbf{Dietary Management}: Keeping a food diary helps identify personal food triggers (such as spicy foods, high-fiber foods, dairy, or caffeine) during flares. Working with a dietitian is recommended to ensure a balanced, nutrient-dense diet.
  \item \textbf{Avoiding NSAIDs and Stress}: Patients should avoid NSAIDs (such as ibuprofen, naproxen, and diclofenac), as they can cause mucosal irritation and trigger flares. Paracetamol is a safer alternative. Managing stress through mindfulness, yoga, or low-impact exercise is beneficial, as stress can affect gut inflammation.
  \item \textbf{Preventive Health and Vaccinations}:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Vaccinations}: Because many therapies suppress the immune system, patients should update their vaccinations before starting treatment. Recommended vaccines include the annual flu vaccine, pneumococcal vaccine, hepatitis B vaccine, and the shingles vaccine (Shingrix). Live vaccines are contraindicated once immunosuppressive therapy has started.
    \item \textit{Bone and Skin Health}: Patients with frequent steroid exposure should undergo regular bone density scans and take calcium and vitamin D supplements. Annual skin cancer screenings and daily sunscreen use are recommended, as certain therapies can increase skin cancer risks.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
