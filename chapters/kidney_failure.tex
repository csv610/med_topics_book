\chapter{Kidney Failure}
\index{Kidney Failure}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Kidney failure, also called renal failure or end-stage kidney disease, is the condition in which the kidneys are no longer able to filter waste products and fluid from the blood adequately. It can be acute, developing suddenly over hours or days, often in hospital during severe illness, or chronic, developing gradually over months or years from conditions such as diabetes and high blood pressure. Chronic kidney failure is progressive and ultimately requires treatment with dialysis or a kidney transplant to sustain life. Acute kidney failure is often reversible if the cause is treated promptly. This chapter explains the types, causes, symptoms, and treatment of kidney failure.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Chronic kidney disease affects around one in ten people, and around 25,000 people receive treatment for end-stage kidney disease with dialysis or a transplant. The two leading causes are diabetes and high blood pressure, which together account for over half of cases. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples have kidney failure rates several times higher than the general population. Acute kidney failure affects a significant proportion of hospitalised patients, especially the critically ill, and is associated with increased mortality. Kidney disease is often silent until advanced, which is why early testing matters.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The kidneys filter about 180 litres of blood daily, removing waste and excess fluid while retaining what the body needs. Chronic kidney failure develops when the filtering units, the nephrons, are progressively destroyed by diabetes, high blood pressure, glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease, and other conditions, until fewer than a tenth of nephrons remain. As function falls, waste products such as urea and creatinine accumulate, fluid builds up, and the balance of potassium, sodium, calcium, and phosphate is disturbed. Acute kidney failure results from sudden injury to the kidneys from severe infection, blood loss, dehydration, medicines, obstruction, or reduced blood flow. Anaemia, bone disease, heart disease, and nerve damage develop as the kidneys fail.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic kidney disease is often silent in its early stages
    \item Fatigue, weakness, and reduced concentration
    \item Swelling of the ankles, legs, and face from fluid retention
    \item Reduced urine output, or foamy, dark, or bloody urine
    \item Shortness of breath from fluid on the lungs
    \item Nausea, vomiting, and poor appetite
    \item Itching, muscle cramps, and restless legs
    \item High blood pressure
    \item Acute kidney failure: sudden drop in urine output, confusion, and severe fatigue
    \item Advanced symptoms: confusion, seizures, and coma without treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The cause and type of kidney failure must be identified.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute versus chronic kidney failure:} onset and duration
    \item \textbf{Pre-renal causes:} dehydration, bleeding, and heart failure reducing kidney blood flow
    \item \textbf{Renal causes:} glomerulonephritis, interstitial nephritis, and toxins
    \item \textbf{Post-renal causes:} obstruction of the urinary tract by stones or prostate disease
    \item \textbf{Diabetes and high blood pressure:} the leading causes of chronic failure
    \item \textbf{Polycystic kidney disease and glomerulonephritis:} other chronic causes
    \item \textbf{Other causes of the symptoms:} heart failure, anaemia, and liver disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with diabetes, high blood pressure, or a family history of kidney disease should have regular kidney function tests, and anyone with swelling, foamy urine, or blood in the urine should be assessed. Urgent care is needed for a sudden drop in urine output, severe swelling, breathlessness, confusion, or vomiting. Medicines that affect the kidneys should be reviewed in people at risk.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, severe breathlessness, or fluid on the lungs
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or a seizure
    \item Passing very little or no urine
    \item Vomiting with an inability to keep fluids down
    \item Chest pain or collapse
    \item Very high blood pressure with headache or visual disturbance
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), electrolytes, and other markers
    \item \textbf{Urine tests:} protein and blood, and urine culture
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} assessment of kidney size and structure
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} CT and other tests for obstruction and anatomy
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} in selected cases to identify the underlying disease
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular testing to track progression
    \item \textbf{Assessment of complications:} anaemia, bone disease, and heart disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Treating the cause:} controlling diabetes, blood pressure, and other conditions
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} blood pressure medicines, and medicines that protect the kidneys
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} healthy diet, low salt, fluid management, and stopping smoking
    \item \textbf{Treatment of complications:} anaemia, bone disease, and heart disease
    \item \textbf{Dialysis:} haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis when kidney function falls below about 10 to 15 per cent
    \item \textbf{Kidney transplant:} the best long-term treatment for suitable people
    \item \textbf{Acute kidney failure:} fluids, stopping harmful medicines, and treatment of the cause
    \item \textbf{Palliative care:} comfort and quality of life for people who choose not to dialyse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fluid overload with swelling and heart failure
    \item High potassium, which can cause dangerous heart rhythms
    \item Anaemia and fatigue
    \item Bone disease and fractures
    \item Heart disease and stroke
    \item Nerve damage and muscle cramps
    \item Malnutrition and weight loss
    \item Complications of dialysis, including infection and access problems
    \item Death without treatment of advanced failure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the cause, stage, and treatment. Early chronic kidney disease can be slowed for many years with good control of blood pressure and diabetes, and many people never progress to failure. Acute kidney failure is reversible in most people when the cause is treated. For end-stage disease, dialysis sustains life and allows many people to live for years, and a transplant offers the best survival and quality of life. Early detection and specialist care improve outcomes substantially.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Control blood pressure and diabetes
    \item Have kidney function tests if you are at risk
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and be active
    \item Eat a healthy diet and limit salt
    \item Do not smoke and limit alcohol
    \item Drink enough fluid and avoid dehydration
    \item Use medicines that affect the kidneys with care
    \item Attend specialist kidney reviews
    \item Discuss dialysis and transplant options early
    \item Seek urgent care for sudden changes in urine or severe symptoms
\end{itemize}
