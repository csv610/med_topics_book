\chapter{High Cholesterol}
\index{High Cholesterol}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
High cholesterol, or hypercholesterolaemia, is an elevated level of cholesterol and other fats in the blood. Cholesterol is a fatty substance needed to build cells and hormones, but when LDL ("bad") cholesterol is too high, it accumulates in the walls of arteries, forming plaques that narrow vessels and cause heart attacks and strokes. High cholesterol usually produces no symptoms, so it is detected by blood tests. It is managed with lifestyle change and, where needed, cholesterol-lowering medicines such as statins, which substantially reduce cardiovascular risk.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Elevated cholesterol affects about one in three adults, and the prevalence rises with age. High cholesterol is a leading contributor to the burden of cardiovascular disease, which remains the leading cause of death in many countries. Around one in four people takes a cholesterol-lowering medicine, and statin use has contributed to the marked fall in heart attack and stroke rates over recent decades. Many people with high cholesterol are unaware, which is why regular cardiovascular risk assessment is important.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diet:} high intake of saturated and trans fats raises LDL cholesterol
    \item \textbf{Genetics:} familial hypercholesterolaemia causes very high cholesterol from birth
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} physical inactivity, obesity, and excess alcohol
    \item \textbf{Medical conditions:} diabetes, hypothyroidism, kidney and liver disease
    \item \textbf{Age and sex:} cholesterol rises with age; women's levels increase after menopause
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} LDL particles enter the artery wall, where they are oxidised and engulfed by immune cells, forming fatty plaques; plaque rupture causes clots that trigger heart attack and stroke
    \item \textbf{HDL ("good") cholesterol:} helps remove cholesterol from arteries; low levels increase risk
    \item \textbf{Triglycerides:} another blood fat that contributes to risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item High cholesterol itself causes no symptoms
    \item It is detected by blood testing, often during routine health checks
    \item Familial hypercholesterolaemia may cause visible signs: fatty deposits around the eyes (xanthelasma), tendons, and knuckles
    \item A white ring around the cornea (arcus) may appear in young people with very high cholesterol
    \item Symptoms arise from complications: angina, heart attack, stroke, and peripheral artery disease
    \item Most people feel entirely well
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The type of lipid abnormality matters: raised LDL, low HDL, and raised triglycerides each carry different implications
    \item Secondary causes such as hypothyroidism, diabetes, and medications must be considered
    \item Familial hypercholesterolaemia is diagnosed by very high LDL levels and family history
    \item Combined risk factors, rather than cholesterol alone, determine treatment decisions
    \item Cardiovascular risk calculators integrate cholesterol with age, blood pressure, smoking, and diabetes
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Adults should have cholesterol checked as part of cardiovascular risk assessment, ideally from age 45 (or younger with risk factors), and people with a family history of early heart disease or high cholesterol should be tested earlier.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, pressure, or heaviness (possible heart attack)
    \item Sudden weakness, facial drooping, or difficulty speaking (stroke)
    \item Pain radiating to the arm, neck, or jaw
    \item Severe breathlessness or collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lipid profile:} fasting or non-fasting blood test measuring total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, and triglycerides
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular risk assessment:} combines lipid levels with age, sex, blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, and other factors
    \item \textbf{Repeat testing:} to confirm elevation before treatment
    \item \textbf{Thyroid, liver, and kidney tests:} to exclude secondary causes
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} for familial hypercholesterolaemia where suspected
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} lipid levels are re-checked after starting treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Diet:} reduce saturated fat, increase vegetables, fruit, wholegrains, and healthy fats such as olive oil and nuts
    \item \textbf{Physical activity:} regular exercise raises HDL and lowers triglycerides
    \item \textbf{Weight and alcohol:} weight loss and limited alcohol improve lipids
    \item \textbf{Statins:} the first-line medicines, reducing LDL and cardiovascular events by a quarter to a third
    \item \textbf{Other medicines:} ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors, and fibrates for specific situations
    \item \textbf{Treatment targets:} based on overall cardiovascular risk, not cholesterol alone
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular prevention:} blood pressure, blood sugar, and smoking are managed together
    \item \textbf{Family screening:} relatives of people with familial hypercholesterolaemia are tested
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Atherosclerosis and its consequences: heart attack, stroke, and angina
    \item Peripheral artery disease with leg pain and poor healing
    \item Carotid artery disease and transient ischaemic attacks
    \item Aortic aneurysm
    \item Kidney artery disease and hypertension
    \item Statin side effects, including muscle aches in a minority, though serious effects are rare
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Treating high cholesterol is highly effective: statin therapy reduces the risk of heart attack and stroke by around 25--35\%, and the benefits are greatest in people at higher overall risk. Lifestyle change alone can lower cholesterol by 5--15\%, and combined with medication, most people reach their targets. For people with familial hypercholesterolaemia, early treatment restores a near-normal life expectancy. With sustained management, high cholesterol becomes a well-controlled risk factor rather than a threat to life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Eat a heart-healthy diet low in saturated fat and rich in plant foods
    \item Be physically active most days of the week
    \item Maintain a healthy weight
    \item Limit alcohol and avoid smoking
    \item Know your cholesterol numbers and overall cardiovascular risk
    \item Take prescribed medicines consistently and attend follow-up
    \item Have family members tested if you have very high cholesterol
    \item Attend routine health checks from middle age
\end{itemize}
