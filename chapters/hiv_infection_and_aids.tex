\chapter{HIV Infection and AIDS}
\index{HIV Infection and AIDS}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
HIV infection is caused by the human immunodeficiency virus, which progressively damages the immune system. AIDS (acquired immunodeficiency syndrome) is the advanced stage of untreated HIV infection. The infection progresses through distinct phases: a brief acute illness shortly after exposure, a long asymptomatic period, and, without treatment, progressive immune failure that allows life-threatening opportunistic infections and cancers to develop. Modern antiretroviral treatment interrupts this progression at any stage, allowing immune recovery and a normal lifespan.

\section*{Epidemiology}
An estimated 30,000 people live with HIV, and around 90\% are diagnosed. Most are on treatment with an undetectable viral load. Globally, around 39 million people live with HIV, of whom three-quarters are on treatment. New diagnoses in many countries have declined by more than 30\% over the past decade, driven by testing, treatment, and pre-exposure prophylaxis. AIDS-related deaths have fallen dramatically worldwide, from a peak of nearly 2 million a year to around 600,000.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} unprotected vaginal, anal, or oral sex; sharing injecting equipment; mother-to-child transmission; and rarely contaminated blood products
    \item \textbf{Primary infection:} the virus enters CD4 T-lymphocytes and replicates rapidly
    \item \textbf{Seroconversion:} the immune response produces antibodies, usually with a brief flu-like illness
    \item \textbf{Clinical latency:} viral replication continues at lower levels for a median of 8--10 years without treatment
    \item \textbf{Progression:} CD4 counts fall, and immune function deteriorates
    \item \textbf{AIDS:} CD4 count below 200 cells/mm³ or the appearance of opportunistic infections and cancers
    \item \textbf{Treatment:} antiretroviral therapy suppresses replication, stops CD4 decline, and prevents AIDS
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute infection: fever, sore throat, swollen glands, rash, and fatigue, 2--6 weeks after exposure
    \item Years of good health with no symptoms
    \item Persistent fatigue, weight loss, night sweats, and recurrent infections as immunity falls
    \item Oral thrush, shingles, and pneumonia become more frequent
    \item AIDS-defining conditions: pneumocystis pneumonia, oesophageal candidiasis, tuberculosis, toxoplasmosis, and Kaposi sarcoma
    \item Neurological complications, including HIV-associated dementia and neuropathy
    \item People on treatment may have no symptoms at all
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute HIV resembles glandular fever and other viral infections
    \item Chronic weight loss and fatigue have many causes
    \item Opportunistic infections occur in other forms of immunosuppression
    \item The diagnosis is made by HIV testing, not clinical features alone
    \item Early testing is essential because symptoms are unreliable
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who may have been exposed should test, and testing is recommended routinely in pregnancy and in at-risk groups. Testing is confidential and free in many settings.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing or high fever in a person with HIV
    \item Severe headache, stiff neck, confusion, or seizures
    \item Coughing blood or severe abdominal pain
    \item Sudden neurological symptoms
    \item Thoughts of self-harm or suicide
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fourth-generation tests:} detect HIV antibodies and p24 antigen; most infections are detected within 4 weeks
    \item \textbf{Confirmatory tests:} differentiate HIV-1 and HIV-2
    \item \textbf{Viral load:} confirms the diagnosis and monitors treatment
    \item \textbf{CD4 count:} measures immune function
    \item \textbf{Resistance testing:} guides treatment choice
    \item \textbf{Co-infection screening:} hepatitis B and C, syphilis, and tuberculosis
    \item \textbf{Routine testing:} recommended in pregnancy and for all sexually active people at risk
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antiretroviral therapy:} recommended for everyone from diagnosis, with an undetectable viral load as the goal
    \item \textbf{Immune recovery:} CD4 counts rise with treatment
    \item \textbf{Opportunistic infection management:} prevention and treatment of infections and cancers
    \item \textbf{Prevention of transmission:} undetectable viral load means no sexual transmission
    \item \textbf{Mother-to-child prevention:} treatment in pregnancy makes transmission rare
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular viral load, CD4, and general health checks
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive care:} mental health, sexual health, and chronic disease management
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Opportunistic infections, including pneumocystis pneumonia and tuberculosis
    \item AIDS-defining cancers: Kaposi sarcoma, lymphoma, and cervical cancer
    \item HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder
    \item Wasting syndrome
    \item Chronic inflammation-related disease in treated people
    \item Mental health conditions and stigma
    \item Death without treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for HIV has been transformed by treatment. People diagnosed early and treated consistently have a life expectancy close to the general population, and AIDS is almost entirely prevented. Those diagnosed late with advanced immune suppression still recover with treatment, though they carry higher risks of complications. Untreated, HIV progresses to AIDS and is fatal within a few years of the AIDS diagnosis. Testing, early treatment, and adherence are the keys to an excellent outcome.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use condoms and consider PrEP if at risk
    \item Use PEP within 72 hours of a potential exposure
    \item Never share injecting equipment
    \item Test regularly if at risk
    \item Start treatment promptly and take it consistently
    \item Attend all monitoring appointments
    \item Vaccinate against hepatitis A and B, influenza, and other preventable infections
    \item Seek support for disclosure, stigma, and mental health
\end{itemize}
