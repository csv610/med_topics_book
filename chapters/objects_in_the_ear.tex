\chapter{Objects in the ear}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An aural foreign body is any object, substance, or insect that becomes lodged in the external auditory canal. Foreign bodies in the ear are common clinical presentations that can range from benign, easily retrievable objects to high-risk emergencies. The external auditory canal is a narrow, sensitive cavity lined with delicate skin that lies in close proximity to the tympanic membrane (eardrum) and the middle ear structures. Improper attempts at extraction can cause lacerations, secondary infection, or permanent damage to the auditory apparatus. Prompt, careful assessment and structured management are crucial to safely remove the object while preserving hearing function.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Objects in the ear occur predominantly in young children, typically between the ages of one and six years, due to exploratory play. In children, common objects include beads, toy parts, pebbles, paper, food items, and button batteries. In adults, foreign bodies are usually inserted accidentally, with cotton bud tips, matchsticks, hearing aid domes, and insects (such as cockroaches or moths) being the most common culprits. The condition has no gender predilection, though individuals with intellectual disabilities or cognitive impairment are at a higher risk across all age groups.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The lodging of foreign bodies and the subsequent pathological processes in the ear canal are driven by several factors:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Paediatric curiosity:} Children frequently place small objects into their ears or nose as part of normal developmental exploration or due to boredom.
    \item \textbf{Inappropriate ear cleaning:} The widespread use of cotton buds (cotton wool buds) or hairpins to clear earwax often pushes wax deeper (cerumen impaction) or leads to the cotton tip detaching and remaining in the canal.
    \item \textbf{Environmental intrusion:} Insects may fly or crawl into the ear canal of sleeping or outdoor-active individuals, where they become trapped due to the canal's blind-ended anatomy.
    \item \textbf{Anatomical constrictions:} The external auditory canal narrows at the junction of the outer cartilaginous portion and the inner bony portion (isthmus). Once an object is pushed past this narrowing, it easily becomes wedged.
    \item \textbf{Hygroscopic swelling:} Organic foreign bodies, such as seeds, beans, or paper, absorb moisture from the ear canal and swell over time, causing increasing pain, pressure, and mechanical occlusion.
    \item \textbf{Chemical burns:} Button batteries lodged in the ear canal can release alkaline substances or generate micro-currents, causing rapid tissue necrosis and potential erosion through the tympanic membrane or ear canal wall.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Patients may present immediately or delayed, depending on whether the insertion was witnessed or if symptoms have developed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Otalgia (ear pain) or localized discomfort
    \item Conductive hearing loss or a sensation of muffled hearing
    \item Aural fullness, pressure, or a blocked ear sensation
    \item Otorrhoea (discharge), which may be watery, purulent, or blood-stained
    \item Tinnitus (ringing in the ear) or a buzzing sensation (particularly if a live insect is present)
    \item Vertigo or dizziness (suggestive of inner ear irritation or tympanic membrane injury)
    \item Chronic coughing or gagging, triggered by stimulation of the auricular branch of the vagus nerve (Arnold's nerve)
    \item Redness, swelling, or visible excoriation of the external ear canal
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The clinical features of an aural foreign body must be distinguished from:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cerumen impaction:} Excess or impacted earwax that presents with hearing loss and fullness but lacks a foreign object history.
    \item \textbf{Otitis externa:} An infectious inflammation of the external ear canal (e.g., swimmer's ear) causing pain, erythema, and discharge.
    \item \textbf{Otitis media with perforation:} Middle ear infection that has ruptured the tympanic membrane, presenting with discharge and pain.
    \item \textbf{Cholesteatoma:} An abnormal collection of skin cells deep inside the ear canal or middle ear, mimicking a chronic, discharging mass.
    \item \textbf{Exostosis:} Benign bony growths in the ear canal (commonly seen in swimmers) that can restrict the canal and trap debris.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any suspected foreign body in the ear should be evaluated by a healthcare professional, as self-removal attempts often push the object deeper and increase the risk of injury.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A suspected button battery in the ear canal (requires emergency removal to prevent severe chemical burns)
    \item Severe bleeding or clear fluid draining from the ear canal following an injury or insertion
    \item Sudden, severe dizziness, vertigo, vomiting, or total loss of hearing
    \item Spreading redness, warmth, or swelling behind the ear, on the pinna, or on the face
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
A thorough evaluation is essential to confirm the diagnosis and assess for structural damage:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Otoscopy:} Direct inspection of the ear canal using an otoscope to identify the object, its composition, position relative to the isthmus, and the condition of the tympanic membrane.
    \item \textbf{Binocular microscopy:} Often performed in specialist clinics to provide magnification and depth perception, facilitating safe instrumentation.
    \item \textbf{Tuning fork tests:} Rinne and Weber tests may be conducted to evaluate the presence and severity of conductive hearing loss.
    \item \textbf{Audiometry:} Indicated if there is concern about persistent hearing loss, ossicular chain disruption, or inner ear damage post-extraction.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The method of removal is determined by the patient's cooperation, the object's characteristics, and the examiner's expertise:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Insect immobilisation:} If a live insect is present, it should be killed or immobilised prior to extraction by instilling mineral oil, olive oil, or 2\% lidocaine (unless tympanic membrane perforation is suspected).
    \item \textbf{Mechanical extraction:} Non-spherical, soft, or fibrous objects can be grasped using alligator forceps. Spherical or smooth objects should not be grasped, but rather extracted using a micro-hook or cerumen loop placed behind the object.
    \item \textbf{Suction:} A small suction catheter can be used to extract light, soft, or round objects under direct vision.
    \item \textbf{Syringing/Irrigation:} Flushing the ear canal with warm water can wash out small, inorganic objects. This is strictly contraindicated for organic objects (which swell), button batteries, and if there is a suspected or confirmed tympanic membrane perforation.
    \item \textbf{General anaesthesia:} Referral to an Ear, Nose, and Throat (ENT) specialist for removal under general anaesthesia is indicated for young children, uncooperative patients, or objects wedged tightly against the tympanic membrane.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Complications can arise from the object itself or from trauma sustained during extraction attempts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Lacerations of the external auditory canal, causing bleeding and pain
    \item Tympanic membrane perforation
    \item Disruption of the middle ear ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes), leading to permanent conductive hearing loss
    \item Otitis externa or perichondritis due to bacterial introduction or chemical irritation
    \item Temporal bone osteomyelitis (rare, usually in immunocompromised individuals)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis is excellent when the foreign body is removed promptly and safely. Most canal lacerations heal rapidly without scarring. Perforations of the tympanic membrane caused by direct trauma often heal spontaneously within six to eight weeks. If complications such as ossicular disruption or severe chemical burns occur, long-term hearing loss or chronic ear issues may result, sometimes requiring surgical reconstruction (myringoplasty or ossiculoplasty).

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Never insert cotton buds, keys, hairpins, or matchsticks into the ear canal to clean or scratch it.
    \item Keep small batteries, beads, buttons, and toys with small parts in secure cabinets out of reach of young children.
    \item Educate children about the dangers of inserting objects into their ears, nose, or mouth.
    \item Ensure hearing aid domes and ear tips are securely attached and checked regularly for wear.
    \item Wear protective earplugs or headgear when sleeping outdoors or working in insect-dense environments.
\end{itemize}
