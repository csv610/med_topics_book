\chapter{Intrauterine Device (IUD)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
An intrauterine device (IUD), also known as a coil, is a small T-shaped device placed inside the uterus for long-acting contraception. There are two types: the copper IUD, which contains no hormones and prevents pregnancy for up to 10 years, and the hormonal IUD, which releases a low dose of progestogen, prevents pregnancy for 5 to 8 years, and also treats heavy periods. IUDs are among the most effective reversible contraceptives, with a failure rate below one per cent, and they do not depend on remembering to take anything. Insertion and removal are simple procedures performed by a doctor or nurse, and fertility returns immediately after removal.

\section*{Epidemiology}
IUD use has grown substantially in Australia and is now one of the most popular long-acting contraceptive methods, used by around one in ten women using contraception. It is recommended by professional bodies as a first-line method for most women, including younger women and those who have not had children. The hormonal IUD is also a leading treatment for heavy menstrual bleeding, reducing the need for surgery. Around half of all pregnancies are unplanned, and increasing the use of highly effective methods such as the IUD is a key public health strategy.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The copper IUD prevents pregnancy primarily by releasing copper ions that are toxic to sperm, preventing fertilisation, and by altering the uterine lining so that implantation is unlikely. The hormonal IUD thickens cervical mucus to block sperm, thins the uterine lining, and reduces menstrual bleeding, with some cycles stopping ovulation. The device is placed in the uterine cavity with threads extending through the cervix so it can be checked. The uterus is not harmed by the device, and long-term use does not affect future fertility. The main mechanisms are local, which is why the hormonal IUD has fewer systemic side effects than hormonal methods absorbed throughout the body.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Insertion takes a few minutes in a clinic, with cramping during and after
    \item Local anaesthetic may be offered, especially for women who have not had children
    \item The copper IUD may cause heavier, longer, or more painful periods
    \item The hormonal IUD makes periods lighter, and many women stop bleeding altogether
    \item Spotting is common in the first three to six months
    \item Cramping and light bleeding for a few days after insertion
    \item Threads can be felt in the upper vagina to confirm the device is in place
    \item A follow-up visit at three to six weeks confirms correct placement
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The choice of contraceptive method is individualised.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Other long-acting methods:} the contraceptive implant
    \item \textbf{Combined hormonal methods:} the pill, patch, and ring
    \item \textbf{Progestogen-only methods:} the mini-pill and injection
    \item \textbf{Barrier methods:} condoms and diaphragms
    \item \textbf{Permanent methods:} sterilisation for those who have completed their family
    \item \textbf{Contraindications:} current pelvic infection, undiagnosed abnormal bleeding, and some uterine abnormalities
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Women considering an IUD should discuss the options with a doctor or nurse and be screened for infection if needed. Seek medical review for pain, abnormal discharge, fever, a missed period, or threads that cannot be felt. If the device is suspected to have moved or been expelled, use back-up contraception and seek review.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal or pelvic pain after insertion
    \item Heavy bleeding with faintness or dizziness
    \item High fever, which may indicate pelvic infection
    \item A positive pregnancy test with pain or bleeding, which may indicate ectopic pregnancy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Consultation:} contraceptive history, pregnancy test, and discussion of options
    \item \textbf{Examination:} assessment of the cervix and uterus, with infection screening if needed
    \item \textbf{Insertion:} placement of the device, sometimes with ultrasound guidance
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} to confirm position if threads are missing or symptoms suggest displacement
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review of symptoms and threads at three to six weeks
    \item \textbf{Screening:} cervical screening and sexually transmissible infection testing as indicated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple analgesics and heat packs for cramping after insertion
    \item \textbf{Local anaesthetic:} offered to reduce discomfort during insertion
    \item \textbf{Checking threads:} taught to the woman and confirmed at follow-up
    \item \textbf{Managing side effects:} review of bleeding patterns and cramps
    \item \textbf{Treatment of infection:} antibiotics for pelvic infection when present
    \item \textbf{Removal:} performed easily at any time, with rapid return of fertility
    \item \textbf{Replacement:} scheduled according to the device type
    \item \textbf{Continuing care:} annual reviews and screening as recommended
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cramping and pain during and after insertion
    \item Bleeding changes: spotting, heavier or lighter periods
    \item Expulsion of the device, most often in the first year
    \item Perforation of the uterus, which is rare
    \item Pelvic infection, mainly in the weeks after insertion
    \item Pregnancy with the device in place, which is uncommon
    \item Missing threads, requiring assessment and ultrasound
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
IUDs are more than 99 per cent effective, and their effectiveness does not depend on daily compliance. Most side effects settle within the first months, and continuation rates are among the highest of any method. The hormonal IUD resolves or greatly improves heavy periods in most women, often avoiding surgery. Fertility returns immediately on removal, and long-term use has no effect on fertility or pregnancy outcomes. For most women, the IUD is a safe, convenient, and highly effective choice.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discuss all contraceptive options to choose the best fit
    \item Have insertion performed by an experienced clinician
    \item Take analgesia and rest after insertion if cramping
    \item Use back-up contraception as advised until the device is effective
    \item Check the threads monthly, especially after a period
    \item Report pain, fever, discharge, or a missed period promptly
    \item Attend the follow-up appointment
    \item Know when the device needs replacing
    \item Remember the device can be removed at any time
\end{itemize}
