\chapter{Hepatitis A}
\index{Hepatitis A}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hepatitis A is a contagious viral infection of the liver caused by the hepatitis A virus. It is spread mainly through contaminated food and water and by close personal contact, and it causes acute inflammation of the liver with symptoms such as fatigue, nausea, and jaundice. Unlike hepatitis B and C, hepatitis A never becomes a chronic infection: most people recover completely within weeks to months, and infection confers lifelong immunity. Safe and effective vaccination is available and is part of the routine childhood schedule in many countries.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hepatitis A occurs worldwide but is most common where sanitation and food safety are poor. In many countries, infection rates have fallen dramatically since vaccination was introduced into the childhood schedule in 2005, and most cases now occur in travellers returning from countries with higher prevalence, and in men who have sex with men. Outbreaks still occur, often linked to contaminated food such as berries or shellfish. Around half of adults have immunity through vaccination or past infection.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} the faecal--oral route, through contaminated food, water, or hands
    \item \textbf{Food-borne spread:} shellfish from contaminated water, unwashed produce, and food handled by infected people
    \item \textbf{Person-to-person:} close household and sexual contact
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} the virus enters the body through the gut and travels to the liver, where it multiplies in liver cells, triggering an immune response that causes acute inflammation and cell damage
    \item \textbf{Incubation:} symptoms appear 15--50 days after exposure
    \item \textbf{Immunity:} the body clears the virus and produces lifelong protective antibodies
    \item \textbf{Severity:} more severe in adults; often mild or silent in children
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Many children have no symptoms or a mild flu-like illness
    \item Fatigue, nausea, vomiting, and poor appetite
    \item Abdominal pain, especially in the upper right side
    \item Dark urine and pale stools
    \item Jaundice: yellowing of the skin and eyes
    \item Fever, joint and muscle aches
    \item Symptoms typically last a few weeks, but fatigue can persist for months
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Other viral hepatitis (B, C, E), distinguished by blood tests
    \item Gastroenteritis and other infections causing nausea and vomiting
    \item Medications and toxins causing liver injury
    \item Gallbladder disease and pancreatitis causing abdominal pain
    \item Epstein--Barr virus and cytomegalovirus infections
    \item Autoimmune hepatitis and other chronic liver conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical advice for symptoms of hepatitis, especially jaundice, dark urine, or persistent fatigue, and for anyone exposed to a confirmed case so that vaccination or immunoglobulin can be given quickly. Travellers should discuss vaccination well before departure.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or unusual behaviour with liver symptoms
    \item Vomiting blood or passing black stools
    \item Severe abdominal pain or swelling
    \item Inability to keep down fluids with severe dehydration
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} hepatitis A IgM antibody confirms acute infection
    \item \textbf{IgG antibody:} indicates past infection or vaccination
    \item \textbf{Liver function tests:} raised transaminases reflect liver inflammation
    \item \textbf{Clotting tests:} in severe cases
    \item \textbf{Notification:} hepatitis A is a notifiable disease, and public health authorities trace contacts
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} rest, fluids, and adequate nutrition are the mainstays
    \item \textbf{Avoid alcohol and paracetamol:} both stress the liver during recovery
    \item \textbf{Symptom relief:} medicines for nausea and fever as needed
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} for severe cases, dehydration, or complications
    \item \textbf{Post-exposure protection:} hepatitis A vaccine or immunoglobulin given within 14 days of exposure can prevent infection
    \item \textbf{Recovery:} gradual return to normal activity as symptoms resolve
    \item \textbf{No chronic treatment needed:} the infection clears on its own
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fulminant hepatitis, a rare but life-threatening liver failure, more common in older adults and people with existing liver disease
    \item Prolonged fatigue lasting several months
    \item Dehydration from vomiting
    \item Spread to household and sexual contacts
    \item Relapsing hepatitis in a small minority, with return of symptoms after recovery
    \item Severe disease in people with chronic liver disease
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Hepatitis A is almost always self-limiting. Most people recover fully within one to two months, and the infection never becomes chronic. Mortality is very low overall, but is higher in older adults and people with pre-existing liver disease, in whom fulminant hepatitis can occur. After recovery, lifelong immunity protects against future infection. The disease is preventable with vaccination, which provides long-lasting protection.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vaccination: hepatitis A vaccine is recommended for all children and for travellers, people with liver disease, and other at-risk groups
    \item Wash hands thoroughly with soap and water after toileting and before handling food
    \item Practise food safety: cook shellfish, wash produce, and avoid food from unsafe sources
    \item Drink safe water when travelling, and avoid ice of unknown origin
    \item Do not share food, drinks, or utensils with infected people
    \item Clean toilets and surfaces with disinfectant during household outbreaks
    \item Check immunisation status before overseas travel
\end{itemize}
