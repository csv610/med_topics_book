\chapter{Hydrocele}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A hydrocele is a collection of fluid around the testicle, within the sac (tunica vaginalis) that surrounds it. It causes a painless swelling of the scrotum. Hydroceles are common in newborn boys, where they usually resolve on their own within the first year, and they can also develop in older boys and men. In adults, a hydrocele may develop for no obvious reason or as a result of injury, infection, or a problem in the testicle. Hydroceles are usually harmless, but they should always be assessed because other conditions, including hernias and testicular tumours, can cause similar swelling.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Around 1--5\% of newborn boys have a hydrocele, and most disappear without treatment by 12 months of age. Hydroceles also occur in men, with an increasing incidence in older age, and they are one of the most common causes of scrotal swelling. Communicating hydroceles, in which fluid moves between the abdomen and scrotum, are associated with inguinal hernias. While hydroceles themselves are benign, they can be confused with more serious conditions, so assessment is essential.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Newborn hydrocele:} the passage between the abdomen and scrotum (processus vaginalis) closes late, allowing fluid to accumulate
    \item \textbf{Communicating hydrocele:} the passage stays open, so fluid moves between abdomen and scrotum
    \item \textbf{Adult hydrocele:} usually idiopathic, or secondary to injury, infection, or inflammation of the testicle
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} fluid accumulates in the tunica vaginalis, the sac around the testicle
    \item \textbf{Transillumination:} hydroceles glow when a light is shone through them
    \item \textbf{Distinction from hernia:} a hydrocele contains fluid; a hernia contains bowel or fat
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A painless, smooth swelling of one side of the scrotum
    \item A feeling of heaviness in the scrotum
    \item The swelling may change size with position in communicating hydroceles
    \item The testicle is usually palpable behind or below the swelling
    \item The swelling transilluminates with light
    \item Most hydroceles cause no pain
    \item Sudden painful swelling suggests other causes, such as torsion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Inguinal hernia, which is reducible and may contain bowel
    \item Testicular tumour, which presents as a firm, painless lump and does not transilluminate
    \item Testicular torsion, which causes sudden severe pain and is an emergency
    \item Epididymitis and infection, which cause pain and swelling
    \item Varicocele, a collection of veins above the testicle
    \item Ultrasound distinguishes hydrocele from these conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any new scrotal swelling should be assessed by a doctor, and swelling in a baby should be checked. Sudden severe pain with swelling is an emergency.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden, severe testicular or scrotal pain (possible torsion)
    \item A painful, tender, or red scrotum with fever
    \item Vomiting with a scrotal swelling (possible strangulated hernia)
    \item Injury to the scrotum with severe pain
    \item A lump that is hard or rapidly growing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Examination:} the swelling is examined, and transillumination is tested
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} the definitive test, confirming fluid and excluding testicular problems
    \item \textbf{Assessment of the testicle:} to exclude tumour
    \item \textbf{Observation in infants:} most newborn hydroceles resolve by 12 months
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} repeated assessment until resolution or referral
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observation:} most infant hydroceles resolve without treatment
    \item \textbf{Adult hydroceles:} many are small and need no treatment
    \item \textbf{Surgery (hydrocelectomy):} for large, uncomfortable, or persistent hydroceles
    \item \textbf{Communicating hydrocele:} surgical repair, usually with hernia repair
    \item \textbf{Aspiration:} draining fluid is sometimes used, though recurrence is common
    \item \textbf{Treatment of underlying causes:} infection or injury is treated
    \item \textbf{Reassurance:} a confirmed simple hydrocele is harmless
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A missed testicular tumour if the swelling is assumed to be a hydrocele
    \item Missed torsion in painful swellings
    \item Surgical complications: bleeding, infection, and recurrence
    \item Chronic discomfort from large hydroceles
    \item In communicating hydroceles, the risk of hernia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Hydroceles have an excellent outlook. Most newborn hydroceles resolve in the first year, and adult hydroceles that need treatment are cured with simple surgery, with a low recurrence rate. The main risk is misdiagnosis, which is why ultrasound is used when there is any doubt. With proper assessment, a hydrocele is a benign condition that rarely causes problems.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Have any scrotal swelling assessed by a doctor
    \item See a doctor promptly for sudden pain, which may indicate torsion
    \item Check the testicles regularly for lumps
    \item Infants with hydroceles are reviewed until they resolve
    \item Follow surgical advice after hydrocelectomy
    \item Report recurrence or new symptoms after treatment
    \item Do not assume any scrotal swelling is a hydrocele without assessment
\end{itemize}
