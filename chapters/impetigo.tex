\chapter{Impetigo (School Sores)}
\index{Impetigo (School Sores)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Impetigo, commonly known as school sores, is a highly contagious bacterial skin infection that most often affects young children. It is caused by bacteria, usually Staphylococcus aureus or Streptococcus pyogenes, entering through small breaks in the skin. Impetigo produces red sores that quickly burst, ooze, and form a golden or honey-coloured crust. It commonly appears around the nose and mouth but can occur anywhere on the body. Although usually mild and easily treated, impetigo is very contagious, spreads rapidly in schools, day care, and households, and can occasionally lead to more serious complications if left untreated.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Impetigo is one of the most common skin infections in children worldwide, affecting millions of children each year. It is most common in children aged 2--5 years, and rates peak in warm, humid climates and in crowded settings such as schools, day care centres, and sports teams. In many countries, impetigo is more common and more severe in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander communities, where overcrowding and limited access to healthcare contribute to high rates and complications. Recurrence is common, particularly in children with eczema, and the infection spreads readily through skin-to-skin contact and shared objects.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Impetigo is caused by bacteria colonising the skin and multiplying in the superficial layers (epidermis). Staphylococcus aureus is now the most common cause in many countries, often in the form of antibiotic-resistant strains (MRSA), while Streptococcus pyogenes causes a significant proportion, particularly the blistering (bullous) form and in community settings. The bacteria enter through minor trauma: scratches, insect bites, eczema, or cuts. The infection provokes a local inflammatory response producing the characteristic sores and crusts. It is spread by direct contact with infected skin or, less commonly, by contact with contaminated objects such as towels and toys, and people remain infectious while sores are weeping.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Red sores or blisters that burst and form a thick, golden-yellow or honey-coloured crust
    \item Sores most commonly around the nose and mouth, but also on the arms, legs, and nappy area
    \item The non-bullous form: small blisters that quickly crust over; the more common type
    \item The bullous form: larger, fluid-filled blisters that burst to leave a thin brown crust, mainly in infants
    \item Mild itching, but generally not painful
    \item Slightly swollen lymph nodes in the affected area in some cases
    \item Spread to other parts of the body through scratching
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other skin conditions can look like impetigo and should be distinguished from it.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Herpes simplex:} cold sores form grouped blisters, often recurrent in the same spot
    \item \textbf{Atopic eczema with infection:} eczema that is weeping and crusted can be secondarily infected
    \item \textbf{Fungal infections:} ringworm and candidiasis have different appearances
    \item \textbf{Contact dermatitis:} caused by irritants or allergens, with itching and scaling
    \item \textbf{Insect bites:} may become infected and crusted
    \item \textbf{Molluscum contagiosum:} pearly, dome-shaped lesions, not usually crusted
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical advice for any suspected impetigo, especially in children, because treatment speeds healing and reduces spread. See a doctor promptly if sores are spreading, painful, or not improving after a few days of treatment, if the child is unwell, or if there are signs of deeper infection.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever, chills, or a child who is drowsy or very unwell
    \item Rapidly spreading redness, warmth, or pain in the affected skin (possible cellulitis or sepsis)
    \item Bluish or very pale skin with fever
    \item Signs of serious complications such as difficulty breathing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical diagnosis:} the appearance of the sores is usually characteristic and treatment is started without testing
    \item \textbf{Skin swab:} taken when the infection is recurrent, severe, not responding to treatment, or to identify antibiotic resistance
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} rarely needed, but considered if deeper infection or sepsis is suspected
    \item \textbf{Review of contacts:} checking household members and playmates for signs of infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antiseptic cream:} mild, localised impetigo in adults can sometimes be treated with an antiseptic such as chlorhexidine
    \item \textbf{Topical antibiotics:} for localised infection, cream or ointment such as mupirocin or fusidic acid
    \item \textbf{Oral antibiotics:} for widespread, severe, or recurrent impetigo, or when topical treatment has failed
    \item \textbf{Choice guided by resistance:} in areas with MRSA, specific antibiotics may be needed
    \item \textbf{Skin care:} gentle washing with soap and water, and soaking crusts to remove them
    \item \textbf{Stopping spread:} covering sores, frequent hand washing, separate towels and bedding, and keeping children home from school or day care until sores are crusted and dry or treated for 24 hours
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Spread of infection to household members and other children
    \item Cellulitis or deeper skin infection
    \item Post-streptococcal complications: acute rheumatic fever, affecting the heart, and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, affecting the kidneys, can follow streptococcal skin infection
    \item Recurrent infection, particularly in children with eczema or poor hygiene conditions
    \item Scarring in some cases
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With appropriate treatment, impetigo usually clears within one to two weeks without scarring. Early treatment reduces spread within families and schools. Most children have no complications, and serious outcomes are rare with access to good primary care. Prompt treatment of streptococcal skin infection is important to reduce the very rare but serious risk of rheumatic fever and kidney disease, particularly in communities where these are more common.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wash hands frequently with soap and water, especially after touching infected skin
    \item Keep nails short and discourage scratching
    \item Cover sores with a clean dressing or clothing
    \item Wash towels, sheets, and clothing in hot water and do not share them
    \item Keep children home from school or day care until sores are dried and crusted, or after 24 hours of antibiotic treatment
    \item Treat underlying eczema promptly to prevent breaks in the skin
    \item Clean minor cuts, scratches, and insect bites immediately
    \item Complete the full course of prescribed antibiotics
\end{itemize}
