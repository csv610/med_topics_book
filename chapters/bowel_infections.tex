\chapter{Bowel Infections}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bowel infections, or gastroenteritis, are infections of the stomach and intestines that cause diarrhoea, vomiting, and abdominal cramps. They are caused by viruses, bacteria, or parasites that are swallowed in contaminated food or water or spread from person to person, usually via unwashed hands. The great majority of bowel infections are mild and resolve on their own within a few days, but they can be serious in infants, young children, older adults, and people with weakened immune systems. The principal danger is dehydration from the loss of fluids and electrolytes, so replacing fluids is the cornerstone of management. The terms gastroenteritis, stomach flu, and food poisoning are commonly used for the same range of infections.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Bowel infections are among the most common illnesses worldwide, and most people experience several over a lifetime. In Australia they are a leading reason for GP consultations and a major cause of outbreaks in childcare centres, schools, hospitals, and aged-care facilities. Children under five and older adults are most severely affected. Rotavirus was a major cause of severe childhood diarrhoea until the introduction of routine vaccination, which has dramatically reduced hospitalisations. Norovirus is now the leading cause of acute gastroenteritis and causes explosive outbreaks in communities, cruise ships, and institutions. Foodborne disease is commonly viral, while bacteria such as \textit{Campylobacter}, \textit{Salmonella}, and \textit{E. coli} cause a smaller proportion of cases but more severe illness.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viruses:} norovirus and rotavirus are the most common causes; they are highly contagious and survive on hands, surfaces, and food, spreading easily in households and institutions.
    \item \textbf{Bacteria:} \textit{Campylobacter}, \textit{Salmonella}, \textit{Shigella}, \textit{E. coli}, and \textit{Clostridium difficile} (often after antibiotics) cause more severe and sometimes bloody diarrhoea.
    \item \textbf{Parasites:} \textit{Giardia} and \textit{Cryptosporidium} cause prolonged diarrhoea, frequently after drinking untreated or contaminated water or swimming.
    \item \textbf{Contaminated food and water:} undercooked poultry, meat, and eggs, raw seafood, unpasteurised milk, unwashed produce, and unsafe drinking water are the main vehicles.
    \item \textbf{Person-to-person spread:} transmission occurs through hands after toilet use, nappy changing, or caring for someone who is ill, and via contaminated surfaces.
    \item \textbf{Mechanisms of disease:} some organisms damage the intestinal lining directly (invasive gastroenteritis), while others release toxins that make the gut secrete fluid (secretory diarrhoea); either way, the result is watery stool and fluid loss. The infection settles once the immune system clears the organism, usually within a few days.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms typically begin within hours to a few days of exposure and include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Watery diarrhoea, which may be frequent, urgent, and explosive
    \item Nausea and vomiting
    \item Abdominal cramps and pain
    \item Fever, headache, and muscle aches
    \item Loss of appetite and fatigue
    \item Blood or mucus in the stool, which points to bacterial infection
    \item Signs of dehydration with significant fluid loss: thirst, dark urine, dizziness, dry mouth, sunken eyes, and, in children, fewer wet nappies and drowsiness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Not every bout of diarrhoea is an infection, and other causes should be considered:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Irritable bowel syndrome:} including diarrhoea-predominant forms, which are chronic rather than acute.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory bowel disease:} Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis cause recurrent diarrhoea, blood, and pain.
    \item \textbf{Food intolerance:} lactose intolerance and other malabsorption can mimic infectious diarrhoea.
    \item \textbf{Medication side effects:} certain drugs, including antibiotics and metformin, cause diarrhoea.
    \item \textbf{Antibiotic-associated colitis:} \textit{Clostridium difficile} infection after antibiotic courses.
    \item \textbf{Appendicitis:} can begin with vomiting and central pain that later moves to the right lower abdomen.
    \item \textbf{Diverticulitis or bowel obstruction:} in older adults, abdominal pain with altered bowel habit may not be infectious.
    \item \textbf{Pregnancy:} nausea and vomiting of early pregnancy can be mistaken for gastroenteritis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most people with bowel infections recover at home with rest and fluids, but medical advice is needed if symptoms are severe, prolonged, or occur in a person at risk -- infants, older adults, pregnant women, and those with chronic illness or weakened immunity. Seek advice for blood in the stool, persistent high fever, or an inability to keep fluids down.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of severe dehydration: confusion, severe dizziness, little or no urine, or a very dry mouth
    \item A baby or child who is drowsy, has sunken eyes, or is not taking fluids
    \item Blood in the stool or black, tarry stools
    \item High, persistent fever, especially in young children, older adults, or immunocompromised people
    \item Inability to keep down any fluids for more than a day
    \item Severe, worsening abdominal pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Most cases are diagnosed on the history alone and need no tests:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} recent food, travel, contacts, and antibiotics, plus assessment of hydration, fever, and abdominal signs.
    \item \textbf{Stool tests:} samples are examined for bacteria, viruses, or parasites when illness is severe, prolonged, bloody, or part of an outbreak, so treatment and public health measures can be targeted.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} may check for dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, kidney function, or signs of infection when the person is unwell.
    \item \textbf{Monitoring urine output:} a practical and important measure of hydration, especially in children.
    \item \textbf{Notification:} infections such as \textit{Salmonella}, \textit{Shigella}, and \textit{Campylobacter} are notifiable to health authorities so sources can be investigated.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is mostly supportive, since most infections clear on their own:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fluid replacement:} the mainstay of care; sip water or, ideally, an oral rehydration solution, and use a commercial rehydration drink for children, giving small amounts frequently.
    \item \textbf{Continue feeding:} breastfed babies should keep breastfeeding; older children and adults can eat bland, easily digested foods as tolerated.
    \item \textbf{Anti-diarrhoeal medicines:} may be used briefly in mild adult cases, but are not suitable for children or when blood is present in the stool.
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} reserved for confirmed bacterial infection when indicated; they do not help viral gastroenteritis and can prolong some infections such as \textit{Salmonella}.
    \item \textbf{Antiemetics:} medicines to control vomiting may be prescribed for selected patients, particularly children.
    \item \textbf{Hospital care:} intravenous fluids for people who cannot drink enough or are severely dehydrated.
    \item \textbf{Infection control:} good hand hygiene, careful disinfection of surfaces, and staying home until symptoms settle to avoid spreading infection.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Most bowel infections resolve completely, but complications can occur, mainly from fluid loss:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dehydration and electrolyte imbalance, the most common serious complication
    \item Acute kidney injury from severe fluid depletion
    \item Reactive arthritis and persistent post-infectious irritable bowel symptoms after some bacterial infections
    \item Guillain--Barr\'e syndrome, a rare but serious nerve disorder that follows \textit{Campylobacter} infection
    \item Haemolytic uraemic syndrome, a rare kidney and blood complication of certain \textit{E. coli} infections, particularly in children
    \item Spread to others in households, childcare settings, and care homes
    \item Prolonged symptoms or an ongoing carrier state in some people
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most bowel infections resolve within a few days to a week without specific treatment. Viral gastroenteritis typically settles within two to four days, while bacterial and parasitic infections can last longer, sometimes two weeks or more. In healthy adults the outlook is excellent, and the main risk -- dehydration -- is greatest at the extremes of age. With prompt and adequate fluid replacement, nearly all children and adults recover fully, although energy and normal digestion may take a week or two to return. Hospital care and intravenous fluids are needed only for a small minority, and deaths are rare in otherwise healthy people.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wash hands thoroughly with soap and water after using the toilet, before eating, and after changing nappies or caring for someone who is ill.
    \item Cook meat, poultry, eggs, and seafood thoroughly, and keep raw and cooked foods separate.
    \item Wash fruit and vegetables, and drink safe, treated water, including when travelling.
    \item Refrigerate leftovers promptly and follow food storage and use-by rules.
    \item Ensure children are vaccinated against rotavirus.
    \item Stay home from work, school, or childcare until symptoms have settled.
    \item Avoid preparing food for others while unwell, and for 48 hours after symptoms stop.
    \item Start an oral rehydration solution early if vomiting or diarrhoea is heavy, especially in children.
\end{itemize}
