\chapter{Gonorrhoea}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Gonorrhoea is a common sexually transmitted infection caused by the bacterium \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae}. It infects the mucous membranes of the genital tract, rectum, and throat, and is spread through unprotected vaginal, anal, and oral sex. Many people, particularly women, have no symptoms and can unknowingly pass the infection to others. Untreated gonorrhoea can cause serious complications, including pelvic inflammatory disease, infertility, and ectopic pregnancy in women, and epididymitis in men. Gonorrhoea is readily cured with antibiotics, but the bacteria are developing resistance to many drugs, making prevention, testing, and complete treatment more important than ever.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Gonorrhoea is the second most common bacterial sexually transmitted infection in Australia, after chlamydia, and rates have been rising for over a decade. In Australia, more than 30,000 cases are reported each year, with the highest rates among young people aged 15--29, and particularly high and rising rates among men who have sex with men. Rates are also disproportionately high among Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people. Worldwide, the World Health Organization estimates over 80 million new cases each year. Untreated infection and repeat infections are common, and antibiotic-resistant gonorrhoea is a growing global threat.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Gonorrhoea is caused by \textit{Neisseria gonorrhoeae}, a bacterium that infects columnar epithelium.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} through vaginal, anal, or oral sex with an infected person; the bacterium is passed in genital, anal, and throat secretions
    \item \textbf{Infection sites:} the urethra and cervix in the genital tract, the rectum, and the throat
    \item \textbf{Incubation:} symptoms usually appear 2--14 days after exposure
    \item \textbf{Bacterial invasion:} the bacterium attaches to and invades mucosal cells, triggering inflammation and a purulent discharge
    \item \textbf{Asymptomatic infection:} many infections, especially in women and in the throat or rectum, produce no symptoms
    \item \textbf{Vertical transmission:} a baby can be infected during birth, causing eye infection (ophthalmia neonatorum)
    \item \textbf{Antibiotic resistance:} the bacterium has developed resistance to many antibiotic classes, including some cephalosporins
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms differ between men and women and by site of infection.

\begin{itemize}
    \item In men: a urethral discharge (often white, yellow, or green), burning on urination, and sometimes testicular pain
    \item In women: often no symptoms; when present, increased or altered vaginal discharge, burning on urination, and pain or bleeding between periods
    \item Rectal infection: discharge, itching, soreness, bleeding, or pain on passing stool; often asymptomatic
    \item Throat infection: usually no symptoms, occasionally a sore throat
    \item Eye infection: in newborns, red, swollen eyes with discharge within days of birth
    \item Disseminated infection: fever, rash, and joint pain when the bacteria spread in the blood (rare)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other infections cause similar symptoms and must be distinguished.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Chlamydia, which frequently coexists with gonorrhoea and causes similar discharge and complications
    \item Trichomoniasis, a parasitic STI causing discharge and itching
    \item Urinary tract infection, causing burning on urination
    \item Bacterial vaginosis and candidiasis (thrush) in women
    \item Non-specific urethritis in men from other causes
    \item Pelvic inflammatory disease from other bacteria
    \item Reactive arthritis, which can follow gonococcal infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone who is sexually active and at risk should have regular STI screening, and anyone with symptoms or a notified sexual contact should be tested promptly. Testing is simple and often free at sexual health clinics.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe abdominal or pelvic pain with fever in a woman, which may indicate pelvic inflammatory disease or ectopic pregnancy
    \item Painful, swollen joints with fever, which may indicate disseminated gonococcal infection
    \item A newborn with eye discharge or redness needs urgent review
    \item Signs of sepsis: high fever, confusion, or collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Gonorrhoea is diagnosed by detecting the bacterium at the site of infection.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{NAAT testing:} nucleic acid amplification tests detect gonorrhoea DNA in urine, vaginal, cervical, urethral, rectal, or throat swabs; this is the standard test
    \item \textbf{Culture:} swabs can be cultured to confirm the diagnosis and test antibiotic sensitivity, important because of resistance
    \item \textbf{Testing at multiple sites:} depending on sexual practices, tests may be taken from the genitals, rectum, and throat
    \item \textbf{Testing for other STIs:} chlamydia, syphilis, and HIV are commonly tested at the same time because infections coexist
    \item \textbf{Partner notification:} sexual partners are tested and treated to prevent reinfection
    \item \textbf{Follow-up testing:} a repeat test three months after treatment is recommended to check for reinfection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Gonorrhoea is treated with antibiotics, and complete treatment is essential.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} a single injection of ceftriaxone is the standard treatment, sometimes combined with oral azithromycin
    \item \textbf{Treatment of contacts:} sexual partners from the preceding two weeks are treated to prevent spread and reinfection
    \item \textbf{Avoiding sex during treatment:} sexual activity is avoided until treatment is complete and symptoms resolve, usually for about a week
    \item \textbf{Treatment in pregnancy:} safe antibiotics are available for pregnant women, and treatment prevents transmission to the baby
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} a test of cure and a repeat test at three months are recommended
    \item \textbf{Resistance:} because resistant strains exist, cases are monitored and treatment is guided by local guidelines
    \item \textbf{Notification:} gonorrhoea is a notifiable disease in Australia, and public health units support partner tracing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item In women: pelvic inflammatory disease, chronic pelvic pain, tubal scarring, infertility, and ectopic pregnancy
    \item In men: epididymitis, prostatitis, and rarely infertility
    \item Infection of the newborn's eyes (ophthalmia neonatorum), which can cause blindness if untreated
    \item Disseminated gonococcal infection, causing joint, skin, and rarely heart or brain infection
    \item Increased risk of acquiring and transmitting HIV
    \item Antibiotic-resistant infection that is harder to treat
    \item Psychological and relationship effects of diagnosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt diagnosis and complete antibiotic treatment, gonorrhoea is cured in almost all cases, and complications are prevented. The main risks are untreated infection, reinfection from untreated partners, and rising antibiotic resistance. Regular testing, especially for young people and those at higher risk, and partner notification greatly reduce the burden of disease. Women who are treated early preserve their fertility, and pregnant women who are treated do not pass the infection to their babies.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use condoms consistently and correctly for vaginal, anal, and oral sex
    \item Have regular STI screening if you are sexually active, especially if under 30 or at higher risk
    \item Get tested whenever you have new or multiple partners
    \item Seek testing promptly if you have symptoms or a notified contact
    \item Complete the full course of treatment and avoid sex until cleared
    \item Tell partners so they can be tested and treated
    \item Have a repeat test at three months, as reinfection is common
    \item Talk openly with sexual health services; they are confidential and often free
\end{itemize}
