\chapter{Iodine and Your Health}
\index{Iodine and Your Health}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Iodine is a mineral that the body needs to make thyroid hormones, which control metabolism, growth, and development. Most people get enough iodine from their diet, particularly from iodised salt, seafood, and bread made with iodised salt, and in many countries iodine is added to bread to protect against deficiency. Inadequate iodine is most dangerous in pregnancy and early childhood, when it can cause miscarriage, stillbirth, and permanent brain damage in the developing baby. In adults, deficiency causes goitre (an enlarged thyroid) and hypothyroidism. This chapter explains the roles of iodine, the signs of deficiency and excess, dietary sources, and supplementation needs, especially in pregnancy.

\section*{Epidemiology}
some countries were historically iodine-sufficient, but dietary changes in the 1990s led to a return of mild deficiency, prompting the mandatory iodisation of bread in 2009. This has improved population iodine status, though mild deficiency persists in some groups. Iodine deficiency is a major global problem, affecting around two billion people worldwide and being the most common preventable cause of intellectual disability. In many countries, the groups most at risk are pregnant and breastfeeding women, people who avoid iodised products, and those on restricted diets. New Zealand also iodises bread, and both countries monitor iodine status closely.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The thyroid gland uses iodine to build the hormones thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), which regulate the body's metabolic rate and are essential for brain development. When iodine intake falls, the thyroid works harder to capture what is available and enlarges, forming a goitre, while hormone production falls, causing hypothyroidism. The developing brain of a fetus and infant is uniquely vulnerable: thyroid hormones are critical for neuronal development, and deficiency during pregnancy and infancy causes permanent cognitive impairment, cretinism in its most severe form, and increased miscarriage and stillbirth. Excess iodine can also cause thyroid dysfunction, particularly in people with pre-existing thyroid disease, and can trigger thyrotoxicosis.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Iodine deficiency in adults: a goitre, an enlarged thyroid visible as a swelling in the neck
    \item Symptoms of hypothyroidism: fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, dry skin, and constipation
    \item In pregnancy: increased risk of miscarriage, stillbirth, and congenital abnormalities
    \item In children: impaired growth, developmental delay, and reduced intelligence
    \item In severe deficiency: cretinism, with intellectual disability, deafness, and growth failure
    \item Iodine excess: can cause thyroid overactivity, with anxiety, tremor, and palpitations
    \item Most mild deficiency causes no symptoms at all
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Thyroid enlargement and dysfunction have causes other than iodine status.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hashimoto's thyroiditis:} autoimmune hypothyroidism, the most common cause in many countries
    \item \textbf{Graves' disease:} autoimmune hyperthyroidism
    \item \textbf{Nodular thyroid disease and cancer:} thyroid nodules and malignancy
    \item \textbf{Other causes of goitre:} genetics, medicines, and thyroid inflammation
    \item \textbf{Subacute thyroiditis:} painful inflammation after viral infection
    \item \textbf{Other causes of fatigue and weight change:} anaemia, depression, and diabetes
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with a neck swelling, symptoms of thyroid dysfunction, or concerns about their diet should be assessed. Pregnant and breastfeeding women should discuss iodine supplementation with their doctor, as requirements increase. Anyone with known thyroid disease should avoid excess iodine, including from supplements and some foods. Assessment is also recommended for women planning pregnancy.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden difficulty breathing or swallowing, which may indicate a large goitre compressing the airway
    \item Rapid heart rate, agitation, fever, and confusion, which may indicate thyroid storm
    \item Severe neck swelling or pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Thyroid function tests:} TSH, free T4, and free T3 levels
    \item \textbf{Urinary iodine:} measurement of iodine intake in population studies and selected individuals
    \item \textbf{Thyroid antibodies:} to diagnose autoimmune thyroid disease
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} assessment of goitre and nodules
    \item \textbf{Thyroid scanning:} in selected cases to assess function
    \item \textbf{Dietary review:} assessment of iodine intake from food and supplements
    \item \textbf{Newborn screening:} congenital hypothyroidism is screened for in all local newborns
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dietary intake:} ensuring adequate iodine from iodised salt, seafood, and bread
    \item \textbf{Supplementation in pregnancy:} 150 micrograms of iodine daily during pregnancy and breastfeeding, as recommended
    \item \textbf{Treatment of deficiency:} iodine supplements or dietary change under medical supervision
    \item \textbf{Treatment of goitre and hypothyroidism:} thyroxine replacement where indicated
    \item \textbf{Avoidance of excess:} caution with kelp and high-dose supplements
    \item \textbf{Management of thyroid disease:} treatment of hyperthyroidism, nodules, and autoimmune disease
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular thyroid function testing where indicated
    \item \textbf{Public health measures:} salt iodisation and bread iodisation programs
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Goitre and its effects on the neck and airway
    \item Hypothyroidism and its wide-ranging effects
    \item In pregnancy: miscarriage, stillbirth, and congenital hypothyroidism
    \item Permanent intellectual disability from deficiency in early life
    \item Thyroid overactivity from excess iodine
    \item Interactions with existing thyroid disease and its treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With adequate iodine intake, thyroid function and development proceed normally, and population programs have effectively eliminated severe deficiency in many countries. Treatment of established deficiency and hypothyroidism with thyroxine restores health in most adults. In pregnancy, supplementation from early on prevents the fetal brain effects of deficiency. The most important message is preventive: adequate iodine for everyone, and mandatory supplementation in pregnancy and breastfeeding.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Use iodised salt in cooking, but in moderation
    \item Eat iodine-containing foods: seafood, fish, eggs, and dairy
    \item Rely on iodised bread products, which are fortified in many countries
    \item Take a 150 microgram iodine supplement daily during pregnancy and breastfeeding
    \item Discuss supplements before starting, especially with thyroid disease
    \item Avoid kelp and high-dose iodine supplements, which can cause harm
    \item Seek medical care for neck swellings or symptoms of thyroid dysfunction
    \item Breastfeeding women should continue iodine supplementation
\end{itemize}
