\chapter{Liver Transplant}
\index{Liver Transplant}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A liver transplant, or hepatic transplantation, is a major, life-saving surgical procedure that involves replacing a diseased, failing liver with a healthy whole or partial liver from a donor. The procedure is the definitive therapeutic intervention for patients with end-stage liver disease (decompensated cirrhosis), acute liver failure, or primary liver malignancies that meet strict transplant criteria. The surgery involves removing the recipient's diseased liver (hepatectomy) and implanting the donor organ in the normal anatomical position (orthotopic transplantation). Because the recipient's immune system will recognise the new liver as foreign, lifelong adherence to immunosuppressive therapy is mandatory to prevent graft rejection.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Liver transplantation is the second most common solid organ transplant procedure globally, surpassed only by kidney transplantation. In many countries, several hundred liver transplants are performed annually across a small network of highly specialised transplant units. The primary indications for transplantation in adults include decompensated cirrhosis (commonly secondary to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis [MASH], alcohol-related liver disease, or viral hepatitis) and hepatocellular carcinoma. In the paediatric population, the most common indication is biliary atresia, a congenital inflammatory disease of the bile ducts.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The need for a liver transplant arises when the liver's essential metabolic, synthetic, and filtration capacities are irreversibly compromised, or when a localised disease cannot be managed conservatively. Indications and their pathophysiology include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Decompensated cirrhosis:} Extensive, irreversible fibrosis and nodule formation that obstructs portal blood flow, leading to portal hypertension and synthetic failure (evidenced by intractable ascites, variceal bleeding, or hepatic encephalopathy).
    \item \textbf{Acute liver failure:} Rapid and massive hepatocyte necrosis (e.g.\ from paracetamol overdose, autoimmune hepatitis, or acute viral infection) causing severe coagulopathy and encephalopathy within weeks of symptom onset.
    \item \textbf{Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC):} Primary liver cancer arising in a cirrhotic liver, where transplantation is curative by removing both the malignancy and the diseased hepatic tissue (often guided by the Milan criteria).
    \item \textbf{Inborn errors of metabolism:} Inherited conditions (such as urea cycle disorders, Wilson's disease, or primary hyperoxaluria) where a genetic defect in the liver produces metabolites that damage other organs.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Prior to transplantation, candidates exhibit signs of advanced liver failure. Following the procedure, clinical features shift to those of postoperative recovery and graft monitoring:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-transplant features:} Profound jaundice, abdominal distension (tense ascites), cachexia, muscle wasting, peripheral oedema, spider naevi, and cognitive impairment (encephalopathy).
    \item \textbf{Immediate postoperative features:} Presence of a large abdominal incision (typically a bilateral subcostal or 'Mercedes-Benz' incision), multiple surgical drains, temporary pain, and transient biliary or vascular drainage.
    \item \textbf{Signs of graft function:} Rapid clearance of jaundice, normalisation of blood coagulation parameters, increased bile production, and improvement in mental status.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
In the post-transplant period, determining the cause of graft dysfunction or systemic symptoms is critical to prevent graft loss. Differential diagnoses include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute cellular rejection vs.\ biliary complications:} Distinguishing immune-mediated graft destruction (which requires high-dose corticosteroids) from anastomotic bile leaks or strictures (which require endoscopic or surgical intervention); both present with elevated transaminases and jaundice.
    \item \textbf{Hepatic artery thrombosis vs.\ portal vein thrombosis:} Acute thrombosis of the hepatic artery (a surgical emergency presenting with high fever, graft necrosis, and sepsis) vs.\ portal vein occlusion (presenting with sudden ascites and variceal congestion).
    \item \textbf{Immunosuppressant toxicity vs.\ viral recurrence:} Calcineurin inhibitor-induced nephrotoxicity or hepatotoxicity vs.\ reactivation or recurrence of Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, or cytomegalovirus (CMV) in the new graft.
    \item \textbf{Opportunistic infection vs.\ surgical site sepsis:} Immunosuppression-related infections (such as Pneumocystis jirovecii or fungal infections) vs.\ typical bacterial complications (pneumonia, wound infection).
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Liver transplant recipients require close lifelong surveillance. They must be educated to recognise early signs of infection, rejection, or drug toxicity.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A fever of 38 degrees Celsius or higher (or chills/rigors), which represents a medical emergency due to the high risk of rapid sepsis under immunosuppression.
    \item Shortness of breath, chest pain, or sudden severe swelling in one leg (suggesting pulmonary embolism or deep vein thrombosis).
    \item Vomiting blood (haematemesis) or passing black, tarry stools (melaena).
    \item Sudden, severe abdominal pain or distension.
    \item Acute confusion, extreme lethargy, or loss of consciousness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The transplant pathway involves a rigorous pre-operative assessment and precise post-operative monitoring:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-transplant evaluation:} Multi-system assessments including cardiopulmonary testing (echocardiography, coronary angiography), malignancy screening, infectious disease serology, and psychosocial evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Post-transplant liver function tests:} Daily or multiple-daily monitoring of ALT, AST, ALP, GGT, and bilirubin in the early postoperative phase to track graft performance.
    \item \textbf{Therapeutic drug monitoring:} Regular measurement of trough blood levels of immunosuppressant medications (such as tacrolimus, cyclosporine, or sirolimus) to keep levels within a narrow therapeutic window.
    \item \textbf{Doplex ultrasonography:} Performed immediately post-surgery and routinely thereafter to assess blood flow through the hepatic artery, portal vein, and hepatic veins.
    \item \textbf{Liver biopsy:} The definitive investigation used to diagnose and grade acute or chronic graft rejection, and to differentiate it from recurrence of primary disease.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is split into the complex surgical phase, early post-operative intensive care, and lifelong maintenance:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Surgical transplantation:} Removal of the recipient's liver, followed by vascular anastomoses of the suprahepatic and infrahepatic vena cava, portal vein, hepatic artery, and reconstruction of the bile duct (usually duct-to-duct or Roux-en-Y choledochojejunostomy).
    \item \textbf{Immunosuppressive regimen:} Initiation of triple-drug therapy (typically tacrolimus or cyclosporine, mycophenolate mofetil, and corticosteroids), which is gradually tapered to a lower-dose maintenance regimen over time.
    \item \textbf{Infection prophylaxis:} Prescribing anti-infective medications, including co-trimoxazole (to prevent Pneumocystis pneumonia), valganciclovir (to prevent CMV reactivation), and oral nystatin (to prevent oral thrush) during the period of intense immunosuppression.
    \item \textbf{Long-term health monitoring:} Screening for and managing the metabolic complications of chronic immunosuppression, such as arterial hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, post-transplant diabetes mellitus, and osteopenia.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The post-transplant course can be complicated by surgical, immunological, and systemic events:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Graft rejection:} Acute cellular rejection (occurring in up to 30\% of recipients, usually within the first few weeks, and highly treatable) or chronic ductopenic rejection (characterized by progressive bile duct loss).
    \item \textbf{Hepatic artery thrombosis:} A catastrophic early vascular complication that can lead to biliary necrosis, graft failure, and sepsis.
    \item \textbf{Biliary strictures and leaks:} Occurring at the site of the bile duct anastomosis, often requiring endoscopic stenting or surgical revision.
    \item \textbf{Severe opportunistic infections:} Reactivation of latent viruses (such as CMV or Epstein-Barr virus [EBV]) or fungal infections (such as Aspergillus).
    \item \textbf{Post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD):} An EBV-driven lymphoid proliferation that can lead to lymphoma, associated with high-dose immunosuppression.
    \item \textbf{Chronic kidney disease:} Primarily caused by the chronic nephrotoxic effects of calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus/cyclosporine).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for liver transplant recipients is highly favourable. Current one-year patient survival rates are approximately 85\% to 90\%, and five-year survival rates exceed 70\% to 75\%. Most recipients report a dramatic improvement in their quality of life, with a resolution of portal hypertension symptoms, a return of energy levels, and the ability to resume employment, travel, and normal physical activities. Long-term outcomes are heavily dependent on adherence to medication and the prevention of cardiovascular and metabolic diseases.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Transplant recipients must take an active role in safeguarding their health and the function of their new organ:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Strict medication adherence:} Taking immunosuppressive medications at the exact same times every day without fail, and never altering doses without transplant team approval.
    \item \textbf{Rigorous sun protection:} Applying broad-spectrum SPF 50+ sunscreen daily, wearing wide-brimmed hats, and avoiding peak sun hours, as immunosuppression dramatically increases the risk of skin cancers.
    \item \textbf{Infection control measures:} Practising frequent hand hygiene, avoiding close contact with individuals who have active infections, and staying up to date with inactivated vaccines (live vaccines are contraindicated).
    \item \textbf{Practice strict food safety:} Avoiding raw or undercooked meats, raw seafood (e.g.\ oysters), unpasteurised milk products, and soft cheeses to prevent foodborne infections like Listeria and Salmonella.
    \item \textbf{Avoid drug interactions:} Avoiding grapefruit, grapefruit juice, and herbal supplements (especially St John's Wort), which can interfere with calcineurin inhibitor metabolism and cause toxic or sub-therapeutic drug levels.
\end{itemize}
