\chapter{Vasculitis}
\index{Vasculitis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Vasculitis represents a heterogeneous group of uncommon disorders characterised by the inflammatory destruction of blood vessels. The term itself is derived from the Latin \textit{vasculum}, meaning a small vessel, combined with the suffix \textit{-itis}, indicating inflammation. When blood vessels become inflamed, their structural integrity is compromised, leading to a cascade of pathological changes: the vessel walls may thicken, narrowing the lumen and restricting blood flow; the walls may weaken, predisposing to the formation of aneurysms or vessel rupture leading to tissue haemorrhage; or the endothelial lining may become damaged, initiating thrombosis (blood clotting). The clinical consequences of these changes are profound, frequently resulting in tissue ischaemia, necrosis, and subsequent organ dysfunction or failure in the territories supplied by the affected vasculature.

To comprehend the clinical spectrum of vasculitis, it is crucial to appreciate the vast complexity of the human circulatory system, which spans arteries, arterioles, capillaries, venules, and veins. Vasculitis can affect any of these vessel types across virtually any organ system, meaning that it is not a single disease entity but rather a syndromic category comprising over twenty distinct disorders. To classify these conditions, clinicians and researchers rely on the size of the dominant vessels affected, a framework formalised by the Chapel Hill Consensus Conference (CHCC). Under this system, vasculitides are categorised primarily into large vessel vasculitis (such as giant cell arteritis and Takayasu arteritis), medium vessel vasculitis (such as polyarteritis nodosa and Kawasaki disease), and small vessel vasculitis (such as anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody [ANCA]-associated vasculitides and immune complex vasculitides), alongside categories for variable vessel vasculitis (such as Beh\c{c}et's disease) and single-organ vasculitis.

Understanding vasculitis is of paramount clinical significance. Because the presenting symptoms are often non-specific and overlap extensively with those of infectious, neoplastic, and other autoimmune conditions, diagnosis is frequently delayed. Delayed diagnosis can lead to irreversible, catastrophic organ damage, such as end-stage renal disease in patients with ANCA-associated vasculitis or permanent blindness in those with giant cell arteritis. Conversely, prompt clinical recognition and the initiation of targeted immunomodulatory therapy can successfully induce remission, preserve organ function, and dramatically improve patient survival. This chapter provides a comprehensive, authoritative reference on the epidemiology, aetiology, pathophysiology, clinical presentation, diagnostic approaches, and therapeutic strategies for the various forms of vasculitis.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of vasculitis is complex and varies significantly depending on the specific type of vasculitis, geographic location, ethnic background, age, and sex. Collectively, systemic vasculitides are classified as rare diseases, but their cumulative burden on healthcare systems is substantial due to their chronic nature, relapse potential, and risk of severe long-term morbidity.

Large vessel vasculitides exhibit distinct demographic profiles. Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA), also known as temporal arteritis, is the most common form of systemic vasculitis in adults. It occurs almost exclusively in individuals aged 50 and older, with the incidence peaking between 70 and 80 years of age. GCA is approximately two to three times more common in females than in males. It has a striking geographic and ethnic distribution, with the highest rates reported in populations of Northern European ancestry (especially Scandinavian countries and regions with high Scandinavian migration, where annual incidence can exceed 20 cases per 100,000 individuals over the age of 50). In contrast, Takayasu Arteritis (TA) is a rare large vessel vasculitis that primarily affects young women, typically presenting before the age of 40. It is most prevalent in East Asian populations, particularly in Japan, Korea, and China, as well as in Latin America and the Middle East. The female-to-male ratio in Takayasu arteritis can be as high as 9:1.

Medium vessel vasculitides also show unique patterns. Polyarteritis Nodosa (PAN) is a rare systemic necrotising vasculitis. Historically, a significant proportion of PAN cases were associated with chronic Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection. With the advent of widespread HBV vaccination, the incidence of HBV-associated PAN has plummeted in developed nations, leaving idiopathic PAN as the predominant form. It typically affects middle-aged and older adults, with a slight male predominance. Kawasaki Disease is an acute, self-limiting medium vessel vasculitis that predominantly affects infants and young children under the age of five. It is the leading cause of acquired heart disease in children in developed countries. The epidemiology shows a marked predisposition for East Asian children, particularly of Japanese heritage, where the annual incidence exceeds 300 cases per 100,000 children under five.

Small vessel vasculitides are broadly split into ANCA-associated vasculitides (AAV) and immune complex vasculitides. AAV, which includes Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (GPA), Microscopic Polyangiitis (MPA), and Eosinophilic Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (EGPA), typically presents in older adults (median age of onset between 60 and 70 years) and has an annual incidence of approximately 10 to 20 cases per million individuals in Western countries. GPA is more common in Northern Europe and in populations of European descent, whereas MPA is more frequently observed in Southern Europe and Japan. Among immune complex vasculitides, IgA Vasculitis (formerly Henoch-Sch\"{o}nlein Purpura) is the most common vasculitis in children, usually occurring between the ages of 3 and 15 years, often following an upper respiratory tract infection. It has an annual incidence of 10 to 20 cases per 100,000 children and is slightly more common in boys. Cryoglobulinaemic vasculitis, another immune complex form, is strongly associated with chronic Hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection, reflecting geographic variations in HCV prevalence.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The underlying causes and biological mechanisms of vasculitis are diverse and represent a complex interplay of genetic susceptibility, environmental triggers, and dysfunctional immune responses. In primary vasculitis, the disease occurs without an identifiable underlying cause. In secondary vasculitis, the inflammation is triggered by an external agent, an active infection, or another pre-existing systemic disease.

The pathophysiological pathways vary depending on the size and type of the affected blood vessels. The key mechanisms and contributors include:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Autoimmune Mechanisms and Autoantibodies}: In ANCA-associated vasculitis, the production of autoantibodies targeting specific proteins within neutrophil granules is central to the disease process. These antibodies target proteinase 3 (PR3-ANCA, typically associated with c-ANCA staining patterns) or myeloperoxidase (MPO-ANCA, associated with p-ANCA). When ANCA bind to these targets on the surface of primed neutrophils, it triggers an intense activation cascade. The activated neutrophils adhere to the vascular endothelium, migrate into the vessel wall, and undergo degranulation. This releases reactive oxygen species, proteolytic enzymes, and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), causing necrotising vascular damage and leukocyte infiltration.
\item \textbf{Immune Complex Deposition}: In conditions like IgA vasculitis, cryoglobulinaemic vasculitis, and urticarial vasculitis, the primary driver is the deposition of antigen-antibody complexes within the vessel walls. These complexes activate the classical complement pathway, recruiting neutrophils and macrophages. The subsequent release of inflammatory mediators damages the endothelial lining, leading to increased vascular permeability, microhaemorrhages, and thrombosis. In IgA vasculitis, abnormally glycosylated IgA1 molecules form circulating complexes that preferentially deposit in the small vessels of the skin, joints, kidneys, and gastrointestinal tract.
\item \textbf{T-Cell-Mediated and Granulomatous Inflammation}: Large vessel vasculitides, such as Giant Cell Arteritis and Takayasu Arteritis, are primarily mediated by cellular immunity. The process begins with the activation of dendritic cells within the adventitia of the vessel wall, likely in response to an unknown local antigen. These dendritic cells recruit and activate CD4+ T helper cells, particularly Th1 and Th17 subsets. The activated T cells secrete interferon-gamma and interleukin-17, which recruit macrophages. The macrophages merge to form multinucleated giant cells and release matrix metalloproteinases and reactive oxygen species, destroying the internal elastic lamina of the artery. This is followed by a dysfunctional healing response characterised by intimal hyperplasia, which narrows the arterial lumen.
\item \textbf{Infectious Triggers}: Infections can induce vasculitis through direct vascular invasion (such as fungal angiitis) or, more commonly, by triggering an aberrant immune response. The association between Hepatitis B virus and Polyarteritis Nodosa, and between Hepatitis C virus and mixed cryoglobulinaemia, are classic examples. Molecular mimicry, where viral or bacterial antigens resemble host endothelial proteins, is thought to play a role in Kawasaki disease and other forms of post-infectious vasculitis.
\item \textbf{Genetic Factors}: Genetic predisposition is evidenced by strong associations with specific Human Leucocyte Antigen (HLA) alleles. For example, GCA is linked to HLA-DRB1*04 alleles, Takayasu arteritis is associated with HLA-B*52, and Beh\c{c}et's disease is strongly associated with HLA-B*51. Genome-wide association studies have also identified non-HLA susceptibility loci, including genes regulating cytokine pathways and immune cell activation.
\item \textbf{Environmental and Drug Exposure}: Certain drugs can induce secondary vasculitis. Hydralazine, propylthiouracil, allopurinol, and minocycline are known to induce ANCA-associated vasculitis. Cocaine contaminated with levamisole, a veterinary deworming agent, is a well-recognised cause of a severe small vessel vasculitis syndrome. Environmental exposures, such as silica dust, have also been epidemiologically linked to an increased risk of developing GPA and MPA.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical presentation of vasculitis is notoriously diverse, reflecting the wide range of potential target organs and vessel sizes. Symptoms can be broadly categorised into general, constitutional features of systemic inflammation and localized, organ-specific manifestations resulting from tissue ischaemia, vessel stenosis, aneurysm formation, or vessel rupture.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Constitutional Symptoms}: A majority of patients with systemic vasculitis experience non-specific symptoms, which are often the initial presentation. These include persistent, low-grade or high-grade fever, profound fatigue, malaise, unexplained weight loss, night sweats, and generalised arthralgia (joint pain) or myalgia (muscle pain).
\item \textbf{Dermatological Manifestations}: Skin lesions are highly common, particularly in small vessel vasculitis. The classic finding is palpable purpura, which present as raised, non-blanching red or purple spots, typically on the lower extremities. Other skin signs include livedo reticularis (a purplish, net-like pattern on the skin), subcutaneous nodules (often seen in Polyarteritis Nodosa), painful skin ulcers, digital gangrene, and urticaria (hives) that persist for more than 24 hours.
\item \textbf{Neurological Manifestations}: Vasculitis can affect both the peripheral and central nervous systems. Peripheral neuropathy is common, classically presenting as mononeuritis multiplex. This condition involves the asymmetric inflammation of two or more unrelated peripheral nerves, leading to sudden sensory loss and motor weakness, such as foot drop or wrist drop. Central nervous system (CNS) vasculitis is rarer but severe, manifesting as severe headaches, cognitive decline, seizures, transient ischaemic attacks (TIAs), or ischaemic and haemorrhagic strokes.
\item \textbf{Renal Manifestations}: Kidney involvement is a major source of morbidity in small vessel vasculitides, particularly AAV and IgA vasculitis. It typically manifests as rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis. Because early renal vasculitis is asymptomatic, it is often detected only through urinalysis (revealing microscopic haematuria and proteinuria) and laboratory blood tests (revealing elevated serum creatinine levels). If left untreated, it can quickly progress to acute kidney injury and end-stage renal failure, requiring dialysis.
\item \textbf{Pulmonary Manifestations}: Lung involvement is characteristic of GPA, MPA, and EGPA. Symptoms include chronic cough, dyspnoea (shortness of breath), pleuritic chest pain, and haemoptysis (coughing up blood). Diffuse alveolar haemorrhage is a catastrophic, life-threatening manifestation where bleeding occurs directly into the alveoli, causing severe hypoxia. Pulmonary nodules, which can cavitate and mimic tuberculosis or lung cancer, are common in GPA. Patients with EGPA classically present with severe, steroid-dependent asthma and allergic rhinitis.
\item \textbf{Ophthalmic and Otorhinolaryngological (ENT) Manifestations}: Giant Cell Arteritis is famous for causing ocular ischaemia. The classic symptoms include a new onset of localized headache, scalp tenderness (e.g., when brushing hair), jaw claudication (pain in the jaw muscles when chewing), and sudden, painless monocular vision loss due to anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy. Visual loss can rapidly become permanent and bilateral if untreated. GPA frequently affects the upper respiratory tract, presenting as chronic sinusitis, nasal crusting, epistaxis (nosebleeds), otitis media, and a characteristic collapse of the nasal bridge known as ``saddle nose'' deformity. Scleritis, episcleritis, and uveitis are common inflammatory eye conditions across various vasculitides.
\item \textbf{Gastrointestinal Manifestations}: Inflammation of the mesenteric vessels can lead to bowel ischaemia. Patients present with postprandial abdominal pain (abdominal angina), nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, and gastrointestinal haemorrhage. In severe cases, bowel infarction, perforation, and peritonitis can occur, representing surgical emergencies.
\item \textbf{Cardiovascular Manifestations}: Large vessel vasculitis can cause limb claudication (fatigue or pain in the arms or legs during activity) and asymmetric blood pressures or absent pulses in the extremities. Aneurysmal dilatation of the aorta can lead to aortic dissection or rupture. Kawasaki disease is unique in its propensity to cause coronary artery aneurysms in children, which can lead to myocardial infarction or sudden death.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because vasculitis is a multi-system disorder with varied and often non-specific presentations, it is frequently a diagnosis of exclusion. Numerous clinical entities can mimic the clinical, laboratory, and radiographic features of vasculitis. Distinguishing vasculitis from these mimics is critical, as the treatment for vasculitis (high-dose immunosuppression) can be harmful or fatal if administered to a patient with a mimicking condition, such as an active infection.

The primary differential diagnoses include:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Systemic Infections}: Chronic or acute infections can mimic vasculitis. Infective endocarditis is a classic mimic, presenting with fever, microemboli, glomerulonephritis, and elevated inflammatory markers. Other infectious mimics include disseminated tuberculosis, syphilis, chronic viral hepatitis (which can also cause secondary vasculitis), fungal infections, and rickettsial diseases (such as Rocky Mountain spotted fever).
\item \textbf{Thromboembolic and Hypercoagulable States}: Disorders that cause vascular occlusion without vessel wall inflammation must be excluded. Antiphospholipid Syndrome (APS) can cause recurrent arterial and venous thromboses, leading to skin ulcers, livedo reticularis, and organ infarction. Cholesterol Embolisation Syndrome, which typically occurs after vascular procedures, causes renal failure, livedo reticularis, and ``blue toe'' syndrome, closely mimicking PAN or small vessel vasculitis.
\item \textbf{Non-Inflammatory Vasculopathies}: Fibromuscular Dysplasia (FMD) is a non-inflammatory, non-atherosclerotic vascular disease that causes stenosis, aneurysms, and dissections in medium-sized arteries, particularly the renal and carotid arteries, mimicking Takayasu arteritis. Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome (specifically the vascular type) can present with spontaneous arterial dissection and rupture. Segmental Arterial Mediolysis is another rare non-inflammatory vascular disease that mimics PAN.
\item \textbf{Malignancies}: Systemic lymphomas, leukaemia, and solid organ tumors can present with ``B-symptoms'' (fever, weight loss, night sweats), cutaneous lesions, and neuropathies that mimic vasculitis. Atrial Myxoma, a benign heart tumor, can shed fragments into the circulation, causing embolic strokes and peripheral vascular occlusions that simulate systemic vasculitis.
\item \textbf{Other Connective Tissue Diseases}: Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE), Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA), and Sj\"{o}gren's Syndrome can exhibit systemic inflammatory features and can themselves be complicated by secondary vasculitis (e.g., rheumatoid vasculitis or lupus nephritis), making it challenging to determine whether the vasculitis is primary or secondary.
\item \textbf{Drug-Induced Pseudovasculitis}: Certain medications and toxic substances can cause vascular damage or thrombosis without classical vasculitic pathology. Cocaine use can mimic GPA due to local nasal septal destruction and systemic vascular symptoms.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Vasculitis is a serious, potentially life-threatening medical condition that requires prompt professional evaluation and ongoing management by specialists, typically rheumatologists. Because the symptoms of vasculitis can overlap with common illnesses, recognizing the warning signs that warrant medical attention is vital.

Patients should consult a general practitioner or rheumatologist if they experience:
\begin{itemize}
\item Unexplained, persistent fever, fatigue, and unintended weight loss.
\item New, unexplained skin rashes, particularly raised, red, or purple spots that do not fade when pressed (palpable purpura), or painful, non-healing skin ulcers.
\item Numbness, tingling, burning pain, or sudden weakness in a hand, arm, foot, or leg.
\item Persistent sinus pain, nosebleeds, or nasal crusting that does not respond to standard treatments.
\item Chronic, unexplained joint or muscle pain, especially when accompanied by feeling generally unwell.
\end{itemize}

Certain symptoms of vasculitis indicate a medical emergency, representing acute organ threat or imminent vascular rupture. You must immediately call your local emergency services (such as \textbf{local emergency services} in many countries, \textbf{911} in the United States, \textbf{999} in the United Kingdom, or \textbf{112} in the European Union) or present to the nearest hospital emergency department if you or someone else experiences:
\begin{itemize}
\item Sudden, painless loss of vision in one or both eyes, or sudden double vision (highly suggestive of Giant Cell Arteritis).
\item Sudden, severe, and localized headache, especially when accompanied by pain in the jaw while chewing or tenderness of the scalp.
\item Difficulty breathing, severe chest pain, or coughing up blood (haemoptysis), which can indicate life-threatening bleeding into the lungs (alveolar haemorrhage).
\item Severe, sudden, and constant abdominal pain, which may indicate bowel ischaemia or infarction.
\item Signs of a stroke or transient ischaemic attack, including sudden weakness or numbness on one side of the body, difficulty speaking or understanding speech, confusion, or loss of balance.
\item Complete or near-complete cessation of urine output, or urine that appears visibly bloody or smoky.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of vasculitis requires a synthesised approach, combining clinical assessment, laboratory testing, advanced imaging, and, where possible, histopathological confirmation. Because treatment involves significant immunosuppression, establishing an accurate diagnosis and ruling out mimics is imperative.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Clinical Evaluation}: A detailed medical history and thorough physical examination are the foundations of diagnosis. The clinician will look for signs of multi-system involvement, evaluate peripheral pulses, measure blood pressure in both arms, and conduct a detailed neurological and dermatological exam.
\item \textbf{Laboratory Investigations}:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Inflammatory Markers}: The Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) and C-Reactive Protein (CRP) are typically elevated, reflecting systemic inflammation. While non-specific, they are useful for screening and monitoring disease activity.
  \item \textit{Full Blood Count (FBC)}: May reveal a normocytic, normochromic anaemia of chronic disease, leucocytosis (elevated white blood cells), and thrombocytosis (elevated platelets). Eosinophilia is a hallmark of EGPA.
  \item \textit{Renal Function and Urinalysis}: Serum creatinine and urea monitor kidney function. Urinalysis is crucial for detecting microscopic haematuria, proteinuria, and cellular casts (specifically red blood cell casts), which are indicative of active glomerulonephritis.
  \item \textit{Immunological Serology}: Testing for Anti-Neutrophil Cytoplasmic Antibodies (ANCA) is vital. Immunofluorescence identifies c-ANCA or p-ANCA patterns, which are confirmed by Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assays (ELISA) targeting PR3 or MPO. Antinuclear Antibodies (ANA) and Rheumatoid Factor (RF) are checked to screen for underlying connective tissue diseases. Complement levels (C3 and C4) may be reduced in immune complex vasculitides.
  \item \textit{Viral Serology}: Screening for Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, and HIV is necessary to rule out secondary infectious vasculitis.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Imaging Modalities}:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Ultrasonography}: High-resolution color Doppler ultrasound of the temporal arteries is increasingly used in GCA diagnosis, showing the classic ``halo sign'' (vessel wall oedema).
  \item \textit{Angiography}: Conventional catheter angiograms, CT angiography (CTA), and MR angiography (MRA) are used to visualise large and medium-sized vessels. They look for stenoses, occlusions, aneurysms, and vessel wall thickening. These are the primary diagnostic tools for Takayasu arteritis and PAN.
  \item \textit{Chest Imaging}: Chest X-ray and high-resolution chest CT scans are used to evaluate pulmonary involvement, revealing nodules, cavities, ground-glass opacities (indicative of haemorrhage), or airway thickening.
  \item \textit{Positron Emission Tomography (PET)}: 18F-FDG PET/CT scans are highly sensitive for detecting active inflammation in the vessel walls of the aorta and its major branches in large vessel vasculitis.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Tissue Biopsy}: The absolute gold standard for diagnosing vasculitis is a biopsy showing vascular inflammation.
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Temporal Artery Biopsy}: A surgical biopsy of the temporal artery is performed if GCA is suspected. A sample of at least 1.5 to 2 cm is required due to the presence of ``skip lesions'' (patchy inflammation).
  \item \textit{Renal Biopsy}: Performed in patients with suspected glomerulonephritis. It classically shows crescentic, necrotising glomerulonephritis with little or no immune deposition (pauci-immune) in AAV, or IgA deposition in IgA vasculitis.
  \item \textit{Skin Biopsy}: A punch biopsy of a fresh purpuric lesion is performed. Histopathology typically reveals leukocytoclastic vasculitis, characterized by neutrophil infiltration of the vessel walls, nuclear debris (karyorrhexis), and extravasated erythrocytes.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of vasculitis has evolved dramatically, transforming these once-fatal conditions into treatable chronic diseases. The primary goals of treatment are to achieve rapid control of inflammation (remission induction), prevent relapse (remission maintenance), minimize drug toxicity, and preserve organ function. Treatment strategies are tailored to the specific type, severity, and distribution of the vasculitis.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Pharmacological Therapy}:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Corticosteroids}: High-dose glucocorticoids (e.g., oral Prednisolone or intravenous Methylprednisolone pulses) are the first-line therapy for almost all forms of active systemic vasculitis. They act rapidly to suppress inflammation. However, due to significant long-term side effects, the dose is gradually tapered once the disease is controlled.
  \item \textit{Conventional Immunosuppressants}: Used as ``steroid-sparing'' agents. In severe, organ- or life-threatening vasculitis (such as GPA, MPA, or PAN), Cyclophosphamide is used for induction therapy. For less severe disease or for remission maintenance, Methotrexate, Azathioprine, or Mycophenolate Mofetil are preferred.
  \item \textit{Biologic Therapies}: Target specific pathways of the immune system. Rituximab (a monoclonal antibody that depletes B-cells) has revolutionized the treatment of ANCA-associated vasculitis and is equivalent to cyclophosphamide for induction and superior for maintenance. Tocilizumab (an interleukin-6 receptor antagonist) is highly effective for maintaining remission in GCA and Takayasu arteritis. Mepolizumab (an anti-IL-5 antibody) is approved for EGPA.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Procedures and Adjunctive Therapies}:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Plasma Exchange (Plasmapheresis)}: Involves removing the patient's blood plasma, separating out the pathogenic autoantibodies and immune complexes, and returning the blood cells suspended in donor plasma or albumin. It is considered in severe cases of AAV presenting with advanced renal failure or diffuse alveolar haemorrhage.
  \item \textit{Surgical Interventions}: In large vessel vasculitis (Takayasu arteritis, GCA) or PAN, chronic inflammation can cause severe arterial narrowing or aneurysms. Surgical bypass grafting, angioplasty, or stenting may be required once active inflammation is controlled. Aneurysm repair is indicated if there is a high risk of rupture.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Supportive Care and Prophylaxis}:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Osteoporosis Prophylaxis}: Patients on long-term corticosteroid therapy must receive calcium and vitamin D supplementation, often combined with bisphosphonates, to prevent steroid-induced osteoporosis.
  \item \textit{Pneumocystis Prophylaxis}: Patients receiving cyclophosphamide, rituximab, or high-dose steroids are at risk for \textit{Pneumocystis jirovecii} pneumonia (PJP). Prophylaxis with co-trimoxazole (trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole) is standard practice.
  \item \textit{Cardiovascular Risk Management}: Chronic inflammation increases the risk of accelerated atherosclerosis. Managing blood pressure, cholesterol, and diabetes (which can be worsened by steroids) is essential.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Complications in vasculitis can arise either directly from the damage caused by the vascular inflammatory process or as adverse effects of the potent immunosuppressive medications required to control the disease. Balancing these risks is one of the primary challenges of clinical management.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Disease-Related Complications}:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Permanent Organ Damage}: Chronic ischaemia can lead to irreversible tissue loss. Examples include end-stage renal disease (requiring long-term dialysis or transplantation), permanent blindness (in GCA), and cognitive deficits or motor deficits from stroke (in CNS vasculitis).
  \item \textit{Vascular Catastrophes}: Aneurysms induced by vasculitis can rupture, leading to fatal internal bleeding (e.g., abdominal aortic aneurysm rupture or cerebral aneurysm rupture). Arterial stenosis can cause critical limb ischaemia, gangrene, or bowel infarction.
  \item \textit{Pulmonary Destruction}: Repeated pulmonary haemorrhage or cavitary lesions can lead to chronic respiratory failure, bronchiectasis, and pulmonary hypertension.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Treatment-Related Complications}:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Severe Infections}: The suppression of the immune system renders patients highly vulnerable to bacterial, viral, and opportunistic fungal infections, which are a major cause of mortality during the induction phase.
  \item \textit{Corticosteroid Toxicity}: Long-term steroid use causes a wide range of side effects, including weight gain, Cushingoid features, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension, cataracts, glaucoma, osteoporosis, aseptic necrosis of bone (especially the femoral head), and mood disturbances or psychosis.
  \item \textit{Cytotoxic Drug Side Effects}: Cyclophosphamide carries risks of bone marrow suppression, haemorrhagic cystitis (bleeding from the bladder), bladder cancer, and permanent infertility in both men and women. Methotrexate can cause hepatotoxicity and pneumonitis.
  \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for patients with vasculitis has improved dramatically over the past few decades. Before the introduction of glucocorticoids and cytotoxic agents, systemic vasculitides like GPA and PAN were almost uniformly fatal, with a one-year survival rate of less than 15\%. Today, with modern immunomodulatory regimens, the 5-year survival rate for most forms of systemic vasculitis exceeds 80\% to 90\%.

However, vasculitis remains a chronic, relapsing condition. Relapses are common, occurring in up to 30\% to 50\% of patients with GCA and ANCA-associated vasculitis over time, requiring adjustment or re-initiation of immunosuppressive therapy. Achieving a state of drug-free remission is rare; many patients require long-term low-dose maintenance therapy.

Predictors of poorer outcomes and higher mortality include advanced age at diagnosis, severe renal impairment (high serum creatinine at presentation), cardiovascular involvement, and the presence of diffuse alveolar haemorrhage. The vasculitis damage index (VDI) is used by clinicians to track permanent damage accumulating over time, which correlates with long-term quality of life and survival.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
For the majority of primary vasculitides, there are no known ways to prevent the initial onset of the disease. However, active self-care, lifestyle modifications, and close collaboration with the healthcare team can prevent disease flares, minimize treatment-related complications, and improve overall quality of life.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Adherence to Medication}: Taking prescribed medications exactly as directed is the most critical factor in keeping vasculitis in remission. Patients must never abruptly stop taking corticosteroids, as this can trigger a life-threatening adrenal crisis or a severe disease relapse.
\item \textbf{Infection Prevention}: Due to immunosuppression, maintaining hygiene is vital. Frequent handwashing, avoiding close contact with sick individuals, and practicing food safety are important.
\item \textbf{Vaccination}: Keeping vaccinations up to date is crucial. Patients should receive the annual influenza vaccine, pneumococcal vaccine, and COVID-19 vaccines. Live vaccines (e.g., yellow fever, oral polio, MMR) are generally contraindicated while on therapeutic immunosuppression and should be administered prior to starting treatment if possible.
\item \textbf{Cardiovascular Health and Lifestyle}:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Smoking Cessation}: Smoking is a critical risk factor that accelerates vascular damage. In Thromboangiitis Obliterans (Buerger's disease), smoking cessation is the \textit{only} effective treatment to stop disease progression and prevent limb amputation.
  \item \textit{Diet and Exercise}: A heart-healthy, low-sodium diet helps manage blood pressure and steroid-induced fluid retention. Adequate calcium and vitamin D intake supports bone health. Regular, low-impact exercise (such as walking or swimming) helps maintain muscle mass, bone density, and joint flexibility.
  \item \textit{Weight Management}: Steroids increase appetite and promote weight gain; nutritional counseling can help manage these effects.
  \end{itemize}
\item \textbf{Self-Monitoring}: Patients should be taught to monitor their blood pressure and temperature at home. Patients with renal involvement may be advised to perform home urine dipsticks to check for blood or protein. Recognizing the early signs of a flare (e.g., return of fever, new rashes, joint pain) and contacting the rheumatologist immediately is essential.
\item \textbf{Psychosocial Support}: Living with a chronic, unpredictable illness like vasculitis can cause anxiety and depression. Joining support groups, talking to a mental health professional, and engaging with patient advocacy organizations can provide significant emotional benefit.
\end{itemize}
