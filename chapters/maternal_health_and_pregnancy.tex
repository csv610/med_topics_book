\chapter{Maternal health and pregnancy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Maternal health refers to the health of women during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period. It encompasses the provision of comprehensive prenatal, intrapartum, and postnatal care to ensure a safe and healthy pregnancy outcome for both mother and child. Optimising maternal health involves managing pre-existing medical conditions, preventing and treating pregnancy-induced complications, and providing nutritional, emotional, and social support.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Globally, maternal health is a critical public health indicator. While maternal mortality rates are low in developed countries like Australia, significant disparities exist, with Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander women and those living in remote areas experiencing higher rates of maternal morbidity and mortality. Major causes of maternal morbidity worldwide include haemorrhage, hypertension (preeclampsia), sepsis, and complications from unsafe abortion or pre-existing medical conditions.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Pregnancy induces profound physiological adaptations across all organ systems to support the developing foetus, which can unmask or worsen maternal health conditions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular adaptations:} Blood volume increases by 30--50\%, cardiac output rises, and systemic vascular resistance falls, placing extra stress on the heart.
    \item \textbf{Endocrine and metabolic changes:} Placental hormones (like human placental lactogen) induce relative insulin resistance, which can lead to gestational diabetes.
    \item \textbf{Immunological modulation:} The maternal immune system undergoes complex alterations to tolerate the semi-allogeneic foetus, increasing susceptibility to certain infections.
    \item \textbf{Hypercoagulability:} Increased production of clotting factors protects against haemorrhage during delivery but elevates the risk of venous thromboembolism.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Maternal health status is monitored through clinical signs and symptoms that occur throughout the stages of pregnancy:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Normal pregnancy changes:} Nausea and vomiting (morning sickness), breast tenderness, fatigue, urinary frequency, and physical changes like weight gain and abdominal enlargement.
    \item \textbf{Pathological signs:} Rapidly progressive swelling of the face and hands, severe headaches, visual disturbances (flashing lights), or epigastric pain (signs of preeclampsia).
    \item \textbf{Vaginal bleeding or fluid loss:} Bleeding of any amount, or a sudden gush or leaking of clear fluid from the vagina before the onset of labour.
    \item \textbf{Foetal movement changes:} A reduction in the frequency or strength of foetal movements after 20--24 weeks of gestation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Symptoms during pregnancy require careful clinical evaluation to distinguish normal physiological discomforts from serious medical conditions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gestational hypertension vs. Preeclampsia:} Elevated blood pressure after 20 weeks without (gestational hypertension) or with (preeclampsia) proteinuria or organ dysfunction.
    \item \textbf{Hyperemesis gravidarum vs. Simple morning sickness:} Severe, persistent vomiting causing dehydration, weight loss, and electrolyte imbalance.
    \item \textbf{Obstetric cholestasis vs. Normal skin itching:} Generalized itching, particularly on the palms and soles, without a rash, associated with elevated bile acids.
    \item \textbf{Gestational diabetes vs. Pre-existing diabetes:} Glucose intolerance first recognized during pregnancy compared to established type 1 or 2 diabetes.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Pregnant women should attend all scheduled antenatal visits with their obstetrician, GP, or midwife. They must contact their maternity hospital or healthcare provider immediately if they experience any warning signs of pregnancy complications.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heavy, bright red vaginal bleeding, with or without severe abdominal pain
    \item Sudden, severe abdominal pain or cramping that does not resolve
    \item A seizure or loss of consciousness (eclampsia)
    \item Severe shortness of breath, chest pain, or coughing up blood
    \item Severe headache, sudden swelling of the face/hands, or visual changes (flashing lights or blurriness)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Routine screening and diagnostic tests are performed throughout pregnancy to monitor maternal and foetal well-being:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood pressure monitoring:} Checked at every antenatal visit to screen for hypertensive disorders.
    \item \textbf{Urinalysis:} Dipstick testing of urine to screen for protein (a sign of preeclampsia) and asymptomatic bacteriuria.
    \item \textbf{Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT):} Conducted between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation to screen for gestational diabetes.
    \item \textbf{Full blood count and iron studies:} Performed to screen for maternal anaemia, which is common during pregnancy.
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound examinations:} Conducted at dating (11--13 weeks) and morphology (18--20 weeks) stages to assess foetal growth, anatomy, and placental location.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of maternal health requires a multidisciplinary approach combining medical care, lifestyle adjustments, and education:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antenatal care:} Regular physical check-ups, health education, and preparation for childbirth and parenting.
    \item \textbf{Nutritional supplementation:} Daily folic acid (at least 400 micrograms) pre-conceptionally and in the first trimester to prevent neural tube defects, and iodine supplementation.
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological management:} Safe antihypertensives (e.g., methyldopa or labetalol) for hypertension, or insulin/metformin for gestational diabetes when diet alone is insufficient.
    \item \textbf{Postpartum care:} Monitoring for postpartum haemorrhage, managing perineal or caesarean wound healing, supporting breastfeeding, and screening for postnatal depression.
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle modifications:} Maintaining a balanced diet, engaging in moderate exercise (like walking or prenatal yoga), and avoiding alcohol, smoking, and recreational drugs.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Preeclampsia and eclampsia, which can lead to maternal organ failure, seizures, or foetal growth restriction
    \item Postpartum haemorrhage, a leading cause of maternal mortality requiring urgent medical or surgical intervention
    \item Gestational diabetes, increasing the risk of macrosomia (large baby), birth trauma, and future type 2 diabetes
    \item Puerperal sepsis, a severe bacterial infection of the genital tract following childbirth
    \item Venous thromboembolism (deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism), which is a major direct cause of maternal death
    \item Postnatal depression and anxiety, affecting maternal-infant bonding and long-term family wellbeing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With high-quality antenatal and intrapartum care, the vast majority of pregnancies result in excellent outcomes for both mother and baby. Early identification of risk factors and prompt management of complications significantly reduce the risk of long-term health issues. However, women who experience complications like gestational diabetes or preeclampsia have a higher risk of developing cardiovascular disease or type 2 diabetes later in life, highlighting the need for long-term health monitoring.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend a pre-conception health check to optimise medical conditions and review medication safety before getting pregnant
    \item Take recommended supplements daily, including folic acid, iodine, and vitamin D or iron if prescribed
    \item Eat a healthy, balanced diet and practice food safety to avoid infections like listeriosis or toxoplasmosis (avoid raw meat, soft cheeses, and unpasteurised milk)
    \item Avoid all alcohol, tobacco, and illicit drugs, and consult a doctor before taking any over-the-counter medications
    \item Monitor foetal movements daily in the third trimester and report any reduction immediately
\end{itemize}
