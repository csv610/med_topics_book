\section*{Definition and Overview}
Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated response to infection. It can develop from infections of the lungs, urinary tract, abdomen, skin, or other sites, and it can affect people of any age. It is not diagnosed by one symptom or one blood test. The important warning is a suspected infection together with new or worsening problems in breathing, circulation, consciousness, or organ function.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Possible features include fever or unusually low temperature, shivering, fast breathing, breathlessness, fast heart rate, low blood pressure, confusion, extreme drowsiness, severe pain, clammy or mottled skin, very little urine, and a rapid overall deterioration. Infants, older adults, pregnant people, and people with weakened immunity may have less typical signs.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact your local emergency services immediately if a person with a possible infection is difficult to wake, confused, struggling to breathe, faint, blue or mottled, producing very little urine, or rapidly getting worse. Do not wait for a fever or for test results. Babies, older adults, pregnant people, and people with impaired immunity need urgent assessment when infection is suspected and they appear significantly unwell.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Clinicians assess the suspected source of infection, vital signs, mental state, urine output, and signs of organ dysfunction. Tests may include blood count, lactate, kidney and liver function, blood cultures, urine testing, imaging, and samples from the suspected infection site. Treatment should not be delayed while waiting for culture results when sepsis is suspected.

\section*{Management}
Sepsis usually requires hospital care. Management may include oxygen or breathing support, intravenous fluids when appropriate, antibiotics chosen for the suspected infection and local resistance patterns, source control such as drainage or removal of an infected device, blood-pressure support, kidney support, and close monitoring. Antibiotics should be reviewed and narrowed or stopped when test results and the clinical course show what is appropriate.

\section*{Complications}
Complications include shock, respiratory failure, kidney injury, clotting or bleeding problems, delirium, limb or tissue injury, and death. Recovery may take weeks or months, and some people develop persistent weakness, fatigue, concentration problems, anxiety, or post-intensive-care symptoms.

\section*{Prognosis and Self-Care}
Outcome depends on the infection, how quickly treatment begins, the organs affected, and the person's underlying health. After discharge, take medicines exactly as prescribed, attend follow-up, gradually rebuild activity, and seek review for recurrent fever, worsening breathlessness, confusion, fainting, reduced urine, or rapid deterioration.
