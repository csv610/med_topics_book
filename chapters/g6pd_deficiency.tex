\chapter{G6PD Deficiency}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency is an inherited condition in which the red blood cells lack an enzyme that protects them from damage caused by certain substances. It is the most common enzyme deficiency in humans, and it mainly affects males, because the gene is on the X chromosome. Most people with G6PD deficiency have no symptoms at all and live normally, but exposure to triggers such as fava beans (causing favism), certain medicines, or severe infection can cause the red blood cells to break down rapidly, producing acute haemolytic anaemia, with jaundice, dark urine, and fatigue. The condition cannot be cured, but it is entirely manageable by avoiding triggers. This chapter explains the condition, its triggers, and how it is managed.

\section*{Epidemiology}
G6PD deficiency affects an estimated 400--500 million people worldwide, with the highest rates in Africa, the Middle East, Asia, the Mediterranean, and in people of these ancestries, including Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Australians. Around 1 in 10 to 1 in 4 males in high-prevalence populations is affected. In Australia, newborn screening detects G6PD deficiency in at-risk infants. Because the gene is on the X chromosome, males are affected more often and more severely, while females are usually carriers with milder or no symptoms. Most affected people are never diagnosed because they have no symptoms.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The enzyme:} G6PD produces NADPH, a molecule that protects red blood cells from oxidative stress
    \item \textbf{The deficiency:} without enough G6PD, red cells are vulnerable to oxidant damage and break down prematurely (haemolysis)
    \item \textbf{Triggers:} fava beans, certain medicines (including some antibiotics, antimalarials, and painkillers such as dapsone, sulfonamides, and aspirin at high doses), infections, and severe illness
    \item \textbf{Favism:} the acute haemolysis caused by fava beans, most common in the Mediterranean form of the deficiency
    \item \textbf{Mechanism of haemolysis:} oxidant stress damages the red cell membrane, the cells are destroyed in the spleen, and the haemoglobin released causes jaundice and dark urine
    \item \textbf{Inheritance:} X-linked recessive; a son inherits it from his carrier mother, and a daughter is affected only if both parents carry the gene
    \item \textbf{Severity:} varies with the specific gene variant, from mild to severe
    \item \textbf{Neonatal jaundice:} newborns with the deficiency can develop significant jaundice
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item No symptoms at all between episodes, in most people
    \item Acute haemolysis hours to days after a trigger: sudden fatigue, pallor, dark tea- or cola-coloured urine, and jaundice
    \item Abdominal or back pain, nausea, and shortness of breath with significant anaemia
    \item A rapid heart rate and feeling faint with severe anaemia
    \item Neonatal jaundice, sometimes severe, in the first days of life
    \item In infants and children, episodes can be triggered by infections and medicines
    \item Chronic mild anaemia and a small risk of gallstones in some forms
    \item Complete recovery between episodes, once the trigger is removed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Haemolysis and jaundice have other causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Other red cell disorders, including hereditary spherocytosis and sickle cell disease
    \item Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia
    \item Liver disease causing jaundice
    \item Infections such as malaria in endemic regions
    \item Other causes of acute anaemia, including bleeding
    \item G6PD testing confirms the diagnosis and distinguishes it from these
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with known G6PD deficiency who develops dark urine, jaundice, pallor, or fatigue after a trigger should seek medical care promptly. Babies with prolonged jaundice should be tested.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe breathlessness, collapse, or a racing heart from severe anaemia
    \item Confusion or reduced responsiveness
    \item Passing very little or no urine, which may indicate kidney injury
    \item Any severe reaction in a child
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} falling haemoglobin, a raised reticulocyte count, and characteristic red cell changes on the blood film
    \item \textbf{Haemolysis markers:} raised bilirubin and LDH, and low haptoglobin
    \item \textbf{G6PD enzyme assay:} the diagnostic test, measuring enzyme activity; it is best done after recovery, because young red cells made during recovery can give falsely normal results
    \item \textbf{Genetic testing:} confirms the diagnosis and the variant, and supports family counselling
    \item \textbf{Urine tests:} for haemoglobin and kidney function
    \item \textbf{Newborn screening:} detects the deficiency in at-risk infants in Australia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Avoid triggers:} the cornerstone of management; avoid fava beans and the medicines on the avoidance list
    \item \textbf{Treat acute haemolysis:} hospital care with fluids, monitoring, and blood transfusion for severe anaemia
    \item \textbf{Treat infection promptly:} infection can trigger or worsen haemolysis
    \item \textbf{Neonatal jaundice:} phototherapy and, if severe, exchange transfusion under paediatric care
    \item \textbf{Medicines review:} every new medicine, including over-the-counter products, is checked against the avoidance list
    \item \textbf{Carry identification:} a medical alert card or bracelet listing the condition
    \item \textbf{Family testing:} test at-risk siblings and parents, and provide genetic counselling
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute haemolytic anaemia requiring transfusion
    \item Acute kidney injury from the breakdown products of haemolysis
    \item Severe neonatal jaundice, with a risk of brain injury if untreated
    \item Gallstones from chronic low-grade haemolysis
    \item Rarely, death from untreated severe haemolysis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
G6PD deficiency is compatible with a completely normal life when triggers are avoided. Between episodes, people with the deficiency are healthy, and even those who experience haemolysis recover fully once the trigger is removed. The main risks are the first episode before diagnosis and severe neonatal jaundice, both of which are prevented by screening and education. With an accurate diagnosis, a clear avoidance list, and medical alert identification, the condition causes little or no disruption to life.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Never eat fava beans if you have G6PD deficiency
    \item Carry the list of medicines to avoid and check every new prescription
    \item Tell every doctor, dentist, and pharmacist about the condition
    \item Wear a medical alert bracelet or carry a card
    \item Have family members tested
    \item Seek prompt medical care for dark urine, jaundice, or fatigue after any possible trigger
    \item Ensure newborn babies of at-risk families are screened
    \item Treat infections early and avoid self-medicating
\end{itemize}
