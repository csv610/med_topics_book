\chapter{Lateral internal sphincterotomy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Lateral internal sphincterotomy (LIS) is a surgical procedure primarily indicated for the treatment of chronic anal fissure that has failed to respond to conservative medical therapies. The procedure involves the division of the distal portion of the internal anal sphincter, which serves to reduce anal canal resting pressure, alleviate sphincter spasm, and restore local blood flow, thereby facilitating fissure healing. It is considered the gold standard surgical intervention for refractory chronic anal fissures due to its high success rates, although it carries a small but significant risk of minor faecal or flatus incontinence.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Anal fissures are common, with an estimated lifetime incidence of approximately 10\% in the general population, affecting males and females equally. While acute fissures often resolve with dietary modifications and topical agents, a subset progress to chronic status, typically defined by symptoms persisting beyond six to eight weeks. Lateral internal sphincterotomy is the most commonly performed surgical procedure for these refractory cases, particularly in patients aged 20 to 50 years. In Australia and globally, the procedure is highly effective, leading to complete fissure healing in over 90\% to 95\% of cases, representing a cornerstone of colorectal surgical practice.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The pathophysiology of chronic anal fissure is characterised by a self-perpetuating cycle of pain, sphincter spasm, and tissue ischaemia. Lateral internal sphincterotomy interrupts this cycle through targeted surgical division of the sphincter muscle.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sphincter hypertonicity and spasm:} Chronic anal fissures are associated with elevated resting anal canal pressure, driven by hyperactivity of the internal anal sphincter. This persistent spasm is a primary source of severe post-defecation pain.
    \item \textbf{Microvascular ischaemia:} The blood supply to the posterior midline of the anal canal—where the vast majority of fissures occur—is relatively deficient. Elevated resting pressure and sphincter spasm compress these small vessels, causing local tissue ischaemia that prevents the fissure from healing.
    \item \textbf{Mechanical trauma:} The initial mucosal tear is typically caused by the passage of hard, dry stools during constipation, or less commonly, by severe diarrhoea, which initiates the cycle of spasm and ischaemia.
    \item \textbf{Anatomic division:} During the procedure, dividing the distal internal anal sphincter reduces resting anal pressure by approximately 30\% to 40\%, which relieves the spasm, improves perfusion, and promotes mucosal healing.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Patients presenting with chronic anal fissures warranting lateral internal sphincterotomy typically exhibit characteristic clinical features that severely impact their quality of life.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Severe anal pain:} Sharp, tearing, or searing pain during defecation, which often persists as a throbbing ache for several hours after bowel movements.
    \item \textbf{Haematochezia:} Passage of bright red blood, typically noticed as a streak on the toilet paper, on the surface of the stool, or dripping into the toilet bowl.
    \item \textbf{Anal pruritus and discharge:} Persistent itching or irritation in the perianal area, sometimes accompanied by a minor mucous discharge due to chronic inflammation.
    \item \textbf{Sentinel skin tag (fibroma):} A small hypertrophied fold of skin at the distal end of the fissure, often accompanied by hypertrophied anal papillae at the proximal end, indicating the chronic nature of the lesion.
    \item \textbf{Visible mucosal tear:} A linear or canoe-shaped ulcer in the anal mucosa, most commonly located in the posterior midline (90\% of cases) or anterior midline (10\% of cases), with exposed fibres of the internal anal sphincter visible at the base in chronic cases.
    \item \textbf{Stool avoidance:} Severe fear of defecation (dyschezia) leading to voluntary withholding of stool, which secondarily worsens constipation and exacerbates the mechanical trauma during subsequent bowel movements.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Before proceeding to lateral internal sphincterotomy, it is vital to differentiate chronic anal fissure from other perianal diseases that present with similar symptoms but require distinct management strategies.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Haemorrhoids:} Characterised by painless bright red bleeding and prolapsing tissue, although thrombosed external haemorrhoids can cause acute, severe pain but lack a visible mucosal tear.
    \item \textbf{Crohn's disease:} May present with atypical, multiple, or painless anal fissures, often off the midline, accompanied by other gastrointestinal symptoms such as abdominal pain, weight loss, and chronic diarrhoea.
    \item \textbf{Anal cancer (Squamous cell carcinoma):} A persistent, non-healing ulcer with indurated edges, which may mimic a chronic fissure but requires biopsy for diagnosis.
    \item \textbf{Anorectal abscess or fistula:} Presents with throbbing pain that is constant rather than defecation-related, often accompanied by fever, perianal swelling, and purulent drainage.
    \item \textbf{Sexually transmitted infections (STIs):} Primary syphilis (chancre), herpes simplex virus (HSV), or chlamydia can cause painful anal ulcerations that mimic fissures.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should seek medical evaluation if they experience persistent anal pain, bleeding with bowel movements, or a non-healing sore in the perianal region.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Massive or uncontrolled rectal haemorrhage.
    \item Signs of systemic sepsis, such as high fever, chills, confusion, or rapid heart rate, especially if associated with worsening perianal pain and swelling.
    \item Severe, sudden abdominal pain, dizziness, or fainting due to blood loss.
\end{itemize}
For non-emergency concerns, consult a general practitioner or colorectal surgeon if there is persistent bleeding, worsening pain that does not respond to simple analgesia, or if you develop urinary retention or purulent perianal discharge post-surgery.

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The diagnosis of chronic anal fissure is primarily clinical, based on a characteristic history and visual inspection of the perianal area, with further investigations reserved for atypical presentations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visual inspection:} Gentle parting of the buttocks to inspect the anal verge, which reveals the linear tear and any associated sentinel skin tag without the need for invasive instrumentation.
    \item \textbf{Digital rectal examination (DRE):} Often deferred or performed gently under local anaesthesia during the acute phase due to severe pain and sphincter spasm, but useful to assess sphincter tone and rule out palpable masses.
    \item \textbf{Anoscopy or sigmoidoscopy:} Performed under sedation or local anaesthetic block if the patient cannot tolerate it, to rule out proximal mucosal pathology or inflammatory bowel disease.
    \item \textbf{Anal manometry:} Used in specialised centres to measure resting and squeeze anal canal pressures, helping to document sphincter hypertonia and guide surgical planning.
    \item \textbf{Endoanal ultrasound:} Sometimes utilised to evaluate the integrity of the internal and external sphincters, particularly in females with a history of obstetric trauma, to assess the risk of incontinence prior to sphincterotomy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The management of anal fissures begins with conservative medical therapy, with lateral internal sphincterotomy reserved for patients with chronic, refractory fissures that fail to heal.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conservative measures:} High-fibre diet, stool softeners, adequate hydration, and warm sitz baths to reduce sphincter spasm and facilitate easy passage of stool.
    \item \textbf{Topical vasodilators:} First-line medical therapy using topical 0.2\% glyceryl trinitrate (GTN) ointment or 2\% diltiazem cream, which relaxes the internal sphincter to improve local perfusion.
    \item \textbf{Botulinum toxin injection:} Injected directly into the internal sphincter to cause temporary muscle paralysis (lasting 2 to 3 months), serving as a chemical sphincterotomy with lower incontinence risks but higher recurrence rates.
    \item \textbf{Lateral internal sphincterotomy (LIS):} The definitive surgical procedure, performed either open or closed (microscopic/subcutaneous), where the distal internal sphincter is divided up to the level of the dentate line. This permanently reduces resting pressure and allows the fissure to heal.
    \item \textbf{Post-operative care:} Adequate analgesia, stool softeners, sitz baths, and avoiding strenuous activity to ensure proper wound healing and prevent constipation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Faecal or flatus incontinence:} The most significant risk, typically presenting as temporary minor incontinence to flatus or liquid stool in 5\% to 15\% of patients, with permanent major incontinence being rare (less than 1\% to 2\% under experienced hands).
    \item \textbf{Bleeding and haematoma:} Post-operative bleeding from the sphincterotomy site, which may require local pressure or, rarely, surgical haemostasis.
    \item \textbf{Perianal infection and abscess:} Infection of the surgical wound, potentially leading to a perianal abscess or fistula-in-ano, requiring drainage and antibiotic therapy.
    \item \textbf{Keyhole deformity:} A structural defect in the anal canal resulting from improper healing, which can cause minor stool leakage or soilage.
    \item \textbf{Urinary retention:} A common reflex complication in the immediate post-operative period due to pain and pelvic floor muscle spasm.
    \item \textbf{Fissure persistence or recurrence:} Occurring in less than 5\% of patients, often due to an incomplete sphincterotomy or persistent risk factors like severe constipation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Lateral internal sphincterotomy has excellent long-term outcomes, with success rates exceeding 90\% to 95\% in terms of complete fissure healing, typically achieved within 4 to 8 weeks post-surgery. Pain relief is often dramatic and immediate, with patients reporting a significant reduction in discomfort within the first few days of the procedure. While minor, transient incontinence is not uncommon in the early post-operative weeks, long-term patient satisfaction remains extremely high, and the risk of recurrence is very low when lifestyle modifications are maintained.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prevent constipation:} Consume a high-fibre diet rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and legumes to keep stools soft and bulky.
    \item \textbf{Maintain hydration:} Drink plenty of fluids, aiming for at least two litres of water daily, to prevent the formation of hard stools.
    \item \textbf{Exercise regularly:} Engage in moderate physical activity to stimulate regular bowel motility.
    \item \textbf{Avoid straining:} Do not strain during bowel movements or sit on the toilet for prolonged periods.
    \item \textbf{Prompt bowel habits:} Respond to the urge to defecate immediately rather than delaying.
    \item \textbf{Sitz baths:} Perform warm sitz baths for 10 to 15 minutes after bowel movements to soothe perianal tissues and reduce sphincter muscle spasm.
\end{itemize}
