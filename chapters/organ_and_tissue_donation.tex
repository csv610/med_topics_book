\chapter{Organ and Tissue Donation}
\index{Organ and Tissue Donation}

\medskip
\noindent\textit{This chapter is for general education. Donation rules and registration processes vary by country and should be checked with the relevant donation authority.}

\section*{What it is}
Organ and tissue donation allows a person to give organs or tissues for transplant after death. Some organs and tissues may also be donated by a living person in carefully selected circumstances. Donation can help people with life-threatening organ failure or serious tissue damage.

\section*{Consent and choice}
Consent systems differ. Learn the system where you live, record your decision through the available official process, and tell the people close to you what you want. A decision can usually be changed. Registration does not guarantee that donation will be possible: medical suitability, timing, consent requirements, and the needs of recipients are assessed at the time.

\section*{What may be donated}
Possible donations include kidneys, liver, heart, lungs, pancreas, intestine, corneas, skin, bone, heart valves, and blood vessels. The exact possibilities depend on the person’s health, the circumstances of death, and local law and clinical practice.

\section*{Living donation}
Living donation requires an informed, voluntary decision without pressure or payment where prohibited. Donors need independent assessment of medical and psychological risks, the opportunity to withdraw, and long-term follow-up. Never donate through an unregulated broker or accept unsafe promises of payment.

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone being assessed as a living donor should report new chest pain, breathing difficulty, severe pain, fever, fainting, heavy bleeding, or rapidly worsening symptoms to the clinical team or emergency service. After donation, follow the transplant team’s instructions for wound care and follow-up.

\section*{Questions and support}
Ask the official donation service or transplant team about consent, privacy, testing, costs, family involvement, religious or cultural concerns, and how donation affects funeral or memorial arrangements. Donation is voluntary; choosing not to donate does not reduce the quality of care a person receives.
