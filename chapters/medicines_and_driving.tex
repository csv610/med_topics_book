\chapter{Medicines and driving}
\index{Medicines and driving}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Medicines and driving refers to the clinical, regulatory, and public safety issues surrounding the use of pharmacological agents—including prescription, over-the-counter (OTC), and complementary medicines—that impair a patient's fitness to operate a motor vehicle. Driving is a complex psychomotor task that requires undivided attention, rapid reaction times, precise coordination, spatial awareness, and intact vision. Many commonly used medications affect the central nervous system (CNS) or cardiovascular system, causing side effects such as drowsiness, dizziness, cognitive slowing, blurred vision, and impaired coordination. Medical practitioners and pharmacists have a dual ethical and legal responsibility to assess driving fitness, counsel patients on medication-related impairment, and advise them on how to manage these risks safely.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Medication-related driving impairment is a major contributor to transport-related morbidity and mortality. In many countries, drugs other than alcohol are detected in approximately 20\% to 30\% of drivers involved in fatal motor vehicle accidents. The classes of medicines most frequently associated with road traffic crashes include benzodiazepines, opioid analgesics, sedating antihistamines, and certain antidepressants. The risk of impairment is particularly high in the elderly population due to age-related changes in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics (increased CNS sensitivity), as well as a high prevalence of polypharmacy (taking five or more daily medications).

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The physiological mechanisms by which medications impair driving vary by drug class, primarily targeting neurotransmitters, receptor pathways, or systemic perfusion.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs:} Agents such as diazepam, temazepam, and zolpidem act as gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptor agonists. They depress the central nervous system, causing sedation, muscle relaxation, prolonged reaction times, and impaired tracking performance.
    \item \textbf{Opioid analgesics:} Morphine, oxycodone, fentanyl, tramadol, and codeine bind to mu-opioid receptors in the brain. They cause somnolence, cognitive slowing, miosis (constricted pupils affecting low-light vision), and delayed decision-making.
    \item \textbf{First-generation antihistamines:} Medications like promethazine, pheniramine, and diphenhydramine cross the blood-brain barrier and block central H1 receptors, causing marked drowsiness, reduced alertness, and impaired coordination.
    \item \textbf{Antidepressants and antipsychotics:} Tricyclic antidepressants (such as amitriptyline) and atypical antipsychotics (such as quetiapine and olanzapine) block histamine H1, alpha-1 adrenergic, and muscarinic receptors, leading to sedation, blurred vision, and orthostatic hypotension.
    \item \textbf{Antidiabetic medications:} Insulin and sulfonylureas (e.g., gliclazide) carry a high risk of inducing hypoglycaemia. A drop in blood glucose deprives the brain of energy, causing sudden confusion, visual disturbances, trembling, and loss of consciousness while driving.
    \item \textbf{Cardiovascular agents:} Antihypertensives, particularly beta-blockers, can cause fatigue, bradycardia, dizziness, and syncope due to reduced cerebral perfusion.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Patients experiencing medication-induced driving impairment may display a range of cognitive, visual, and motor symptoms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CNS depression:} Drowsiness, difficulty maintaining alertness, frequent yawning, microsleeps, and feeling ``foggy'' or detached.
    \item \textbf{Psychomotor slowing:} Delayed reaction times, difficulty responding to sudden traffic changes, and slow braking response.
    \item \textbf{Cognitive impairment:} Poor concentration, divided-attention deficits, difficulty estimating distance or speed, and impaired judgment.
    \item \textbf{Visual disturbances:} Blurred vision, double vision, and difficulty adapting to glare or low-light conditions.
    \item \textbf{Physical instability:} Dizziness, lightheadedness, loss of coordination, tremors, and muscle weakness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When evaluating a patient with apparent driving impairment, clinicians must differentiate medication side effects from other contributing factors.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fatigue and sleep disorders:} Chronic sleep deprivation, shift work, or obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) cause daytime hypersomnolence and impaired reaction times.
    \item \textbf{Alcohol or illicit drug intoxication:} Ethanol, cannabis, MDMA, or amphetamines cause acute cognitive and motor impairment, often synergising with prescribed medicines.
    \item \textbf{Primary neurological conditions:} Early-stage dementia, Parkinson's disease, epilepsy, or previous cerebrovascular accidents (strokes) directly impair cognitive and motor skills required for driving.
    \item \textbf{Age-related functional decline:} Natural declines in visual acuity, peripheral vision, joint mobility, and cognitive processing speed.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should consult their general practitioner or pharmacist if they feel unsafe or notice changes in their driving ability after starting a new medication.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A motor vehicle accident resulting in injury, severe property damage, or entrapment.
    \item Sudden loss of consciousness, collapse, or seizure while driving.
    \item Severe confusion, disorientation, or acute hypoglycaemic crisis (e.g., diaphoresis, confusion, and loss of motor control) in a driver.
    \item Accidental or intentional overdose of sedating medications.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The assessment of a patient's fitness to drive is guided by regulatory frameworks and clinical testing.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Austroads Guidelines:} In many countries, medical practitioners assess driving capacity using the ``Assessing Fitness to Drive'' national standards, which classify drivers as private or commercial and define medical criteria for licensing.
    \item \textbf{Comprehensive medication review:} A systematic evaluation of all prescribed, OTC, and complementary medicines to identify high-risk drugs, drug-drug interactions, and polypharmacy.
    \item \textbf{Cognitive and neurological testing:} Screening tools such as the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) or Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) to assess attention, memory, and executive function.
    \item \textbf{Visual assessment:} Visual acuity testing using a Snellen chart, and visual field testing to ensure compliance with licensing standards.
    \item \textbf{On-road driving assessment:} Referral to an occupational therapy driving assessor (OTDA) for an objective, functional evaluation of driving skills under real-world conditions.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management strategies focus on selecting safer therapeutic options, educating patients, and modifying driving behaviours.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pharmacological optimization:} Switch to non-sedating alternatives where possible (e.g., using second-generation non-sedating antihistamines like loratadine or fexofenadine). Titrate doses slowly to minimise acute side effects.
    \item \textbf{Medication timing:} Advice patients to take sedating medications at night or to coordinate driving times with periods when blood drug concentrations are at their lowest.
    \item \textbf{Restricted driving periods:} Instruct patients not to drive during the initiation of a high-risk medication, after a dosage increase, or when experiencing any symptoms of drowsiness or dizziness.
    \item \textbf{Patient education and labelling:} Dispense medications with standard cautionary labels (e.g., Pharmacist Label 1: ``This medicine may cause drowsiness and may increase the effects of alcohol. If affected, do not drive a motor vehicle or operate machinery'').
    \item \textbf{Alternative transportation planning:} Assist patients in organizing public transport, community transport services, or relying on family and friends for mobility when they are temporarily or permanently unfit to drive.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Continuing to drive while impaired by medications can lead to severe personal, legal, and social consequences.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Motor vehicle crashes:} The most critical risk, resulting in severe physical trauma, permanent disability, or death for the driver, passengers, and other road users.
    \item \textbf{Legal liabilities:} Driving while impaired by legal medications can result in prosecution, heavy fines, licence suspension, or imprisonment if the driver is found to be driving under the influence (DUI).
    \item \textbf{Insurance invalidation:} Car insurance policies may be voided if a crash occurs while driving against medical advice or while impaired by medications.
    \item \textbf{Loss of independence and isolation:} The psychological impact of losing one's driver's licence, which can lead to social withdrawal, depression, and difficulty accessing community services.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for patients requiring medications that affect driving is generally good, provided they receive appropriate guidance. For many medications, initial side effects such as mild drowsiness or dizziness improve over several days or weeks as the body develops pharmacological tolerance, allowing safe driving to resume. For chronic conditions requiring long-term sedating therapy, modifying driving habits (e.g., driving only short distances, during daylight hours, and in familiar areas) or transitioning to alternative transport allows patients to maintain quality of life and community access.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Patients can take several practical steps to ensure they remain safe on the road while taking medications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ask the question:} When prescribed a new medicine or buying an over-the-counter product, always ask: ``Will this medicine affect my ability to drive?''
    \item \textbf{Read all labels:} Check medicine packaging for warning symbols, particularly yellow or red warning labels indicating drowsiness risks.
    \item \textbf{Never mix with alcohol:} Avoid alcohol entirely when taking sedating medications, as alcohol synergises with these drugs, causing extreme, unpredictable impairment.
    \item \textbf{Avoid driving when starting new drugs:} Refrain from driving for the first few days after starting a new CNS-acting medication or changing the dosage.
    \item \textbf{Monitor for warning signs:} Stop driving immediately if you feel sleepy, lightheaded, lose concentration, or find yourself drifting out of your lane.
\end{itemize}
