\section*{Definition and scope}
Sunscreen is a topical preparation applied to the skin that protects it from ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the sun. UV radiation is invisible but biologically powerful, and in many countries it is the leading preventable cause of skin cancer and of much of the skin ageing that people notice over time. Sunscreen works either by absorbing UV energy before it reaches the living layers of the skin, or by reflecting and scattering it away from the surface. It is available in many forms---lotions, creams, gels, sprays, sticks, and roll-ons---and is a normal part of daily life for most people rather than a medicine. This chapter is a practical reference for anyone who wants to understand sunscreen: how it works, how to choose a suitable product, how to apply it properly, and what its limits are. It also explains where sunscreen fits in the broader sun-safe approach recommended by local health authorities, and when a doctor should be consulted. The aim is to help readers use sunscreen confidently and effectively as part of a balanced sun-protection routine that also includes clothing, hats, shade, and avoiding the sun during the hottest part of the day. Sunscreen is safe for almost everyone, including babies over six months of age, children, pregnant women, and people with sensitive skin, although the product chosen and the way it is applied may need to be adapted to skin type, age, and individual circumstances. Because local summers are characterised by very high UV levels, sun protection is not only a beach or pool activity: it is relevant on the school run, in the garden, at sport, and even on cloudy days.

\section*{Benefits and purpose}
The central purpose of sunscreen is prevention. It reduces the amount of UV radiation reaching the skin, which in turn reduces the risk of sunburn, skin cancer, and premature skin ageing. some countries have one of the highest rates of skin cancer in the world, and exposure to UV radiation, particularly in childhood, is the strongest modifiable risk factor. Regular sunscreen use lowers the risk of developing melanoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and basal cell carcinoma, the three main types of skin cancer that people commonly experience. The benefit is cumulative: every sunburn adds to lifetime risk, so protecting the skin every day, even in winter, is more effective than protecting it only on holidays. Sunscreen also prevents the visible effects of UV exposure such as wrinkles, sagging, freckling, and uneven pigment, which many people value regardless of their cancer risk. A further benefit is consistency: unlike clothing, which covers only some of the skin, sunscreen can be applied to every exposed area, including the ears, the back of the neck, the tops of the feet, and the hands, which are common sites for skin cancer. For people with lighter skin, a history of sunburn, many moles, a family history of melanoma, or a suppressed immune system, sunscreen is an especially important part of daily care. The benefits are also visible in the short term: a person who burns easily will notice that consistent sunscreen use prevents the pain, peeling, and sleep disruption of sunburn, and holidaymakers who reapply properly come home without the uncomfortable "burn line" that marks missed patches. It is worth remembering that protection in childhood is particularly powerful, because skin that is well protected in the early years carries a lower lifetime risk of melanoma, which is why schools and childcare centres across the affected region have "no hat, no play" policies. Sunscreen is best understood as one layer of protection rather than a complete solution. It is most effective when combined with sun-protective clothing, a broad-brimmed hat, sunglasses, and time in the shade, a combination that local cancer agencies promote with the well-known message "slip, slop, slap, seek, slide".

\section*{How it works}
Sunlight contains different types of UV radiation, chiefly UVA and UVB. UVB is the main cause of sunburn and plays a central role in the development of skin cancer; UVA penetrates deeper into the skin and is the main driver of skin ageing, and it also contributes to cancer risk. Sunscreen products are rated by their sun protection factor (SPF), which measures protection against UVB. An SPF of 50 filters about 98 per cent of UVB rays when applied correctly, while SPF 30 filters about 97 per cent---the difference is small, which is why both are acceptable, although local guidelines recommend SPF 50 or higher as the first choice. "Broad spectrum" on the label means the product also protects against UVA. A product's performance depends heavily on how it is applied, and most people apply far less than the amount tested in the laboratory. Two broad types of sunscreen exist. Chemical (or organic) sunscreens contain compounds that absorb UV energy and release it as heat; they are often cosmetically light and rub in easily. Physical (or mineral) sunscreens contain particles such as zinc oxide or titanium dioxide that sit on the surface of the skin and reflect and scatter UV; they are a good choice for sensitive skin and for children, though some find them thicker or whiter in appearance. Modern "water-resistant" products retain much of their protection after swimming or sweating, but no sunscreen is fully waterproof, and reapplication is always required. It is worth checking the label for the TGA registration number, which confirms the product has met local standards for safety and sun protection.

\section*{What to expect}
Using sunscreen should become a routine rather than an occasional event. Apply it to all exposed skin about fifteen to thirty minutes before going outside, so it has time to dry and bind to the skin, then reapply every two hours, and immediately after swimming, towelling, or heavy sweating. A generous amount is required: the local "teaspoon rule" recommends about one teaspoon for the head, neck, and each arm, and two teaspoons for the trunk, and each leg, which adds up to about 35 to 40 millilitres for a full body application on an adult. People often notice that a sunscreen feels greasy, leaves a white cast, or stings the eyes, and switching products can improve comfort; in general, lighter gel and fluid formulations suit oily skin, while cream and stick formulations suit dry skin and small areas such as the face. Children's sunscreens are usually fragrance-free and gentler, but the same rules of generous application and reapplication apply to them. Daily moisturisers and cosmetics with an SPF are convenient but rarely applied in the volume needed for full protection, so they are best treated as a bonus rather than a replacement for sunscreen. Sunscreen has a shelf life and should be replaced if it has passed its expiry date, smells unusual, or has separated or changed texture. In many countries, the ultraviolet index is high for much of the year, and most health authorities advise checking the daily UV index and treating sun protection as necessary whenever the index reaches three or higher, which in many parts of some countries are most days of the year.

\section*{When to seek professional advice}
Sunscreen itself rarely requires medical advice, but a GP or dermatologist should be consulted if a new mole or skin lesion appears, an existing mole changes in size, shape, colour, or sensation, or any sore fails to heal within a few weeks. These features warrant a proper skin check regardless of sunscreen use. A pharmacist or doctor can also help if a particular product causes repeated irritation, redness, itching, or a rash, which may indicate a contact allergy to an ingredient, and alternative formulations, including physical sunscreens, are usually available. People with a personal or strong family history of skin cancer, many moles, or fair skin that burns easily should ask their GP about regular skin checks, and those with a suppressed immune system or a history of organ transplantation have an especially high risk and deserve individualised advice. Parents and carers should ask their child health nurse or GP if unsure about the appropriate products for babies, remembering that babies under six months are best kept out of direct sun and protected with clothing and shade rather than relying on sunscreen. There is no medical emergency caused by sunscreen itself; however, anaphylaxis to a sunscreen ingredient is extremely rare, and \textbf{contact local emergency services} if, after applying a product, someone develops sudden difficulty breathing, swelling of the lips or face, widespread hives, or collapse.

\section*{Risks and cautions}
Sunscreen is safe for the vast majority of people, but a few cautions are worth noting. Contact dermatitis is uncommon but real, and usually responds to changing to a fragrance-free or physical sunscreen. Sprays should not be inhaled, and care should be taken near the eyes and mouth. No sunscreen blocks all UV, so relying on it alone, especially with an insufficient amount or without reapplication, gives a false sense of security, and sunburn can still occur. There is a widely discussed but minor concern that regular sunscreen use can lower vitamin D levels, since the body makes vitamin D in the skin in response to UV. In practice, people rarely achieve the degree of protection tested in laboratories, and most people maintain adequate vitamin D through normal incidental exposure even with daily sunscreen; for those at risk of deficiency, a blood test and sensible supplementation are safer strategies than intentional burning. Some sunscreen ingredients have been linked to environmental effects on coral reefs, and although the science is still evolving, using mineral-based products, choosing reef-safe brands, and wearing UV-protective clothing when snorkelling are simple ways to reduce environmental impact. Aerosol cans should be kept away from heat and never disposed of in household recycling. Finally, expired or degraded sunscreen loses effectiveness and should be discarded, since it offers little protection while giving a false sense of safety.

\section*{Tips and practical advice}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Choose a broad-spectrum sunscreen with SPF 50 or higher that is labelled water-resistant, and check for the TGA registration number on the pack
    \item Apply sunscreen to all exposed skin fifteen to thirty minutes before going outside, before dressing if possible, so that no patches are missed
    \item Use generous amounts: about one teaspoon for the face, neck, and each arm, and two teaspoons for the trunk, and each leg
    \item Reapply every two hours and immediately after swimming, towelling, or heavy sweating, even on overcast days, since UV is not blocked by cloud
    \item Combine sunscreen with sun-protective clothing, a broad-brimmed hat, sunglasses, and time in the shade, especially between 10 am and 4 pm when UV is highest
    \item Protect the easy-to-forget areas: ears, back of the neck, hair parting, tops of the feet, and the backs of the hands
    \item Use physical (mineral) sunscreens for babies, children, and people with sensitive skin, and keep babies under six months out of direct sun entirely
    \item Keep sunscreen out of heat, check the expiry date, and replace any tube that has changed colour, smell, or texture
\end{itemize}
