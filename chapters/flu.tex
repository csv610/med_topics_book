\chapter{Influenza (Flu)}
\index{Influenza (Flu)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Influenza, commonly called the flu, is a contagious respiratory illness caused by influenza viruses that infect the nose, throat, and lungs. It is much more than a bad cold: the flu comes on suddenly with fever, muscle aches, headache, and a dry cough, and it can cause serious complications, including pneumonia, especially in young children, older adults, pregnant women, and people with chronic conditions. Influenza spreads easily through coughs, sneezes, and contaminated surfaces, and it occurs in seasonal epidemics each winter in many countries. Most people recover within a week or two, but the flu causes thousands of hospitalisations and hundreds of deaths each year. Vaccination is the best protection. This chapter explains the causes, symptoms, and management of influenza.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Influenza affects hundreds of thousands of people each year, usually between May and October. In a typical season, it is associated with tens of thousands of GP visits and several thousand hospitalisations, and it contributes to hundreds of deaths, mostly in older adults and people with chronic disease. Influenza A and B viruses cause the seasonal epidemics, and the virus changes each year, which is why the vaccine is updated annually. Rates are highest in children, who spread the virus efficiently, while the most severe illness occurs in older adults and high-risk groups. Vaccination coverage of around 50--60\% of the population reduces the burden.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The virus:} influenza A and B viruses, which change constantly through mutation, escaping immunity and requiring annual vaccine updates
    \item \textbf{Transmission:} droplets from coughing and sneezing, and contact with contaminated hands and surfaces
    \item \textbf{Incubation:} symptoms appear 1--4 days after exposure
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} the virus infects the lining of the airways, causing inflammation and damage; the immune response produces the fever and muscle aches
    \item \textbf{Contagiousness:} people are contagious from about a day before symptoms until 5--7 days after onset
    \item \textbf{Complications:} the virus and secondary bacteria can cause pneumonia; it can also worsen heart disease, asthma, and diabetes
    \item \textbf{Severe forms:} some influenza strains, and new pandemic strains, cause more severe disease
    \item \textbf{Immunity:} infection gives immunity to that strain, but not to future variants
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden onset of fever, often high
    \item Muscle aches and pains, headache, and chills
    \item A dry cough, sore throat, and runny or blocked nose
    \item Fatigue and weakness, which can last for weeks
    \item Loss of appetite and nausea in some people
    \item In children: vomiting and diarrhoea can also occur
    \item In older adults: confusion and reduced function can be prominent
    \item Symptoms usually peak within a few days and resolve within 1--2 weeks, though the cough and fatigue can persist
    \item Warning features: difficulty breathing, chest pain, confusion, and dehydration
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The flu resembles other respiratory infections.
\begin{itemize}
    \item The common cold, which is milder with more sneezing and less fever
    \item COVID-19, which has overlapping symptoms and needs a test to distinguish
    \item Other viral respiratory infections, including RSV
    \item Bacterial pneumonia, which causes productive cough and chest signs
    \item Tonsillitis, with a prominent sore throat
    \item Meningitis, in the differential of fever with severe headache and neck stiffness
    \item Testing distinguishes influenza from these
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most healthy people manage the flu at home with rest and fluids, but medical care is needed for difficulty breathing, chest pain, confusion, dehydration, or severe symptoms, and for high-risk people who develop the flu, including those with chronic conditions.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, rapid breathing, or chest pain
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or seizures
    \item Signs of severe dehydration: no urine, dizziness, or collapse
    \item A blue tinge to the lips
    \item The flu in a person who is very unwell, including babies and older adults
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical diagnosis:} the sudden onset of fever, cough, and muscle aches in the flu season strongly suggests influenza
    \item \textbf{Rapid tests:} swabs from the nose or throat, including PCR tests that also detect COVID-19
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} for complications, including pneumonia and dehydration
    \item \textbf{Chest X-ray:} when pneumonia is suspected
    \item \textbf{Most cases:} no testing is needed, and treatment is the same
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Rest and fluids:} the mainstay of treatment for most people
    \item \textbf{Pain and fever relief:} paracetamol or ibuprofen
    \item \textbf{Antiviral medicines:} oseltamivir (Tamiflu) reduces the duration and severity when started within 48 hours, and is used for high-risk people and severe illness
    \item \textbf{Do not use aspirin in children}, because of the risk of Reye's syndrome
    \item \textbf{Treat complications:} antibiotics for secondary bacterial infections, and hospital care for pneumonia and severe illness
    \item \textbf{Stay home:} until at least 24--48 hours after the fever resolves, to avoid spreading the virus
    \item \textbf{Symptom relief:} cough medicines and lozenges as needed
    \item \textbf{High-risk monitoring:} people with chronic conditions should have early medical review
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pneumonia, either from the virus itself or secondary bacterial infection
    \item Worsening of asthma, heart failure, diabetes, and other chronic conditions
    \item Bronchitis and ear infections, especially in children
    \item Dehydration, especially in children and older adults
    \item Rarely, inflammation of the heart or brain
    \item Death, mainly in older adults and high-risk groups
    \item Prolonged fatigue and reduced function after the infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people with the flu recover fully within 1--2 weeks, though the cough and fatigue can linger longer. High-risk groups have substantially higher rates of complications, but vaccination, early antiviral treatment, and prompt care reduce these risks. The flu is usually a self-limiting illness in healthy people, and the most important preventive measure is the annual vaccine, which reduces the risk of illness and severe disease. For people who develop complications, early hospital care is highly effective.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Get the flu vaccine every year, ideally from April, before the season
    \item Vaccination eligibility and funding vary by country; higher-risk groups commonly include older adults, young children, pregnant people, and people with chronic conditions
    \item Wash hands frequently and cover coughs and sneezes
    \item Stay home when unwell and avoid contact with high-risk people
    \item Keep surfaces and shared items clean
    \item Rest, drink fluids, and use paracetamol or ibuprofen for symptoms
    \item Seek early medical care if you are in a high-risk group or symptoms are severe
    \item Do not smoke, and manage chronic conditions well
\end{itemize}
