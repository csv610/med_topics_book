\section*{Definition and Overview}
Claustrophobia is an intense, persistent fear of enclosed or confined spaces. It is a type of specific phobia, a form of anxiety disorder in which a particular situation or object triggers a strong anxiety response that is out of proportion to any real danger.

People with claustrophobia may feel panic in lifts, small rooms, tunnels, crowded public transport, aircraft, or scanning machines such as MRI scanners. The fear leads them to avoid these situations, and this avoidance can restrict everyday life and, in some cases, interfere with receiving necessary medical care. Claustrophobia is very treatable, most effectively with psychological therapy.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Specific phobias are among the most common mental health conditions. Around 5--12\% of people experience claustrophobia at some point in their lives, and it is roughly twice as common in women as in men. It can begin at any age but most often develops during childhood or adolescence.

Many people have mild forms that cause little distress, but in a significant proportion the fear is severe enough to cause avoidance and impairment. Claustrophobia is one of the more common reasons people refuse or delay MRI scans and other medical procedures.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Claustrophobia usually develops from a combination of learned experiences and biological factors.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Classical conditioning:} a frightening or distressing past experience in an enclosed space, such as being locked in a lift or trapped in a crowded place, can link that situation with fear
    \item \textbf{Learned fear from others:} observing a parent or family member react with fear to enclosed spaces
    \item \textbf{Evolutionary preparedness:} humans may be biologically primed to fear confinement because of its association with being trapped or suffocating
    \item \textbf{Brain circuitry:} the amygdala, the brain's fear centre, overreacts to the perception of confinement, triggering a rapid "fight or flight" response
    \item \textbf{Cognitive factors:} catastrophising thoughts, such as believing the space cannot be escaped or that one will suffocate or panic uncontrollably
    \item \textbf{Genetic predisposition:} anxiety disorders, including phobias, tend to run in families
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms of claustrophobia are triggered by being in, or even anticipating being in, an enclosed space, and reflect the body's anxiety response.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Intense fear, dread, or a sense of doom when in or near confined spaces
    \item Rapid heartbeat, palpitations, or chest tightness
    \item Sweating, trembling, or shaking
    \item Shortness of breath or feeling of suffocation or choking
    \item Dizziness, faintness, or feeling detached from reality
    \item Nausea or an urge to escape immediately
    \item Urgency to get out, open windows or doors, or avoid entering lifts, tunnels, or scans
    \item Anticipatory anxiety and avoidance of situations that might be enclosed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions can cause similar fear or panic and should be considered:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Agoraphobia, where fear centres on open spaces, crowds, or being unable to escape generally
    \item Panic disorder, where panic attacks occur unpredictably rather than only in enclosed spaces
    \item Generalised anxiety disorder, with pervasive worry rather than a specific trigger
    \item Post-traumatic stress disorder after an actual entrapment or trauma
    \item Separation anxiety or social anxiety in some presentations
    \item Medical causes of panic-like symptoms, such as an overactive thyroid or arrhythmias
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor or mental health professional if claustrophobia causes significant distress, leads you to avoid important activities or medical care, or has begun to limit work, study, or relationships.

\textbf{Call 000 for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, severe breathlessness, or fainting during a panic episode that does not settle
    \item Symptoms that suggest a heart attack or other medical emergency rather than anxiety
    \item Thoughts of self-harm or suicide, which can occur with severe anxiety and low mood
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Claustrophobia is diagnosed through clinical assessment; there is no specific test.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical interview:} a detailed discussion of the feared situations, symptoms, triggers, and how much the fear interferes with daily life
    \item \textbf{Screening questionnaires:} standardised tools for anxiety and phobia symptoms (such as the GAD-7 or specific phobia scales)
    \item \textbf{Physical examination and blood tests:} to exclude medical causes of panic-like symptoms if the presentation is atypical or new in an older person
    \item \textbf{Mental state assessment:} to check for other anxiety, mood, or related disorders
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Claustrophobia is highly treatable, and psychological therapy is the mainstay of treatment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT):} helps identify and challenge catastrophic thoughts about enclosed spaces
    \item \textbf{Exposure therapy:} gradual, repeated and structured contact with feared situations, from imagining them to entering real enclosed spaces, allowing fear to reduce with time
    \item \textbf{Relaxation and breathing techniques:} to manage the physical symptoms of anxiety during exposure
    \item \textbf{Virtual reality therapy:} computer-simulated enclosed environments used as a safe way to practise exposure
    \item \textbf{Medication:} short-term medicines may occasionally be used briefly, under medical supervision, to help with a one-off scan or flight
    \item \textbf{Practical support:} telling staff at an MRI department or on a plane about the phobia so adjustments can be made (e.g.\ a mirror, music, or open scan machines)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
If claustrophobia is severe and untreated, it can have wide-ranging effects:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Avoidance of medical care, including MRI scans, dental treatment, and other enclosed procedures
    \item Difficulty travelling by plane, train, or lift, limiting work and leisure
    \item Panic attacks, which may become more frequent with avoidance
    \item Secondary depression, low self-esteem, or social withdrawal
    \item Increased reliance on alcohol or sedatives to manage anxiety
    \item Reduced quality of life and independence
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for claustrophobia is good. Most people who complete psychological treatment, particularly exposure-based CBT, experience a substantial reduction in fear, and many become able to use lifts, travel, and undergo scans without difficulty. Symptoms may not disappear completely, but most people learn to manage them so that the phobia no longer controls their choices. Left untreated, claustrophobia tends to persist, but it rarely becomes more severe over time and is not dangerous in itself.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek help early; phobias are easier to treat before avoidance becomes entrenched
    \item Practise gradual exposure -- repeatedly entering slightly uncomfortable situations and staying until anxiety settles
    \item Use slow breathing and muscle relaxation techniques during anxious moments
    \item Avoid alcohol and excessive caffeine, which can worsen anxiety
    \item Plan ahead for enclosed situations, such as telling the MRI radiographer and agreeing on a stop signal
    \item Ask about open (less confining) MRI scanners or short-scan options if imaging is needed
    \item Build general stress-management and coping skills through exercise, sleep, and social connection
\end{itemize}
