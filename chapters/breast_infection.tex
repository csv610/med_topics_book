\chapter{Breast Infection}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A breast infection is an infection of the breast tissue, the most common form of which is \textbf{mastitis} -- inflammation and infection of the breast. It occurs most often in breastfeeding women, typically within the first few weeks after childbirth, but it can also affect women who are not breastfeeding, in whom it is usually linked to inflammation of the ducts behind the nipple (periductal mastitis). Mastitis ranges from a mild, painful, reddened area that settles with feeding and simple self-care to a severe infection with high fever that requires antibiotics. If not treated promptly and effectively, mastitis can progress to a breast abscess -- a discrete collection of pus that requires drainage. Because mastitis and inflammatory breast cancer can look alike, any breast inflammation that does not resolve with appropriate treatment must be investigated further.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Mastitis is common, affecting roughly 1 in 5 to 1 in 10 breastfeeding women, usually in the first six weeks after birth, although it can occur at any point during lactation. It is more likely when there are cracked or sore nipples, infrequent or skipped feeds, or a poor latch. A minority of women with mastitis -- perhaps 3--11\% -- develop a breast abscess. Non-lactational mastitis is less common and is strongly associated with smoking, and also with being overweight, diabetes, and certain chronic skin conditions; it tends to recur, particularly in women who continue to smoke. Recurrent infection on one side, especially behind the nipple, should always prompt review because of the small possibility of an underlying abnormality or cancer.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Breast infection is almost always bacterial. The most common organism is \textit{Staphylococcus aureus}, including methicillin-resistant strains (MRSA); streptococci and, in non-breastfeeding women, anaerobic bacteria are also important causes. In breastfeeding women, organisms enter through cracks or damage in the nipple, and \textbf{milk stasis} -- milk that is not being removed -- provides the stagnant medium in which they multiply. In non-breastfeeding women, the ducts behind the nipple become inflamed and infected, sometimes with anaerobes that thrive in the absence of oxygen and produce foul-smelling discharge.

Risk factors include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Cracked, sore, or damaged nipples
    \item Infrequent feeding, skipped feeds, or a poor latch
    \item Milk stasis and breast engorgement
    \item Smoking, particularly in non-breastfeeding women
    \item A weakened immune system or chronic illness such as diabetes
    \item A previous breast abscess or recurrent mastitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A painful, swollen, red, and warm area of the breast, often with a wedge-shaped patch of inflammation
    \item Fever, chills, aches, and a general feeling of being unwell, which may come on quickly
    \item Flu-like symptoms that can begin before the breast looks obviously red or inflamed
    \item Tenderness and difficulty feeding from the affected breast
    \item A firm or fluctuant lump if an abscess has formed
    \item In non-lactational mastitis: inflammation and sometimes discharge around or behind the nipple, often recurring
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Breast engorgement, which usually affects both breasts without localised redness or high fever
    \item A blocked duct or milk stasis without true infection
    \item Breast abscess, which is a complication of mastitis rather than a separate cause
    \item Inflammatory breast cancer, a rare and aggressive cancer that can closely mimic mastitis
    \item Duct ectasia, a benign inflammatory condition of the ducts
    \item Granulomatous mastitis, a rare inflammatory condition of unknown cause
    \item Skin conditions such as eczema, cellulitis, or a superficial abscess of the breast skin
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical review if you have a painful, reddened breast, especially with fever or flu-like symptoms, or if mastitis does not improve within 24--48 hours of feeding frequently and taking simple measures at home. Early treatment makes complications such as abscess formation far less likely.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item High fever, shaking chills, or feeling faint
    \item Rapid breathing, a rapid heart rate, or confusion -- possible sepsis
    \item Redness spreading rapidly across the breast or chest wall
    \item A hard, tense, very painful breast with a purple or bluish appearance
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the doctor assesses the affected breast, checks the temperature, and enquires about feeding history, previous episodes, and risk factors
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} used when an abscess is suspected, to detect and locate any collection of pus and to guide drainage
    \item \textbf{Microbiology:} a sample of breast milk or wound discharge can be cultured to identify the bacteria and guide antibiotic choice, particularly in severe, recurrent, or resistant infection
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count and inflammatory markers if the person is systemically unwell
    \item \textbf{Biopsy:} if a lump does not resolve with treatment or looks unusual, tissue sampling to exclude breast cancer
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} prescribed by a doctor for confirmed mastitis and active against the likely organism; the full course should be completed even if symptoms improve quickly
    \item \textbf{Continued feeding or expressing:} keeping the breast drained is essential to resolution, and feeding from the affected breast is safe for the baby
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple analgesics such as paracetamol and ibuprofen
    \item \textbf{Warm compresses before feeding} and gentle massage during feeding, with cool compresses between feeds to reduce discomfort
    \item \textbf{Rest and fluids:} to support the immune response and recovery
    \item \textbf{Drainage:} if an abscess forms, the pus is removed by needle aspiration, often repeated and ultrasound-guided, or by a small surgical incision for larger collections
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review to confirm the infection has fully resolved and that no lump or collection remains
    \item \textbf{Surgery for recurrent non-lactational mastitis:} excision of the affected duct may be offered, and stopping smoking is strongly advised
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Breast abscess formation
    \item Recurrent mastitis, which is more common in non-breastfeeding women and smokers
    \item A mammary fistula -- an abnormal channel connecting a duct to the skin
    \item Difficulty continuing breastfeeding and early, unplanned weaning
    \item Scarring or dimpling of the breast after severe or repeated infection
    \item Rarely, spread of infection into the bloodstream causing sepsis
    \item Delay in diagnosing an underlying breast cancer that was mistaken for infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With early treatment, most cases of mastitis settle within a few days, and most women are able to continue breastfeeding successfully. When antibiotics are started promptly and the breast is kept drained, the formation of an abscess is much less likely. Non-lactational mastitis is more troublesome: it frequently recurs and may require repeated courses of antibiotics, drainage, or definitive duct surgery, particularly in women who continue to smoke. Stopping smoking substantially improves the outlook in this group, and any breast change that does not resolve with treatment warrants further investigation to exclude cancer.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Get early help with breastfeeding technique, latch, and any sore nipples
    \item Feed frequently and on demand, and express or feed when the breast feels full
    \item Vary feeding positions to drain all areas of the breast
    \item Seek early review for engorgement or blocked ducts before infection develops
    \item Complete the full course of antibiotics if prescribed
    \item Stop smoking, which is the main modifiable risk factor in non-breastfeeding women
    \item See a doctor about any lump or skin change that persists after the infection has settled
\end{itemize}
