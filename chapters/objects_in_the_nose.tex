\chapter{Objects in the nose}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A nasal foreign body (NFB) is any object or substance placed inside the nasal cavity that is not part of the normal anatomy. Nasal foreign bodies are common clinical occurrences, primarily in children and individuals with cognitive impairments. While many nasal foreign bodies cause only minor localized discomfort, they carry risks of mucosal ulceration, secondary bacterial infection, septal perforation, and, most critically, aspiration into the lower respiratory tract. Certain objects, such as button batteries and magnets, represent true medical emergencies due to their capacity for rapid tissue destruction.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Nasal foreign bodies are most commonly observed in children aged one to five years, corresponding to a stage of development characterised by intense exploration and fine motor coordination. In adults, nasal foreign bodies are rare and typically occur in the context of psychiatric disease, intellectual disability, or facial trauma. The most frequently inserted items include beads, paper, toy parts, food items (such as peas or seeds), foam rubber, button batteries, and small magnets. NFBs are usually inserted into the right nostril, likely reflecting the predominance of right-handedness.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The pathological effects of a nasal foreign body depend on the object's physical properties and the duration of its presence:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anatomical obstruction:} Objects typically lodge in the anterior nasal cavity, just inferior to the turbinate or at the nasal valve. This obstructs the flow of air and nasal secretions.
    \item \textbf{Inflammatory response:} The foreign body irritates the nasal mucosa, triggering local inflammation, mucosal oedema, and hypersecretion. The stasis of secretions creates a medium for bacterial overgrowth, leading to purulent rhinitis or sinusitis.
    \item \textbf{Chemical and electrical burns:} Button batteries lodged in the moist nasal environment generate an electrical circuit that hydrolyses tissue fluids, producing sodium hydroxide. This causes rapid liquefactive necrosis, which can erode through the nasal septum in less than four hours.
    \item \textbf{Magnetic compression:} Ingestion or insertion of bilateral nasal magnets (one in each nostril) results in them clamping onto the nasal septum, causing pressure necrosis, ischaemia, and subsequent septal perforation.
    \item \textbf{Rhinolith formation:} An undetected foreign body that remains in the nasal cavity for years can become a nidus for the deposition of calcium, magnesium, and phosphate salts. Over time, this forms a hard, chalky calcareous mass known as a rhinolith.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The presentation of a nasal foreign body can vary from asymptomatic to severe local inflammation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unilateral, foul-smelling, purulent or mucopurulent nasal discharge (the classic clinical hallmark)
    \item Unilateral nasal obstruction, congestion, or mouth breathing
    \item Epistaxis (nosebleeds), which may be recurrent or blood-stained mucus
    \item Nasal pain, irritation, or a scratching sensation inside the nostril
    \item Frequent sneezing, snorting, or nose-picking
    \item Halitosis (foul breath) due to secondary bacterial decomposition
    \item Visible object in the anterior nostril on direct inspection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A unilateral nasal discharge or obstruction must be differentiated from:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Acute or chronic sinusitis:} Bacterial or viral infection of the sinuses, which is typically bilateral and accompanied by facial pain or headache.
    \item \textbf{Allergic or infectious rhinitis:} Presenting with bilateral watery discharge, sneezing, and pruritus.
    \item \textbf{Nasal polyposis:} Non-cancerous grape-like growths in the nasal cavity, usually bilateral and presenting with chronic congestion and anosmia.
    \item \textbf{Adenoid hypertrophy:} Enlarged adenoidal tissue in the nasopharynx causing chronic bilateral congestion, snoring, and sleep-disordered breathing.
    \item \textbf{Nasal septal haematoma:} A collection of blood within the septum following facial trauma, presenting as a dark, boggy septal swelling.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
All nasal foreign bodies require assessment and removal by a healthcare professional to avoid pushing the object into the pharynx or airway.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Any suspected or confirmed insertion of a button battery or magnets
    \item Choking, coughing, wheezing, or stridor (indicating the object has migrated into the trachea or bronchus)
    \item Severe, uncontrollable nosebleeds or bleeding that does not stop after ten minutes of direct pressure
    \item Spreading facial swelling, redness around the eye, high fever, or severe headache
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Nasal foreign bodies are primarily diagnosed through clinical history and physical examination:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anterior rhinoscopy:} Inspection of the nasal cavity using a nasal speculum or an otoscope with a wide speculum, coupled with a bright light source.
    \item \textbf{Flexible or rigid endoscopy:} Indicated if the object is located in the posterior nasal cavity or if the diagnosis is unclear.
    \item \textbf{Plain radiography:} Lateral and anteroposterior X-rays are useful for identifying radiopaque foreign bodies, multiple magnets, or suspected rhinoliths. They are not routinely recommended for radiolucent, anterior objects.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Removal should be attempted using techniques that minimise mucosal trauma and prevent posterior displacement:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Preparation:} Instillation of a topical vasoconstrictor (e.g., 0.5\% oxymetazoline) and a topical anaesthetic (e.g., 2\% lidocaine) to reduce mucosal swelling, minimise bleeding, and improve patient comfort.
    \item \textbf{The ``Parent's Kiss'' (positive pressure):} A safe first-line technique where the parent places their mouth over the child's open mouth, occludes the unaffected nostril with a finger, and delivers a quick puff of air. This often dislodges anterior objects.
    \item \textbf{Manual instrumentation:} Extraction under direct vision using alligator forceps, a cerumen loop, or a right-angle hook. The hook is passed behind the object and pulled forward.
    \item \textbf{Balloon catheter (Katz extractor):} A small catheter (e.g., 5-French Fogarty catheter) is lubricated, passed past the object, inflated with air or water, and gently withdrawn to pull the object out.
    \item \textbf{Suction:} A suction device can be applied to smooth, round objects to extract them.
    \item \textbf{General anaesthesia:} Required for posterior, wedged, or sharp objects, or for highly anxious, uncooperative children.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Complications are typically related to delayed diagnosis or traumatic removal attempts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Epistaxis due to mucosal abrasion
    \item Aspiration of the foreign body into the trachea or lungs, potentially causing acute airway obstruction
    \item Nasal septal perforation, particularly with button batteries or magnets, which can lead to saddle nose deformity
    \item Sinusitis, dacryocystitis, or orbital cellulitis due to obstructed drainage pathways
    \item Toxic shock syndrome (rare, associated with long-standing packing or retained organic foreign bodies)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis is excellent if the nasal foreign body is detected and removed promptly. Most mucosal abrasions heal within a few days without scarring or sequelae. However, delayed removal of button batteries can cause irreversible septal perforation within hours, requiring complex surgical reconstruction. Similarly, rhinoliths require careful debridement but generally resolve well after extraction.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep small household items (beads, buttons, batteries, coins) and toys with small parts out of reach of infants and toddlers.
    \item Choose toys that are age-appropriate and have secure battery compartments.
    \item Supervise young children closely during arts, crafts, and play.
    \item Do not attempt to retrieve a deeply wedged nasal object at home using cotton buds or tweezers, as this frequently pushes the object further back.
    \item Educate children on the physical dangers of placing objects into their nose or ears.
\end{itemize}
