\chapter{Codeine}
 
\section*{Definition and Overview}
Codeine (3-methylmorphine) is an opioid analgesic and antitussive drug derived from the opium poppy (Papaver somniferum) or synthesized from morphine. Clinically, it is used to manage mild to moderate pain and to suppress non-productive coughs. In the body, codeine acts as a prodrug; it is converted into morphine by the hepatic enzyme CYP2D6 to exert its analgesic effects. Because of its opioid properties, codeine carries risks of misuse, tolerance, dependency, and respiratory depression. In Australia, codeine was reclassified as a prescription-only medicine (Schedule 4) in 2018 to address public health concerns regarding addiction and misuse.
 
\section*{Epidemiology}
Codeine has historically been one of the most widely used opioids globally. Prior to its reclassification in Australia in 2018, over-the-counter codeine combination products (often combined with paracetamol or ibuprofen) were major contributors to opioid misuse, accidental overdoses, and drug-induced liver or kidney injuries. Since rescheduling, the overall prescribing of codeine and associated poisoning presentations have declined. However, codeine remains a commonly prescribed analgesic, and opioid-related disorders continue to be a significant focus of public health, with thousands of Australians seeking treatment for pharmaceutical opioid dependence annually.
 
\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The pharmacology of codeine and its clinical risks are determined by its metabolism and mechanism of action:
 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prodrug metabolism:} codeine undergoes O-demethylation in the liver by the cytochrome P450 enzyme CYP2D6, converting it into active morphine.
    \item \textbf{Genetic variability (CYP2D6):} individuals vary widely in their CYP2D6 activity. Ultra-rapid metabolisers convert codeine to morphine very quickly, risking toxic levels, while poor metabolisers derive little to no pain relief.
    \item \textbf{Mu-opioid receptor agonism:} active morphine binds to mu-opioid receptors in the central and peripheral nervous systems, inhibiting pain transmission pathways and altering the perception of pain.
    \item \textbf{Antitussive action:} codeine suppresses the cough reflex through a direct central action in the medulla oblongata.
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{Clinical Features}
The effects of codeine range from therapeutic response to overdose symptoms and withdrawal:
 
\begin{itemize}
    \item Analgesia and relief of mild to moderate pain
    \item Suppression of dry, tickly coughs
    \item Common side effects: constipation, drowsiness, nausea, vomiting, dry mouth, and dizziness
    \item Signs of acute toxicity or overdose: miosis (pinpoint pupils), central nervous system depression (stupor, coma), and respiratory depression (slow, shallow breathing)
    \item Signs of chronic misuse: severe constipation, cognitive impairment, hypogonadism, and development of opioid use disorder
    \item Withdrawal symptoms: anxiety, irritability, lacrimation, rhinorrhea, sweating, muscle aches, abdominal cramps, diarrhoea, and insomnia
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Codeine-related clinical issues (like overdose or withdrawal) must be distinguished from other causes of altered consciousness, respiratory distress, or acute illness:
 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Other sedative or opioid overdoses:} differentiated by specific toxicology panels, though emergency management (naloxone) remains similar.
    \item \textbf{Organophosphate poisoning:} presents with miosis and respiratory distress, but includes cholinergic signs (salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation).
    \item \textbf{Severe hypothyroidism or myxoedema coma:} presents with hypothermia, bradycardia, and lethargy, but without a history of drug exposure.
    \item \textbf{Acute abdomen:} severe abdominal pain or bowel obstruction can be simulated by severe opioid-induced constipation.
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical care if side effects are severe or if there are signs of dependence.
 
\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unresponsiveness, severe drowsiness, or inability to wake up
    \item Extremely slow, shallow, or irregular breathing
    \item Blue or pale skin, lips, or fingernails (cyanosis)
    \item Suspected accidental or intentional overdose, especially if combined with alcohol or other sedatives
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} evaluation of airway, breathing, circulation, level of consciousness, and pupillary size.
    \item \textbf{Toxicology screens:} urine or blood tests for opioids and metabolites, and screening for co-ingestants like paracetamol or ibuprofen (which are commonly combined with codeine).
    \item \textbf{Genotyping:} CYP2D6 genetic testing may be performed in rare cases to investigate unusual response or toxicity.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} liver and kidney function tests, especially if paracetamol- or ibuprofen-containing combination products were misused.
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{Management}
Management involves treating acute toxicity and addressing long-term dependency:
 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Airway support:} maintaining a clear airway and providing supplemental oxygen or mechanical ventilation.
    \item \textbf{Naloxone:} the specific opioid antagonist, administered intravenously, intramuscularly, or intranasally to rapidly reverse respiratory depression.
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} monitoring vitals, managing bowel function (laxatives for constipation), and treating nausea.
    \item \textbf{Opioid substitution therapy:} for long-term dependence, utilizing buprenorphine or methadone, combined with psychological support and counseling.
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Life-threatening respiratory depression and hypoxic brain injury
    \item Severe, chronic opioid-induced constipation, which can lead to faecal impaction or bowel perforation
    \item Paracetamol toxicity (acute liver failure) or ibuprofen toxicity (gastric ulcers, renal failure) from misuse of combination pills
    \item Neonatal opioid withdrawal syndrome in infants born to mothers using codeine during pregnancy
    \item Physical dependence, tolerance, and opioid use disorder
\end{itemize}
 
\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt administration of naloxone and respiratory support, the prognosis for acute codeine overdose is excellent, and patients typically make a full recovery. Long-term outcomes for codeine dependence depend on the patient's access to addiction medicine specialists, participation in medication-assisted treatment for opioid dependence, and counseling. CYP2D6 genetic variations mean that individuals will continue to have variable responses, and alternative non-opioid analgesics are preferred for future pain management.
 
\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Take codeine strictly as prescribed by a medical professional, and never exceed the recommended dose
    \item Avoid combining codeine with alcohol, sleeping pills, or other sedatives, which increases the risk of fatal overdose
    \item Discuss alternative, non-opioid pain management strategies (e.g., physiotherapy, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) with your doctor
    \item Store codeine securely out of reach of children and pets, and dispose of unused medication safely at a pharmacy
    \item Recognize early signs of dependence (e.g., needing larger doses, cravings) and seek help from a health provider or helpline
\end{itemize}
