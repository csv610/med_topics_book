\chapter{Deep Abscess}
\index{Deep Abscess}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A deep abscess is a collection of pus that forms in tissues below the skin, in organs, or in body cavities, usually as a result of bacterial infection. Unlike a superficial skin boil, a deep abscess may not be visible or easily felt and can cause significant illness. Common sites include the dental roots, the abdominal cavity, the liver, the brain, and muscles. Deep abscesses require prompt diagnosis and drainage, usually combined with antibiotics, because untreated collections of pus can spread infection and cause sepsis. This chapter covers the causes, features, diagnosis, and treatment of deep abscesses.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Deep abscesses occur across all age groups and are more common in people with impaired immunity, diabetes, or recent surgery. Intra-abdominal abscesses complicate 1--3\% of abdominal operations and are also seen after appendicitis, diverticulitis, or bowel perforation. Liver abscesses are more common in tropical regions and in people with bile duct disease. Dental abscesses are among the most frequent deep infections, and brain abscesses, although rare, carry significant mortality. In many countries, delays in dental care and increasing antibiotic resistance influence the frequency and severity of deep infections.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bacterial entry:} infection reaches deep tissues through wounds, surgery, spread from adjacent infection, or via the bloodstream
    \item \textbf{Encapsulation:} the immune system walls off the infection, producing a cavity filled with pus, dead tissue, and bacteria
    \item \textbf{Common organisms:} mixed bowel bacteria in abdominal abscesses, skin organisms such as staphylococci after wounds, and specific organisms such as amoeba or Klebsiella in liver abscesses
    \item \textbf{Risk factors:} diabetes, immunosuppression, intravenous drug use, dental neglect, and recent surgery or trauma
    \item \textbf{Pus formation:} the abscess is largely impenetrable to antibiotics, so drainage is usually essential
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fever, chills, sweats, and a general feeling of being unwell
    \item Localised pain and tenderness over the affected site
    \item Swelling, redness, or a palpable mass where the abscess is near a body surface
    \item Organ-specific symptoms: abdominal pain and vomiting in intra-abdominal abscesses; headache, seizures, or neurological signs in brain abscess; toothache and jaw swelling in dental abscess
    \item Loss of appetite and weight loss
    \item Signs of sepsis in severe cases, including rapid heart rate, low blood pressure, and confusion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Cellulitis or superficial infection without pus collection
    \item Cysts, benign tumours, or lymph node enlargement
    \item Inflammatory masses, such as an inflammatory bowel disease mass or a collection of fluid without infection
    \item Malignancy, which can present as a mass with fever and weight loss
    \item Aortic aneurysm or other vascular abnormalities in abdominal presentations
    \item Haematoma, a collection of blood that can mimic an abscess after injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any person with persistent fever and localised pain or swelling should be assessed by a doctor promptly. Dental pain with facial swelling, abdominal pain with fever, or headache with neurological symptoms all require urgent review. People with diabetes or weakened immunity should seek help earlier, as their infections can progress quickly.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of sepsis: confusion, rapid breathing, rapid heart rate, or very low blood pressure
    \item Difficulty breathing, swallowing, or opening the mouth with facial or neck swelling
    \item Sudden severe headache with vomiting, weakness, or seizures
    \item Severe abdominal pain with fever and vomiting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} elevated white cell count and inflammatory markers; blood cultures when sepsis is suspected
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} first-line imaging for many deep abscesses, including dental, liver, and some abdominal collections
    \item \textbf{Computed tomography (CT):} the most sensitive test for intra-abdominal and pelvic abscesses, and used to plan drainage
    \item \textbf{Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI):} preferred for brain abscesses and infections in muscles or bones
    \item \textbf{Aspiration and culture:} sampling the pus to identify the organism and its antibiotic sensitivities
    \item \textbf{Dental examination and X-ray:} for dental abscesses
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Drainage:} the definitive treatment; may be performed surgically, by image-guided needle drainage, or by dental extraction or root canal treatment
    \item \textbf{Antibiotics:} given after or alongside drainage, initially broad-spectrum and then tailored to culture results
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} paracetamol or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and specific care for dental pain
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} intravenous fluids, fever control, and nutritional support in hospital
    \item \textbf{Treating the cause:} management of the underlying condition, such as appendicitis, diverticulitis, or tooth decay
    \item \textbf{Follow-up imaging:} to confirm the abscess has resolved and no re-accumulation has occurred
    \item \textbf{Management of risk factors:} optimising diabetes control and addressing immunosuppression where possible
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sepsis and septic shock, the most serious complication
    \item Spread of infection to adjacent structures or the bloodstream
    \item Recurrence if drainage is incomplete or the underlying cause is untreated
    \item Organ damage or permanent loss of function at the affected site
    \item Complications of treatment, including bleeding, anaesthetic risks, or injury to nearby structures
    \item Chronic infection or sinus tract formation in neglected abscesses
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt drainage and appropriate antibiotics, the majority of deep abscesses resolve completely without long-term consequences. Outcomes depend on the site, the patient's general health, and the speed of treatment. Brain and intra-abdominal abscesses carry higher risks and require specialist care, but modern imaging-guided drainage has greatly improved survival. Dental abscesses resolve well with root canal treatment or extraction. Early recognition and treatment are the keys to a good outcome.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain good dental hygiene and attend regular dental check-ups to prevent dental abscesses
    \item Practise good wound care and seek early review of infected wounds
    \item Control diabetes and other conditions that increase infection risk
    \item Complete prescribed antibiotic courses fully
    \item Avoid intravenous drug use, which is a major risk factor for deep infections
    \item Seek prompt medical attention for persistent fever and pain rather than waiting
\end{itemize}
