\chapter{Achilles Tendon}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
The Achilles tendon is the thick, powerful band of connective tissue that connects the calf muscles (gastrocnemius and soleus) to the heel bone (calcaneus). It is the largest and strongest tendon in the human body and plays an essential role in walking, running, jumping, and rising onto the toes. Because it transmits enormous forces during weight-bearing activity, it is also one of the most frequently injured tendons.

The tendon has a relatively poor blood supply throughout its middle portion, an area that is therefore especially vulnerable to injury and slow to heal. Problems affecting the Achilles tendon fall broadly into two groups: chronic overuse conditions (tendinopathy, historically called tendonitis) and acute tears (partial or complete rupture). Both can cause significant pain and disability, and both respond best to early recognition and structured management.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Achilles tendon problems are common, particularly among active and athletic populations. Tendinopathy is seen in both sexes and becomes more frequent with age, as the tendon gradually loses strength and elasticity. Runners, people who play sports involving sprinting, jumping, or sudden changes of direction, and individuals who are overweight are at increased risk.

Rupture is most common in men aged roughly 30 to 50 years, and often occurs during recreational sport after a period of relative inactivity. The lifetime incidence of Achilles rupture has been rising in many countries, partly reflecting increased participation in high-intensity sports among middle-aged adults. Use of certain medicines, including fluoroquinolone antibiotics and corticosteroids, adds further risk.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Most chronic Achilles problems develop through a combination of repetitive loading and age-related change. With overuse, the tendon is subjected to forces greater than its capacity to repair, and microscopic collagen tears accumulate. The normal healing response can fail, leaving disorganised, thickened, and sometimes calcified tissue. This state is known as tendinopathy; despite the older name ``tendonitis'', active inflammation is often not the dominant feature, and the condition is better understood as a failed healing response with degeneration of tendon fibres.

A rupture occurs when a sudden forceful contraction of the calf muscles loads the tendon beyond its breaking point, typically during push-off of the foot in sprinting, jumping, or a sudden lunge. Risk factors include a history of previous tendon problems, prolonged use of fluoroquinolone antibiotics or corticosteroids, inflammatory joint disease, and a sedentary period followed by a sudden burst of unaccustomed activity. Ruptured tendons usually show pre-existing degenerative change even when the patient has had no prior symptoms.

\section*{Clinical Features}
The presentation differs according to whether the problem is tendinopathy or a rupture.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tendinopathy:} pain and aching at the back of the heel or lower calf, often worse at the start of activity and after exercise
    \item \textbf{Morning stiffness:} the tendon feels tight and stiff on first walking, easing as it warms up
    \item \textbf{Swelling and thickening:} a tender lump or nodule may be felt within the tendon
    \item \textbf{Functional pain:} difficulty rising onto the toes or pushing off while walking, running, or climbing stairs
    \item \textbf{Rupture:} a sudden ``pop'' or snapping sensation at the back of the leg, followed by sharp pain and an inability to push off
    \item \textbf{Rupture signs:} swelling and bruising that spreads up the calf, a palpable gap in the tendon, and weakness on calf squeeze
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
A number of conditions can produce similar pain around the back of the ankle and heel.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Plantar fasciitis (pain under the sole of the heel)
    \item Retrocalcaneal bursitis (inflammation of the bursa behind the heel)
    \item Calf muscle strain, tear, or deep vein thrombosis
    \item Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction (pain along the inner side of the ankle)
    \item Stress fracture of the calcaneus or distal tibia
    \item Ankle arthritis, tarsal tunnel syndrome, or posterior ankle impingement
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor promptly after a sudden snap at the back of the ankle, or if you have difficulty pushing off the foot, as these features may indicate a rupture that requires urgent assessment. Seek care if pain has not improved after a few weeks of self-care, if it is severe enough to interfere with walking and daily activities, or if there is swelling, redness, or warmth suggestive of infection.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe pain after a major injury, with inability to walk or bear weight
    \item A foot that feels numb, cold, or turns pale below the injury (possible nerve or blood-vessel damage)
    \item An open wound, visible bone, or suspected fracture after trauma to the ankle
    \item Any sudden, severe, or concerning symptom, including signs of a blood clot such as a hot, swollen, tense calf
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} how the injury happened, the type and timing of pain, and whether there was a snapping sensation
    \item \textbf{Examination:} inspection for swelling, bruising, and a palpable gap, together with assessment of range of motion and strength
    \item \textbf{Thompson (calf squeeze) test:} squeezing the calf should plantarflex the foot; absence of movement suggests a complete rupture
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} shows tendon thickening, tears, and neovascularity, and can monitor healing over time
    \item \textbf{MRI:} provides detailed images of the tendon and surrounding tissues when the diagnosis is uncertain or surgery is planned
    \item \textbf{X-ray:} used mainly to exclude fractures of the heel or ankle bones
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Relative rest:} reduce or modify aggravating activities without complete immobilisation where possible
    \item \textbf{Ice and elevation:} help control pain and swelling in the early stages
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} short-term paracetamol or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, following pharmacy or medical advice
    \item \textbf{Physiotherapy:} the cornerstone of care for tendinopathy, including graduated loading and eccentric (lengthening) exercises
    \item \textbf{Footwear and orthoses:} heel lifts or supportive shoes reduce strain on the tendon
    \item \textbf{Rupture treatment:} non-operative management in a cast or walking boot, or surgical repair; both require structured rehabilitation
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} regular review to monitor healing and guide a graded return to sport
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Complete rupture of the tendon, sometimes without prior warning
    \item Re-rupture, or a new injury in the opposite leg, particularly in the first year
    \item Chronic, persistent tendinopathy that is slow to settle
    \item Persistent loss of push-off strength, even after successful treatment of a rupture
    \item Complications of surgery, including wound infection, nerve injury, and scar adhesions
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most cases of Achilles tendinopathy improve significantly with physiotherapy and activity modification, although symptoms may take several months to settle and can recur if training is increased too quickly. With a rupture, the outlook is generally good for most people, but recovery is slow: a return to full sport typically takes 6 to 12 months, and some permanent weakness of the calf is possible even with optimal treatment. Non-operative and surgical management produce broadly similar long-term results, with the main difference being a slightly higher re-rupture rate in the non-operative group. Early diagnosis, structured rehabilitation, and a patient, gradual return to activity give the best outcomes.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Increase training distance, intensity, and frequency gradually rather than suddenly
    \item Warm up properly and include regular calf stretching, especially before sport
    \item Wear well-fitting, supportive footwear appropriate to the activity
    \item Include calf and foot strengthening exercises in a regular training programme
    \item Cross-train with low-impact activities to reduce repetitive strain
    \item Be cautious after a long break from exercise and when taking medicines that affect tendons
\end{itemize}
