\chapter{Japanese Encephalitis Virus (JEV)}
\index{Japanese Encephalitis Virus (JEV)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV) is a mosquito-borne flavivirus that can cause inflammation of the brain, known as Japanese encephalitis. It is the most common cause of viral encephalitis in Asia, where it is spread between pigs, waterbirds, and Culex mosquitoes, with humans infected incidentally. The virus was first detected in mainland the affected region in 2022, with locally acquired cases in south-eastern some countries, and a vaccine is now recommended for people at risk. Most infections cause no symptoms, but in a minority the virus reaches the brain, causing fever, seizures, confusion, and paralysis, with a substantial risk of death or permanent disability. There is no specific antiviral treatment, so vaccination and mosquito-bite prevention are the main defences.

\section*{Epidemiology}
JEV causes an estimated 70,000 clinical cases and 10,000 to 15,000 deaths each year, mainly in rural Asia, and it is the leading cause of viral encephalitis in children in endemic countries. Transmission occurs where rice paddies, pig farms, and mosquito vectors coexist, and risk is highest in the rainy season. In many countries, no locally acquired cases were reported until 2022, when infections occurred in the Murray River region of New South Wales, Victoria, southern regions, and Queensland, linked to wet conditions and mosquito breeding. Since then, JEV vaccination has been funded for people living in or travelling to at-risk areas.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
JEV is transmitted by the bite of infected Culex mosquitoes, particularly Culex tritaeniorhynchus in Asia and Culex annulirostris in many countries. The virus circulates between mosquitoes and amplifying hosts, mainly pigs and wading birds; humans are dead-end hosts who do not transmit the virus further. After a mosquito bite, the virus multiplies locally and in lymph nodes, then enters the blood. In most people the immune system clears it, causing no or mild illness. In about one in 250 infections, the virus crosses the blood-brain barrier and infects nerve cells, causing encephalitis with inflammation, nerve damage, and brain swelling. The incubation period is 5 to 15 days.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Most infections are asymptomatic or cause a mild flu-like illness
    \item Encephalitis: high fever, severe headache, neck stiffness, and vomiting
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, and reduced consciousness
    \item Seizures, particularly in children
    \item Tremor, rigidity, and paralysis
    \item Sensitivity to light
    \item Coma in severe cases
    \item Long-term effects in survivors: movement disorders, cognitive problems, and behavioural change
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other causes of encephalitis and fever with neurological features must be distinguished.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Murray Valley encephalitis:} a related local mosquito-borne virus
    \item \textbf{Kunjin and West Nile viruses:} related flaviviruses
    \item \textbf{Herpes simplex encephalitis:} a treatable viral cause
    \item \textbf{Bacterial meningitis:} requiring urgent antibiotics
    \item \textbf{Dengue and other febrile illnesses:} common travel-related infections
    \item \textbf{Non-infectious causes:} stroke, tumours, and autoimmune encephalitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with fever and headache after travel to or residence in a risk area should be assessed, and fever with confusion, seizures, weakness, or altered consciousness is an emergency. People planning travel to JEV-affected areas should seek travel health advice about vaccination, and those living or working near pigs and mosquitoes in affected regions should consider vaccination.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item High fever with confusion, drowsiness, or a seizure
    \item Stiff neck, severe headache, and sensitivity to light
    \item Weakness, paralysis, or difficulty speaking
    \item Collapse or loss of consciousness
    \item Difficulty breathing
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} JEV antibodies, with paired samples to confirm recent infection
    \item \textbf{PCR testing:} detection of the virus in blood or cerebrospinal fluid early in illness
    \item \textbf{Lumbar puncture:} cerebrospinal fluid analysis
    \item \textbf{MRI of the brain:} characteristic changes in the thalami, basal ganglia, and brainstem
    \item \textbf{Electroencephalogram:} for seizures
    \item \textbf{Testing for other viruses:} to exclude related and treatable infections
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Supportive hospital care:} the mainstay, as there is no specific antiviral
    \item \textbf{Intensive care:} for seizures, brain swelling, and breathing support
    \item \textbf{Anticonvulsant medicines:} for seizures
    \item \textbf{Management of brain swelling:} medicines and monitoring
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy, speech and occupational therapy, and cognitive support
    \item \textbf{Public health action:} case notification, mosquito control, and vaccination programs
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} monitoring of recovery and long-term effects
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Death in around 20 to 30 per cent of clinical cases
    \item Permanent neurological disability in up to half of survivors
    \item Seizure disorders after recovery
    \item Cognitive and behavioural problems
    \item Prolonged intensive care and rehabilitation
    \item Psychological effects on patients and families
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis depends on the severity of brain involvement. Among people with clinical encephalitis, around one in four dies and up to half of survivors have lasting deficits, though many with milder disease recover fully. Because treatment is supportive, prevention is critical: the JEV vaccine is highly effective, and avoiding mosquito bites reduces exposure. In many countries, funded vaccine programs for at-risk communities and ongoing surveillance aim to prevent further spread.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Get vaccinated if you live, work, or travel in at-risk areas
    \item Seek travel health advice before visiting JEV-affected regions
    \item Use insect repellent containing DEET or picaridin
    \item Wear long sleeves, long pants, and shoes outdoors, especially at dawn and dusk
    \item Sleep under mosquito nets and use screens where mosquitoes are present
    \item Remove standing water around the home
    \item Report fever and headache after exposure promptly
    \item Support community mosquito control programs
\end{itemize}
