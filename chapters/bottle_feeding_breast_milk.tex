\chapter{Bottle Feeding (Breast Milk)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bottle feeding with breast milk means giving a baby expressed breast milk from a bottle rather than, or in addition to, feeding directly from the breast. It is a common, safe, and flexible way to feed, used when a mother is separated from her baby, when a baby cannot latch well, when the baby is premature or unwell, or when the mother returns to work or study. Expressed breast milk retains most of the nutritional and immunological benefits of breastfeeding, so bottle feeding it is a good option whenever direct feeding is difficult or impossible. Bottle feeding expressed milk can be used occasionally, so that partners or carers can share feeding, or as the sole method of feeding. It is a skill that takes practice, and support from a midwife, lactation consultant, or child health nurse makes the transition easier.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Most breastfed babies receive expressed milk from a bottle at some point in their first year. Common reasons include returning to work or study, premature birth, maternal illness, and feeding difficulties such as a poor latch or tongue-tie. Rates of exclusive breastfeeding are highest in the first weeks and decline over the first year, and many mothers who cannot feed directly choose to express instead. Some mothers express exclusively for the whole of the breastfeeding period, while others use bottles occasionally so that others can feed. The proportion of babies receiving breast milk by bottle has risen with the availability of effective electric breast pumps and supportive workplace policies.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Bottle feeding itself is not a disease, but feeding technique and milk handling have direct effects on a baby's health:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Expression:} milk is removed by hand or with a manual or electric breast pump; a correctly fitted flange and a comfortable, regular routine protect supply.
    \item \textbf{Storage:} expressed milk must be stored at the correct temperature and used within the recommended time to prevent bacterial growth.
    \item \textbf{Preparation:} bottles and teats must be cleaned and sterilised, since babies have immature immune systems and are vulnerable to gastroenteritis.
    \item \textbf{Feeding mechanics:} paced bottle feeding allows the baby to control flow and swallowing, avoiding gulping air or taking in too much milk.
    \item \textbf{Breast health:} incomplete removal of milk can lead to blocked ducts, engorgement, or mastitis in the mother.
    \item \textbf{Immune factors:} breast milk provides antibodies, immune cells, and prebiotic sugars that protect against infection; these are preserved through proper expression and storage.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
There are no symptoms of bottle feeding as such; rather, parents watch for signs that feeding is going well or that something needs attention:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Contented feeding with steady sucking and swallowing
    \item Wet and dirty nappies in normal numbers for the baby's age
    \item Steady weight gain along the growth chart
    \item An unsettled baby may signal hunger, too fast a milk flow, or overfeeding
    \item Refusing the bottle may indicate the wrong teat flow, cold milk, or a preference for the breast
    \item Fussiness, wind, or spilling after feeds may suggest the need for slower pacing or more burping
    \item Sore or cracked nipples, breast pain, or fever in the mother may indicate mastitis
    \item Drowsiness, poor feeding, or few wet nappies can indicate underfeeding or illness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Problems seen with bottle feeding can be confused with other causes of unsettled behaviour:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A hungry baby:} growth spurts and feeding demands can look like an inadequate supply.
    \item \textbf{Reflux or wind:} can mimic overfeeding or a poorly fitted teat.
    \item \textbf{Lactose overload or cow's milk intolerance:} can be mistaken for feeding-technique problems.
    \item \textbf{Illness:} ear infections, oral thrush, or a viral infection may make a baby refuse feeds.
    \item \textbf{Poor weight gain:} may reflect technique, supply, or an underlying medical problem such as tongue-tie.
    \item \textbf{Teething:} causes temporary fussiness around feeding.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek advice from a midwife, child health nurse, or lactation consultant if feeding is difficult, painful, or if you are worried about your milk supply or the baby's intake. See a doctor promptly if the baby is feeding much less than usual, is drowsy and hard to wake, has very few wet nappies, is losing weight, or has a fever. Mothers with breast pain, redness, fever, or flu-like symptoms should be assessed for mastitis.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A baby who is floppy, unresponsive, or difficult to wake
    \item Difficulty breathing, choking, or a bluish tint around the mouth
    \item Repeated forceful (projectile) vomiting
    \item Signs of severe dehydration, such as a sunken fontanelle, very dry mouth, or no wet nappies for many hours
    \item A fever in a baby under three months of age
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment of bottle feeding usually involves a feeding review rather than medical tests:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Feeding observation:} watching a feed to check positioning, latch on the teat, pacing, and swallowing.
    \item \textbf{Serial weighing:} repeated, accurate weights confirm expected growth; this is the most important objective measure.
    \item \textbf{Nappy count:} recording wet and dirty nappies over 24 hours gauges milk intake.
    \item \textbf{Breast assessment:} examination for engorgement, blocked ducts, or mastitis in the mother.
    \item \textbf{Review of milk handling:} checking expression technique, storage temperatures, and sterilising routines.
    \item \textbf{Medical review:} investigation of underlying problems such as tongue-tie, thrush, reflux, or poor weight gain, with referral to a paediatrician or lactation specialist as needed.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Bottle feeding expressed milk works best when a few practical habits are followed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Express comfortably:} use a properly fitted flange and express regularly and fully enough to maintain supply.
    \item \textbf{Store milk safely:} refrigerate or freeze expressed milk promptly, label it with the date, and follow current guidance on storage times.
    \item \textbf{Sterilise equipment:} clean and sterilise bottles and teats until the baby is at least twelve months old.
    \item \textbf{Paced bottle feeding:} hold the baby upright, keep the teat filled with milk, and pause regularly to let the baby rest and burp, allowing the baby to control the pace.
    \item \textbf{Warm milk gently:} stand the bottle in warm water; never use a microwave, which heats unevenly and can scald the baby.
    \item \textbf{Discard leftovers:} throw away any milk left in a bottle after a feed rather than refrigerating it again.
    \item \textbf{Offer the breast first:} where possible, breastfeed at feeds to protect supply and the breastfeeding relationship.
    \item \textbf{Get support:} a lactation consultant or child health nurse can help with pumps, technique, and supply concerns.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Problems can arise when technique or milk handling is not ideal:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Mastitis or blocked ducts in the mother if the breasts are not drained adequately
    \item Reduced milk supply if pumping is infrequent or ineffective
    \item Gastroenteritis from poorly cleaned equipment or unsafe milk storage
    \item A preference for the fast flow of the bottle in some babies, which can undermine breastfeeding
    \item Overfeeding, causing excessive weight gain, reflux, or wind
    \item Underfeeding and poor weight gain if the baby cannot manage the flow or the teat is faulty
    \item Ear infections, which can be slightly more common in bottle-fed babies if they feed lying down
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With attention to hygiene, pacing, and milk handling, bottle feeding expressed breast milk is safe, and most babies thrive on it. Breast milk retains its nutritional and immune benefits even when given by bottle, and babies fed this way generally grow and develop normally. Mothers can maintain a good milk supply with regular, effective expression, though some find expressing more demanding than feeding directly. Feeding patterns usually settle within a few weeks, and most difficulties respond to simple adjustments with the help of a lactation professional. Many mothers successfully combine direct feeding and bottles for many months, giving the baby the benefits of breast milk and the family the flexibility of shared feeding.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Establish breastfeeding before introducing a bottle where possible, so that milk supply is well established.
    \item Express regularly and fully to protect supply, and use a pump that fits well.
    \item Follow safe milk handling: wash hands, use clean containers, and store milk at the correct temperature.
    \item Sterilise feeding equipment as recommended until the baby is at least twelve months old.
    \item Learn paced feeding so the baby can control its own intake.
    \item Feed on demand and respond to early hunger cues rather than a strict clock.
    \item Drink water, eat well, and rest to support milk production.
    \item Seek early help for sore nipples, engorgement, or unsettled feeding rather than waiting for problems to worsen.
\end{itemize}
