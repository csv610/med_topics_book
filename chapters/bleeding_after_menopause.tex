\chapter{Bleeding After Menopause}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Bleeding after menopause (postmenopausal bleeding) is any vaginal bleeding that occurs twelve months or more after a woman's final menstrual period. It ranges from light spotting and blood-stained discharge to heavy, sustained bleeding. Although most cases are caused by harmless conditions, postmenopausal bleeding is always taken seriously because it can be the first and only sign of endometrial (womb) cancer. For this reason, all postmenopausal bleeding -- however slight and whatever the cause -- warrants prompt investigation, and national guidelines recommend referral through a rapid-access pathway.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Postmenopausal bleeding is common, affecting approximately 1 in 10 postmenopausal women at some point. The majority of episodes are due to benign conditions, particularly atrophic changes in the vagina and uterine lining. Nevertheless, postmenopausal bleeding is the cardinal symptom of endometrial cancer, and around one in ten women presenting with it is found to have endometrial cancer or precancerous endometrial hyperplasia. The risk of underlying malignancy rises with age, being highest in women over the age of 60, and also with increasing time since the menopause.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
After the menopause the ovaries cease producing oestrogen, and the tissues of the vagina and uterine lining become thin, dry, and fragile, bleeding more easily. The principal causes of postmenopausal bleeding are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Atrophic vaginitis and endometrial atrophy:} thinning and inflammation of fragile tissues, the most common causes
    \item \textbf{Endometrial and cervical polyps:} benign growths that bleed readily
    \item \textbf{Endometrial hyperplasia:} thickening of the uterine lining, some forms of which can progress to cancer
    \item \textbf{Endometrial cancer:} the most important cause to exclude, more likely in women with unopposed oestrogen exposure, obesity, diabetes, or tamoxifen use
    \item \textbf{Hormone replacement therapy:} some continuous and all cyclical regimens can cause bleeding
    \item \textbf{Tamoxifen:} the breast-cancer drug causes stimulation of the uterine lining
    \item \textbf{Infection:} vaginitis, cervicitis, or endometritis
    \item \textbf{Misattribution:} blood from the urinary tract or bowel that is mistaken for vaginal bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The key clinical feature is any bleeding occurring a year or more after the menopause, which may take various forms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Vaginal spotting or staining on underwear or toilet paper
    \item Blood-stained or brown vaginal discharge
    \item Single episodes of bleeding or repeated, ongoing bleeding
    \item Light bleeding after sexual intercourse or after a pelvic examination
    \item Rarely, heavy bleeding with clots
    \item Bleeding may be accompanied by pelvic pain, pressure, or discomfort
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The causes listed above overlap in their presentation, and more than one may coexist; the priority is to distinguish benign atrophic and polyp causes from endometrial cancer.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Atrophic vaginitis and endometrial atrophy
    \item Endometrial or cervical polyps
    \item Endometrial hyperplasia
    \item Endometrial cancer
    \item Cervical cancer and cervical ectropion
    \item Infection of the vagina, cervix, or uterus
    \item Bleeding related to hormone replacement therapy or tamoxifen
    \item Haematuria or rectal bleeding mistaken for vaginal bleeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Any bleeding after the menopause should be reported to a doctor promptly, even a single episode of light spotting. It is not a symptom to wait out, because the earliest stages of endometrial cancer are the most curable, and they are frequently detected only through investigation of bleeding.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Heavy vaginal bleeding with clots, or bleeding that soaks through pads rapidly
    \item Bleeding accompanied by severe pelvic or abdominal pain
    \item Fever with pelvic pain, suggesting infection
    \item Faintness, light-headedness, or dizziness from blood loss
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment is designed to identify the source of bleeding and to exclude cancer with high confidence.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} timing, amount, and recurrence of bleeding; use of hormone replacement therapy, tamoxifen, or anticoagulants; and gynaecological, breast, and family history
    \item \textbf{Physical examination:} speculum inspection of the vulva, vagina, and cervix, and bimanual palpation of the uterus and ovaries
    \item \textbf{Transvaginal ultrasound:} the first-line test; measurement of the endometrial thickness, where a thin lining makes cancer very unlikely and a thickened lining prompts sampling
    \item \textbf{Endometrial biopsy:} a small sample of the uterine lining taken in the outpatient clinic for histology
    \item \textbf{Hysteroscopy:} a camera passed into the uterine cavity to inspect the lining and take targeted biopsies
    \item \textbf{Cervical screening and swabs:} to exclude cervical pathology and infection
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} MRI or CT where a cancer is confirmed, to plan treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management depends on the cause identified, but the underlying principle is that benign causes are treated simply and malignant causes are treated promptly by a specialist team.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Atrophic changes:} vaginal moisturisers, lubricants, and low-dose vaginal oestrogen on medical advice
    \item \textbf{Polyps:} removal, usually by a short outpatient procedure
    \item \textbf{Endometrial hyperplasia:} progestogen therapy for benign hyperplasia, or hysterectomy where there is atypical change
    \item \textbf{Endometrial cancer:} surgery (hysterectomy with removal of both tubes and ovaries), sometimes with radiotherapy or chemotherapy, planned by a gynaecological oncology team
    \item \textbf{Medication review:} adjustment of hormone replacement therapy or specialist advice on tamoxifen
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} repeat review if bleeding continues or recurs after treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Delayed diagnosis of endometrial or cervical cancer, with a worse outcome
    \item Anaemia where bleeding is heavy, prolonged, or repeated
    \item Infection after endometrial biopsy or hysteroscopy (uncommon)
    \item Significant anxiety while awaiting tests and results
    \item Complications of treatment for the underlying cause, including surgical risks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook depends on the underlying cause. For the large majority of women the cause is benign, and symptoms settle with simple measures or a minor procedure. Endometrial cancer confined to the womb is frequently cured, and five-year survival for early-stage disease exceeds 90\%, which is why prompt investigation of postmenopausal bleeding is so important. Where cancer has spread beyond the uterus, the outlook is more guarded, but modern treatment still achieves durable control for many women.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Report any postmenopausal bleeding promptly, no matter how light or infrequent
    \item Attend cervical screening when it is due
    \item Discuss the risks and benefits of hormone replacement therapy with a doctor and review it regularly
    \item If you take tamoxifen, report any bleeding to your breast-care team
    \item Maintain a healthy weight, as obesity raises the risk of endometrial cancer
    \item Keep a simple record of bleeding episodes to describe at your appointment
\end{itemize}
