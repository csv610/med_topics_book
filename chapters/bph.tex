\chapter{Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)}
\index{Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a non-cancerous enlargement of the prostate gland that becomes increasingly common as men age. The prostate is a small, walnut-sized gland that sits below the bladder and surrounds the urethra, the tube that carries urine out of the body. As the gland enlarges, it can compress the urethra and obstruct the flow of urine, producing a group of symptoms collectively known as lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS). The word "benign" is important: BPH is not cancer, does not turn into cancer, and does not increase the risk of prostate cancer, although the two conditions can coexist. BPH can, however, significantly affect quality of life and, in some men, leads to complications such as urinary infection, bladder stones, and acute urinary retention.

\section*{Epidemiology}
BPH is one of the most common conditions of ageing men. Around half of men in their 50s and up to 90\% of men in their 80s have some degree of prostate enlargement, although many have no symptoms. Roughly one in four men with BPH will eventually need treatment. The condition is rare before the age of 40 and its prevalence rises steeply with each decade thereafter. A family history of BPH increases the risk, and obesity, diabetes, and physical inactivity are associated with more troublesome symptoms. The frequency of symptoms in the community means that BPH accounts for a very large number of primary-care consultations and specialist referrals worldwide.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Ageing:} the fundamental risk factor; prostate growth begins in early adulthood and continues for life, and clinical BPH is rare in younger men.
    \item \textbf{Androgens:} testosterone and its active metabolite dihydrotestosterone (DHT) drive prostate cell proliferation throughout adult life; men castrated before puberty do not develop BPH.
    \item \textbf{Genetic factors:} BPH tends to run in families, and a strong family history predicts earlier and more severe symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle:} obesity, metabolic syndrome, and diabetes are associated with faster prostate growth and more severe symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} the inner (transitional) zone of the prostate enlarges and presses on the urethra, while the smooth muscle of the gland also tightens; both components -- static enlargement and dynamic muscle tone -- contribute to obstruction.
    \item \textbf{Bladder response:} the bladder muscle (detrusor) initially works harder to force urine past the obstruction and becomes thickened; over time it can weaken, leaving a residual volume of urine, or become overactive, producing urgency and frequency.
    \item \textbf{Consequences:} progressive obstruction leads to incomplete emptying, recurrent infection, bladder stones, and, in advanced cases, damage to the upper urinary tract and kidneys.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The symptoms of BPH, called lower urinary tract symptoms, fall into two groups. Voiding (obstructive) symptoms include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A weak or interrupted urine stream
    \item Difficulty starting to urinate (hesitancy) and straining to pass urine
    \item Dribbling after urination and a feeling that the bladder is not fully emptied
    \item A prolonged time to finish voiding
\end{itemize}
Storage (irritative) symptoms include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Passing urine more often than usual, including several times at night (nocturia)
    \item Urgency to urinate, with leaking before reaching the toilet
    \item Sometimes blood in the urine
\end{itemize}
Symptoms are typically gradual in onset and worsen over years, although the severity fluctuates, and they can be exacerbated by cold weather, alcohol, and certain medicines.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions cause the same urinary symptoms and must be considered:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prostate cancer:} can present with identical symptoms and must be excluded by assessment including PSA testing.
    \item \textbf{Urinary tract infection and prostatitis:} inflammation of the prostate or bladder causes frequency, urgency, and pain.
    \item \textbf{Urethral stricture:} a narrowing of the urethra from injury or past infection, producing a weak stream.
    \item \textbf{Overactive bladder:} a bladder-muscle problem causing urgency and frequency without obstruction.
    \item \textbf{Medication effects:} decongestants, some antidepressants, and other drugs can impair bladder emptying or increase urine output.
    \item \textbf{Diabetes:} high blood sugar increases urine production and can affect bladder function.
    \item \textbf{Neurological conditions:} spinal cord disease, Parkinson's disease, and stroke can disturb bladder control.
    \item \textbf{Excess fluid intake or diuretics:} cause frequency that mimics bladder storage symptoms.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Men with bothersome urinary symptoms should see a doctor rather than dismissing them as an unavoidable part of ageing, because effective treatment is available and complications can be prevented. New or rapidly worsening symptoms, blood in the urine, or pain with urination also require assessment, as does any change that suggests infection.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Being completely unable to pass urine, with a painfully full bladder, which is a medical emergency
    \item Severe lower abdominal or back pain with fever and chills
    \item Heavy bleeding in the urine, or blood with clots
    \item Inability to urinate together with vomiting, confusion, or other signs of kidney failure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Medical history:} details of the urinary symptoms, fluid intake, medicines, and family history, with a symptom questionnaire such as the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) to grade severity.
    \item \textbf{Digital rectal examination:} the doctor feels the prostate through the rectal wall to assess size and texture and to check for suspicious nodules.
    \item \textbf{Urine tests:} a dipstick and urine sample to exclude infection and blood.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} kidney function, and prostate-specific antigen (PSA) to help assess the risk of prostate cancer.
    \item \textbf{Urinary flow rate:} measuring the speed of urine flow to quantify obstruction.
    \item \textbf{Bladder ultrasound:} checking the volume of urine left in the bladder after voiding (post-void residual).
    \item \textbf{Further tests:} cystoscopy or urodynamic studies in selected men, particularly when the diagnosis is uncertain or surgery is planned.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is guided by how much the symptoms bother the man and whether complications are present:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Watchful waiting:} for men with mild symptoms, with regular review and advice about lifestyle measures.
    \item \textbf{Lifestyle measures:} reducing evening fluid, caffeine, and alcohol; avoiding decongestants and other aggravating medicines; and emptying the bladder fully at each visit.
    \item \textbf{Alpha-blockers:} relax the muscle of the prostate and bladder neck, improving flow within days to weeks.
    \item \textbf{5-alpha reductase inhibitors:} such as finasteride and dutasteride, which slowly shrink the gland over three to six months, most useful in men with larger prostates.
    \item \textbf{Combination therapy:} an alpha-blocker together with a 5-alpha reductase inhibitor for men with significant enlargement and troublesome symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Minimally invasive procedures:} options such as water vapour therapy (Rezum), prostatic urethral lift, or prostate artery embolisation for selected men.
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) or laser prostate surgery for severe symptoms, complications, or failure of medical treatment; these provide the most reliable and durable relief.
    \item \textbf{Catheterisation:} temporary or intermittent catheter use for men who cannot pass urine or who are not fit for other treatment.
    \item \textbf{Regular follow-up:} monitoring the response to treatment and screening for complications.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
Left untreated, BPH can cause complications, and treatment itself carries some risks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Acute urinary retention -- a sudden, painful inability to pass urine requiring catheterisation
    \item Urinary tract infections from incomplete bladder emptying
    \item Bladder stones, formed from urine stagnating in the bladder
    \item Haematuria (blood in the urine), sometimes heavy
    \item Hydronephrosis and chronic kidney disease in advanced, long-standing obstruction
    \item Overflow incontinence, when the overfull bladder leaks urine
    \item Side effects of medicines, including dizziness and low blood pressure with alpha-blockers, and reduced libido or erectile dysfunction with 5-alpha reductase inhibitors
    \item Surgical complications, including bleeding, infection, retrograde ejaculation, and, rarely, incontinence or erectile dysfunction
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
BPH is not life-threatening and does not become cancer, but it usually progresses slowly without treatment. Symptoms may remain stable, worsen gradually, or occasionally improve. Men with mild symptoms often manage well with monitoring, while medication improves symptoms in the majority. Surgery provides the most reliable relief, with most men experiencing a clear and lasting improvement in urine flow and quality of life for many years, and modern minimally invasive techniques offer good results with faster recovery. A minority of men develop recurrent symptoms or complications and require ongoing urological care, so regular follow-up remains important.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a healthy weight and stay physically active.
    \item Limit caffeine and alcohol, especially in the evening, and avoid large drinks before bed.
    \item Pass urine regularly and do not delay when the urge arises.
    \item Take time to empty the bladder fully at each visit.
    \item Avoid medicines that worsen symptoms, such as some cold and allergy decongestants, unless discussed with a doctor.
    \item Manage diabetes and other health conditions well.
    \item See a doctor for a regular check-up, including an informed discussion about PSA testing.
    \item Seek early treatment for new or worsening symptoms rather than waiting for complications such as acute retention.
\end{itemize}
