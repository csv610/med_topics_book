\chapter{Head Injuries}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A head injury is any trauma to the scalp, skull, or brain. Most head injuries are minor, causing a bump, bruise, or cut, and heal without complications. However, some head injuries cause concussion (a temporary disturbance of brain function) or more serious damage, including bleeding inside the skull, which can be life-threatening. Head injuries are common in falls, sport, road crashes, and assaults, and children and older people are at particular risk. Anyone with a head injury that involves loss of consciousness, confusion, vomiting, or other concerning symptoms needs urgent assessment. Most minor head injuries are managed with rest and observation.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Head injuries are very common. Around 1 in 5 Australians experiences a head injury at some stage, and head injuries account for a large number of emergency department presentations each year. Falls are the leading cause, especially in children and older people, followed by sport, road crashes, and assaults. Concussion is estimated to affect many thousands of Australian athletes each year. Traumatic brain injury is a major cause of disability, with road trauma being the leading cause of severe brain injury in young people. Most head injuries are mild and recover fully.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Head injury causes damage through several mechanisms.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Direct impact:} blunt force to the head causes bruising of the scalp, skull fracture, or injury to the brain
    \item \textbf{Acceleration-deceleration:} sudden movement of the head causes the brain to move within the skull, stretching and tearing nerve fibres (concussion and diffuse axonal injury)
    \item \textbf{Concussion:} a temporary disturbance of brain function without structural damage visible on scans
    \item \textbf{Intracranial bleeding:} bleeding between the skull and brain (epidural and subdural haematoma) or within the brain (contusion) raises pressure
    \item \textbf{Brain swelling:} inflammation and swelling of the brain increase pressure inside the skull
    \item \textbf{Secondary injury:} low oxygen, low blood pressure, and raised pressure worsen the initial injury
    \item \textbf{Blood thinners:} medicines such as warfarin increase the risk of bleeding after head injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on the severity of the injury.

\begin{itemize}
    \item A bump, bruise, or cut on the scalp
    \item Headache
    \item Dizziness, nausea, or feeling dazed
    \item Confusion, memory loss, or difficulty concentrating
    \item Loss of consciousness, which may be brief
    \item Vomiting
    \item Blurred or double vision
    \item Sensitivity to light and noise
    \item In serious injuries: worsening headache, repeated vomiting, drowsiness, confusion, seizures, weakness, or unequal pupils
    \item In babies: irritability, poor feeding, a bulging fontanelle, and vomiting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions can present with similar symptoms after a fall or impact.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Neck (cervical spine) injury, which commonly accompanies head injury
    \item Fainting or seizures that caused the fall
    \item Low blood sugar and other metabolic causes of confusion
    \item Stroke, which can cause confusion and falls
    \item Alcohol or drug intoxication
    \item Migraine and other headache disorders
    \item Middle ear problems causing dizziness
    \item Intracranial bleeding, which must be excluded in at-risk people
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Seek medical assessment for any head injury with loss of consciousness, confusion, vomiting, severe headache, or other concerning symptoms, and for injuries in babies, older people, people on blood thinners, and those with bleeding disorders. Even minor injuries in these groups may need assessment.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Loss of consciousness at any point
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or difficulty waking
    \item Repeated vomiting
    \item A seizure or fit
    \item Weakness or numbness on one side of the body
    \item Slurred speech or difficulty understanding
    \item Unequal pupils or vision problems
    \item Clear fluid or blood leaking from the nose or ears
    \item In babies: a bulging fontanelle, high-pitched crying, or poor feeding
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Assessment determines the severity and the need for imaging.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the mechanism of injury, symptoms, and any loss of consciousness
    \item \textbf{Examination:} neurological assessment, pupil responses, and scalp examination
    \item \textbf{Concussion assessment:} standardised tools evaluate memory, balance, and coordination
    \item \textbf{CT scan:} performed when there are risk factors for bleeding, including loss of consciousness, vomiting, age, blood thinners, or concerning examination findings
    \item \textbf{Observation:} people at low risk may be observed at home with clear instructions
    \item \textbf{Repeat assessment:} symptoms are monitored and reviewed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management depends on severity and ranges from home care to emergency treatment.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Minor injuries:} rest, ice, pain relief, and observation at home
    \item \textbf{Concussion:} physical and cognitive rest, then a gradual return to normal activity, including a graduated return to sport
    \item \textbf{Hospital admission:} for people with moderate injury or risk factors, for observation
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} urgent drainage of bleeding (haematoma) when it compresses the brain
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} oxygen, blood pressure management, and intensive care for severe injuries
    \item \textbf{Rehabilitation:} physiotherapy, speech therapy, and neuropsychology for persistent symptoms
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} review for ongoing symptoms such as headache and poor concentration
    \item \textbf{Preventing second concussion:} avoiding contact sport until cleared
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Post-concussion syndrome: persistent headache, dizziness, poor concentration, and mood changes lasting weeks or months
    \item Intracranial bleeding, which can be fatal if not treated urgently
    \item Brain swelling with raised intracranial pressure
    \item Long-term disability from traumatic brain injury
    \item Seizures after severe injury
    \item Second-impact syndrome in athletes who return too soon
    \item Chronic traumatic encephalopathy with repeated concussions
    \item Effects on memory, behaviour, and personality
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most head injuries are minor and people recover fully within days to a few weeks. Concussion typically resolves within one to four weeks, though some people have symptoms for months. Severe head injuries can cause lasting disability, but with modern emergency and rehabilitation care, outcomes have improved. The keys to good outcomes are prompt assessment of serious injuries, avoiding a second concussion, and managing persistent symptoms with a structured return to activity. Children and older people recover well with appropriate care and follow-up.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wear a properly fitted helmet for cycling, skateboarding, skiing, and contact sport
    \item Wear a seatbelt in the car
    \item Prevent falls at home: remove trip hazards, improve lighting, and use handrails
    \item Use appropriate safety equipment at work
    \item Never return to sport after a concussion until cleared by a health professional
    \item After a minor head injury, rest and take simple pain relief
    \item Follow the observation instructions provided by your doctor
    \item Seek urgent care for worsening symptoms
    \item Older people on blood thinners should be assessed after any head injury
\end{itemize}
