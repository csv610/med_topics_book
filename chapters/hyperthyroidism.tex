\chapter{Hyperthyroidism}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Hyperthyroidism is a condition in which the thyroid gland produces too much thyroid hormone, speeding up the body's metabolism. The excess hormone causes symptoms such as weight loss, a rapid heartbeat, sweating, anxiety, and tremor. The most common cause is Graves' disease, an autoimmune condition, and other causes include thyroid nodules and thyroiditis. Hyperthyroidism is treatable with medication, radioactive iodine, or surgery, and most people achieve good control. If untreated, it can cause serious complications, including an irregular heartbeat and thyroid storm.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Hyperthyroidism affects around 1--2\% of the population and is five to ten times more common in women than in men. It can occur at any age but is most common between 20 and 50. Graves' disease accounts for about 70--80\% of cases, and it runs in families. The condition is often detected by blood tests, sometimes before symptoms are noticed. With treatment, most people recover fully, though Graves' disease can recur or lead to underactive thyroid after treatment.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Graves' disease:} autoantibodies stimulate the thyroid to overproduce hormone
    \item \textbf{Toxic nodules:} one or more thyroid nodules produce excess hormone independently
    \item \textbf{Thyroiditis:} inflammation releases stored hormone, causing temporary excess
    \item \textbf{Excess iodine:} rarely, from supplements or medication
    \item \textbf{Pathophysiology:} high thyroid hormone levels increase metabolism in every organ system
    \item \textbf{Graves' eye disease:} the same antibodies can affect the eyes, causing bulging and irritation
    \item \textbf{Thyroid storm:} a rare, life-threatening surge in hormone levels
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Weight loss despite a normal or increased appetite
    \item Rapid or irregular heartbeat and palpitations
    \item Sweating, heat intolerance, and warm moist skin
    \item Anxiety, irritability, and difficulty sleeping
    \item Tremor of the hands
    \item Frequent bowel motions
    \item Fatigue and muscle weakness
    \item In women, lighter or absent periods
    \item In Graves' disease: bulging eyes, and sometimes a goitre
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Anxiety and panic disorder can mimic the symptoms
    \item Menopause symptoms overlap with heat intolerance and palpitations
    \item Weight loss has many causes
    \item Heart rhythm problems may be the first presentation
    \item Thyroid function tests confirm the diagnosis
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for persistent symptoms of weight loss, palpitations, sweating, or anxiety, and for a goitre or eye changes.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chest pain, palpitations with collapse, or breathlessness
    \item High fever, confusion, and agitation (possible thyroid storm)
    \item Sudden vision loss or severe eye pain
    \item Vomiting and severe weakness in a person with known hyperthyroidism
    \item Fainting or loss of consciousness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Thyroid function tests:} low TSH with high free T4 and T3 confirms hyperthyroidism
    \item \textbf{Antibody tests:} thyroid-stimulating antibodies confirm Graves' disease
    \item \textbf{Thyroid ultrasound:} identifies nodules and inflammation
    \item \textbf{Radioactive iodine uptake scan:} distinguishes Graves' disease from nodules and thyroiditis
    \item \textbf{ECG:} detects atrial fibrillation
    \item \textbf{Regular monitoring:} thyroid levels during and after treatment
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Antithyroid medicines:} carbimazole or propylthiouracil reduce hormone production
    \item \textbf{Beta-blockers:} control the rapid heartbeat and tremor until hormone levels fall
    \item \textbf{Radioactive iodine:} destroys overactive thyroid tissue, often causing permanent hypothyroidism requiring thyroxine
    \item \textbf{Surgery:} thyroidectomy for large goitres, nodules, or where other treatment is unsuitable
    \item \textbf{Thyroiditis:} often resolves on its own with symptom control
    \item \textbf{Eye care:} specialist management for Graves' eye disease
    \item \textbf{Monitoring:} regular blood tests and review
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Atrial fibrillation and heart failure
    \item Osteoporosis from prolonged high hormone levels
    \item Graves' eye disease with eye irritation, bulging, and rarely vision loss
    \item Thyroid storm, a life-threatening emergency
    \item Effects of treatment: permanent hypothyroidism after radioactive iodine or surgery
    \item Pregnancy complications if untreated
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most people with hyperthyroidism respond well to treatment. Antithyroid medicines control the condition within weeks, and around half of people with Graves' disease achieve long-term remission. Radioactive iodine and surgery provide a permanent cure, usually by making the thyroid underactive, which is then simply replaced with thyroxine. With treatment, symptoms resolve and complications are prevented. The outlook is excellent with appropriate care.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seek early review of symptoms
    \item Take medicines exactly as prescribed and attend blood tests
    \item Avoid excess iodine from supplements
    \item Manage stress and get adequate rest
    \item Protect bone health with calcium, vitamin D, and exercise
    \item Follow up after treatment to detect hypothyroidism
    \item In pregnancy, have thyroid levels monitored
    \item Seek urgent care for fever, confusion, or chest pain
\end{itemize}
