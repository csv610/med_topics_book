\chapter{Copd Treatment}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
COPD treatment encompasses a comprehensive, multi-modal therapeutic framework designed to reduce symptoms, decrease the frequency and severity of acute exacerbations, improve exercise tolerance, and optimise overall health status. Because lung damage in Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease is largely irreversible, the treatment focus is on clinical management, halting disease progression, and improving quality of life. The therapeutic landscape integrates pharmacological interventions, such as inhaled bronchodilators and anti-inflammatory agents, with non-pharmacological strategies, including smoking cessation, pulmonary rehabilitation, long-term oxygen therapy, and, in selected cases, surgical or interventional bronchoscopic procedures. Treatment must be individualised, reflecting the patient's symptom burden, risk of exacerbations, and specific disease phenotype.

\section*{Epidemiology}
The epidemiology of COPD treatment highlight significant challenges in clinical adherence, access to services, and economic burden. In Australia, despite the high efficacy of maintenance therapies, studies indicate that up to 50\% of patients do not adhere to their prescribed inhaler regimen, and incorrect inhaler technique is observed in over 70\% of users. Access to pulmonary rehabilitation programs remains low, with only a small fraction of eligible patients completing a course, primarily due to geographical and socioeconomic barriers. Hospitalisations for acute exacerbations of COPD represent one of the leading causes of preventable, high-cost medical admissions in Australia, underscoring the critical need for effective outpatient treatment implementation.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
COPD treatment targets specific physiological mechanisms to relieve airflow limitation, counter airway inflammation, and prevent systemic decline.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bronchoconstriction and smooth muscle tone:} Inhaled bronchodilators target G-protein coupled receptors on airway smooth muscle. Long-acting muscarinic antagonists (LAMAs) block M3 acetylcholine receptors to inhibit vagal bronchoconstriction, while long-acting beta2-agonists (LABAs) stimulate beta2-adrenergic receptors to promote smooth muscle relaxation.
    \item \textbf{Airway mucosal inflammation:} Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) are used to target persistent eosinophilic inflammation, suppress inflammatory gene expression, and reduce mucosal oedema and mucus hypersecretion.
    \item \textbf{Chronic hypoxaemia:} In advanced disease, destruction of the alveolar-capillary membrane impairs gas exchange. Long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT) targets arterial hypoxaemia to prevent hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction and reduce right ventricular workload.
    \item \textbf{Deconditioning and muscle wasting:} Chronic dyspnoea leads to physical inactivity and subsequent skeletal muscle deconditioning. Pulmonary rehabilitation targets this cycle by improving muscular oxidative capacity and cardiovascular efficiency.
    \item \textbf{Infective exacerbation triggers:} Viral and bacterial infections trigger acute inflammatory surges in the airways. Treatment with antibiotics and systemic corticosteroids targets bacterial eradication and rapid suppression of severe inflammation during flares.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features of a patient undergoing COPD treatment involve indicators of therapeutic response, signs of drug-related side effects, and triggers for treatment escalation.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Therapeutic response signs:} Successful treatment is characterized by a reduction in exertional dyspnoea, decreased cough frequency, easier clearance of sputum, improved walking distance, and a reduced reliance on short-acting reliever inhalers.
    \item \textbf{Inhaler side effects:} Patients may present with fine muscle tremor or mild tachycardia (beta-agonist effect), dry mouth or urinary hesitancy (anticholinergic effect), or oral candidiasis (thrush) and skin bruising (inhaled corticosteroid effect).
    \item \textbf{Systemic corticosteroid signs:} Prolonged oral steroid use during exacerbations can lead to features of Cushingoid changes, fluid retention, hyperglycaemia, and mood disturbances.
    \item \textbf{Exacerbation warning signs:} Clinical features indicating a treatment failure or acute flare include a progressive increase in dyspnoea, increased sputum volume, change in sputum colour to green or yellow (purulence), and chest tightness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
When managing a patient whose COPD symptoms do not respond to standard treatment, clinicians must differentiate treatment strategies and rule out co-existing pathologies.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Asthma-COPD Overlap (ACO):} Requires early introduction of inhaled corticosteroids alongside bronchodilators, whereas pure COPD is managed initially with bronchodilators alone.
    \textbf{Congestive heart failure:} Exertional dyspnoea unresponsive to bronchodilators may be due to left ventricular dysfunction, requiring diuretics and ACE inhibitors rather than respiratory therapy escalation.
    \item \textbf{Pulmonary embolism:} Sudden worsening of dyspnoea that does not respond to increased doses of short-acting bronchodilators must be differentiated from an acute COPD exacerbation.
    \item \textbf{Chronic bronchitic vs. emphysematous phenotypes:} Patients with chronic productive cough benefit more from mucolytics and phosphodiesterase-4 inhibitors, whereas those with severe emphysema and hyperinflation are candidates for lung volume reduction.
    \item \textbf{Vocal cord dysfunction:} Can mimic refractory COPD wheezing but does not respond to bronchodilators; diagnosed by laryngoscopy and managed with speech therapy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Patients should seek a review from their GP if they find their reliever inhaler is less effective, if they are experiencing frequent mild flare-ups, or if they develop side effects from their medications.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe breathlessness that does not improve after using a reliever inhaler (e.g., 6 to 8 puffs of salbutamol)
    \item Inability to speak a full sentence without pausing for breath, or extreme difficulty swallowing
    \item Signs of carbon dioxide retention, including severe confusion, drowsiness, extreme lethargy, or a flapping tremor of the hands (asterixis)
    \item Sudden, sharp chest pain or a sensation of choking
    \item Bluish discoloration of the lips, tongue, or fingertips (cyanosis)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Monitoring the efficacy and safety of COPD treatment requires regular diagnostic assessment.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Serial spirometry:} Performed annually to monitor the rate of FEV1 decline and evaluate response to bronchodilator therapy.
    \item \textbf{Blood eosinophil count:} A simple blood test used to predict the likelihood of a beneficial response to the addition of inhaled corticosteroids (ICS is highly recommended if blood eosinophils are $\ge$ 300\,cells/$\mu$L).
    \item \textbf{6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT):} Evaluates functional exercise capacity and monitors for exertional oxygen desaturation, guiding the prescription of ambulatory oxygen.
    \item \textbf{Arterial Blood Gas (ABG) analysis:} Essential to confirm chronic daytime hypoxaemia (PaO2 $\le$ 55\,mmHg, or $\le$ 59\,mmHg with polycythaemia or cor pulmonale) to qualify the patient for long-term oxygen therapy.
    \item \textbf{Inhaler technique assessment:} Direct observation of the patient using their inhaler device, which must be performed at every clinical consultation.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
COPD management follows a stepped care plan, combining daily maintenance medication with non-pharmacological support and emergency action plans.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Maintenance pharmacotherapy:} Stepwise escalation starting with a single long-acting bronchodilator (LAMA or LABA), moving to dual bronchodilator therapy (LAMA+LABA) if symptoms persist, and triple therapy (LAMA+LABA+ICS) for patients with recurrent exacerbations and blood eosinophilia.
    \item \textbf{Short-acting relievers:} As-needed inhalation of short-acting beta2-agonists (SABA, e.g., salbutamol) or short-acting muscarinic antagonists (SAMA, e.g., ipratropium) for rapid symptom relief.
    \item \textbf{Pulmonary rehabilitation:} Completion of an 8- to 12-week supervised exercise and education programme is highly effective and recommended for all patients with symptomatic COPD.
    \item \textbf{Oxygen and ventilatory support:} Long-term oxygen therapy (used for $\ge$ 15 hours per day) for severe resting hypoxaemia. Domiciliary non-invasive ventilation (NIV) may be considered for patients with chronic, severe daytime hypercapnia.
    \item \textbf{Surgical and interventional therapies:} Endobronchial valve placement or lung volume reduction surgery (LVRS) for patients with severe heterogeneous emphysema to reduce hyperinflation. Lung transplantation is considered for eligible patients with end-stage disease.
    \item \textbf{Exacerbation management:} Prompt treatment of acute flares with oral systemic corticosteroids (e.g., prednisolone 30\,mg to 50\,mg daily for 5 days) and oral antibiotics (e.g., amoxicillin or doxycycline) if sputum volume and purulence increase.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
The therapies used to treat COPD can themselves lead to significant clinical complications.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pneumonia risk:} Inhaled corticosteroids are associated with a dose-dependent increase in the risk of non-fatal pneumonia in patients with COPD.
    \item \textbf{Systemic steroid toxicity:} Recurrent courses of oral prednisolone accelerate osteoporosis, increase cataract risk, worsen glycaemic control in diabetes, and cause proximal muscle weakness.
    \item \textbf{Cardiac arrhythmias:} Overuse of beta2-agonists can precipitate tachyarrhythmias, including atrial fibrillation, particularly in patients with pre-existing ischaemic heart disease.
    \item \textbf{Anticholinergic side effects:} LAMAs can cause urinary retention, particularly in men with prostatic hypertrophy, and may worsen narrow-angle glaucoma if sprayed directly into the eyes.
    \item \textbf{Oxygen-induced hypercapnia:} In patients with chronic carbon dioxide retention, uncontrolled high-flow oxygen therapy can suppress the hypoxic respiratory drive and worsen respiratory acidosis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Appropriate COPD treatment significantly alters the natural history of the disease. While no drug therapy has been conclusively shown to reverse the long-term decline in lung function, combined LAMA+LABA+ICS therapy has been demonstrated to reduce all-cause mortality in patients with severe airflow limitation. Smoking cessation combined with long-term oxygen therapy in hypoxic patients significantly prolongs life. Pulmonary rehabilitation achieves clinically meaningful improvements in dyspnoea and exercise capacity, surpassing the effect sizes of most pharmacological agents.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
Self-care strategies are essential to optimise treatment delivery and prevent acute hospitalisations.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Master inhaler technique:} Check and practice the specific steps for each inhaler (e.g., checking spacer use, ensuring deep inhalation, and rinsing the mouth after steroid inhalers to prevent thrush).
    \item \textbf{Utilise a written COPD Action Plan:} Recognise personal baseline symptoms and initiate prescribed action steps (such as starting oral steroids or antibiotics) immediately when an exacerbation begins.
    \item \textbf{Maintain up-to-date vaccinations:} Receive the annual influenza vaccine, up-to-date pneumococcal vaccines, and recommended COVID-19 doses to prevent severe respiratory infections.
    \item \textbf{Perform daily chest clearance:} Patients with high sputum volume should practice active cycle of breathing techniques or use positive expiratory pressure (PEP) devices to clear secretions.
    \item \textbf{Stay active and hydrated:} Engage in home-based exercise daily and drink adequate water to thin bronchial secretions, making them easier to cough up.
\end{itemize}
