\chapter{Kidney Failure Treatment}
\index{Kidney Failure Treatment}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Treatment for kidney failure aims to replace the filtering work of the kidneys and manage the complications of their failure. For chronic end-stage kidney disease, the two main options are dialysis, which mechanically filters the blood, and kidney transplantation, which replaces the failed kidneys with a healthy donor kidney. Treatment also includes medicines and lifestyle measures that slow the decline, manage fluid, blood pressure, anaemia, bone disease, and other complications, and acute kidney failure is treated by addressing the cause while supporting the kidneys. Choosing among treatments involves the person's health, preferences, and lifestyle, and most people are supported by a multidisciplinary kidney team.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Around 25,000 people are treated for end-stage kidney disease, with roughly equal numbers on dialysis and living with a functioning transplant. About two-thirds of people starting treatment begin with dialysis, and transplantation is the most common long-term goal. The incidence of treated kidney failure is around 110 per million people per year and is higher in Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples. Acute kidney failure affects many hospitalised patients, and most recover with treatment. Outcomes on dialysis have improved, and transplant survival rates are excellent.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Kidney failure treatment replaces functions that the failed kidneys can no longer perform: removing waste products and excess fluid, balancing electrolytes, and producing hormones that regulate blood counts and bone health. Haemodialysis filters the blood through a machine via an access point, usually in the arm, three times a week. Peritoneal dialysis uses the lining of the abdomen as a filter, with fluid instilled and drained through a catheter, often overnight. A kidney transplant replaces the kidneys entirely, providing full function from a living or deceased donor. Supporting medicines treat high blood pressure, anaemia with erythropoietin and iron, bone disease with phosphate binders and vitamin D, and fluid overload with diuretics.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Treatment is offered when kidney function falls below about 10 to 15 per cent of normal
    \item Haemodialysis: sessions of about four hours, three times a week, at a centre or at home
    \item Peritoneal dialysis: exchanges of fluid through an abdominal catheter, often overnight
    \item Kidney transplant: major surgery followed by lifelong anti-rejection medicines
    \item Medicines manage blood pressure, fluid, anaemia, bone disease, and cholesterol
    \item Dietary changes: control of salt, potassium, phosphate, and protein
    \item Conservative care: symptom management without dialysis for those who choose it
    \item Acute kidney failure: supportive care with fluids and treatment of the cause
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The choice of treatment is individualised.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Transplantation:} the best survival and quality of life, for suitable people
    \item \textbf{Home dialysis:} peritoneal or home haemodialysis, offering flexibility
    \item \textbf{Centre haemodialysis:} supervised treatment at a dialysis unit
    \item \textbf{Conservative care:} for frail people or those who decline dialysis
    \item \textbf{Acute treatment:} temporary dialysis for acute kidney failure
    \item \textbf{Suitability factors:} age, health, lifestyle, and support
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
People with kidney failure should be managed by a kidney team and attend regular reviews. Urgent care is needed for symptoms of fluid overload, high potassium, or worsening health: breathlessness, swelling, confusion, vomiting, or a very high blood pressure. Dialysis patients should seek urgent review for access problems, fever, or signs of infection.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing or severe swelling, which may indicate fluid overload
    \item Confusion, drowsiness, or a seizure
    \item Chest pain or a racing, irregular heartbeat, which may indicate high potassium
    \item Fever, shaking, or signs of infection of a dialysis access or catheter
    \item Vomiting with an inability to keep fluids down
    \item Collapse or loss of consciousness
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} creatinine, eGFR, potassium, and other electrolytes
    \item \textbf{Monitoring of dialysis:} dialysis adequacy and blood tests before sessions
    \item \textbf{Assessment of access:} ultrasound and review of fistulas, grafts, and catheters
    \item \textbf{Transplant work-up:} blood tests, heart and other assessments, and tissue typing
    \item \textbf{Imaging:} ultrasound and other scans as indicated
    \item \textbf{Review of complications:} anaemia, bone disease, and heart disease
    \item \textbf{Planning discussions:} with the kidney team about treatment choices
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Haemodialysis:} vascular access creation, regular sessions, and monitoring
    \item \textbf{Peritoneal dialysis:} catheter placement, training, and home treatment
    \item \textbf{Transplantation:} assessment, waiting list or live donor evaluation, surgery, and immunosuppression
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} blood pressure, diuretics, erythropoietin, iron, phosphate binders, and vitamin D
    \item \textbf{Diet and fluids:} individualised to the treatment and blood results
    \item \textbf{Treatment of complications:} heart disease, infection, and malnutrition
    \item \textbf{Acute kidney failure:} fluids, stopping harmful medicines, and treatment of the cause
    \item \textbf{Psychosocial support:} counselling, peer support, and advance care planning
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Dialysis-related: access infection and failure, low blood pressure, cramps, and fatigue
    \item Peritonitis in peritoneal dialysis
    \item Rejection and infection in transplant recipients
    \item Side effects of immunosuppressive medicines
    \item Anaemia, bone disease, and malnutrition
    \item Heart disease and fluid overload
    \item Depression, anxiety, and effects on work and family
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Survival and quality of life are best with transplantation, with most grafts lasting a decade or more and recipients enjoying near-normal health. Dialysis sustains life for many years for most people, with home dialysis offering flexibility and better outcomes in some studies. Acute kidney failure resolves in the majority with treatment. Conservative care, with symptom management and support, provides dignity and comfort for those who choose it. The key is informed choice, early planning, and a strong support team.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Attend all kidney team reviews and dialysis sessions
    \item Take medicines as prescribed and report side effects
    \item Follow the diet and fluid plan
    \item Care for dialysis access and catheters, and report problems early
    \item Consider transplant assessment early
    \item Stay active within your capacity
    \item Manage blood pressure and blood sugar
    \item Seek urgent care for fluid overload, fever, or access problems
    \item Access counselling and peer support
    \item Discuss end-of-life preferences with the team
\end{itemize}
