\chapter{Diabetes}
\index{Diabetes}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Diabetes is a chronic condition in which the level of glucose (sugar) in the blood is too high because the body cannot produce enough insulin, cannot use insulin effectively, or both. Insulin is a hormone made by the pancreas that allows glucose to enter cells to be used for energy. The two main types are type 1 diabetes, an autoimmune condition that usually begins in childhood or young adulthood and requires lifelong insulin, and type 2 diabetes, which develops when the body becomes resistant to insulin and typically affects adults, though increasingly younger people. Diabetes, if poorly managed, damages the blood vessels, nerves, eyes, kidneys, and heart over time, but good management allows people to live long, healthy lives.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Diabetes is one of the fastest-growing chronic diseases worldwide. Type 2 diabetes accounts for most cases and is associated with age, family history, body composition, physical inactivity, and social and environmental factors. Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune condition that can occur at any age. Rates and access to diagnosis and treatment vary between countries and populations.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The two main types of diabetes have different causes but share the result of elevated blood glucose.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Type 1 diabetes:} the immune system mistakenly attacks and destroys the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas; the cause is not fully understood but involves genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers
    \item \textbf{Type 2 diabetes:} the body's cells become resistant to insulin, and the pancreas gradually loses its ability to produce enough insulin to compensate
    \item \textbf{Gestational diabetes:} diabetes that develops during pregnancy because pregnancy hormones make the body more insulin-resistant
    \item \textbf{Risk factors for type 2:} overweight and obesity, especially around the waist, physical inactivity, family history, age, ethnicity, and a history of gestational diabetes
    \item \textbf{Consequences of high glucose:} high blood glucose damages the small blood vessels of the eyes, kidneys, and nerves, and accelerates disease of the larger vessels supplying the heart, brain, and legs
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Symptoms depend on the type of diabetes and how high the blood glucose has risen.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Increased thirst and passing large volumes of urine
    \item Unexplained weight loss
    \item Fatigue and weakness
    \item Blurred vision
    \item Slow-healing wounds and frequent infections
    \item Tingling or numbness in the hands and feet
    \item In type 1 diabetes, symptoms often develop quickly and can progress to a medical emergency called diabetic ketoacidosis, with nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and rapid breathing
    \item Type 2 diabetes often develops gradually, and many people have no symptoms at all for years
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions can cause similar symptoms or affect blood glucose.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Prediabetes, with blood glucose above normal but not in the diabetic range
    \item Diabetes insipidus, a rare condition causing thirst and excessive urine, unrelated to glucose
    \item Other causes of fatigue, weight loss, or frequent urination, including thyroid disease, chronic infection, and kidney disease
    \item Stress hyperglycaemia, a temporary rise in blood glucose during illness
    \item Medication-induced hyperglycaemia, such as from corticosteroids
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with symptoms of diabetes, risk factors for type 2 diabetes, or a family history should have blood glucose checked. People with diabetes need regular reviews of their blood glucose control, blood pressure, cholesterol, feet, eyes, and kidneys.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Signs of diabetic ketoacidosis: nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, rapid breathing, fruity breath, or confusion
    \item Very low blood glucose (hypoglycaemia) with unconsciousness, seizures, or inability to swallow
    \item Chest pain, breathlessness, or signs of stroke
    \item Fainting or collapse
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diabetes is diagnosed with blood tests, usually repeated to confirm.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fasting blood glucose:} a fasting level of 7.0 mmol/L or higher indicates diabetes
    \item \textbf{Oral glucose tolerance test:} blood glucose measured two hours after a glucose drink
    \item \textbf{HbA1c:} a blood test that reflects average glucose over the previous three months; 6.5 per cent or higher indicates diabetes
    \item \textbf{Random blood glucose:} used when symptoms are present
    \item \textbf{Monitoring tests once diagnosed:} regular HbA1c, blood pressure, cholesterol, kidney tests, eye examinations, and foot checks
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Diabetes is managed with a combination of lifestyle and medication, tailored to the individual.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Healthy eating:} a balanced diet with regular meals, controlled portions of carbohydrates, and plenty of vegetables, wholegrains, and lean protein
    \item \textbf{Physical activity:} regular exercise improves insulin sensitivity and glucose control
    \item \textbf{Weight management:} achieving a healthy weight is one of the most powerful treatments for type 2 diabetes
    \item \textbf{Insulin:} essential for type 1 diabetes and sometimes needed in type 2; delivered by injection or pump
    \item \textbf{Oral medicines:} tablets such as metformin and newer agents that lower blood glucose and often support weight loss and heart health
    \item \textbf{Blood glucose monitoring:} regular self-monitoring, including continuous glucose monitors for many people
    \item \textbf{Management of risk factors:} blood pressure, cholesterol, and smoking cessation to protect the heart and blood vessels
    \item \textbf{Education and support:} diabetes education, specialist clinics, and support groups improve outcomes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Eye disease (retinopathy), which can cause blindness
    \item Kidney disease (nephropathy), which can progress to kidney failure
    \item Nerve damage (neuropathy), causing tingling, pain, and numbness, particularly in the feet
    \item Foot problems, including ulcers and amputation
    \item Heart attack, stroke, and peripheral vascular disease
    \item Hypoglycaemia, dangerously low blood glucose from insulin or certain tablets
    \item Ketoacidosis in type 1 diabetes
    \item Mental health effects, including diabetes distress and depression
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With good management, people with diabetes can live long and full lives, and the risk of complications falls dramatically when blood glucose, blood pressure, and cholesterol are well controlled. Type 2 diabetes can even go into remission with significant weight loss. The key is early diagnosis, consistent self-care, and regular medical review. Modern treatments, including glucose monitors and new medicines, have transformed the outlook.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Maintain a healthy weight through balanced eating and regular activity
    \item Follow a balanced eating pattern suited to your culture, health needs, and access to food; limit sugary drinks and highly processed foods
    \item Have blood glucose checked if you have risk factors for type 2 diabetes
    \item Take medicines as prescribed and attend all diabetes reviews
    \item Check feet daily for cuts, blisters, and changes, and wear well-fitted shoes
    \item Carry treatment for hypoglycaemia and wear identification if you use insulin
    \item Manage stress and seek support for the emotional side of living with diabetes
\end{itemize}
