\chapter{Eye Discharge}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Eye discharge is the material that collects in the corner of the eye or along the eyelashes. It can be watery, sticky, stringy, or purulent, and its appearance and colour provide clues to the cause. The most common causes are conjunctivitis (pink eye), blocked tear ducts, allergies, and eye irritation. Most causes of eye discharge are minor and settle on their own, but some require treatment, and a red, painful eye with discharge should always be assessed.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Eye discharge is extremely common, particularly in children, in whom viral and bacterial conjunctivitis spread easily through child care and schools. Allergic eye disease affects many people, especially in spring, and blocked tear ducts are common in newborns. Most cases are mild and self-limiting, but eye infections are a common reason for GP and pharmacy visits.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Discharge reflects what is happening on the surface of the eye.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Viral conjunctivitis:} usually watery discharge with a red, gritty eye, often with a cold
    \item \textbf{Bacterial conjunctivitis:} thick, yellow-green, crusty discharge, often with the eyelids stuck together in the morning
    \item \textbf{Allergic conjunctivitis:} watery, stringy discharge with intense itching
    \item \textbf{Blocked tear duct:} persistent watering with discharge in babies
    \item \textbf{Dry eye:} can cause a stringy discharge as the eye reacts to dryness
    \item \textbf{Mechanism:} inflammation of the conjunctiva or blockage of the drainage increases secretions, which collect as discharge
    \item \textbf{Spread:} viral and bacterial conjunctivitis are contagious and spread by hand contact
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The features depend on the cause.
\begin{itemize}
    \item Watery discharge with a red, gritty eye suggests a virus
    \item Thick, crusty, yellow-green discharge suggests bacteria
    \item Itchy eyes with watery, stringy discharge suggest allergy
    \item Crusting on the lashes, especially on waking
    \item Sticking together of the eyelids in the morning
    \item Redness, tearing, and mild discomfort
    \item In babies: persistent watering with discharge, which may indicate a blocked tear duct
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other conditions cause discharge or redness.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A foreign body in the eye
    \item Keratitis, infection of the cornea, which is serious and causes pain and reduced vision
    \item Acute glaucoma, with severe pain and redness
    \item Iritis and uveitis
    \item Blepharitis, inflammation of the eyelid margins
    \item A red eye from trauma
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor for discharge with pain, reduced vision, or a red eye that does not settle, and for discharge in a newborn, a contact lens wearer, or someone with a weakened immune system.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe eye pain
    \item Sudden loss or change of vision
    \item A red eye with severe pain, nausea, and blurred vision
    \item A chemical injury to the eye
    \item Discharge with severe headache and fever
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is usually clinical.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} the type of discharge, itching, pain, and contact with others with red eyes
    \item \textbf{Examination:} inspection of the eye, lids, and discharge
    \item \textbf{Swabs:} rarely needed, for severe or recurrent bacterial infection
    \item \textbf{Assessment of the cornea:} to exclude serious infection
    \item \textbf{Tear duct assessment:} in babies with persistent watering
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment depends on the cause.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Self-care:} gentle cleaning of the discharge with a clean cloth and warm water
    \item \textbf{Viral conjunctivitis:} settles on its own within one to two weeks; cool compresses help
    \item \textbf{Bacterial conjunctivitis:} antibiotic drops or ointment, as prescribed
    \item \textbf{Allergic conjunctivitis:} antihistamine or anti-allergy drops
    \item \textbf{Blocked tear duct:} gentle massage and most settle in the first year
    \item \textbf{Hygiene:} wash hands frequently and avoid sharing towels to prevent spread
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Spread of infection to others, especially in households and child care
    \item Recurrent infection if hygiene is poor
    \item Corneal infection, particularly in contact lens wearers
    \item Blocked tear duct requiring probing if persistent
    \item Chronic eye irritation
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most eye discharge settles completely within days to a couple of weeks. Viral conjunctivitis resolves on its own, bacterial conjunctivitis responds quickly to antibiotics, and allergic discharge settles with anti-allergy treatment. With good hygiene, spread to others is prevented, and serious complications are rare.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wash hands often, especially after touching the eyes
    \item Do not share towels, pillows, or eye cosmetics
    \item Discard eye makeup and replace contact lens solution after an infection
    \item Clean the discharge gently with warm water
    \item Use cool compresses for comfort
    \item Do not wear contact lenses until the eye has settled
    \item Seek assessment for a red, painful eye
\end{itemize}
