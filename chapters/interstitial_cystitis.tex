\chapter{Interstitial Cystitis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Interstitial cystitis, also called painful bladder syndrome or bladder pain syndrome, is a chronic condition characterised by bladder pressure, pain, and urinary frequency and urgency, in the absence of infection or other identifiable cause. The pain typically increases as the bladder fills and eases after passing urine. The cause is not fully understood, and diagnosis is made after other conditions are excluded. Interstitial cystitis is not an infection and is not contagious, and it is not a cancer. It is a chronic condition, but most people improve with a combination of self-care, medicines, and specialist treatments. It is often managed by urologists and continence specialists.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Interstitial cystitis is more common than once thought, affecting around 1 in 100 to 1 in 500 people, with women affected several times more often than men. It can begin at any age but is most commonly diagnosed between 30 and 60. Many people experience symptoms for years before diagnosis, and the condition is associated with other chronic pain and sensitivity conditions, including irritable bowel syndrome, fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue, and migraine. It significantly affects quality of life, sleep, work, and intimate relationships.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The exact cause of interstitial cystitis is unknown, and it is likely a syndrome with several contributing mechanisms rather than a single disease. The protective lining of the bladder may be defective, allowing urine components to irritate the bladder wall; the nerves supplying the bladder may be hypersensitive, amplifying pain signals; and there may be a local allergic or inflammatory response. Mast cells, immune cells involved in allergy and inflammation, are increased in the bladder wall in some people. Chronic pain mechanisms, including central sensitisation, mean the pain can persist and spread even when the bladder is not obviously inflamed. No single cause explains all cases, which is why treatment is individualised.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bladder pain, pressure, or discomfort that worsens as the bladder fills
    \item Relief of pain after passing urine
    \item Urinary frequency, often passing small amounts many times a day and night
    \item Urgency, a sudden strong need to pass urine
    \item Pain in the pelvis, lower abdomen, urethra, or genitals
    \item Pain with intercourse
    \item Symptoms that fluctuate, with flares and periods of relative relief
    \item Aggravation by certain foods, stress, and menstruation in some women
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The diagnosis of interstitial cystitis is made after excluding other causes of bladder symptoms.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Urinary tract infection:} excluded by urine culture
    \item \textbf{Overactive bladder:} urgency and frequency without significant pain
    \item \textbf{Bladder cancer:} blood in the urine and older age warrant investigation
    \item \textbf{Endometriosis and pelvic inflammatory disease:} in women, pelvic pain with other features
    \item \textbf{Prostatitis:} in men, a similar chronic pain syndrome
    \item \textbf{Radiation or drug-induced cystitis:} after pelvic radiotherapy or certain medicines
    \item \textbf{Urethral syndrome and pelvic floor dysfunction:} overlapping pain conditions
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with persistent bladder pain, frequency, or urgency should be assessed, since these symptoms can also signal infection or other conditions. Urgent review is needed for blood in the urine, fever, or severe pain. People with interstitial cystitis should have ongoing specialist care and should seek review when symptoms flare or new symptoms develop.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Inability to pass urine with severe pain, which may indicate retention
    \item High fever with loin or pelvic pain, which may indicate kidney infection
    \item Passing large amounts of blood in the urine
    \item Sudden severe pelvic or abdominal pain
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and bladder diary:} pattern of pain, frequency, and triggers
    \item \textbf{Urinalysis and culture:} to exclude infection
    \item \textbf{Cystoscopy:} examination of the bladder under anaesthetic, which may show characteristic changes
    \item \textbf{Bladder distension:} performed during cystoscopy, which can both assess and sometimes improve symptoms
    \item \textbf{Urodynamics:} bladder function testing in selected cases
    \item \textbf{Exclusion of other conditions:} imaging and, where indicated, biopsy
    \item \textbf{Assessment of associated conditions:} screening for pelvic floor, bowel, and pain syndromes
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Self-care and diet:} avoiding trigger foods such as caffeine, alcohol, citrus, and spicy food
    \item \textbf{Bladder training and fluid management:} scheduled voiding and adequate but not excessive fluids
    \item \textbf{Pelvic floor physiotherapy:} releasing tight pelvic floor muscles, which often contribute
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} oral treatments to reduce pain, inflammation, and bladder lining irritation
    \item \textbf{Bladder installations:} medicines placed directly into the bladder
    \item \textbf{Pain management:} a multidisciplinary approach including psychology for chronic pain
    \item \textbf{Procedures:} bladder distension, neuromodulation, and in severe cases surgery
    \item \textbf{Stress management:} relaxation, mindfulness, and pacing activity
    \item \textbf{Support groups:} connecting with others living with the condition
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Chronic pain and disability
    \item Sleep disturbance and fatigue
    \item Depression, anxiety, and reduced quality of life
    \item Effects on work, relationships, and intimacy
    \item Frequent courses of antibiotics for symptoms that are mistaken for infection
    \item Reduced bladder capacity in severe long-standing disease
    \item Complications of invasive treatments
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Interstitial cystitis is a chronic condition that fluctuates, but most people improve with treatment. There is no cure, and treatment aims to control symptoms and maintain quality of life. Symptoms may flare and remit over years, and a minority experience progressive disease requiring more intensive therapy. Multidisciplinary care, combining bladder-directed treatment with pain and psychological management, offers the best outcomes, and most people continue to work, socialise, and enjoy relationships.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Keep a symptom and food diary to identify personal triggers
    \item Avoid foods and drinks that worsen symptoms, especially caffeine and alcohol
    \item Drink fluids steadily through the day without excessive volumes
    \item Practise relaxation and stress management, as stress flares symptoms
    \item Use pelvic floor physiotherapy to release tight muscles
    \item Pace activities to avoid overdoing things on good days
    \item Take medicines as prescribed and discuss new options with the specialist
    \item Seek psychological support for pain and mood
    \item Connect with support groups for information and encouragement
\end{itemize}
