\chapter{Anaemia}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Anaemia is a condition in which the blood has too few red blood cells, or too little of the oxygen-carrying protein haemoglobin, to deliver oxygen effectively around the body. Because tissues receive less oxygen, affected people commonly feel tired, weak, and breathless. Anaemia is not a single disease but a sign of an underlying problem, and there are many different types with different causes.

The most common type worldwide is iron-deficiency anaemia. Other important types include vitamin B12 and folate deficiency, the anaemia of chronic disease, the haemolytic (destructive) anaemias such as sickle cell disease and thalassaemia, and the anaemias caused by bone-marrow failure. Treatment depends on identifying and correcting the underlying cause while restoring the blood count to normal.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Anaemia is one of the most common health problems in the world, affecting roughly one in four people globally. It is particularly common in women of childbearing age, in pregnant women, and in children, largely because of iron losses from menstruation and the increased demands of growth and pregnancy. It is also frequent in older adults, in people with chronic kidney disease, inflammatory conditions, and poor diets, and in low-income regions where malnutrition, intestinal parasites, and infections are common.

In developed countries, iron deficiency remains common, but vitamin B12 deficiency and the anaemia of chronic disease are also important causes, particularly in older people. The World Health Organization regards anaemia as a major public-health problem in many regions, and correcting it improves the health of mothers, infants, and working-age adults.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Red blood cells are produced in the bone marrow, circulate for about 120 days, and are removed by the spleen. Anaemia develops when production falls, destruction rises, or blood is lost:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Iron deficiency:} from low dietary iron, poor absorption (as after gastric surgery or in coeliac disease), or chronic blood loss such as heavy periods or slow gut bleeding
    \item \textbf{Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency:} both are needed to make red blood cells; deficiency may reflect diet, or absorption problems including pernicious anaemia, in which the stomach cannot absorb vitamin B12
    \item \textbf{Anaemia of chronic disease:} inflammation, infection, cancer, or kidney disease reducing red-cell production or shortening red-cell survival
    \item \textbf{Bleeding:} acute loss from injury, surgery, or childbirth, or slow chronic loss from the gastrointestinal or urinary tract
    \item \textbf{Increased destruction (haemolysis):} inherited conditions such as sickle cell disease and thalassaemia, or acquired causes such as autoimmune attack on red cells
    \item \textbf{Bone-marrow disorders:} leukaemia, myelodysplasia, or aplastic anaemia, in which the marrow fails to produce adequate cells
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Mild anaemia may cause no symptoms at all, especially when it develops slowly. As it becomes more severe, common symptoms include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fatigue, weakness, and a persistent feeling of being run down
    \item Pale skin, especially of the face and inside the lower eyelids
    \item Shortness of breath, particularly on exertion
    \item Dizziness, light-headedness, or fainting
    \item Headaches, poor concentration, and irritability
    \item A rapid or pounding heartbeat (palpitations)
    \item Cold hands and feet
    \item Pica: an unusual craving to eat non-food items such as ice, soil, or clay, which is more typical of iron deficiency
\end{itemize}
Severe or rapidly developing anaemia can cause chest pain, confusion, and collapse, and requires urgent treatment.

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The causes of anaemia are distinguished mainly by blood tests, but the symptoms overlap with many other conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hypothyroidism, which causes fatigue, weakness, and feeling cold
    \item Chronic fatigue syndrome and depression
    \item Heart failure or chronic lung disease causing breathlessness
    \item Malignancy presenting with tiredness and weight loss
    \item Kidney disease, which itself causes anaemia through reduced production of erythropoietin
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
See a doctor if you have persistent fatigue, breathlessness, dizziness, or unusual tiredness, or if you look pale and your symptoms are not improving. Seek advice if you have symptoms of bleeding, such as heavy periods, blood in the stools, or black, tarry stools. Seek urgent care if symptoms are worsening rapidly, you have chest pain or severe breathlessness, you faint or feel about to faint, or you are vomiting blood or passing large amounts of blood.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe chest pain, difficulty breathing, or collapse
    \item Vomiting blood or passing large volumes of blood with dizziness or fainting
    \item Sudden severe weakness or confusion
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History:} diet, medications, menstrual and pregnancy history, family history of anaemia, and any symptoms of bleeding
    \item \textbf{Examination:} pallor, signs of nutritional deficiency, jaundice (suggesting haemolysis), and examination of the abdomen and rectum where relevant
    \item \textbf{Full blood count:} measures haemoglobin and the number and size of red blood cells; the mean cell volume helps classify the anaemia as microcytic, normocytic, or macrocytic
    \item \textbf{Blood film:} microscopic examination of the red cells, which can point to specific causes
    \item \textbf{Iron studies:} serum ferritin, iron, and transferrin saturation to diagnose iron deficiency and iron overload
    \item \textbf{Vitamin B12 and folate levels:} to diagnose deficiency states
    \item \textbf{Reticulocyte count:} a measure of new red-cell production that distinguishes underproduction from increased destruction
    \item \textbf{Further tests as needed:} kidney, liver, and thyroid function; haemolysis tests; and haemoglobin studies for inherited forms
    \item \textbf{Investigation of blood loss:} endoscopy or colonoscopy for gut bleeding, and gynaecological review for heavy menstrual loss
    \item \textbf{Bone marrow biopsy:} occasionally required if a marrow disorder is suspected
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Treat the cause:} stop or reduce bleeding, treat the underlying disease, and address dietary problems; this is the most important step
    \item \textbf{Iron:} oral iron supplements, with intravenous iron when tablets are not tolerated, ineffective, or needed quickly
    \item \textbf{Vitamin B12 and folate:} replacement tablets or injections, sometimes lifelong as in pernicious anaemia
    \item \textbf{Diet:} foods rich in iron (red meat, legumes, leafy greens, fortified cereals) and vitamin C to aid absorption
    \item \textbf{Blood transfusion:} for severe, symptomatic, or rapidly developing anaemia
    \item \textbf{Medicines:} erythropoiesis-stimulating agents for anaemia of chronic kidney disease and selected other cases
    \item \textbf{Specialist treatments:} immunosuppression, transfusion programmes, and other therapies for haemolysis, marrow disorders, and inherited anaemias under a haematologist
    \item \textbf{Follow-up:} repeat blood tests to confirm the anaemia has corrected and the cause is controlled
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe fatigue affecting work, study, and daily life
    \item Heart strain, palpitations, and worsening of pre-existing heart failure
    \item Complications of pregnancy, including low birth weight and maternal exhaustion
    \item Increased risk of falls and accidents from dizziness and reduced concentration
    \item Impaired growth and development in children
    \item Iron overload if supplements are taken without need
    \item Rarely, heart failure or shock in very severe or sudden anaemia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The outlook for anaemia is generally excellent once the cause is found and corrected. Iron-deficiency anaemia usually resolves within weeks to months of treatment, although the underlying source of blood loss must be treated to prevent relapse. Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency respond well to replacement therapy. The anaemia of chronic disease improves when the underlying condition is managed effectively. Inherited and bone-marrow anaemias tend to require long-term specialist care, but many people with these conditions live full and active lives with appropriate treatment.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Eat an iron-rich diet and include foods containing vitamin C, which helps iron absorption
    \item Avoid drinking tea or coffee immediately with meals if you are at risk of iron deficiency
    \item Have heavy or very painful periods investigated and treated
    \item Report any blood in the stools or black, tarry stools to a doctor promptly
    \item Ask about anaemia screening if you are pregnant or planning a pregnancy
    \item Take supplements exactly as directed; more is not better and can cause harm
    \item Manage chronic conditions such as kidney disease and inflammatory disorders with your medical team
    \item If you have an inherited anaemia, attend specialist follow-up and seek genetic counselling before having children
\end{itemize}
