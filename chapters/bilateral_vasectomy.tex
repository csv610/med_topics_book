\chapter{Bilateral Vasectomy}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A vasectomy is a surgical procedure that provides permanent contraception for men. The operation cuts and seals the two vas deferens, the tubes that carry sperm from the testicles to the urethra, so that sperm can no longer enter the semen. It is performed on both sides, hence the term bilateral vasectomy.

Vasectomy does not affect the production of testosterone, sexual desire, or the ability to achieve an erection or ejaculate. It should be regarded as permanent, although reversal surgery is technically possible in some cases. Because it is safe, quick, and highly effective, vasectomy is one of the most commonly chosen methods of permanent contraception worldwide.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Vasectomy is one of the most effective and widely used methods of permanent contraception, including in Australia.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Effectiveness:} more than 99 per cent effective at preventing pregnancy once a follow-up semen test confirms that no sperm are present
    \item \textbf{Age and life stage:} most commonly chosen by men in their 30s to 50s, usually after completing their families
    \item \textbf{Prevalence:} an estimated one in six married or partnered men of reproductive age in high-income countries has undergone vasectomy
    \item \textbf{Safety:} serious complications are rare, and most men return to work within a few days
\end{itemize}

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Sperm are produced continuously in the testicles and travel along the vas deferens to be mixed into the semen at the time of ejaculation. After a vasectomy, sperm continue to be produced, but their passage is blocked. The trapped sperm are absorbed and recycled by the body's immune system.

Because the tubes on both sides are sealed, the semen no longer contains sperm. The volume of the ejaculate changes very little, because sperm make up only a small fraction of the total fluid. It is important to understand that vasectomy is not immediately effective: sperm already present below the point of blockage must be cleared by repeated ejaculation, which is why a semen test is required approximately three months after the procedure before the man can rely on it for contraception.

\section*{Clinical Features}
Vasectomy is usually performed as an outpatient procedure under local anaesthetic, taking about 20 to 30 minutes. After the operation, it is normal to experience the following:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Mild pain, swelling, and bruising of the scrotum for a few days
    \item Slight discomfort or a pulling sensation in the groin
    \item Occasionally a small amount of blood in the semen during the first few ejaculations
    \item A firm, slightly tender area at the site of the procedure, which settles gradually
    \item Most men can return to light activity within a day or two and to vigorous exercise after about a week
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Symptoms that develop after a vasectomy need to be distinguished from the normal healing process.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scrotal haematoma:} a collection of blood within the scrotum, causing a firm, painful swelling, versus ordinary bruising
    \item \textbf{Wound infection:} redness, heat, discharge, and fever, versus the normal inflammatory healing response
    \item \textbf{Epididymitis:} inflammation of the coiled tube at the back of the testicle, versus routine postoperative discomfort
    \item \textbf{Post-vasectomy pain syndrome:} chronic scrotal or testicular pain lasting months, versus normal self-limiting discomfort
    \item \textbf{Testicular torsion:} sudden severe testicular pain with nausea, which is a surgical emergency
    \item \textbf{Sperm granuloma:} a small, usually painless lump where sperm leak into surrounding tissue
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Contact the doctor or clinic promptly if, after a vasectomy, you develop:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Severe or worsening pain, or pain that does not settle after several days
    \item Increasing swelling, redness, or heat in the scrotum
    \item Discharge from the wound or a fever, which may indicate infection
    \item Heavy bleeding or a bruise that continues to enlarge
    \item Difficulty passing urine
    \item A persistent painful lump in the scrotum
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden severe pain in the scrotum or lower abdomen, which may indicate testicular torsion
    \item Collapse, heavy uncontrolled bleeding, or signs of an allergic reaction to the local anaesthetic
    \item Chest pain or difficulty breathing, which can rarely occur after any procedure
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pre-procedure assessment:} a full discussion of the decision, expectations, and the permanent nature of the procedure, with informed consent
    \item \textbf{Examination:} the doctor examines the scrotum and confirms that both vas deferens can be felt and identified
    \item \textbf{Review of medicines:} assessment of bleeding risk, with advice about medicines such as anticoagulants that may need to be temporarily paused
    \item \textbf{Semen analysis:} the essential follow-up test, usually performed at about three months or after around 20 ejaculations, to confirm that no sperm remain
    \item \textbf{Repeat semen analysis:} arranged if the first test shows any sperm, with further testing until the result is clear
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The procedure:} performed under local anaesthetic, with the tubes reached either through two small incisions or through a single puncture using the no-scalpel technique; each vas deferens is cut, tied, and often sealed with heat (cautery)
    \item \textbf{Recovery:} rest, ice packs applied to the scrotum, a supportive garment, and simple pain relief for the first day or two
    \item \textbf{Sexual activity:} can usually resume after about a week, once comfortable
    \item \textbf{Continuing contraception:} essential until the follow-up semen test confirms that no sperm are present
    \item \textbf{Follow-up test:} vital; the procedure cannot be relied upon until it is clear
    \item \textbf{Reversal:} possible for some men but not always successful, and frequently not covered by private insurance, so vasectomy should be chosen as a permanent method
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Bleeding and bruising, with an occasional haematoma that requires time to reabsorb
    \item Wound infection, usually managed with simple wound care and sometimes antibiotics
    \item \textbf{Post-vasectomy pain syndrome:} chronic discomfort in the scrotum or testicles affecting a small minority of men
    \item \textbf{Sperm granuloma:} a harmless lump where sperm leak into the surrounding tissue; it usually resolves on its own
    \item \textbf{Failure and recanalisation:} very rare spontaneous rejoining of the tubes, which the follow-up semen test is designed to detect
    \item \textbf{Regret:} more common in younger men, in those without children, and in those in unstable relationships
    \item \textbf{Reversal outcome:} reversal surgery restores fertility in some but not all cases, and the chance of success falls with time since the vasectomy
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Vasectomy is a safe, highly effective, and permanent method of contraception.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Once the follow-up semen test is clear, the failure rate is around 1 in 2,000
    \item Most men recover fully within a few days and notice no change in sexual function or ejaculation
    \item Serious complications are uncommon
    \item Vasectomy does not affect hormone levels, masculinity, or general health
    \item Reversal is possible for some men, but success is not guaranteed, so the decision should be treated as permanent
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discuss the decision fully with your partner and the doctor, and regard the procedure as permanent
    \item Use contraception, not the procedure, until the follow-up semen test is confirmed clear
    \item Follow the aftercare advice: rest, ice, and scrotal support for the first day or two
    \item Keep the wound clean and dry, and report any signs of infection promptly
    \item Attend the follow-up semen test; it is the only way to confirm the procedure has worked
    \item Remember that vasectomy does not protect against sexually transmitted infections; continue safe-sex practices as needed
\end{itemize}
