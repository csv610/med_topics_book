\chapter{Lymph nodes}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Lymph nodes are small, bean-shaped organs of the lymphatic system distributed widely throughout the body, acting as essential biological filters. They are encapsulated structures containing dense collections of B and T lymphocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells, linked by a network of lymphatic vessels. Lymph nodes play a dual role: they filter foreign particles, cellular debris, and pathogens from interstitial fluid (lymph) before returning it to the blood vascular system, and they serve as primary sites for antigen presentation and the initiation of adaptive immune responses. Clinically, localised or generalised enlargement of lymph nodes (lymphadenopathy) is a vital sign, serving as a primary indicator of localised infection, systemic inflammatory disease, or haematological and metastatic malignancies.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Lymphadenopathy is a common clinical finding in both primary and tertiary healthcare settings. The epidemiology is highly dependent on age, environmental exposures, and geography. In paediatric and adolescent populations, lymphadenopathy is highly prevalent and predominantly benign, representing a reactive response to common viral or bacterial upper respiratory tract infections. In adults, particularly those aged over 40, the probability of an underlying malignant cause (either primary lymphoma or metastatic cancer) rises significantly, accounting for approximately 4\% of cases presenting in primary care and up to 10--15\% in referral settings. Global statistics highlight geographic variation in infectious lymphadenopathy; for instance, tuberculous lymphadenitis (scrofula) remains highly prevalent in developing countries, whereas in Australia, viral illnesses, localised skin infections, and neoplastic metastases are the leading causes.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Lymph node enlargement occurs due to lymphocyte proliferation, macrophage hyperplasia, or direct infiltration by inflammatory or neoplastic cells.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infectious pathogens:} Proliferation of immune cells in response to local or systemic viral (e.g. Epstein-Barr virus, cytomegalovirus), bacterial (e.g. Streptococcus, Staphylococcus, Mycobacterium tuberculosis), or fungal infections.
    \item \textbf{Primary haematological malignancies:} Clonal proliferation of malignant lymphocytes within the node, characteristic of Hodgkin lymphoma and non-Hodgkin lymphoma.
    \item \textbf{Metastatic neoplasia:} Infiltration of tumour cells from a primary cancer site (e.g. breast, lung, melanoma) into the regional lymph nodes via afferent lymphatic drainage.
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune and rheumatological diseases:} Lymphocyte hyperplasia driven by chronic systemic inflammation, as seen in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) or rheumatoid arthritis.
    \item \textbf{Granulomatous disorders:} Chronic inflammatory reactions forming non-caseating granulomas, typically seen in sarcoidosis or foreign-body reactions.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The evaluation of lymph nodes depends heavily on physical characteristics and associated systemic signs.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Enlargement (Lymphadenopathy):} Nodes exceeding normal size parameters, typically defined as greater than 1 cm in diameter (or greater than 1.5 cm for inguinal nodes).
    \item \textbf{Tenderness and warmth:} Pain upon palpation and local warmth, strongly indicating acute inflammatory or infectious causes (lymphadenitis).
    \item \textbf{Consistency:} Soft or rubbery nodes are typical of reactive hyperplasia or lymphoma; hard, stony nodes are highly suggestive of metastatic carcinoma.
    \item \textbf{Mobility:} Free-moving nodes suggest benign processes; fixed, matted, or adherent nodes suggest malignant infiltration or chronic tuberculous infection.
    \item \textbf{Systemic symptoms:} Coexisting fever, night sweats, unexplained weight loss (so-called "B symptoms"), fatigue, or localised symptoms like sore throat or wound healing issues.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Clinicians must differentiate lymphadenopathy from other anatomical masses and distinguish its underlying benign or malignant causes.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Thyroglossal duct or branchial cleft cyst:} Congenital neck masses presenting in the anterior midline or lateral neck, respectively, mimicking cervical lymphadenopathy.
    \item \textbf{Lipoma or sebaceous cyst:} Benign subcutaneous soft-tissue masses that lack the deep fascial attachment and lymphatic association of lymph nodes.
    \item \textbf{Salivary gland neoplasm or sialadenitis:} Enlargement or inflammation of the submandibular or parotid glands mimicking upper cervical nodes.
    \item \textbf{Hernia (inguinal or femoral):} Abdominal wall protrusions in the groin region that must be distinguished from inguinal lymphadenopathy.
    \item \textbf{Aneurysm or vascular malformation:} Pulsatile masses (especially in the groin or neck) that must not be confused with lymph nodes or biopsied.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Most swollen lymph nodes are reactive and resolve within 2 to 4 weeks. However, medical evaluation should be sought if swelling is persistent, progressive, or accompanied by concerning systemic symptoms. Immediate emergency care is rarely needed for lymphadenopathy itself but is required if local mass effect compromises vital structures.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Airway obstruction:} Severe swelling in the neck accompanied by difficulty breathing, stridor, or inability to swallow.
    \item \textbf{Superior vena cava syndrome:} Swelling of the neck and face, shortness of breath, and prominent superficial chest veins due to mediastinal mass compression.
    \item \textbf{Severe systemic infection:} High fever, confusion, rapid breathing, and spreading skin redness originating from an infected lymph node.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnostic workup focuses on identifying the cause of lymphadenopathy and guiding definitive management or staging.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical history and physical examination:} Detailed assessment of nodal characteristics across all lymph node stations (cervical, axillary, supraclavicular, epitrochlear, inguinal) and search for localised infection or organomegaly.
    \item \textbf{Full blood count and blood film:} Evaluation for leukocytosis, atypical lymphocytosis (suggestive of glandular fever), or cytopenias indicating bone marrow involvement.
    \item \textbf{Serological and microbiological testing:} Blood tests for EBV, CMV, HIV, toxoplasmosis, or cat scratch disease (Bartonella henselae).
    \item \textbf{Ultrasonography:} The first-line imaging modality to evaluate nodal size, shape, loss of fatty hilum, and vascularity.
    \item \textbf{CT, MRI, or PET-CT scans:} Indicated to assess deep thoracic or abdominal lymphadenopathy, stage malignancy, or monitor response to cancer treatment.
    \item \textbf{Excisional or core needle biopsy:} The diagnostic gold standard, providing intact tissue architecture for histological and immunohistochemical evaluation (fine-needle aspiration is useful for metastasis but often insufficient for lymphoma).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is directed entirely at the underlying cause of the nodal enlargement.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infectious management:} Antibiotics for bacterial infections (e.g. cefalexin for cellulitis or lymphadenitis), antivirals for specific viral pathogens, or anti-tuberculous chemotherapy for tuberculosis.
    \item \textbf{Oncological therapies:} Systemic chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or targeted biological agents for lymphomas, leukaemias, or metastatic solid tumours.
    \item \textbf{Surgical intervention:} Lymphadenectomy (excision of regional lymph nodes) for staging or local control of solid tumours (e.g. sentinel node biopsy in breast cancer or melanoma).
    \item \textbf{Immunosuppressive therapy:} Corticosteroids or disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) to manage lymphadenopathy secondary to autoimmune conditions.
    \item \textbf{Symptomatic support:} Adequate hydration, rest, and simple analgesia (paracetamol or ibuprofen) for painful reactive nodes during viral illnesses.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Abscess formation and suppuration:} Progression of lymphadenitis to a localised, purulent fluid collection requiring surgical drainage.
    \item \textbf{Lymphedema:} Swelling of an extremity due to lymphatic drainage obstruction, commonly occurring after surgical removal (lymphadenectomy) or radiation of regional lymph nodes.
    \item \textbf{Fistula formation:} Development of abnormal connections between infected lymph nodes and the skin (e.g. scrofuloderma in tuberculosis).
    \item \textbf{Compression of adjacent structures:} Large nodal masses compressing local nerves, blood vessels, or the trachea or oesophagus.
    \item \textbf{Malignant progression:} Delayed diagnosis of lymphoma or metastatic disease, leading to advanced clinical stage and poorer prognosis.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for lymph node enlargement is highly variable, depending almost entirely on the primary aetiology. For the vast majority of cases, which are reactive to viral or bacterial infections, the prognosis is excellent, with nodes returning to normal size over several weeks without long-term consequences. For malignant causes, the prognosis depends on the cancer type, clinical stage at diagnosis, and response to therapy. Hodgkin lymphoma, for example, is highly curable with modern multi-agent chemotherapy, whereas prognosis for metastatic solid tumours depends on the primary site. Overall, early clinical evaluation of unexplained persistent lymphadenopathy is the single most important factor in securing a favourable outcome.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Practice good hand hygiene:} Washing hands regularly to prevent common viral and bacterial infections.
    \item \textbf{Promptly treat skin wounds:} Cleaning and covering cuts or abrasions to prevent localised bacterial entry and subsequent lymphadenitis.
    \item \textbf{Avoid contact with stray animals:} Reducing exposure to animal scratches or bites to prevent cat scratch disease.
    \item \textbf{Stay up to date with vaccinations:} Immunising against infectious agents that cause lymphadenopathy (e.g. measles, rubella).
    \item \textbf{Monitor nodal changes:} Performing regular self-examinations of the neck, axilla, and groin, reporting persistent changes to a doctor.
\end{itemize}
