\chapter{Motion sickness}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Motion sickness (kinetosis) is a common, transient physiological response characterised by gastrointestinal and neurological symptoms, triggered by exposure to real or perceived motion. It arises from a sensory conflict within the central nervous system, where discordant inputs are received from the vestibular system (inner ear), visual system, and proprioceptors (body position sensors). Symptoms typically include nausea, vomiting, dizziness, pallor, cold sweating, and hyperventilation. Clinically, motion sickness is categorised by the triggering environment (e.g. seasickness, carsickness, airsickness, or space motion sickness) and can also be induced by virtual reality and simulators (simulator sickness). While motion sickness does not cause long-term organic harm, it can cause significant distress, temporary disability, and anxiety regarding travel. Effective clinical management relies on combining preventive behavioural habits with pharmacological therapies administered before travel.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Motion sickness is a highly prevalent condition that can affect almost any individual with an intact vestibular system if the motion stimulus is sufficiently intense or prolonged. Epidemiological studies indicate that approximately one-third of the population is highly susceptible to motion sickness under normal travel conditions. Susceptibility varies significantly with age: it is relatively rare in infants under the age of two, peaks during childhood between the ages of 9 and 12, and progressively declines through adolescence and adulthood. Women report higher rates of motion sickness than men, a difference that is particularly pronounced during pregnancy and specific phases of the menstrual cycle, suggesting a hormonal component. Individuals with a history of migraine headaches or vestibular disorders (such as Ménière's disease) also exhibit significantly higher rates of susceptibility.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
The physiological basis of motion sickness is centred on sensory mismatch and neural signalling pathways.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sensory conflict theory:} The primary model, stating that motion sickness occurs when the brain (specifically the vestibular nuclei and cerebellum) receives mismatching signals from the eyes, inner ear semicircular canals, and proprioceptive receptors.
    \item \textbf{Vestibular system overstimulation:} Angular and linear accelerations detected by the semicircular canals and otolith organs that exceed the brain's expected motion patterns (e.g. on a pitching boat).
    \item \textbf{Visual-vestibular mismatch:} Occurs when the visual system detects motion but the vestibular system does not (e.g. reading in a moving car), or vice versa (e.g. in a flight simulator).
    \item \textbf{Neurochemical pathways:} Activating histaminergic (H1) and muscarinic cholinergic receptors in the vestibular nuclei, which project to the vomiting centre (area postrema) in the brainstem.
    \item \textbf{Hormonal fluctuations:} Elevated levels of progesterone and oestrogen that alter central vestibular processing and lower the threshold for nausea.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The onset of motion sickness follows a typical progression of symptoms, starting with mild abdominal discomfort and advancing to systemic distress.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Stomach awareness and nausea:} A vague sensation of epigastric discomfort or fullness, progressing rapidly to mild or severe nausea.
    \item \textbf{Autonomic signs:} Marked facial pallor, cold sweating (diaphoresis), and increased salivation.
    \item \textbf{Hyperventilation and yawning:} Frequent yawning, deep sighing, and rapid breathing, which can cause secondary hypocapnia.
    \item \textbf{Dizziness and headache:} A feeling of instability, mild lightheadedness, and a dull frontal headache.
    \item \textbf{Vomiting and apathy:} Active emesis, which often provides only temporary relief, accompanied by severe lethargy, depression, and a strong desire to stop the motion.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
It is important to distinguish motion sickness from other causes of acute nausea, vomiting, and vertigo.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV):} Characterised by brief, intense episodes of spinning sensation triggered by specific head movements, occurring independently of external travel.
    \item \textbf{Acute labyrinthitis or vestibular neuritis:} An inflammatory condition causing sudden, severe, persistent vertigo, nystagmus, and nausea, lasting days regardless of motion.
    \item \textbf{Migraine-associated vertigo (vestibular migraine):} Episodic vertigo in patients with a history of migraines, often accompanied by photophobia, phonophobia, or visual aura.
    \item \textbf{Gastroenteritis or food poisoning:} Acute nausea, vomiting, and diarrhoea caused by viral or bacterial pathogens, characterised by abdominal cramping and fever.
    \item \textbf{Hypoglycaemia or dehydration:} Systemic states presenting with dizziness, sweating, and nausea, distinguished by clinical history and blood glucose monitoring.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Motion sickness typically resolves spontaneously once the triggering motion stops. Medical advice should be sought if symptoms are unusually severe, persist long after travel has ended, or prevent necessary hydration. Immediate emergency services are rarely needed unless severe dehydration or other red flag symptoms occur.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Signs of severe dehydration:} Confusion, extreme lethargy, rapid heart rate, or inability to pass urine after prolonged vomiting.
    \item \textbf{Acute neurological signs:} Sudden weakness, numbness, difficulty speaking, double vision, or loss of balance that is not related to motion.
    \item \textbf{Severe chest pain or dyspnoea:} Occurring concurrently with severe vomiting or distress during travel.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Diagnosis is clinical, based on a clear history of symptoms during travel or motion exposure. Diagnostic investigations are reserved for ruling out underlying vestibular pathology in chronic or atypical cases.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical history:} Delineating the onset, progression, triggers, and relieving factors of the symptoms.
    \item \textbf{Neurological and otological examination:} Assessing cranial nerves, cerebellar function, gait, and inspecting the tympanic membranes.
    \item \textbf{Vestibular function testing:} Electronystagmography (ENG) or video-nystagmography (VNG) to evaluate inner ear function if chronic vestibular disease is suspected.
    \item \textbf{Audiometry:} Hearing tests to rule out Ménière's disease or acoustic neuroma in patients with chronic dizziness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Management is most effective when focused on prevention, combining behavioural modifications with pharmacological interventions before exposure to motion.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anticholinergic agents:} Hyoscine hydrobromide, administered as an oral tablet 30 minutes before travel or as a transdermal patch applied behind the ear, which blocks muscarinic receptors in the vestibular nuclei.
    \item \textbf{First-generation antihistamines:} Sedating antihistamines (e.g. promethazine, dimenhydrinate, or pheniramine) that cross the blood-brain barrier to block H1 and cholinergic pathways.
    \item \textbf{Non-pharmacological ginger supplements:} Oral ginger (Zingiber officinale) capsules or sweets, which act locally in the gastrointestinal tract to reduce gastric tachyarrhythmias.
    \item \textbf{Behavioural adjustments:} Sitting in the area of least motion (e.g. front seat of a car, over the wing of an aircraft, or mid-ship at water level) and keeping the eyes fixed on a stable horizon.
    \item \textbf{Acupressure bands:} Elastic wristbands applying pressure to the P6 (Neiguan) acupuncture point, which some patients find helpful for nausea control.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dehydration and electrolyte imbalance:} Resulting from persistent, intractable vomiting, especially on long sea voyages.
    \item \textbf{Travel anxiety and avoidance:} Development of anticipatory anxiety and phobic avoidance of cars, planes, or boats.
    \item \textbf{Mallory-Weiss tear:} A tear in the mucosal lining at the junction of the oesophagus and stomach, caused by severe, forceful retching.
    \item \textbf{Impaired occupational performance:} Severe disability in pilots, mariners, or military personnel, compromising safety.
    \item \textbf{Accidental injury:} Increased risk of falls or accidents on boats or transport due to impaired coordination and dizziness.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for motion sickness is excellent. Symptoms are almost always self-limiting and resolve rapidly within minutes to hours once the offending motion ceases. Habituation (or "getting one's sea legs") naturally occurs in most individuals with repeated, continuous exposure to the same motion stimulus over 36 to 72 hours, as the brain adapts to the new sensory inputs. Pharmacological and behavioural strategies are highly effective, allowing most susceptible individuals to travel comfortably. There are no long-term physical sequelae, and susceptibility typically decreases with advancing age.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Focus on the horizon:} Looking straight ahead at a stable point outside the vehicle rather than looking down.
    \item \textbf{Avoid reading or screens:} Refraining from reading books or looking at phones or tablets while in motion.
    \item \textbf{Ensure fresh air flow:} Opening a window, turning on air vents, or going up on the deck of a boat to ensure cool air flow.
    \item \textbf{Avoid heavy meals and alcohol:} Restricting intake of greasy foods, alcohol, and carbonated drinks immediately before and during travel.
    \item \textbf{Stay head-stable:} Keeping the head as still as possible by resting it against a seat support or headrest.
\end{itemize}
