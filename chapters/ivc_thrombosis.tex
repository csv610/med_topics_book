\chapter{Inferior Vena Cava (IVC) Thrombosis}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Inferior vena cava (IVC) thrombosis is a blood clot that forms in the inferior vena cava, the large vein that returns blood from the lower half of the body to the heart. It is an uncommon but serious form of deep vein thrombosis that usually occurs in people with IVC filters, cancer, abdominal surgery, or extension of a clot from the leg or pelvic veins. Because the IVC is a major vessel, a clot here can cause swelling of both legs, back and abdominal pain, and can break off and travel to the lungs, causing a pulmonary embolism. Prompt diagnosis with imaging and treatment with anticoagulation are essential.

\section*{Epidemiology}
IVC thrombosis is uncommon, but its incidence has increased with the use of IVC filters, which are placed to prevent pulmonary embolism in people who cannot take anticoagulants. It complicates around 10 to 30 per cent of IVC filter placements over time. Other risk groups include people with cancer, particularly abdominal and pelvic cancers, those who have had abdominal or pelvic surgery, pregnant women, and people with inherited clotting disorders. The condition can affect any age but is more common in older people and in those with multiple risk factors for thrombosis.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
As with all blood clots, IVC thrombosis results from a combination of reduced blood flow, injury to the vessel wall, and a tendency to clot (Virchow's triad). The IVC has slow flow and is prone to stasis, particularly when compressed by abdominal masses, tumours, or the pregnant uterus, and when blood flow is sluggish from immobility. IVC filters themselves are a major cause, as they trap clot and can themselves become blocked. Cancer promotes clotting through the tumour's effects on the blood, and surgery, trauma, and pelvic infection injure the vessel or surrounding tissues. A clot in the IVC can extend from the iliac and femoral veins or form within the cava itself.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Swelling of one or both legs, sometimes with pain and heaviness
    \item Back, flank, or abdominal pain
    \item Prominent superficial veins over the abdomen and thighs as collaterals develop
    \item Fever in some cases
    \item Pulmonary embolism may be the first event: sudden breathlessness, chest pain, or collapse
    \item Some people have no symptoms, especially with gradual onset
    \item In severe cases, the legs may become tense, pale, or blue, indicating obstruction
    \item Kidney problems if the clot extends into the renal veins
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
The symptoms of IVC thrombosis overlap with other conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bilateral leg swelling:} heart failure, kidney disease, and fluid retention
    \item \textbf{Compression of the IVC:} from a tumour or the pregnant uterus, without a clot
    \item \textbf{Deep vein thrombosis of the legs:} more distal clots
    \item \textbf{Cellulitis and lymphoedema:} causes of leg swelling
    \item \textbf{Abdominal causes:} pancreatitis, kidney stones, and bowel conditions
    \item \textbf{Budd-Chiari syndrome:} clot in the veins draining the liver
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Anyone with new swelling of one or both legs, abdominal or back pain, or risk factors such as an IVC filter, cancer, or recent surgery should be assessed promptly. Seek urgent assessment for sudden breathlessness or chest pain, which may indicate pulmonary embolism.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sudden breathlessness, chest pain, coughing up blood, or collapse, which may indicate a pulmonary embolism
    \item A leg that becomes very tense, pale, blue, or cold
    \item Sudden severe abdominal or back pain
    \item Fainting or a rapid heartbeat with breathing difficulty
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Clinical assessment:} risk factors, symptoms, and examination of the legs and abdomen
    \item \textbf{D-dimer blood test:} may support the diagnosis, though it is not specific
    \item \textbf{Venous ultrasound:} first-line for leg and iliac vein clot
    \item \textbf{CT venography:} the best test for visualising the IVC and confirming the clot
    \item \textbf{Magnetic resonance venography:} an alternative without radiation
    \item \textbf{Chest imaging:} CT pulmonary angiography if pulmonary embolism is suspected
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} full blood count, clotting, and screening for cancer and thrombophilia
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Anticoagulation:} blood-thinning medicines are the cornerstone, continued for months or longer
    \item \textbf{Treatment of the cause:} management of cancer, removal of an IVC filter, and treatment of infection
    \item \textbf{Catheter-directed thrombolysis:} clot-dissolving medicine delivered directly in selected cases
    \item \textbf{Mechanical thrombectomy:} removal of the clot with a catheter device
    \item \textbf{IVC filter management:} removal of a filter once anticoagulation is possible
    \item \textbf{Compression stockings:} to manage leg swelling and prevent post-thrombotic syndrome
    \item \textbf{Long-term follow-up:} monitoring for recurrence and complications
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Pulmonary embolism, which can be life-threatening
    \item Post-thrombotic syndrome with chronic leg swelling and skin changes
    \item Obstruction of the IVC with severe bilateral leg swelling
    \item Extension into the renal veins, affecting kidney function
    \item Bleeding from anticoagulation
    \item Recurrence if the cause is not addressed
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
With prompt anticoagulation, the risk of pulmonary embolism falls markedly and many IVC clots resolve or organise without causing lasting problems. Outcomes depend on the underlying cause: cancer-related and filter-related clots carry higher risks of recurrence and complications. Post-thrombotic syndrome affects a significant proportion of people and can cause chronic leg symptoms. Managing the cause, including filter removal, improves outcomes, and most people recover with treatment.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Discuss the risks and benefits before agreeing to an IVC filter
    \item Ensure filters are removed as soon as they are no longer needed
    \item Move regularly and stay hydrated, especially during illness and long travel
    \item Take anticoagulants exactly as prescribed
    \item Know the signs of a new clot or pulmonary embolism and act promptly
    \item Wear compression stockings as advised
    \item Attend follow-up appointments and imaging
    \item Manage cancer and other risk factors with your medical team
\end{itemize}
