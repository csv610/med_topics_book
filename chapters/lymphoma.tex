\chapter{Lymphoma}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Lymphoma is a type of blood cancer that develops in the lymphatic system, which is part of the body's immune and disease-fighting network. It occurs when lymphocytes (white blood cells) undergo genetic mutations, causing them to multiply uncontrollably and collect in lymph nodes, the spleen, bone marrow, or other organs. Lymphomas are broadly categorized into Hodgkin lymphoma and Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), each having distinct cellular characteristics, clinical behaviours, and treatment regimens.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Lymphoma is the most common blood cancer in both Australia and globally. Non-Hodgkin lymphoma is far more prevalent, representing approximately 90\% of all cases, and its incidence rises with age, typically affecting older adults. Hodgkin lymphoma exhibits a bimodal age distribution, peaking in young adults (aged 15 to 30) and again in older adults (aged over 55). Together, lymphomas rank as the sixth most common cancer diagnosed in Australia.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Lymphoma is caused by DNA mutations that alter the growth and survival pathways of lymphocytes, primarily B-cells or T-cells:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Genetic mutations:} Chromosomal translocations and genetic alterations disrupt cellular division, preventing normal programmed cell death (apoptosis).
    \item \textbf{Immune system dysfunction:} Individuals with autoimmune diseases or immunodeficiencies (including HIV or immunosuppressive therapy after organ transplants) have a significantly higher risk.
    \item \textbf{Infectious agents:} Certain viruses and bacteria are linked to lymphoma development, such as Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) in Hodgkin lymphoma and Burkitt lymphoma, and \textit{Helicobacter pylori} in gastric MALT lymphoma.
    \item \textbf{Environmental exposures:} Exposure to certain chemicals, pesticides, and radiation has been associated with an increased risk of developing lymphoma.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
Patients with lymphoma present with a variety of localized and systemic symptoms depending on the sub-type and stage:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Painless lymphadenopathy:} Swollen, firm, rubbery, and painless lymph nodes, most commonly in the neck, armpits, or groin.
    \item \textbf{B-symptoms:} Systemic symptoms including unexplained fever, drenching night sweats, and unexplained weight loss (greater than 10\% of body weight in 6 months).
    \item \textbf{Pruritus:} Generalized, severe itching of the skin, particularly common in Hodgkin lymphoma.
    \item \textbf{Fatigue and weakness:} Persistent, profound exhaustion that is not relieved by rest.
    \item \textbf{Organ-specific symptoms:} Shortness of breath or coughing if mediastinal lymph nodes are enlarged, or abdominal bloating if the spleen or liver is enlarged.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Many benign and malignant conditions can cause lymph node swelling and systemic symptoms similar to lymphoma:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Infectious mononucleosis:} Glandular fever caused by EBV, presenting with fever, sore throat, and swollen lymph nodes in young people.
    \item \textbf{Tuberculosis or atypical mycobacterial infections:} Chronic bacterial infections causing lymph node enlargement.
    \item \textbf{Sarcoidosis:} An inflammatory disease causing granulomas in lymph nodes and lungs.
    \item \textbf{Metastatic carcinoma:} Cancer spreading from solid tumours (e.g., breast or lung cancer) to regional lymph nodes.
    \item \textbf{Autoimmune diseases:} Systemic lupus erythematosus or rheumatoid arthritis causing reactive lymphadenopathy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Individuals who notice persistent, unexplained swelling of the lymph nodes lasting more than 2 to 4 weeks, or those experiencing unexplained weight loss, fevers, or night sweats, should consult a GP. Immediate emergency assessment is required for severe complications.

\textbf{Call 000 (or local emergency services) for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Difficulty breathing, severe chest tightness, or a high-pitched breathing sound (stridor) due to airway compression
    \item Swelling of the face, neck, and upper arms, which may indicate superior vena cava syndrome
    \item Sudden, severe weakness in the legs or loss of bowel/bladder control, indicating spinal cord compression
    \item High fever accompanied by confusion, severe neck stiffness, or signs of sepsis
    \item Sudden, severe abdominal pain or vomiting
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
Accurate diagnosis and staging are crucial to guide treatment:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lymph node biopsy:} Excisional biopsy (removing the entire lymph node) is the gold standard; core needle biopsy is used if surgical removal is not feasible.
    \item \textbf{PET-CT scan:} Used for staging to detect active lymphoma sites throughout the body and to monitor treatment response.
    \item \textbf{Bone marrow biopsy:} Removing a small sample of bone marrow to check if the lymphoma has spread there.
    \item \textbf{Immunophenotyping and cytogenetics:} Lab tests (like flow cytometry and FISH) on the biopsy sample to identify the specific lymphoma sub-type.
    \item \textbf{Blood tests:} Full blood count, liver and kidney function tests, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and viral screening (HIV, hepatitis B and C).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
Treatment is highly individualised based on the specific lymphoma type, stage, patient age, and general health:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chemotherapy:} Using cytotoxic drugs to kill cancer cells, often administered in cycles (e.g., CHOP or ABVD regimens).
    \item \textbf{Immunotherapy:} Using monoclonal antibodies (such as rituximab) to target specific markers on lymphoma cells.
    \item \textbf{Radiation therapy:} High-energy beams targeted at specific lymph node regions, often used for localized disease.
    \item \textbf{Targeted therapies:} Small molecule inhibitors (e.g., BTK inhibitors) that block specific growth signals within lymphoma cells.
    \item \textbf{Stem cell transplant:} High-dose chemotherapy followed by autologous or allogeneic stem cell rescue for recurrent or resistant lymphoma.
    \item \textbf{Active surveillance (watch and wait):} Monitoring slow-growing (indolent) lymphomas closely without immediate treatment.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Increased risk of severe infections due to immunodeficiency caused by the disease or chemotherapy
    \item Anaemia, bleeding, and bruising from bone marrow suppression
    \item Secondary malignancies later in life, particularly after radiation therapy or chemotherapy
    \item Organ damage, such as cardiotoxicity from specific chemotherapy drugs or pulmonary fibrosis from bleomycin
    \item Infertility or early menopause as a result of systemic cancer treatments
    \item Tumor lysis syndrome during initial treatment of fast-growing lymphomas, leading to kidney injury
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis for lymphoma has improved dramatically with modern targeted treatments. Hodgkin lymphoma is now one of the most curable forms of cancer, with a 5-year survival rate exceeding 85-90\% for early-stage disease. Non-Hodgkin lymphoma outcomes vary widely: aggressive lymphomas can often be cured with prompt chemotherapy, while indolent lymphomas are usually not curable but can be managed successfully as chronic conditions for many years or decades.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item There are no established screening tests or specific ways to prevent lymphoma, but maintaining general health is key
    \item Practice strict hand hygiene and avoid contact with sick individuals during chemotherapy to prevent infections
    \item Consume a nutritious, well-balanced diet and drink plenty of water to support the body during therapy
    \item Engage in gentle, regular exercise (like walking) as tolerated to manage cancer-related fatigue
    \item Seek psychological support, such as cancer support groups or counselling, to manage emotional distress
\end{itemize}
