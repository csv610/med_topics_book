\chapter{Ganglion Cyst}
\index{Ganglion Cyst}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
A ganglion cyst is a common, benign fluid-filled swelling that develops near a joint or tendon, most often on the back of the wrist, but also on the front of the wrist, fingers, feet, and ankles. It contains a thick, jelly-like fluid similar to joint fluid, and it usually feels firm or rubbery and can vary in size. Ganglion cysts are not cancerous and do not spread, and many cause no symptoms at all. Some disappear on their own, and many can be left alone or aspirated, though they can recur. Treatment is only needed when the cyst is painful, growing, or interfering with movement. This chapter explains what causes ganglion cysts and the options for management.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Ganglion cysts are the most common soft-tissue lump of the hand and wrist, affecting around 1 in 500 people, with a peak in people aged 20--40, and they are more common in women than in men. They also occur in children and in gymnasts and others whose wrists are heavily loaded. Most develop on the back of the wrist, and around half of all wrist ganglions are accompanied by no symptoms. Many cysts resolve spontaneously, but the recurrence rate after any treatment is significant, which shapes the advice given.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Formation:} the cyst arises from the lining of a joint or tendon sheath, through which joint fluid leaks and collects in a pouch
    \item \textbf{The fluid:} thick, clear, jelly-like material similar to joint fluid
    \item \textbf{Location:} typically connected to the underlying joint or tendon by a small stalk
    \item \textbf{Triggers:} repetitive joint use, injury, and irritation are associated, though many appear without an obvious cause
    \item \textbf{Why they vary:} the size can change with activity, and cysts can come and go
    \item \textbf{Occult ganglions:} small cysts deep in the wrist can cause pain without a visible lump
    \item \textbf{Benign nature:} they are not tumours, do not spread, and do not become cancerous
    \item \textbf{Mechanism of symptoms:} pain and limitation arise from pressure on nearby structures or from the joint itself
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A smooth, round, firm swelling, typically on the back of the wrist, but also on the fingers, foot, or ankle
    \item The lump can change size, sometimes enlarging with activity
    \item It may be painless or cause aching, especially with joint use
    \item A feeling of weakness or altered sensation if the cyst presses on a nerve
    \item Transillumination: the cyst glows when a light is shone through it
    \item In the fingers, cysts can affect the nail or cause a small groove
    \item Symptoms that come and go, with some cysts disappearing spontaneously
    \item No redness, fever, or skin change, which would suggest infection
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other hand and wrist lumps must be distinguished.
\begin{itemize}
    \item A lipoma or other benign soft-tissue tumour
    \item A swollen tendon sheath (tenosynovitis) or nodule
    \item A foreign body reaction
    \item A bone spur or bony prominence
    \item Rarely, a malignant soft-tissue tumour, which is usually firm, fixed, and enlarging
    \item An inflamed or infected joint
    \item Imaging and examination distinguish these reliably
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Ganglion cysts are usually assessed by a doctor to confirm the diagnosis. Medical care is needed if the cyst is painful, rapidly growing, causing numbness or weakness, or if there is any doubt about what it is.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A swelling that is red, hot, and painful, with fever, which may indicate infection
    \item Sudden severe pain, deformity, or inability to move the joint after injury
    \item Rapidly increasing numbness, weakness, or loss of function
    \item Any emergency symptoms
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Examination:} the lump's size, texture, mobility, and relation to the joint; transillumination is a helpful bedside test
    \item \textbf{Ultrasound:} confirms the fluid-filled nature and shows the connection to the joint or tendon
    \item \textbf{MRI:} for deep, occult, or uncertain cysts
    \item \textbf{Aspiration:} withdrawing fluid with a needle can both confirm and treat, though recurrence is common
    \item \textbf{No blood tests:} ganglions are a local mechanical condition
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Observation:} many cysts are harmless and disappear without treatment; reassurance is often all that is needed
    \item \textbf{Aspiration:} draining the fluid with a needle, which helps some people, though the cyst recurs in up to half
    \item \textbf{Splinting:} limiting joint movement can relieve symptoms and shrink the cyst
    \item \textbf{Activity modification:} reducing repetitive loading of the joint
    \item \textbf{Surgery (excision):} removing the cyst and its stalk, offered for persistent pain, nerve compression, or cosmetic concerns; recurrence is lower than with aspiration but still occurs in about 10%
    \item \textbf{Pain relief:} simple analgesics for discomfort
    \item \textbf{What to avoid:} "busting" the cyst with a book or other object is an old practice that is not recommended, as it does not remove the stalk and can damage surrounding tissue
    \item \textbf{Post-operative care:} wound care and gradual return to activity after surgery
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Recurrence after aspiration or surgery
    \item Nerve compression causing numbness or weakness, rarely
    \item Pain that limits joint use
    \item Surgical complications: infection, scarring, stiffness, and damage to nearby nerves or vessels
    \item Cosmetic concern, though treatment is not required for appearance alone
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Ganglion cysts are benign and self-limiting in many cases: up to half disappear without treatment, and most that remain are harmless. For symptomatic cysts, aspiration provides relief for some people, and surgery cures the majority, though recurrence is possible with either. The outcome is excellent in the sense that the condition never threatens health. Most people are reassured, keep the cyst under observation, and seek treatment only if symptoms develop.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Have any new lump confirmed by a doctor to exclude other causes
    \item Leave a painless cyst alone, since many resolve spontaneously
    \item Reduce repetitive joint strain if activity aggravates it
    \item Use a splint if the joint is painful
    \item Do not attempt to burst the cyst
    \item Seek review if the cyst grows rapidly, becomes painful, or causes numbness
    \item Discuss the pros and cons of treatment with a doctor before choosing surgery
\end{itemize}
