\chapter{Intertrigo}
\index{Intertrigo}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Intertrigo is a rash that develops in the skin folds, where two areas of skin rub together and moisture collects. It most often affects the groin, armpits, under the breasts, the folds of the abdomen, and between the buttocks and toes. The warm, damp environment damages the skin surface and encourages infection with yeasts and bacteria. Intertrigo is common in people who are overweight, sweat heavily, or have diabetes, and it is more likely in hot, humid weather. It is usually treated with keeping the area dry, barrier creams, and, when infection is present, antifungal or antibacterial treatment. Prevention centres on weight management and skin-fold care.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Intertrigo is very common and affects people of all ages, though it is more frequent in adults and in people who are overweight or obese. People with diabetes, particularly those with poor glucose control, are prone to recurrent and stubborn intertrigo, and it is also more common in people with reduced mobility, who may have skin folds that stay damp. Hot, humid climates increase the risk, as does heavy sweating. Candidal infection, usually with the yeast Candida albicans, complicates many cases. Infants can develop intertrigo in the nappy area and neck folds.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Skin folds are prone to friction and trapped moisture because the two surfaces rub together and sweat cannot evaporate. The resulting warmth and dampness weaken the stratum corneum, the skin's outer barrier, causing redness and soreness. The damaged skin is then readily colonised by organisms that thrive in moist environments: the yeast Candida albicans is the most common, and bacteria such as staphylococci and streptococci, as well as other skin organisms, contribute. The interaction of friction, moisture, and infection produces the characteristic rash. Diabetes, obesity, and immune suppression increase susceptibility, and chronic cases can become lichenified or ulcerated.

\section*{Clinical Features}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Red, inflamed, sometimes shiny skin in the body folds
    \item Itching, burning, or soreness in the affected areas
    \item Moist, weeping skin, sometimes with a white or yellowish coating
    \item Small satellite spots or pustules at the edge of the rash, typical of candida
    \item A musty or yeasty smell in some cases
    \item Cracking, peeling, or raw areas where skin has broken down
    \item Worsening in hot weather, with sweating, or with tight clothing
    \item Symmetrical appearance, often on both sides of the body
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Other skin conditions in the body folds can resemble intertrigo.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Candidal infection:} the most common complication, with satellite lesions
    \item \textbf{Tinea (ringworm):} fungal infection of the skin, more defined and scaly edges
    \item \textbf{Contact dermatitis:} reactions to soaps, deodorants, or clothing
    \item \textbf{Eczema and psoriasis:} inflammatory skin conditions that can affect folds
    \item \textbf{Erythrasma:} a bacterial infection causing brown-red patches in folds
    \item \textbf{Inverse psoriasis:} psoriasis in skin folds without scaling
    \item \textbf{Bacterial cellulitis or abscess:} spreading infection requiring antibiotics
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Mild intertrigo often responds to home care, but medical review is appropriate for persistent, severe, or spreading rashes; rashes with pustules, cracks, or pain; and in people with diabetes or weakened immunity. Recurrent intertrigo warrants assessment for underlying causes such as diabetes. Any rapidly spreading redness with fever should be treated as urgent.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Rapidly spreading redness, pain, and swelling, which may indicate cellulitis
    \item Fever, chills, or feeling generally unwell
    \item Red streaks extending from the rash
    \item Signs of a serious infection in a person with diabetes or immune suppression
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{History and examination:} the sites affected, appearance of the rash, and risk factors
    \item \textbf{Skin scrapings:} microscopy and culture to identify candida, tinea, or bacteria
    \item \textbf{Blood glucose testing:} to exclude diabetes, especially in recurrent or persistent cases
    \item \textbf{Review of medicines and immune status:} in people with poor healing
    \item \textbf{Assessment of other skin conditions:} eczema, psoriasis, or contact dermatitis
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Keeping the area dry:} drying folds after washing, using absorbent pads or powders, and wearing loose, breathable clothing
    \item \textbf{Barrier creams:} zinc-based creams and emollients to protect the skin
    \item \textbf{Antifungal treatment:} creams, and sometimes oral medicines, for candidal infection
    \item \textbf{Antibacterial treatment:} for bacterial infection when present
    \item \textbf{Mild steroid creams:} short-term use for inflammation, under medical advice
    \item \textbf{Weight management:} reducing the depth of skin folds over time
    \item \textbf{Managing diabetes and sweating:} improving glucose control and treating hyperhidrosis
    \item \textbf{Optimising skin care:} gentle cleansing with non-soap washes and thorough drying
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Secondary bacterial infection and cellulitis
    \item Chronic fissures, ulceration, and pain
    \item Spread of infection to other areas
    \item Hyperpigmentation and skin changes with repeated episodes
    \item Superinfection and resistance with repeated or inappropriate treatment
    \item Effects on comfort, activity, and confidence
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
Most cases of intertrigo improve quickly with drying, barrier protection, and treatment of any infection, and many clear within one to two weeks. Recurrence is common unless the underlying factors are addressed, especially weight, sweating, and diabetes control. With consistent skin-fold care and attention to the causes, most people can keep intertrigo at bay. Chronic or repeated cases respond well to specialist dermatology review.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wash skin folds daily with a gentle, non-soap cleanser and dry thoroughly
    \item Use a hairdryer on a cool setting to dry folds completely
    \item Wear loose, natural-fibre clothing that allows air circulation
    \item Use absorbent powders or cloths in deep folds
    \item Avoid tight clothing, nylon, and synthetic underwear
    \item Treat sweating with antiperspirants or medical advice if severe
    \item Maintain a healthy weight
    \item Control diabetes and other underlying conditions
    \item Seek early treatment for signs of infection
    \item Change damp clothing promptly after exercise or swimming
\end{itemize}
