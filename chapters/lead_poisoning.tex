\chapter{Lead Poisoning}
\index{Lead Poisoning}

\section*{Definition and Overview}
Lead poisoning, or plumbism, is a toxicological condition caused by the accumulation of lead in the body. Lead is a heavy metal with no known physiological role in human biology, and it is highly toxic even at very low levels of exposure. It affects multiple organ systems, including the haematological, cardiovascular, renal, and reproductive systems, but its most devastating effects are on the central and peripheral nervous systems. Children under the age of five are particularly vulnerable because their developing brains and nervous systems are highly sensitive to lead's neurotoxic effects, and their bodies absorb a higher percentage of ingested lead than adults. Lead poisoning can present as an acute crisis, but it is more commonly a chronic, insidious condition that can cause permanent developmental and cognitive deficits before any physical symptoms become apparent.

\section*{Epidemiology}
Lead poisoning remains a significant global public health concern, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, although it also persists in high-income nations. In many countries, the prevalence of elevated blood lead levels has decreased dramatically since the phasing out of leaded petrol, lead-based paints, and lead-soldered cans starting in the late 20th century. However, legacy sources continue to pose risks, particularly in older residential areas where lead-based paint remains, and in specific industrial and mining communities such as Port Pirie in southern regions, Mount Isa in Queensland, and Broken Hill in New South Wales. Occupational exposure remains a major cause of poisoning in adults working in mining, smelting, battery recycling, radiator repair, and painting. The National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) of local guidance recommends investigation and intervention for any individual with a blood lead level of 5 micrograms per decilitre (\textmu{}g/dL) or greater.

\section*{Aetiology and Pathophysiology}
Lead enters the body through inhalation or ingestion, after which it is distributed to the blood, soft tissues, and bone, where it accumulates over time.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inhalation and ingestion sources:} Ingestion of lead-contaminated dust or soil (often via hand-to-mouth behaviour in children), consumption of water from lead-soldered pipes, ingestion of paint chips from older houses, use of lead-containing traditional cosmetics or medicines, and inhalation of industrial fumes.
    \item \textbf{Mimicry of essential cations:} Lead interferes with cell signalling and enzyme function by mimicking calcium, iron, and zinc. It binds to and activates calcium-dependent proteins, disrupting neurotransmitter release and mitochondrial function.
    \item \textbf{Enzyme inhibition:} Lead directly inhibits delta-aminolevulinic acid dehydratase ($\delta$-ALAD) and ferrochelatase, two critical enzymes in the haem synthesis pathway. This leads to an accumulation of protoporphyrin and a failure to incorporate iron into haem, resulting in microcytic, hypochromic anaemia.
    \item \textbf{Oxidative stress:} Lead induces the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and depletes intracellular glutathione, leading to lipid peroxidation, cell membrane damage, and cell death.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Clinical Features}
The clinical features of lead poisoning are non-specific and vary widely depending on the age of the patient and the duration and intensity of the exposure.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Paediatric symptoms:} Irritability, developmental delay, learning difficulties, loss of appetite, weight loss, sluggishness, fatigue, abdominal pain, vomiting, constipation, and pica (the ingestion of non-food items).
    \item \textbf{Adult symptoms:} Hypertension, joint and muscle pain, headache, memory loss, concentration difficulties, mood disorders, abdominal colic, and peripheral neuropathy (typically motor-dominant, presenting as wrist drop or foot drop).
    \item \textbf{Haematological signs:} Pallor due to microcytic anaemia, and basophilic stippling on a peripheral blood film.
    \item \textbf{Reproductive effects:} Reduced sperm count and abnormal sperm morphology in men; increased risk of miscarriage, stillbirth, and premature birth in pregnant women.
    \item \textbf{Severe acute features:} Encephalopathy, characterised by seizures, altered mental state, persistent projectile vomiting, ataxia, coma, and death.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Differential Diagnosis}
Because the symptoms of lead poisoning are diverse and non-specific, it must be differentiated from several other conditions.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Iron deficiency anaemia:} Presents with similar microcytic, hypochromic red blood cells and fatigue, but lacks the neurological and systemic manifestations of lead toxicity.
    \item \textbf{Acute abdomen:} Conditions such as appendicitis, diverticulitis, or biliary colic can be mimicked by the severe abdominal pain (lead colic) associated with acute lead toxicity.
    \item \textbf{Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and learning disabilities:} Behavioural problems and cognitive delay in children can be attributed to primary psychiatric or developmental disorders rather than lead neurotoxicity.
    \item \textbf{Peripheral neuropathies:} Diabetic neuropathy, Guillain-Barr\textsuperscript{e} syndrome, and other toxic neuropathies must be distinguished from lead-induced motor neuropathy.
    \item \textbf{Encephalopathy of other origins:} Viral encephalitis, bacterial meningitis, metabolic encephalopathies, and intracranial mass lesions must be ruled out in cases of acute neurological deterioration.
\end{itemize}

\section*{When to Seek Medical Care}
Parents who suspect their child has been exposed to lead, or adults who have occupational or environmental exposure and develop persistent headaches, fatigue, or abdominal pain, should consult a general practitioner for blood lead screening.

\textbf{Contact your local emergency services for:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Seizures, status epilepticus, or sudden loss of consciousness
    \item Acute confusion, lethargy, stupor, or inability to wake up
    \item Recurrent, severe vomiting associated with a high fever or severe headache
    \item Sudden weakness in the limbs, inability to walk, or loss of coordination
    \item Severe, unremitting abdominal pain accompanied by signs of shock (pallor, sweating, rapid heart rate)
\end{itemize}

\section*{Diagnosis and Investigations}
The definitive diagnosis of lead poisoning requires laboratory confirmation of elevated lead levels in the blood, along with investigations to assess organ damage.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Blood lead level (BLL) test:} The gold standard for diagnosis. Venous blood samples are preferred over capillary samples, which are highly prone to environmental contamination. A BLL of $\ge 5$~\textmu{}g/dL ($\ge 0.24$~\textmu{}mol/L) is the threshold for medical and environmental intervention in many countries.
    \item \textbf{Full blood count and blood film:} Demonstrates microcytic, hypochromic anaemia, and may show basophilic stippling of red blood cells (ribosomal precipitates).
    \item \textbf{Iron studies:} Performed to evaluate for concurrent iron deficiency, which increases lead absorption in the gastrointestinal tract.
    \item \textbf{Renal function tests:} Serum urea, creatinine, and electrolytes, along with urinalysis, to screen for lead-induced nephropathy (Fanconi-like syndrome).
    \item \textbf{Imaging studies:} Plain abdominal radiographs can identify ingested lead-containing foreign bodies or paint chips. Radiographs of long bones in children may reveal "lead lines" (increased radiodensity at the metaphyses), indicating chronic exposure.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Management}
The primary management of lead poisoning is the identification and immediate removal of the source of exposure. Pharmacological treatment is reserved for moderate-to-severe cases.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Environmental remediation:} Identifying and eliminating lead hazards in the home or workplace, including professional removal of lead paint, replacing lead pipes, and wet-cleaning contaminated dust.
    \item \textbf{Nutritional intervention:} Ensuring adequate intake of iron, calcium, and zinc, as deficiencies in these minerals enhance gastrointestinal absorption of lead. Vitamin C supplementation may also reduce lead absorption.
    \item \textbf{Chelation therapy:} Indicated for severe symptoms or BLL $\ge 45$~\textmu{}g/dL in children, and symptomatic adults with high levels. Chelation agents bind to lead in the blood and tissues, forming soluble complexes excreted in urine and bile.
    \item \textbf{Chelating agents:} Oral succimer (dimercaptosuccinic acid, DMSA) is the preferred agent for moderate poisoning. Intravenous sodium calcium edetate (CaNa\textsubscript{2}EDTA) and intramuscular dimercaprol (British Anti-Lewisite, BAL) are used for severe poisoning and lead encephalopathy.
    \item \textbf{Supportive care:} In cases of lead encephalopathy, intensive care monitoring, fluid restriction, and management of cerebral oedema are vital.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Complications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Neurological deficits:} Permanent reduction in IQ, learning difficulties, developmental delays, and behavioural problems in children.
    \item \textbf{Lead nephropathy:} Chronic interstitial nephritis leading to progressive renal impairment and chronic kidney disease.
    \item \textbf{Hypertension and cardiovascular disease:} Increased arterial stiffness and endothelial dysfunction, leading to elevated risks of stroke and ischaemic heart disease.
    \item \textbf{Reproductive impairment:} Persistent subfertility or infertility in both men and women, and poor obstetric outcomes.
    \item \textbf{Lead encephalopathy:} Severe cerebral oedema, herniation, and death in acute, massive poisoning.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Prognosis and Outcomes}
The prognosis depends heavily on the patient's age, the maximum blood lead concentration, and the duration of exposure. Mild-to-moderate lead poisoning in adults often resolves completely once the source of exposure is eliminated, and blood lead levels decline over months to years as lead is cleared from soft tissues. In contrast, the neurodevelopmental damage caused by lead exposure in young children is largely irreversible. Even low blood lead levels ($< 10$~\textmu{}g/dL) can have a lasting impact on cognitive performance, school achievement, and behavioural regulation. Chelation therapy reduces mortality and limits further toxicity in acute, severe cases, but it does not reverse pre-existing neurological damage.

\section*{Prevention and Self-Care}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Regular hand washing:} Wash children's hands, bottles, and toys regularly to remove lead-contaminated dust.
    \item \textbf{Wet cleaning techniques:} Clean floors, windowsills, and other surfaces with a damp mop or cloth rather than dry sweeping or vacuuming, which can disperse lead dust.
    \item \textbf{Run tap water first:} Run cold water for at least one to two minutes before using it for drinking or cooking if lead-soldered pipes are suspected. Never use hot tap water to prepare baby formula.
    \item \textbf{Use safe paint removal practices:} Do not sand, scrape, or burn older paint unless it has been tested and confirmed to be lead-free. Hire professionals trained in lead-safe work practices.
    \item \textbf{Occupational hygiene:} Change clothes, shower, and wash work clothes separately if you work in an industry with lead exposure, to prevent bringing lead dust home.
    \item \textbf{Avoid traditional cosmetics and remedies:} Do not use untested traditional cosmetics (like kohl or surma) or traditional medicines that may contain lead.
\end{itemize}
